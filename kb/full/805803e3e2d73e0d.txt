Unveiling the Potential of Biogenic Nanoparticles 
for Wastewater Remediation: A Systematic Review
Somani C. Rath · Upasana Bhagawati · 
Rakesh Kumar  · Arti Goel
Received: 7 August 2025 / Accepted: 16 September 2026 / Published online: 28 September 2026

Abstract In recent decades, escalating water pollu-
tion driven by demographic growth, industrial devel-
opment, agricultural intensification, and rapid urban 
expansion has emerged as a critical global environ-
mental challenge. Contamination of water bodies by 
heavy metals, organic compounds, and pathogenic 
microorganisms has intensified the need for sustain-
able wastewater remediation strategies. This review 
elucidates the mechanistic potential and strategies for 
wastewater remediation using biogenic nanoparticles 
(BNPs) synthesized via plant, bacterial, fungal, and 
algal systems as emerging multifunctional materials 
for addressing complex wastewater matrices. Conven-
tional wastewater treatment methods, such as coagu-
lation, flocculation, and sedimentation, have limita-
tions in efficiency, chemical demand, and operational 
costs, prompting interest in innovative BNP-based 
approaches. BNPs demonstrated enhanced adsorp-
tion, catalytic transformation, and antimicrobial 
activity due to their biomolecule-rich surfaces, ena-
bling efficient contaminant removal across industrial, 
municipal, textile, pharmaceutical, and agricultural 
wastewater systems. It has been evaluated BNP syn-
thesis routes and mechanisms, pollutant removal effi-
ciencies, environmental behavior, and implementa-
tion challenges. This review summarized that BNPs 
achieve enhanced adsorption, catalytic transforma-
tion, and antimicrobial activity, with removal effi-
ciencies of 80–98% for dyes and 70–95% for heavy 
metals, while offering improved biocompatibility and 
reduced ecotoxicity compared with chemically syn-
thesized nanoparticles. This review also highlights 
critical barriers to large-scale application, including 
synthesis variability, economic scalability, regulatory 
uncertainty, and community acceptance. Moreover, 
environmental fate analyses reveal that post-treatment 
transformations, including sulfidation, organometallic 
complexation, and surface passivation, play a decisive 
role in governing BNPs’ persistence, bioavailability, 
and ecological impact.

Rapid urbanization has led to high concentrations 
of pollutants, including heavy metals, organic &

microorganisms, in wastewater. When wastewater is 
properly treated, it can be reused for various fresh-
water-demanding purposes (Xiao et  al., 2017). Over 
the past few decades, conventional water treatment 
techniques, including sedimentation, flocculation, and 
coagulation, have consistently shown high efficiency 
in removing pollutants, ensuring effluent quality suit-
able for discharge or reuse. Furthermore, inadequate 
removal of emerging contaminants necessitates rig-
orous advancements and exploration of alternative 
approaches (Gedda et  al., 2021). Therefore, using 
nanoparticles to remove pollutants from wastewater 
is an interesting and emerging area of research (Ali 
et al., 2020).
1.1 Economic Considerations and Cost Challenges in 
Nanotechnology-Based Wastewater Treatment
While nanotechnology-based wastewater treatment is 
widely regarded as a cost-effective and efficient alter -
native to conventional methods, its overall economic 
feasibility remains context-dependent. The synthesis, 
stabilization, and large-scale deployment of nanopar -
ticles, particularly biogenic variants, entail high costs 
for precursor materials, energy inputs, and recovery 
processes (Velamakanni et  al., 2026). According to 
Zhang et al. (2025), the estimated production cost of 
high-purity nanoparticles used for wastewater treat-
ment ranges from USD 180 to 300 per gram, pri-
marily influenced by precursor selection, biological 
source, and synthesis efficiency. Moreover, challenges 
in nanoparticle regeneration, reusability, and separa-
tion from treated effluents further increase opera-
tional expenses, often raising total treatment costs by 
20–35% relative to conventional adsorption methods. 
Similarly, Ahmed et al. (2021) noted that while green 
and biogenic synthesis routes reduce the need for haz-
ardous chemicals and lower environmental costs, it 
still require optimized process control, microbial cul-
ture maintenance, and waste biomass management, 
which can offset some anticipated economic benefits. 
However, as scalable biosynthesis and nanoparticle

1.2 Nanotechnology as a Foundation for Advanced 
Pollutant Removal Strategies
Nanotechnology encompasses the synthesis and appli-
cation of a diverse range of nanomaterials, including 
structures between 1 and 100 nm that exhibit unique 
optical, catalytic, and physicochemical properties 
not found in their bulk counterparts (Mekuye et  al., 
2023). Biogenic nanoparticles are synthesized using 
plants, bacteria, fungi, and algae. Nanotechnology 
can  help to  reduce and stabilize the nanoparticles, 
therefore reducing the use of toxic chemicals. Dur -
ing the process, substances such as proteins, enzymes, 
sugars, and other naturally produced chemicals help 
turn metal ions into nano-sized particles. It also 
reduce aggregation and help maintain the nanostruc-
ture (Mughal et  al., 2021). Evaluating nanoparticles 
in environmental matrices and the resulting residues 
is essential for assessing their efficacy, stability, and 
potential ecological impact during wastewater treat-
ment. Standard testing approaches quantify removal 
performance using parameters such as adsorption 
capacity, removal efficiency, and residual pollutant 
concentration, typically determined by techniques 
such as atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS), induc-
tively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), 
and UV–Vis spectrophotometry (Bersier et al., 1994).
In addition, physicochemical characterization of 
nanoparticles before and after treatment uses meth-
ods such as dynamic light scattering (DLS) for par -
ticle size distribution, zeta potential analysis for 
surface charge and colloidal stability, and electron 
microscopy (SEM/TEM) for morphological assess-
ment (Wadhwa et al., 2022). Leaching studies, stabil-
ity assessments, and toxicity assays (e.g., microbial 
inhibition or ecotoxicity tests) further evaluate nano-
particle fate and transformation in treated water and 
sludge residues, helping minimize secondary environ-
mental risks. Collectively, these analytical and moni-
toring strategies provide a comprehensive framework 
for assessing nanoparticle behavior, treatment perfor -
mance, and environmental safety in wastewater reme-
diation systems (Abdulsada et al., 2021).
Nanomaterials are widely used in biomedicine, 
pharmaceuticals, electronics, environmental detec-
tion, cosmetics, and textile industries (Ilangovan et al., 
2021). In wastewater treatment, their exceptionally 
high surface area-to-volume ratio enhances photocata-
lytic activity, increases adsorption capacity, and ena-
bles rapid pollutant removal. Thus, nano-adsorbents 
and nanocatalysts are widely used to remediate organic 
and inorganic contaminants; for instance, cobalt oxide 
nanoparticles for dye degradation and metal oxide or 
carbon-based nanoparticles for heavy metal removal 
(Dehghani et al., 2019; Rashid et al., 2021).
In recent years, research attention has also increased 
on biogenic nanoparticles, a subclass of nanomaterials 
synthesized via biological routes using plants, bacte-
ria, fungi, algae, or yeast (Bokolia et al., 2025). These 
BNPs are generated from naturally occurring biomol-
ecules that serve as reducing, stabilizing, and capping 
agents, thereby imparting biocompatibility and unique 
surface functionalities. Their biologically derived 
coatings enhance selectivity, catalytic efficiency, and 
environmental safety, making them particularly rel-
evant for wastewater management, treatment facili-
ties, and soil amendment authorities (Sharma et  al., 
2024). Introducing BNPs into this framework offers 
a sustainable alternative to chemically synthesized 
nanoparticles because of their eco-friendly synthe-
sis, reduced toxicity, and use of renewable biological 
resources, while enabling a deeper understanding of 
their mechanisms and applications in advanced waste-
water remediation (Nain et al., 2025).
Nanoparticles such as carbon nanotubes, zero-
valent nanoparticles, metal oxide nanoparticles, and 
nanocomposites have been widely used in wastewa-
ter remediation because of their high surface reactiv -
ity and tunable physicochemical properties (Reza-
nia et  al., 2024). Researchers quantitatively assess 
their effectiveness using parameters such as adsorp-
tion capacity, removal efficiency, reaction kinetics, 
and residual pollutant concentrations, which col-
lectively guide their integration into advanced treat-
ment systems, including membranes, functionalized 
adsorbents, and catalytic materials (El-Sayed et  al., 
2020). Nanoparticles enhance the adsorptive and 
controlled properties of certain materials for specific 
applications. This provides a variety of options for

Therefore, this review investigates the potential 
implications of BNPs for wastewater treatment and 
pollutant removal, including organic pollutants. Fur -
thermore, the objectives of this review are (a) chal-
lenges with respect to conventional treatment meth-
ods, (b) multifaceted decontamination strategies, (c) 
fate and barriers to BNPs in water treatment, and (d) 
regulatory frameworks for environmental risk mitiga-
tion. This work advances water technologies, focus-
ing on environmentally friendly remediation and 
sustainability.
The novelty of the present review lies in its com-
prehensive, multidisciplinary synthesis of recent 
advances in biogenic nanoparticle-mediated wastewa-
ter remediation. Unlike earlier reviews that primarily 
emphasized metal- or plant-derived nanoparticles, 
this work provides holistic coverage of biogenic sys-
tems, encompassing microbial-, fungal-, algal-, and 
plant-mediated nanoparticles, and elucidates their col-
lective mechanisms for the removal of heavy metals, 
dyes, organic pollutants, and pathogenic microorgan-
isms. Furthermore, the review provides mechanistic 
insights across three distinct nanoscale strategies like 
nanoadsorbents, nanocatalysts, and nanomembranes, 
emphasizing how biogenic synthesis influences their 
physicochemical properties, surface reactivity, and 
pollutant removal efficiencies. This comparative 
mechanistic evaluation remains underexplored in the 
existing literature. Another distinctive contribution is 
its critical evaluation of the post-treatment fate, trans-
formation, and potential ecotoxicological impacts of 
biogenic nanoparticles in aquatic environments, sup-
ported by recent quantitative studies (Morales et al., 
2022; Mukhopadhyay et  al., 2022). Additionally, 
the manuscript assesses emerging global regulatory 
frameworks and environmental risk mitigation poli-
cies, with particular attention to European Union and 
Indian perspectives, addressing an area often over -
looked in prior reviews. Finally, this review follows a 
PRISMA-guided systematic approach and synthesizes 
evidence from peer-reviewed studies (2019–2025). 
Together, these features make this work a novel and 
comprehensive contribution to sustainable wastewater 
nanoremediation.
2 Literature Review: Methodology
2.1 Search Strategy and Databases Used
A systematic literature search was conducted on June 
26, 2025, in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 
guidelines to ensure transparency, reproducibility, 
and scientific integrity. The search was conducted 
exclusively using peer-reviewed, citation-indexed 
databases, Web of Science (WoS) and Scopus, to 
maintain academic rigor and source reliability. Search 
strings combined Boolean operators and specific key-
words, including: “biogenic nanomaterials,” “green 
synthesis,” “biogenic nanoparticles,” “nanotechnol-
ogy,” “wastewater remediation,” and “water treat-
ment.” The search was restricted to peer-reviewed 
articles published between 2019 and 2025 and writ-
ten in English. The reference lists of key papers were 
also manually screened to ensure inclusion of any 
additional relevant studies. Clearly defined inclusion 
and exclusion criteria guided study selection, ensur -
ing methodological transparency and academic rigor. 
Only peer-reviewed, English-language research and 
review articles published between 2019 and 2025 
were included. Studies were further filtered through 
explicit inclusion criteria: (i) use of plant, microbial, 
fungal, or algal systems for nanoparticle synthesis; (ii)

Vol.: (0123456789)
evaluation of BNP performance in removing heavy 
metals, dyes, organic pollutants, pharmaceuticals, or 
pathogens; and (iii) mechanistic or application-spe-
cific insights relevant to environmental remediation. 
Exclusion criteria eliminated studies focused solely 
on chemical synthesis, nanoparticle characterization 
without application, or unrelated nanomaterial types. 
Each full-text article was critically evaluated based 
on methodological rigor, experimental relevance, and 
contribution to the central objective of this review. 
Eligible studies were required to provide quantitative 
data, mechanistic explanations, or comparative analy-
ses of pollutant removal efficiency using biogenic 
nanomaterials derived from microbial, algal, fungal, 
or plant sources. Publications written in English and 
containing sufficient experimental or analytical detail 
to enable critical evaluation were prioritized.
To maintain scientific reliability, non-peer-
reviewed materials, such as conference proceedings, 
theses, reports, and editorials, were excluded. Studies 
that addressed only chemically or physically synthe-
sized nanoparticles, without any biogenic component, 
were also excluded, as were papers lacking clearly 
described methodologies, quantitative outcomes, or 
pollutant-specific data relevant to wastewater remedi-
ation. Duplicate records and publications outside the 
predefined time frame were removed during screen-
ing. This systematic inclusion–exclusion frame-
work ensured that only high-quality, methodologi-
cally sound, and contextually relevant studies were 
included in the final synthesis, thereby enhancing the 
validity and reproducibility of the review’s findings.
2.2 Data Collection Procedure
The articles obtained from the literature search were 
selected based on their topics of interest, including 
conventional wastewater treatment techniques and 
the  role of nanoparticles as nanoadsorbents, nano-
catalysts, and nanomembranes. Additionally, various 
decontamination methods, including the  removal of 
heavy metals, organic pollutants, and dyes, as well as 
the  elimination of pathogenic organisms, were stud-
ied. This systematic review followed the PRISMA 
2020 guidelines to ensure transparency, reproducibil-
ity, and academic reliability. The literature search was 
conducted exclusively in two highly credible, peer-
reviewed, citation-indexed databases: Web of Sci-
ence (WoS) and Scopus, to ensure inclusion of only 
high-quality scientific studies. The search was con-
ducted on June 26, 2025, and included publications 
from January 2019 to June 2025. A combination of 
Boolean operators and specific keywords was applied 
to identify relevant studies, including: “green synthe-
sis,” “biogenic nanoparticles,” “biogenic nanomateri-
als,” “nanotechnology,” “wastewater treatment,” and 
“water remediation.”
During the identification phase, a total of 779 stud-
ies were retrieved, 412 from Web of Science and 367 
from Scopus. Out of all, 128 duplicate records were 
identified and removed, leaving 651 unique articles 
for initial screening. In the screening phase, the titles 
and abstracts of these 651 records were reviewed to 
determine their relevance to biogenic nanoparticle 
applications in wastewater treatment. At this stage, 
509 articles were excluded due to irrelevance, dupli-
cation of concepts, or lack of a clear connection to 
the review’s focus area. The remaining 142 articles 
proceeded to the eligibility assessment phase. Dur -
ing the eligibility phase, we evaluated each of the 
142 full-text articles against predefined inclusion and 
exclusion criteria, including experimental design, 
methodological soundness, data reliability, and direct 
relevance to biogenic nanotechnology-based waste-
water remediation. After this analysis, 70 studies 
were excluded for inadequate methodological detail, 
lack of quantitative data, limited focus on biogenic 
processes, or insufficient reproducibility. Finally, 72 
studies met all eligibility criteria and were included 
in the qualitative synthesis of this review. These stud-
ies collectively represent the most recent and meth-
odologically robust advances in biogenic nanomate-
rial synthesis, characterization, and application in 
wastewater remediation. Figure 1 illustrates the entire 
stepwise selection process, including identification, 
screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion.

The twentieth century saw significant advancements 
in wastewater remediation, including sedimenta-
tion, flocculation, coagulation, chemical precipita-
tion, and trickling filters (Saxena et  al., 2025). The

3.1 Sedimentation
Sedimentation is one of the oldest and most funda-
mental physical treatment processes used for waste-
water clarification. It primarily relies on the gravi-
tational settling of suspended particles and colloids 
to separate solids from liquid effluents (Peters et al., 
1984). Although it remains a critical pretreatment 
step in most wastewater treatment plants, traditional 
sedimentation is often inefficient at removing fine 
particulates and dissolved pollutants, and it requires 
large spaces and long retention times (Rezai et  al.,

3.2 Flocculation
Flocculation uses flocculants to agglomerate dis-
persed particles into larger, settleable flocs. It 
improves the  removal of colloidal and suspended 
particles, enhances solid–liquid separation, and 
can be combined with other methods (Wang et al., 
2025a, b, c). However, it raises operational costs 
due to chemical additions. Another risk is inef-
ficient floc formation from poor mixing or over -
dosing, leading to sludge that requires further 
treatment. Coagulation destabilizes colloidal and 
suspended particles by neutralizing their surface 
charges. It effectively removes turbidity, suspended 
solids, and colloidal particles. Coagulation does 
not effectively remove dissolved contaminants 
(Zhao et al., 2021).
3.3 Chemical Precipitation
Chemical precipitation is a widely employed phys-
icochemical method for the removal of dissolved 
inorganic contaminants, particularly heavy metals, 
phosphates, and fluorides, through the formation 
of insoluble precipitates (Wang et al., 2021). While 
this process is operationally simple, effective, and 
can achieve high removal efficiencies (up to 95% for 
metal ions such as Cu2⁺, Pb2⁺, and Cd2⁺), it presents 
considerable challenges related to sludge generation 
and post-treatment disposal (Pohl, 2020). Recent 
assessments indicate that the chemical sludge pro-
duced during precipitation processes often contains 
concentrated levels of heavy metals and residual 
reagents, posing a risk of secondary pollution if not 
properly stabilized or managed. According to Wang 
et al. (2023), the leaching potential of metal-laden 
sludge can lead to groundwater contamination and 
soil toxicity, particularly under uncontrolled land-
disposal conditions. Moreover, commonly used 
chemical reagents (e.g., lime, alum, and ferric salts) 
increase salinity and cause pH fluctuations in efflu-
ents, complicating downstream biological treatment 
(Samer et al., 2015).
To mitigate these issues, recent studies have 
explored sludge valorization and recovery 
approaches, including converting metal-rich resi-
dues into value-added adsorbents or catalysts, 
thereby promoting a circular resource recovery 
model. Integrating precipitation with biogenic 
nanoparticle systems or membrane-assisted hybrid 
processes has also been shown to reduce sludge 
volume by nearly 40%, improve removal efficiency, 
and minimize the risk of secondary environmen-
tal contamination (Wang et al., 2023). Thus, while 
chemical precipitation remains a reliable treatment 
method for specific pollutants, sustainable sludge 
management and minimization strategies with bio-
genic nanoparticles are critical for reducing its envi-
ronmental footprint and ensuring long-term viabil-
ity in modern wastewater treatment systems (Sanni 
et al., 2024).

4 New Innovative Strategies for Wastewater 
Remediation: Biogenic Nanoparticles 
for Sustainable Wastewater Treatment 
and their Mechanistic Synthesis Pathways
Unlike conventional chemical synthesis, biogenic 
approaches rely on the intrinsic biochemical machin-
ery of plants, bacteria, fungi, or algae to reduce 
metal ions into nanoscale particles under mild, non-
toxic conditions (Sheershwal et  al., 2025). BNPs 
have emerged as a transformative class of materials 
precisely because their biomolecule-rich surfaces, 
redox-active cores, and structural tunability enable 
mechanisms inaccessible via conventional treat-
ment systems (Tl et  al., 2025). BNPs synthesized 
from plant extracts, bacteria, fungi, and algae show 
enhanced electron-transfer capabilities, abundant 
surface functional groups, and catalytic activity, ena-
bling pollutant transformation in diverse wastewater 
systems (Rath et al., 2025a, b). For example, biogenic 
metal oxides (e.g., Fe₃O₄, ZnO) demonstrate high 
adsorption capacities for Cr(VI), Pb(II), and As(III) 
in industrial effluents; biogenic TiO₂ and Ag nano-
particles exhibit strong photocatalytic and antimicro-
bial activity in pharmaceutical and hospital wastewa-
ter; and carbonaceous BNPs derived from microbial 
and algal biomass efficiently remove textile dyes and 
polyaromatic hydrocarbons through π–π interactions 
and surface oxidation pathways (Gouda et al., 2025; 
Mabudi et al., 2025).
In plant-mediated synthesis, phytochemicals such 
as flavonoids, terpenoids, alkaloids, and phenolic 
acids act as both reducing and capping agents, facili-
tating the conversion of metal precursors into stable 
nanoparticles with controlled morphology and crys-
tallinity (Shahzadi et  al., 2025). Bacterial synthesis 
involves enzymatic reduction through nitrate reduc-
tases, hydrogenases, and metallothioneins, often 
yielding nanoparticles with uniform dispersion and 
high biocompatibility (Xie et al., 2025). Meanwhile, 
fungal-mediated pathways exploit extracellular secre-
tion of enzymes and polysaccharides, yielding nano-
particles with superior surface functionalization and 
colloidal stability (Rath et  al., 2025a, b). According 
to Wang et  al., (2025a, b, c), the synergy between 
metabolic precursors and secreted biomolecules in 
these biological systems provides precise control 
over nucleation and growth kinetics, enhancing the 
catalytic and adsorptive properties of the resulting 
nanoparticles. Across the reviewed studies, BNPs 
consistently demonstrate advantages stemming from 
their biological capping agents, enhanced reactiv -

chemically synthesized nanomaterials (Mughal et al.,

4.1 Nanoadsorbents
Nanomaterials are effective adsorbents and suit all 
classes of anionic and cationic pollutants. Nanoadsor-
bents possess a large surface area, variable pore size 
and surface chemistry, easy delivery, low-temperature 
modification, and short intraparticle diffusion dis-
tance. Like traditional adsorbents, biogenic nano-
particles adsorb contaminants via physicochemical 
mechanisms (Naser et  al., 2021). Adsorption is the 
process by which materials (adsorbates) build up on 
the surface-active sites of an adsorbent through chem-
ical or physical bonding. Various adsorbents can effi-
ciently remove organic and inorganic pollutants with-
out producing secondary contamination. The amount 
of adsorbate that a material can hold before reaching 
equilibrium is measured by its adsorption capacity 
(Mofokeng et al., 2024).
Biological macromolecules in the extract, whether 
from green synthesis or chemical reduction, reduce

and cap zero-valent metallic nanoparticles, which 
are then used as nano-adsorbents (Bakhtiari et  al., 
2024). Nanoparticles employed as adsorbents for 
heavy metal removal should be non-toxic, have a high 
adsorption capacity, be able to adsorb pollutants at 
low concentrations, be easily removed from the adsor-
bent surface, and be recyclable multiple times (Man-
cardi et al., 2023). The structure and morphology of 
nanoadsorbent materials also significantly affect their 
characteristics and efficiency (Malhotra et al., 2023). 
This adsorbent has numerous reactive sites, a large 
surface area, and the catalytic properties necessary 
for water remediation. Beyond morphology, surface 
chemistry influences how nanoadsorbents interact 
with harmful pollutants, determining mechanisms 
such as electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, 
and specific chemical reactions. Surface functional 
groups and charge distribution in nano-adsorbents 
significantly affect their adsorption capacity, selec-
tivity, and overall efficacy in removing harmful pol-
lutants from wastewater, as shown in Fig.  2 (Nworie 
et al., 2024).
Adsorbents play a crucial role in treating biogenic 
wastewater through physical and chemical interac-
tions. Physical adsorption is based on weak van der 
Waals forces, which allow contaminants, such as 
organic molecules, to bind to the adsorbent surface. 
Chemical adsorption strengthens the interactions 
between pollutants and the nanoadsorbent, thereby 
enhancing the removal of heavy metals and certain 
organic compounds (Alijamali et al., 2021).
Ion exchange is a type of chemical adsorption in 
which ions are exchanged between the adsorbent and 
wastewater. It is used to remove pollutants such as 
dyes and heavy metals. Some adsorbents incorporate 
biological processes to removal contaminants through 
microbial metabolism and surface colonization, 
which is particularly beneficial for removing organic 
pollutants and pathogens (Velusamy et  al., 2021). 
Effective biogenic wastewater management thus 
requires combining these mechanisms, each tailored 
to the specific pollutants present and aimed at achiev-
ing treatment outcomes (Dehghani et al., 2023).

oxide, and black carbon. Other adsorbents used for 
wastewater remediation include copper nanoparticles, 
iron oxides, gold, silver–gold, zinc oxide, silver, iron, 
and magnesium oxide (Sekar et  al., 2023). Magnetic

highly sensitive, exhibit adequate selectivity, have 
high adsorption efficiency for molecular oxygen, have 
a large surface area, and are low-toxicity, so these are 
used for the photocatalytic degradation of pollutants 
in wastewater. Nanoparticles of selenium, palladium, 
and platinum are present in an amorphous form and 
exhibit ligand-like adsorption processes, allowing 
them to successfully adsorb heavy metals, such as 
zinc, nickel, copper, mercury, and cadmium (Chan-

Selenium (Se), palladium (Pd), and platinum (Pt) 
nanoparticles have attracted increasing attention for 
their exceptional catalytic, redox, and antimicro-
bial properties, making them promising candidates 
for advanced wastewater remediation (Barani et al., 
2023). Biogenically synthesized Se nanoparticles 
exhibit strong antioxidative and photocatalytic 
activity, enabling efficient degradation of organic 
pollutants and detoxification of heavy metals. How -
ever, recent studies show that oxidative biotransfor -
mation of Se nanoparticles into selenite or selenate 
can induce cytotoxic and genotoxic effects in aquatic 
organisms at concentrations above threshold levels 
(Yu et al., 2025). These findings underline the need 
for controlled dosage and post-treatment monitoring 
to prevent secondary environmental contamination. 
Similarly, lead (Pb) and platinum (Pt) nanoparticles 
exhibit excellent catalytic activity in hydrogenation 
and oxidation reactions, thereby enabling the degra-
dation of recalcitrant organic compounds and per -
sistent dyes. Despite their high functional efficiency, 
concerns remain regarding their long-term stability 
and potential ecotoxicity. Prolonged environmental 
exposure can cause surface oxidation, aggregation, 
and partial ion release, altering nanoparticle reac-
tivity and reducing catalytic performance over time 
(Yu et al., 2025). Furthermore, chronic exposure to 
Pd and Pt nanoparticles has been linked to oxidative 
stress and inflammatory responses in aquatic biota, 
underscoring the importance of lifecycle assess-
ment and safe disposal strategies (Leso et al., 2018).
To mitigate these challenges, recent approaches 
focus on surface modification and biomolecule cap-
ping to enhance the stability, dispersion, and bio-
compatibility of selenium, lead, and platinum nano-
particles (Javed et  al., 2020). Incorporating natural 
polymeric stabilizers (e.g., chitosan, alginate) and 
proteinaceous capping agents derived from microbial 
or plant extracts has been shown to prolong nano-
particle stability for over six months under ambient 
conditions while reducing leaching and toxicity (Zare 
et  al., 2021). Thus, future research should prioritize 
optimizing green synthesis and ecotoxicological eval-
uation to ensure the sustainable deployment of noble 
and metalloid biogenic nanoparticles in wastewater 
treatment systems.
The Langmuir isotherm model assumes monolayer 
adsorption on a homogeneous surface with a finite 
number of binding sites, representing systems in which 
adsorption occurs uniformly, without interactions 
between adsorbed molecules (Butyrskaya et  al., 2024). 
Conversely, the Freundlich isotherm model accounts for 
multilayer adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces, indicat-
ing non-uniform energy distribution and varying adsorp-
tion affinities. The applicability of each model depends 
on the physicochemical nature of both the nanoadsorbent 
and the pollutant species (Isiuku et al., 2021).
Recent studies have demonstrated that biogenic 
metal oxide and carbon-based nano-adsorbents fol-
low the Langmuir model for heavy metal removal 
(e.g., Cu2⁺, Pb2⁺), indicating monolayer binding, 
whereas organic dye adsorption often aligns with the 
Freundlich model due to complex surface interac-
tions (Helmi et al., 2025; Panda et al., 2025). Despite

4.2 Nanocatalysts
Nanocatalysis is an innovative approach that uses 
nanoparticles and plays a significant role in wastewa-
ter remediation, alongside adsorption, by converting 
contaminants into more innocuous forms (Mallikar -
junaiah et  al., 2020). It degrades persistent organic 
compounds by transferring electrons to contaminants, 
causing them to break down into benign molecules 
(Abbas et  al., 2017). Biogenic nanoparticles have 
shown superior catalytic performance in eliminating 
hazardous, refractory organic contaminants. Indus-
trial processes that use pesticides, organic or inor -
ganic solvents, pharmaceuticals, and hazardous met-
als generate most of these pollutants (Fig. 3).
In nanocatalysis, light interacting with metallic 
nanoparticles leads to high, broad-spectrum photocat-
alytic activity. Thus, nanophotocatalysts can aggre-
gate in water and air, limiting their nanocatalytic 
activity. Their high size-to-volume ratio and shape-
dependent characteristics enhance catalytic respon-
siveness and make them useful for wastewater reme-
diation (Mohd Raub et al., 2024).
Copper oxide nanoparticles, zinc oxide nanopar -
ticles, iron oxide nanoparticles, and titanium oxide 
nanoparticles are used for the photocatalytic degra-
dation of various substances, including dyes, heavy 
metals, pesticides, chlorinated and non-chlorinated 
hydrocarbons, and aromatic compounds, into harm-
less byproducts (Rathod et al., 2024). Biogenic nan-
oparticles, such as cadmium sulfide nanoparticles 
synthesized from Trichoderma harzianum, act as 
photocatalysts for degrading methylene blue under 
UV light. Cadmium sulfide nanoparticles destroyed 
38% of the methylene blue dye after 60  min. This

Biogenically synthesized nanocatalysts, particu-

such as CO₂ and H₂ O (Kabir et al., 2025). However, 
beyond their catalytic efficiency, the environmen-

have emerged as critical factors influencing their 
sustainability. According to Jiang et al. (2025), pro-

systems can lead to surface oxidation, partial dissolu-

metal ions, such as Cu2⁺, Fe2⁺, or Zn2⁺, into the envi-

toxicity, disrupt microbial balance, and induce oxida-
tive stress in aquatic organisms. Moreover, aggrega-
tion of nanocatalysts under natural water conditions 
can reduce their active surface area, thereby altering 
their catalytic efficiency and transport dynamics in 
sediments (Onotu et al., 2025).
Recent studies emphasize the importance of 
nanocatalyst recovery, immobilization, and lifecycle 
management to mitigate these environmental risks 
(Damian et al., 2025; Fahim et al., 2025). Strategies 
such as magnetic recovery, polymer encapsulation, 
and surface functionalization with biopolymers or 
green ligands have been shown to significantly reduce 
leaching and enhance reusability. Jiang et  al. (2025) 
showed that immobilized nanocatalysts retained over 
90% of their catalytic efficiency after five cycles 
while releasing negligible metal (< 2%) into treated 
effluents. Therefore, while nanocatalysts remain a 
cornerstone of advanced oxidation and photocatalytic 
treatment technologies, future research must integrate 
fate assessment, ecotoxicological monitoring, and cir-
cular recovery mechanisms to ensure safe, sustainable 
application in real-world wastewater systems.
4.3 Nanomembranes
Nanomembranes use nanoparticles to improve 
toxin removal from wastewater by separating parti-
cles from the water. Due to their smaller pore size, 
nanomembranes can filter out even the smallest par -
ticles, including viruses and bacteria (Shukla et  al., 
2024). Nanomembranes are typically 100  nm thick, 
selectively permeable, and highly uniform, offer -
ing efficiency, reduced processing time, and ease of 
handling, making them suitable for water remedia-
tion (Bhoj et  al., 2021). Nanomembranes effectively 
remove dyes, heavy metals, and other contaminants 
from wastewater (Fig.  4). To enhance specific prop-
erties, researchers blend biogenic nanocomposite 
membranes with synthetic and biological materials. 
These adaptable membranes are used in environmen-
tal applications (pollutant removal and water purifica-
tion) and in biomedicine (drug delivery, antimicro-
bial coatings, and wound healing). Their multilayer 
construction maintains filtration effectiveness while 
strongly rejecting unwanted materials. Due to their 
affordability and selectivity, nanomembranes, which 
fall between ultrafiltration and reverse osmosis, hold 
great promise for removing heavy metals (Chauke 
et al., 2025).
Various types of nanotubes, nanofibers, and nanor-
ibbons function as membranes for wastewater reme-
diation. These nanostructures have large surface areas 
and high reactivity, enabling effective adsorption and 
catalytic degradation of contaminants in wastewater 
(Das et al., 2024). Furthermore, their small size facili-
tates easier dispersion throughout the water matrix, 
increasing contact with pollutants. Additionally, their 
tunable features allow modification for specific con-
taminants, making them well-suited to various treat-
ment processes, including filtration, adsorption, and 
photocatalysis (Khan et al., 2022).
The mechanism behind using a nanomembrane for 
wastewater remediation involves both physical and 
electrostatic interactions. Impurities larger than the 
membrane pore size are physically blocked, while 
charged particles are repelled or attracted depending 
on the membrane’s surface charge (Tripathy et  al., 
2023). This selective filtration technique effectively 
removes impurities, yielding treated water that meets 
quality standards for reuse or environmental dis-
charge. Nanomembranes consequently offer a long-
term, efficient solution for treating biogenic waste-
water, thereby conserving water and protecting the 
environment (Alshehri et al., 2025).
Advances in nanotechnology have made fabricating 
nanocomposite membranes increasingly important and 
efficient (Esfahani et al., 2019). As a result, researchers

remains a major operational bottleneck that critically 
limits long-term performance and scalability (Zhang

biofilms on the membrane surface or within its pores 
(Liu et al., 2025a, b). This phenomenon induces pore 
blockage, increases transmembrane pressure, and 
reduces permeate flux, ultimately reducing filtration 
efficiency and shortening the membrane’s operational 
lifespan (Diez et al., 2020).

nanostructural and surface-engineering approaches.

Nanofiltration is a very crucial aspect of nanomem-

nanoscale range (0.5–2  nm) and a molecular weight

and ultrafiltration. Nanofiltration membranes exhibit 
higher hydrodynamic resistance and are more com-

including surface coating, surface graft modifica-

membranes have become increasingly important 
because of improvements in lifespan, permeate qual-

have been widely used as membrane materials in 
wastewater treatment; however, concerns remain 
about their fouling tendencies and long-term stabil-
ity (Karami et  al., 2020). Nanofiltration membranes 
have also played a crucial role in removing organic 
pollutants. Chlorine disinfection is crucial for elimi-
nating microbial growth observed in nanofiltration 
distribution systems. Nanofiltration membranes, char-
acterized by high removal of organic materials and 
low retention of inorganic materials, can produce 
high-quality water while reducing microbial growth 
(Allaoui et al., 2022).
5 Mechanistic Insights and BNPs Advantages
Modern wastewater consists of a complex mixture of 
heavy metals, dyes, organic pollutants, pharmaceuti-
cals, and pathogenic microorganisms (Fig.  5). While 
nanomaterials have gained prominence as versatile 
tools for contaminant removal, the synthesis reveals 
that biogenic nanoparticles generated through plant, 
microbial, fungal, or algal pathways offer distinct 
mechanistic advantages over conventional nanomate-
rials and treatment methods (Kapoor et al., 2021).

mechanisms are particularly effective in electroplat-

removal efficiencies of 70–95% have been consistently 
reported. Such activity directly addresses limitations of 
conventional precipitation and flocculation, which often 
fail at trace concentrations or produce excessive sludge 
(Wang et al., 2025a, b, c).
One of the most common heavy metal pollutants is 
hexavalent chromium (Cr6+), which adversely affects 
aquatic environments (He et  al., 2020). Eliminating 
Cr(VI) from wastewater before release is crucial, as 
excessive amounts can harm the respiratory tract, 
induce various malignancies, and be fatal. Bacterial 
species such as Shewanella and Geobacter can syn-
thesize biogenic palladium nanoparticles that effi-
ciently remove chromium from wastewater. High 
concentrations of arsenic, iron, and manganese are 
present in both groundwater and surface water, which 
can impact human health and aquatic ecology (Tong 
et  al., 2021). Pseudomonas QJX-1 extracted from 
the manganese mines is capable of oxidizing manga-
nese (II) and is utilized in the production of biogenic 
iron–manganese oxides. These oxides can both oxi-
dize and adsorb arsenic (Ye et al., 2023).
Another extremely hazardous heavy metal naturally 
present in the environment is cadmium. Contamina-
tion of water with cadmium causes diseases, such as 
itai-itai, and too much consumption of Cd2+ can cause 
other health-related risks, which include high blood 
pressure, bone marrow cancer, renal damage, and gas-
trointestinal problems. Biogenic nanoparticles have 
been applied extensively to remove cadmium from 
water. EPS, or Extracellular polymeric substances of 
the marine bacteria P. aeruginosa JP-11, were used to 
synthesize cadmium nanoparticles, which were then 
utilized for preconcentrating Cadmium from simulated 
wastewater (Ghosh et al., 2022).

5.2 Organic Pollutants and Pharmaceuticals: 
Catalysis-Driven BNPs Transformation Pathways
Rapid urbanization has significantly increased organic 
pollutant concentrations in water. Organic pollutants 
encompass a range of substances, including xenobiotics 
such as insecticides, synthetic dyes, pesticides, aromatic 
hydrocarbons, pharmaceuticals, halogenated hydro-
carbons, and phenols (Akhtar et  al., 2021) (Fig.  6). 
Human health and the environment will be negatively 
affected by trace amounts of organic pollutants found in 
soil, water, air, and even food (Madhav et al., 2020). To 
eliminate organic pollutants, nano-enabled technologies 
use novel products such as nanoadsorbents, nanocata-
lysts, and nanofilms (Fig.  6). Organic environmental 
pollutants can also be monitored using nanobiosensors 
in which the functional element includes a transducer 
element that signals the downstream event and a rec-
ognition element that binds to the target component 
(Ekrami et al., 2021).
The degradation of persistent micropollutants is 
facilitated by biogenic palladium nanoparticles (bio-
Pd NPs). An electrochemical cell that generated H2 as 
the electron donor enabled the regulated synthesis of 
bio-Pd NPs of various sizes (Law et al., 2022). Supe-
rior catalytic activity was demonstrated by larger NPs 
(39.0  nm) generated at lower H2 flow rates, which 
removed 92.1% of the methyl orange, whereas smaller 
NPs (23.2 nm) removed only 44.3%. The 39.0 nm bio-
Pd NPs successfully removed eight micropollutants at 
environmentally relevant concentrations (ng/L to μg/L) 
when applied to secondary-treated municipal wastewa-
ter. The removal efficiencies ranged from 69.5% (ibu-
profen) to 97.2% (lorazepam). More than 90% of fluor-
inated antibiotics were eliminated (Law et al., 2023).
Unlike the previously discussed inorganic pollutants, 
these compounds exhibit complex molecular structures, 
high chemical stability, and resistance to conventional 
biological degradation, which contribute to their long-
term persistence in aquatic ecosystems (Thakur et  al., 
2025). Many organic pollutants, including polycyclic 
aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), synthetic dyes, endo-
crine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), and pharmaceutical 
active compounds (PhACs), are highly hydrophobic, 
facilitating adsorption onto sediments and biomagnifi-
cation across trophic levels (Zou et al., 2021). Molecu-
lar polarity, functional group chemistry, and electron-
withdrawing substituents govern their environmental 
behavior by influencing reactivity and interactions with 
advanced nanomaterials used in wastewater treatment. 
BNPs play a synergistic role not only in promoting 
degradation but also in accelerating mineralization 
and reducing toxic intermediate formation, a limita-
tion commonly observed in classical photocatalysis 
(Hamza et  al., 2024). In pharmaceutical wastewater 
treatment, BNPs have achieved 80–95% degradation 
within 60–120 min, surpassing conventional oxidation 
processes that often require chemical additives or high-
energy inputs (Qin et al., 2019).

Catalysis Synergy
Dyes are a major concern because of their adverse 
effects on the environment and human health. When

and dyeing industries disrupt aquatic plant photosyn-

Various physicochemical methods, including ozo-
nation, membrane filtration, adsorption, ultrafiltration, 
and advanced oxidation processes via photocatalysis 
or chemical reduction, can treat textile effluents and 
address, this issue. Because of their exceptional stabil-
ity, these methods are ineffective for degrading dyes 
(Katheresan et  al., 2018). Because metallic nanopar -

for wastewater treatment. Thus, catalytic degradation 
is considered one of the most effective techniques for 
dye degradation. By photocatalysis, harmful com-
pounds can be converted into less hazardous or non-
toxic ones. Reports have described the use of several 
metal nanoparticles, including silver, gold, platinum, 
palladium, and iron, as catalysts for degrading various 
harmful dyes (Harikumar et al., 2022).
Removal of methylene blue via biological pro-
cesses is challenging due to its toxicity. The algal 
species Chlorella pyrenoidosa was used to bio-
genically synthesize silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs), 
which exhibited nanophotocatalytic activity in 
remediating methylene blue. Biogenic silver nano-
particles outperformed commercially synthesized 
silver nanoparticles in photocatalytic degradation of 
methylene blue (Ifijen et al., 2022). Remarkably, E. 
harbicola biomass produced selenium nanoparticles 
that strongly absorbed methylene blue from waste-
water via photocatalytic degradation. Biogenic zinc 
oxide nanoparticles derived from Hibiscus rosa-sin-
ensis leaf extract can adsorb Congo red from waste-
water, thereby playing a crucial role in wastewater 
remediation (El-Behery et al., 2023).
Ag NPs synthesized via green methods using 
Linum usitatissimum extract demonstrate multi-
ple applications  and achieved 67.13% photodeg-
radation of methylene blue, demonstrating effec-
tive photocatalytic performance for wastewater 
treatment. Additionally, these biogenic Ag NPs 
serve as enzyme-free electrochemical sensors for 
hydrogen peroxide detection, exhibit potential as 
antibacterial and anticancer agents, and thus rep-
resent versatile biological agents for environmen-
tal and medical applications (Karimi et  al., 2023). 
Ag NPs were synthesized from T. foenum-graecum 
(fenugreek) seed extract for the degradation of dyes 
such as methyl orange, methylene blue, and eosin 
Y. To effectively remove dyes such as 4-nitrophe-
nol, methylene blue, methyl orange, phenol red, and 
direct blue 24, which are harmful to human health 
and the environment, silver nanoparticles derived 
from S. acuminata fruit extract can be used (Patel 
et al., 2021). In a fermentative environment, Kleb-
siella oxytoca GS-4–08 produced palladium nano-
particles that reduced azo dye concentrations in 
wastewater (Ma et  al., 2022). To extract methyl 
orange (MO) colour from water, researchers cre-
ated bimetallic core–shell Pd/Fe nanoparticles on 
aerobic microbial granules (Bio-Pd/Fe). The gran-
ules produced Bio-H 2, which catalyzed Bio-Pd/Fe 
synthesis. Bio-Pd/Fe achieved 99.33% MO removal 
under optimal circumstances identified by response 
surface methods, which was much better than Bio-
Pd (68.91%), Bio-Fe (76.8%), and other controls. 
The analysis demonstrated the efficacy of Bio-Pd/Fe 
for dye removal by confirming MO degradation into 
less hazardous compounds, a finding corroborated 
by bioassays using freshwater algae and Allium 
cepa (Kubendiran et al., 2021).
Photocatalysts such as TiO₂, ZnO, and Fe₂O₃ 
nanoparticles generate reactive oxygen species under 
light irradiation, initiating partial oxidation of com-
plex dye molecules and breaking them down into 
smaller, more biodegradable intermediates (Senthil 
et  al., 2024). These transformed intermediates are 
then mineralized by dye-degrading microbial con-
sortia, including species of  Pseudomonas,  Bacillus, 
and  Aspergillus, which possess enzymatic pathways 
for azoreductase-, peroxidase-, and laccase-mediated 
degradation (Rajendran et  al., 2025). Hybrid sys-
tems, such as TiO₂-assisted photocatalysis coupled 
with Pseudomonas putida or ZnO nanoparticle pre-
treatment followed by fungal biodegradation, have 
achieved 95–98% decolorization and > 80% reduc-
tion in total organic carbon (TOC), out-performing 
standalone photocatalytic or biological processes 
(O’neill et  al., 2023). Moreover, immobilizing

sustainable strategy for treating dye-laden wastewater, 
especially when traditional methods are inefficient 
(Fahim et al., 2025).
5.4 Microbial Pathogen Reduction: BNPs 
Antimicrobial Mechanisms in Wastewater 
Systems
Using biogenic metal nanoparticles to removal patho-
genic microorganisms is a promising and sustainable 
approach. Microorganisms, including bacteria, actino-
mycetes, and fungi, can absorb precursor metal ions and 
produce corresponding nanoparticles as a detoxification 
mechanism (Rocha et  al., 2024). This method is cost-
effective, environmentally benign, and does not require 
capping or stabilizing agents, making it an attractive 
option for pathogen removal (Kapoor et al., 2021).
The antimicrobial properties of metal nanopar -
ticles, such as silver nanoparticles, are well inves-
tigated. Metal nanoparticles disrupt pathogen cell 
walls, increase permeability, and show strong antimi-
crobial activity against a wide range of Gram-nega-
tive and Gram-positive bacteria (Prasad et al., 2017). 
Furthermore, combining metallic nanoparticles with 
antibiotics has shown synergistic effects against bac-
teria, including multidrug-resistant strains, and may 
reduce the emergence of antimicrobial resistance 
(Singh et al., 2020). In wastewater treatment, biogenic 
metal nanoparticles, such as magnetite nanoparticles 
produced by microorganisms, have been explored for 
the removal of heavy metals and bacterial pathogens. 
These nanoparticles have demonstrated high effi-
ciency in removing toxic metal ions and pathogenic 
bacteria, making them promising candidates for water 
treatment applications (Ojha et al., 2020).
Exposure to nanoparticles can induce oxidative 
stress, membrane disruption, and metabolic inhibition, 
thereby contributing to pathogen inactivation; however, 
these same stress conditions may also promote hori-
zontal gene transfer (HGT) among surviving micro-
bial populations (Ngoepe et al., 2025). Stress-induced 
secretion of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), 
together with increased cell membrane permeability, 
can facilitate the exchange of mobile genetic elements, 
including plasmids and integrons that carry antimicro-
bial resistance (AMR) genes (Balta et al., 2025). Fur -
thermore, prolonged or sublethal exposure to metal- 
and metalloid-based biogenic nanoparticles may exert 
selective pressure on environmental microbes, enrich-
ing resistant strains and accelerating the dissemination 
of antimicrobial resistance genes—key determinants 
in wastewater ecosystems (Balta et  al., 2025). Recent 
studies report that nanoparticle-induced reactive oxy -
gen species can activate bacterial SOS responses, 
increase mutation rates, and enhance the likelihood of 
resistance gene propagation (Shao et al., 2025). There-
fore, while biogenic nanoparticles offer promising 
antimicrobial capabilities, their deployment must be 
accompanied by comprehensive AMR risk assessment, 
monitoring of gene transfer events, and the develop-
ment of controlled-exposure strategies to mitigate 
unintended ecological and public-health consequences 
(Murugan et al., 2025).
In summary, using biogenic metal nanoparticles 
to removal pathogenic microorganisms offers a sus-
tainable and effective approach (Rath et  al., 2025a, 
b). Microbial synthesis of these nanoparticles offers 
advantages such as environmental benignity and 
high biocompatibility. Furthermore, the antimicro-
bial properties of metal nanoparticles suggest their 
potential for developing effective wastewater treat-
ment strategies. Further research and development in 
this field could lead to the widespread application of 
biogenic metal nanoparticles in water treatment and 
public health initiatives (Wahab et al., 2023).
6 Metal Oxide Nanoparticles in Wastewater 
Remediation
Researchers use pure metal precursors to synthesize 
metal oxide nanoparticles (Jeevanandam et al., 2018). 
Due to their dynamic properties, these metal oxide 
nanoparticles are extremely useful for various envi-
ronmental applications, including the removal of haz-
ardous azo dyes, heavy metals, pesticides, and color -
ants from wastewater and have promising applications 
in environmental contexts (Senthil et al., 2024).
Applications of metal and metal oxide nanopar -
ticles span various industries, including molecular 
sensing, environmental remediation, medicine, and 
catalysis. Metal nanoparticles include manganese 
oxides, nanosized iron oxides, titanium oxides, cerium

1317 Page 20 of 31
Vol:. (1234567890)
oxides, zinc oxide, magnesium oxides, aluminum 
oxides, and zirconium oxides (Jain et al., 2022). Metal 
nanoparticles exhibit well-known localized surface 
plasmon resonance, which confers distinctive opto-
electronic properties and makes them suitable for vari-
ous research fields (Marinescu et  al., 2020). Among 
the many properties of nanosized metal oxides exhibit 
high removal capacity and selectivity for heavy met-
als. These application-focused examples highlight 
the significant potential of metal oxide nanoparticles 
to address pollutant-specific challenges in complex 
wastewater matrices and reinforce their role as high-
efficiency, scalable solutions for modern wastewater 
remediation (Swathika et al., 2025).
Incorporating metallic nanoparticles into a wide 
range of industrial products exposes them to various 
environmental media, including air, water, oil, and 
renewable energy sources. Therefore, biogenic syn-
thesis of these metal oxide nanoparticles is crucial 
because it has no negative environmental or human 
health impacts (Gebre et al., 2019). For example, Oci-
mum sanctum leaf extract was used to biogenically 
synthesize nickel nanoparticles and remove industrial 
dyes, including sulfate, nitrate, anionic pollutants, 
Orange II azo, crystal violet, and eosin Y (Fatima 
et al., 2025). Researchers studied zinc oxide nanopar-
ticles synthesized from plant extracts of Hibiscus sub-
darifa,  Azadirachta indica, and  Aloe vera for their 
potential bactericidal and antifungal activities and 
as photocatalysts for degrading organic dyes (Basnet 
et al., 2018).
Zinc oxides are excellent photocatalysts because 
of their high chemical stability and strong photocata-
lytic activity in removing water contaminants. Zinc 
oxide nanostructures come in various forms, including 
nanosheets, nanorods, nanowires, and complex hybrid 
structures. Zinc oxides are among the most promising 
catalysts for remediating contaminated water because 
of their unique qualities, including availability, low 
cost, and non-toxicity (Mohd Adnan et al., 2016). In a 
recent study, zinc oxide nanoparticles were synthesized 
from Acalypha indica leaf extract, and their potential 
antibacterial properties, required for wastewater reme-
diation, were examined (Karthik et  al., 2017). Simi-
larly, copper oxide nanoparticles possess a range of 
advantageous chemical and physical properties, includ-
ing high-temperature superconductivity, solar energy 
efficiency, relative stability, low cost, and antibacte-
rial activity. When methylene blue dye was subjected 
to photocatalytic degradation in an aqueous Aloe vera 
solution at room temperature, the resulting copper 
oxide nanoparticles exhibited an active bandgap in vis-
ible light, and the dye was effectively degraded (Jabeen 
et  al., 2024). Synthesized copper oxide nanoparticles 
have demonstrated strong antibacterial activity against 
Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus and are 
resistant to methicillin at minimal bactericidal concen-
trations in wastewater (Naseem et al., 2021).
7 Fate of Biogenic Nanoparticles 
after Purification
The environmental fate of BNPs following wastewater 
treatment is governed by their partitioning, transfor -
mation, and long-term mobility in sludge and effluent. 
Studies indicate that 60–75% of BNPs accumulate in 
sewage sludge, while 25–40% remain dispersed in the 
treated effluent, depending on particle size, capping 
agents, and wastewater chemistry (Liu et  al., 2023). 
Although sulfidation of biogenic silver nanoparticles 
(Ag NPs), leading to their conversion into less soluble 
Ag₂S under anaerobic digestion within approximately 
40  h, is a well-documented transformation pathway, 
recent research highlights that BNPs undergo multiple 
concurrent transformations, including oxidation, dis-
solution, aggregation, and formation of organometallic 
complexes with humic substances and natural organic 
matter (Hassan et  al., 2023). These processes signifi-
cantly influence nanoparticle bioavailability, toxicity, 
and persistence. Importantly, retaining BNPs in biosol-
ids destined for land application has direct implications 
for soil quality and terrestrial ecosystems. In soils, 
BNPs may interact with minerals and organic ligands, 
altering their mobility, potentially releasing metal ions, 
or incorporating them into soil aggregates. Their long-
term presence can influence microbial activity, nutrient 
cycling, and plant uptake pathways, particularly with 
repeated biosolids application (Denisse et  al., 2021). 
Conversely, BNPs discharged in effluent can enter sur-
face waters, where exposure to sunlight and dissolved 
organic matter can further transform them into new 
chemical species with distinct ecotoxicological profiles 
(Shiade et al., 2024).
Considerations must include sludge stabilization, 
leachate monitoring, biosolid application guidelines, 
effluent quality control, and the development of trans-
formation-aware risk assessments. These expanded

insights underscore the need for comprehensive envi-

only BNP performance in treatment systems but also 
their long-term ecological behavior after disposal 
(Khan et  al., 2025a, b). The study found that particle 
size distribution changes substantially during treatment, 
with 70% of the initial 20–50 nm particles growing into 
larger aggregates (> 100  nm) (Mukhopadhyay et  al., 
2022). One crucial aspect to consider is the soil reten-
tion capacity of biosolids treated on land, as it directly 
affects their effectiveness in BNPs (Sánchez-Monedero 
et  al., 2024). Research indicates that approximately 
25% of BNPs gradually migrate to deeper soil horizons 
over 6–12 months, while the remaining 30–40% remain 
attached to soil particles in the top 15 cm. Soil pH and 
organic matter content significantly affect mobility. 
Acidic soils (pH < 6) exhibit 40% higher particle mobil-
ity than neutral soils (Pérez-Esteban et al., 2019).
Complex transformation pathways govern the fate 
of BNPs and dictate their reactivity, mobility, and 
long-term ecological impact (Dubey et  al., 2024). 
While sulfidation, oxidation, and surface passiva-
tion are well-recognized transformation routes, recent 
studies have revealed that BNPs can also undergo 
organometallic and organocomplex formation in natu-
ral waters. This occurs through interactions with natu-
ral organic matter (NOM), humic and fulvic acids, 
and dissolved organic ligands, which possess abun-
dant functional groups, such as carboxyl, hydroxyl, 
and phenolic moieties, that can coordinate to nano-
particle surfaces (Nanditha et  al., 2025). These reac-
tions can produce stable or semi-stable organometallic 
complexes by coordinating to nanoparticle surfaces 
via functional groups or active binding sites, thereby 
altering surface charge, colloidal stability, dissolution 
kinetics, and redox behavior (Kostova, 2023).
Such transformations significantly influence BNP 
bioavailability and organo-complexation and may 
either reduce toxicity by passivating reactive sur -
face sites or enhance biological uptake by increasing 
nanoparticle dispersion and affinity for cellular mem-
branes (Nanditha et  al., 2025). Emerging evidence 
indicates that metal-based BNPs, particularly those 
composed of Ag, Cu, Fe, and Zn oxides, can bind 
strongly to NOM-derived ligands, altering aggre-
gation behavior and slowing sedimentation rates, 
thereby increasing residence time in aquatic systems 
(Liao et al., 2017).
8 Barriers to Biogenic Nanomaterials in Water 
Treatment
Despite the potential benefits of biogenic nanoparticles 
in wastewater treatment, several significant challenges 
must be addressed before their widespread implemen-
tation (Sanni et al., 2024). These microorganisms’ ten-
dency to aggregate in complex wastewater mixtures 
poses a considerable challenge, as it reduces their sur-
face area and hampers treatment efficiency (Sharma 
et al., 2023). Recent studies show that BNPs can lose 
up to 40–50% of their catalytic activity within the first 
24  h of deployment (Lee et  al., 2024). Furthermore, 
pH significantly affects BNP stability. Gautam et  al. 
(2024) reported that silver BNPs are most effective at 
pH 6.5–7.5, with removal efficacy decreasing by 30% 
outside this range. Temperature fluctuations in waste-
water treatment facilities can affect BNP performance. 
Typically ranging from 10 to 35 °C. For every 5 °C rise 
above 25 °C, pollutant removal efficiency decreases by 
approximately 15% (Delgado et al., 2019).
Scale-up challenges remain a significant hurdle. 
Laboratory studies show that 95% of organic contam-
inants and heavy metals can be effectively removed 
(Kumari et al., 2025). However, in pilot-scale imple-
mentations, efficiency has only reached 60–70%. 
Multiple ions and the varied characteristics of real 
wastewater systems are the primary factors con-
tributing to this efficiency gap (Qasem et al., 2021). 
Given current production methods, which require an 
investment of $200-$300 per gram for high-quality 
BNPs, cost considerations are crucial. Despite efforts 
to recover and reuse particles, most systems achieve 
only a 40–50% success rate, making the process tech-
nically challenging (Maslin et al., 2021).
Beyond technical and environmental constraints, 
socioeconomic and community-level factors signifi-
cantly influence the widespread adoption of biogenic 
nanoparticle-based wastewater treatment technologies 
(Choi et al., 2024). Public acceptance of nanotechnol-
ogy remains mixed, largely because of concerns about 
perceived toxicity, nanoparticle release, and poten-
tial long-term ecological impacts. Studies have shown 
that communities with limited scientific literacy often 
express heightened apprehension toward novel nanoma-
terials, especially when their environmental fate is not 
clearly communicated. This underscores the need for 
transparent risk communication strategies, community

engagement, and regulatory assurance to build trust in 
BNP-based interventions (Huang et al., 2024).
Additionally, the economic feasibility and long-term 
scalability of BNP technologies pose notable challenges. 
Although biogenic synthesis is environmentally benign 
and cost-effective at the laboratory scale, transitioning 
to industrial-scale production requires consistent bio-
mass availability, bioreactor optimization, and reliable 
quality control to ensure nanoparticle uniformity (Abdi 
et al., 2024). Regions with limited funding or infrastruc-
ture may struggle to adopt BNP-based systems without 
financial incentives or public–private partnerships. Fur-
thermore, regulatory ambiguity surrounding nanoparticle 
discharge limits, lifecycle assessment, and environmental 
monitoring creates uncertainties that slow down com-
mercialization and investment. Addressing these socio-
economic factors through community-centered educa-
tion, robust ecological policies, inclusive governance 
frameworks, and economically viable scale-up pipelines 
is crucial to ensuring that BNP-based wastewater treat-
ment technologies achieve long-term societal acceptance 
and sustainable implementation (Rath et al., 2025a, b).
9 Regulatory Framework for Environmental Risk 
Mitigation
Most nations are still working to implement regu-
lations, and the current regulatory frameworks for 
BNPs are dispersed across jurisdictions. The Euro-
pean Union has adopted a risk-based regulatory 
framework that requires manufacturers to demon-
strate that BNPs do not pose significant environ-
mental risks when production exceeds 1 ton per year 
(Bracking et  al., 2021). Before using the particles 
commercially, manufacturers must determine their 
size distribution, surface chemistry, and aggrega-
tion behavior as part of essential management prac-
tices. Treatment facilities must maintain real-time 
monitoring systems that indicate threshold levels of 
0.1–0.5  mg/l for total nanoparticle concentration in 
effluent discharges. Best-practice standards strongly 
encourage multiple containment barriers, includ-
ing advanced filtration systems capable of captur -
ing particles as small as 10 nm (Farka et al., 2024). 
Facilities must establish emergency action plans for 
accidental leaks and report any incidents involv -
ing concentrations exceeding 1 mg/L (Mäkkä et al., 
2021). When handling concentrated BNP solutions, 
worker safety guidelines require personal protective 
equipment and regular exposure evaluations. As part 
of environmental monitoring programs, it is crucial 
to conduct quarterly assessments of soil and water 
bodies within a 5  km radius of treatment facilities 
(Kumar et al., 2024).
10 Conclusion and Future Perspectives
The current global scenario is heavily influenced by 
the availability of safe, clean water, which is signifi-
cantly affected by factors such as climate change and 
the release of toxic substances into water sources. 
BNPs are a promising class of environmentally compat-
ible nanomaterials with significant potential to advance 
wastewater treatment processes. Unlike conventional 
nanoparticles, plant-, bacterial-, fungal-, algal-, and 
yeast-derived BNPs possess biomolecule-rich surfaces 
that confer enhanced catalytic activity, selective adsorp-
tion, redox reactivity, and antimicrobial activity, mak-
ing them particularly effective for the removal of heavy 
metals, dyes, organic pollutants, pharmaceuticals, and 
pathogenic microorganisms. This review shows that 
the sustainability of BNPs arises not only from their 
biological origin but also from their reduced chemical 
toxicity, lower energy requirements during synthesis, 
and multifunctional mechanisms that outperform sev-
eral traditional remediation techniques. The emerging 
literature indicates that BNPs can achieve high removal 
efficiencies across diverse wastewater systems, includ-
ing industrial, municipal, agricultural, and hospital 
effluents, where limited treatment infrastructure and 
high pollutant loads necessitate cost-effective, scalable 
solutions. BNPs offer a feasible pathway for decentral-
ized, adaptable water treatment technologies in these 
regions, provided synthesis consistency and regula-
tory frameworks are strengthened. International trends, 
including recent developments in the nano-risk govern-
ance and nanotechnology safety initiatives, reflect a 
growing recognition of the need for responsible inno-
vation in nano-enabled remediation strategies. How -
ever, the future deployment of BNPs must be guided 
by a clear understanding of their environmental fate. 
Post-treatment transformations, such as sulfidation, 
oxidation, dissolution, aggregation, and organometal-
lic complex formation, significantly affect the mobil-
ity, persistence, and ecological impact of BNPs in soils, 
sediments, and surface waters. These processes require

 Page 23 of 31 1317
Vol.: (0123456789)
transformation-aware risk assessments, improved bio-
solid management protocols, and long-term monitoring 
frameworks to ensure environmental safety.
Future research should prioritize scalable synthe-
sis platforms, cost-reduction strategies, nanoparticle 
recovery techniques, and real-world pilot studies 
reflect the chemical and biological complexities of 
wastewater systems. Advancing BNP-based tech-
nologies will also require harmonized international 
regulations, community acceptance, and collabora-
tive initiatives among academia, industry, and gov -
ernment. Overall, BNPs offer substantial potential 
as sustainable nanotechnological tools for wastewa-
ter remediation. Still, their widespread adoption will 
depend on bridging existing knowledge gaps in envi-
ronmental fate, economic feasibility, regulatory gov -
ernance, and large-scale implementation. Strengthen-
ing these dimensions will enable BNPs to transition 
from laboratory innovation to practical, environmen-
tally safe water treatment solutions.
