ORIGINAL ARTICLE
Grounds for comparison: linking Arabica cup scores to soil 
fertility and plant nutrition in rust‑resistant cultivars 
and traditional American coffees
Chase S. Kasmerchak · Taya Brown · Heidi Allen Asensio · Devon Barker · 
Juan Charuc · Andrew J. Margenot
Received: 21 March 2026 / Accepted: 1 September 2026 
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract The cup quality of rust-resistant Arabica 
(Coffea arabica  L.) × Robusta (C. canephora Pierre 
ex Froehner) cultivars is thought to be inferior to 
traditional Arabicas, but few studies have examined 
the extent to which coffee cup scores are affected by 
local variation in soil fertility and plant nutrition. We 
evaluated coffee cup quality using the Specialty Cof-
fee Association’s 100-point Q-grading system and 
characterized the timing and formulation of soil and 
foliar nutrient additions across 23 fields in south-cen-
tral Guatemala managed by smallholder farmers, then 
compared relationships between soil extractable and 
total leaf and green bean nutrient concentrations and 
coffee cup scores for traditional versus rust-resistant 
Arabica cultivars. All cultivars had similar coffee 
cup qualities and obtained final cup scores suitable 
for special coffee market access (> 80.0 points). The 
timing of nutrient additions closely aligned with local 
weather conditions, but formulations of foliar nutri-
ent additions differed markedly between cultivars. In 
> 80% of fields, soil inorganic N, K, Mg and B and 
leaf S, Zn, Cu, and B concentrations were below 
minimum thresholds generally ideal for crop produc-
tion. Despite potential nutrient deficiencies, most cup 
qualities were positively correlated with leaf Zn for 
traditional cultivars (R2
adj = 0.23–0.82) and soil K for 
rust-resistant cultivars (R2
adj = 0.34–0.78). Our study 
demonstrates rust-resistant Arabicas grown in this 
region of Guatemala can have similarly appealing 
coffee cup flavor profiles and organoleptic qualities as 
Bourbon and Typica lineages. However, a larger net-
work of sites is needed to establish reliable soil and/
or foliar indicators of coffee cup quality across vari-
able management practices and soil conditions.

resource-constrained coffee farmers in Latin America

Unlike low-priced, poor quality commodity blends 
(Pendergrast 2010), the superior cup quality of sin-
gle-origin coffee sold in specialty markets appeals 
to consumers willing to pay high prices for coffee 
with unique flavor profiles and sensory characteris-
tics (Grabs and Ponte 2019). The volcanic highlands 
of Central America are widely known for producing 
some of the world’s best tasting coffees (McCook and 
Montero‐Mora 2024). Here, coffee is predominately 
grown by smallholder farmers in shaded agroforestry 
systems at elevations (≈ 700–2200 m asl) and air tem-
pertures (≈ 18–21 °C) optimal for Arabica productiv -
ity (van der Vossen et al. 2015), proper cherry devel-
opment maturation (Perfecto and Vandermeer 2002), 
and favorable cup qualities (Bosselmann et al. 2009; 
DaMatta et al. 2012; Sánchez-Hernández et al. 2018). 
Coffee farmers tend to prioritize management knowl-
edge and cup quality over pest and disease manage-
ment costs in cultivar selection (Turreira-García 
2022). As a result, many farmers prefer traditional 
cultivars known for producing high quality coffee 
beans with high cup quality (e.g., Caturra) despite 
greater susceptibility to coffee leaf rust (Hemileia 
vastatrix) and other pathogens (e.g., Cercospora 
coffeicola) (McCook and Montero‐Mora 2024). In 
contrast, rust-resistant cultivars derived from Ara-
bica × C. canephora progenies are generally con-
sidered to produce inferior cup quality (Tay 2025), 
in particular bitter and astringent flavors in some 
of the early Catimors (McCook 2019; McCook and 
Montero‐Mora 2024). However, recently developed, 
first-generation (F1) hybrids of rust-resistant cultivars 
have been found to obtain higher cup scores than tra-
ditional cultivars across a range environmental condi-
tions (Marie et al. 2020).
The extent to which soil fertility impacts cultivar-
specific coffee cup quality remains largely unclear 
and understudied across the global Coffee Belt (Geer-
aert et al. 2019; Tassew et al. 2021; Mintesnot et al. 
2015) and within Latin America specifically (Are-
nas-Clavijo and Armbrecht 2018; Rekik et  al. 2019; 
Márquez et al. 2020). The general lack of field-tested, 
evidence-base fertilizer recommendations for tropical 
perennial crops translates challenges economically 
optimized fertilizer use, in contrast to many annual 
crops (e.g., Wang et al. 2015; Eash et al. 2019), fur -
ther compromising the economic stability of resource-
limited coffee farmers. Soil properties can impose 
constraints on soil nutrient availability (e.g., low 
cation exchange capacity (CEC) of coarse textured 
soils) that may indirectly influence coffee cup quality 
(Mitchell 1988; Feria-Morales 2002; Läderach 2007). 
However, soil tests used to prescribe soil fertility at 
0–15 cm depth may offer less diagnostic insight into 
deep-rooted perennial crops like coffee compared to 
leaf nutrient concentrations used to gauge plant nutri-
tion (Stephens 1968; Wang et al. 2015; Kasmerchak 
et al. 2025). Establishing such relationships between 
cup scores and measuress of soil fertility and plant 
nutrition would help expand smallholder access to 
specialty coffee markets and tailor management prac-
tices to better optimize cup quality.
We evaluated potential relationships between cof-
fee cup scores and soil fertility and/or plant nutri-
tion in 23 coffee fields under realistic conditions of 
resource-limited, low input coffee production in the 
south-central Guatemalan highlands across highly 
similar soil types and growing conditions (Brown 
2019; Allen Asensio et al. 2024). Smallholder farm-
ers in this region currently represent 97% of coffee 
producers in Guatemala and generate 44% of coun-
try’s coffee harvest (Tay 2024). Our specific objec-
tives were to (i) assess coffee cup quality for Arabica 
cultivars traditionally grown in Central America and 
rust-resistant Arabica × C. canephora cultivars, (ii) 
characterize nutrient management practices and soil 
fertility (e.g., pH, extractable nutrients) and plant 
nutrition (i.e., leaf nutrient concentrations), and (iii)

Methods
Study area and design
We tested hypotheses on cup quality × soil fertil-
ity × plant nutrition relationships across 23 coffee 
fields managed by 23 farmers in the highlands of 
south-central Guatemala in the township of San Pedro 
Yepocapa within the Department of Chimaltenango, 
Guatemala, Hermogenes Montellano (14°29′57.88″ 
N; 90°59′31.47″ W). Coffee production in this 
region is representative of the relatively low-input 
coffee production systems historically found in the 
steeply-sloped volcanic highlands of Central Amer -
ica (Harvey et al. 2018, 2021). The regional climate 
is classified as sub-tropical highland (Cwb Koppen 
classification), with mean annual temperature ranging 
22–28 °C and 2500–3000 mm of rainfall, largely con-
centrated from May to October. Soils in this region 
formed in pyroclastic material from the active Volcán 
de Fuego stratovolcano located ≈ 12 km to the south-
west of our sampling region. Analysis of ash samples 
collected during a period of high volcanic activity in 
2018 (Albino et  al. 2020; Charbonnier et  al. 2023) 
suggests ash deposition potentially provides appreci-
able Ca, Cu, Mn, and Zn inputs (Table S1). However, 
high sand content (> 70%), steep slopes (30°–68°), 
and relatively low cation exchange capacity (CEC) 
(median 6.0 meq 100  g−1) (Allen Asensio et al. 2024), 
entails high infiltration rates and entails substantial 
nutrient leaching under the relatively high rainfall in 
this region (Allen Asensio et al. 2026).
We selected fields planted with Arabica cultivars 
representative of smallholder coffee production in 
Central America (Tay 2025): (1) traditional culti-
vars commonly grown in Central America ascended 
from Bourbon and Typica lineages, including Bour -
bon, Caturra, Pacas, and Cautaí, (n = 9 fields), and 
(2) rust-resistant cultivars derived from Arabica × C. 
canephora progenies, including Sarchimors, Cati-
mors, Icatú, and Centroamericano (n = 14 fields) 
(Table  1). With farmer consent, we selected fields 
from a comprehensive 2018–2019 baseline soil fer -
tility assessment that revealed prevalent soil nitrogen 
(N) and potassium (K) deficiencies throughout region 
(Allen Asensio et  al. 2024). Fields needed to have 
at least 24 mature coffee plants of uniform cultivar 
that were actively bearing fruit for at least 3 y post-
establishment. Coffee was planted at densities rang-
ing from 2000 to 3333 trees ha−1 (mean = 2975 ± 91 
trees ha−1) under varying degrees of shade from 
native and introduced tree species (e.g., Grevillea 
robusta) and common agroforestry species used for 
fruit crops (e.g., Persea americana), N-fixation (e.g., 
Inga edulis), and timber (e.g., Cedrus libani) planted 
at 138–625 trees ha−1 (4:1 to 24:1 coffee-to-shade 
ratio) (Table  S2). We expected local variation in 
shade tree density in the high-elevation fields in our 
study (≈ 800–1600 m asl) to minimally impact coffee 
cup quality compared to low-elevation systems where 
cherry maturation is highly sensitive to relative shade 
cover (< 700 m asl) (Guyot et al. 1996; Salazar et al. 
2000; Muschler 2001).
Sample collection and processing
We harvested coffee cherries, sampled coffee leaves 
and soils, and conducted on-farm interviews during 
the same visit to each field from December 2019 to 
January 2020, prior to fertilizer application in April. 
This timing was chosen to coincide with peak har -
vest when cherries are most likely to have optimal 
quality attributes (i.e., ripeness, exterior conditions 
indicative of minimal defects), thus maximizing cup 
quality potential. We harvested approximately dou-
ble the mass of ripe coffee cherries needed for pro-
cessing (≈ 2.2  kg) and used a hand-crank depulper 
to remove exocarps from cherries of optimal ripe-
ness and no physical signs of defects within 4 h post-
harvest. To mimic the wet processing (i.e., washed) 
method most used by farmers in this region (Figueroa 
Campos et  al. 2020), we fermented depulped cher -
ries in unsealed 1-gallon sealable plastic bags for 48 h 
without any additional water (excluding low density 
“floater” cherries), then removed all visible mucilage

During cherry harvest, we collected opposing 
pairs of mature leaves from the middle section of four 
branches at the mid-line of the tree (roughly chest 
height; 8 total leaves per plant) from three plants 
per field at each cardinal direction. Leaf samples 
were then composited and stored in paper bags in 
open air to dry in at ambient conditions then ground 
(< 0.1  mm) for nutrient analysis using a mechanical 
stainless-steel mill and prepared for shipment. Below 
the same coffee trees selected for cherry harvesting, 
we sampled soils (0–15 cm depth) using a hand auger 
at eight randomized points within each field, which 
were then composited before air-drying and siev -
ing (< 2  mm). During the same visit, we conducted 
on-site interviews to characterize nutrient manage-
ment practices implemented by local farmers over the 
growing season. Farmers were asked to describe the 
timing, frequency, and formulation of foliar nutrient 
additions and soil fertilizer inputs. Interviews took

Data collection
We used SCA cup grading protocols to ensure our 
assessment of coffee cup quality was objective and 
reflected current standards used to differentiate spe-
cialty market prices (SCA 2020b). Briefly, a roast-
ing expert used a gas-flame-heated sample roaster to 
whole green coffee beans processed from harvested 
cherries. Roasted coffee beans were then ground and 
steeped in ceramic coffee cupping bowls with non-
distilled water (125–175  ppm total dissolved solids) 
and heated to 92.2–94.4  °C. Three Coffee Quality 
Institute (CQI) certified Arabica coffee Q-graders 
characterized flavor profiles and tasting notes, then 
assigned scores to aroma, flavor, aftertaste, acidity, 
body, balance, uniformity, clean cup, sweetness, and 
overall quality attributes to three brewed cups (modi-
fied from the five-cup SCA protocol) (Table  S3). 
Scores assigned to these then cup qualities were then 
summed to obtain a final cup score on the 100-point 
SCA Q-grading scale. We differentiated the coffee 
cup quality using final cup score ranges reported in 
the specialty coffee transaction guide (Roberts and 
Trewick 2024): (i) 81.0–81.99 points, (ii) 82.0–83.99 
points, (iii) 84.0–85.99 points, (iv) 86.0–86.9 points, 
and (v) > 87.0 points. Although these intervals cor -
respond with free on board (i.e., pre-export) specialty 
coffee price estimates, a robust economic analysis 
describing the earning potential of coffee produced in 
this region was beyond the scope of our study, thus 
free on-board prices are not discussed directly.
Coffee leaf and green (i.e., unroasted) bean sub-
samples were digested in nitric acid (90 °C) and ana-
lyzed for total concentrations of phosphorus (P), K, 
calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), boron (B), zinc (Zn), 
copper (Cu), manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), and alu-
minum (Al) using inductively coupled plasma opti-
cal emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) (Zarcinas et al. 
1987) and total N by dry combustion. The agronomic 
and cup quality focus of our research meant charac-
terizing green bean primary metabolite profiles and 
aromatic compound concentrations related to Central 
American coffee cup quality (e.g., Marie et al. 2020) 
was out of scope. Mehlich-3 extractable soil nutrient 
concentrations were quantified on air-dried and 
sieved soils for P, K, Ca, Mg, B, Zn, Cu, Fe, and Al 
using ICP-OES (Ziadi and Sen Tran 2007). Following 
extraction by 0.5 M K2SO4 for 30 min using a 1:5 soil 
mass:extract volume ratio (Nelson 1983), inorganic 
N concentrations were quantified for ammonium-N 
 (NH4
+-N) by salicylate using nitroprusside as a cat-
alyst (Kempers and Zweers 1986) and NO3
−-N by 
vanadium (III) reduction (Hendrix and Braman 1995; 
Doane and Horwath 2003). We estimated soil cation 
exchange capacity (CEC) as the sum soil Ca, Mg, K, 
and sodium concentrations (Mattila and Rajala 2022), 
and measured soil pH using a pH probe on 1:1 soil 
mass:water slurry (USDA NRCS 2004). Total soil 
organic carbon (SOC) and total soil N (TSN) were 
quantified by dry combustion using a total elemental 
analyzer following HCl fumigation to remove poten-
tial carbonates (Harris et al. 2001).
Statistical analyses
To test our first and second hypotheses, we compared 
the distributions of coffee cup qualities, final cup 
scores, and soil fertility and plant nutrition measures 
for traditional versus rust-resistant cultivars using a 
Wilcoxon rank-sum test (i.e., non-parametric t-test). 
In R-Studio we used the pairwise.wilcox.test() func-
tion followed by the Benjamini–Hochberg false-dis-
covery rate (FDR) p-value adjustment method in the 
{stats} package within base R (v4.2.1; R Core Team 
2022). On-farm interview responses for the timing, 
frequency, and formulation of foliar nutrition addi-
tions and soil fertilizer inputs were analyzed qualita-
tively to better characterize between-cultivar differ -
ences in nutrient management practices and inform 
potential variation in soil extractable and total leaf/
green bean nutrient concentrations. We assessed 
coffee plant nutrition using minimum leaf nutrient 
concentration thresholds recommended for optimal 
Arabica coffee production in Central America (Lla-
tch-Hill 2017); no minimum concentrations thresh-
olds are currently reported for green bean nutrients. 
Due to the conspicuous absence of peer-reviewed, 
evidence-based soil fertility recommendations for 
coffee (van Asten et  al. 2010; Bhattarai et  al. 2017; 
Wang et  al. 2015) or woody perennials in Latin 
America in general (Chavez et al. 2021), we used soil 
fertility thresholds developed for annual crops in U.S.

agroecosystems a general basis for estimating relative 
nutrient deficiency for optimal crop production.
To test our third hypothesis, we first calculated 
Pearson’s correlation coefficients (PCCs) for cup 
qualities and final cup scores across individual 
measures of soil fertility (n = 19) and plant nutrition 
(n = 12 for leaf and green beans) in R-Studio using the 
cor() function in {Hmisc} package (v.4.7.2; Harrell 
2025). Univariate relationships were then visualized 
as correlation matrices generated using corrplot() 
function in the {corrplot} package (v.0.92; Wei and 
Simko 2024). To determine which variable(s) the 
most important predictors of coffee cup qualities and 
final cup scores for traditional versus rust-resistant 
cultivars, we constructed least absolute shrinkage and 
selection operator (LASSO) regression models using 
the cv.glmnet() command in the {glmnet} package 
(v4.1.8; Friedman et  al. 2010). The tuning param-
eter (λ) the LASSO regression penalization function 
shrinks β coefficients equally (based on their absolute 
sum) as the number of predictor variables increase in 
size (McNeish 2015), and predictor variables with β 
coefficients shrunk to zero discarded from the final 
model (Tibshirani 1996). Thus, LASSO regression 
more effectively minimizes the bias-variance trade-
off and over-fitting commonly observed least squares 
variable selection methods (Tibshirani 1996; James 
et al. 2017). We used k-fold cross-validation (k = 10) 
with 1000 iterations of the tuning parameter (λ) with 
the lowest mean-square error to ensure standard-
ized β coefficients were not overly penalized (Zou 
and Hastie 2005; Zou et  al. 2007), then compared 
the adjusted correlation coefficients (R2
adj.) and root-
mean square errors (RMSE) of final LASSO mod-
els to best-fit univariate regression models to avoid 
over-fitting. All data visualizations were generated as 
vector files using the {tidyverse} collection (v2.0.0; 
Wickham et  al. 2019), then exported and edited in 
Adobe Illustrator (v26.3.1; Adobe Inc. 2019).
Results
Coffee cup qualities and final scores
Traditional and rust-resistant cultivars obtained 
final cup scores suitable for specialty coffee mar -
ket access (i.e., > 80 points), though with appreci-
able within-cultivar variation among cup qualities 
(Fig.  1). Excluding perfect scores for uniformity, 
sweetness, and clean cup across cultivars, cup quali-
ties were scored lower on average for Pacas and Icatú 
(7.5 points) compared to Caturra and Centroameri-
cano (mean = 7.8 points). Although cup quality scores 
were similar (P = 0.33–0.75), traditional cultivars 
exhibited markedly greater variation across cup quali-
ties on average (coefficient of variation [CV] = 10%) 
compared to rust-resistant cultivars (CV = 1%). 
Rust-resistant cultivars also obtained similar final 
cup scores as traditional cultivars known for high 
cup quality (P = 0.49). However, a larger proportion 
of rust-resistant cultivars (43%) obtained final cup 
scores capable of fetching higher free on-board spe-
cialty coffee prices (84–85.99 points) compared to 
traditional cultivars (22%).
On-farm management and soil fertility and plant 
nutrition
Farmers responses to on-farm interviewers suggest 
the timing and formulation of foliar nutrient additions 
varied appreciably between cultivars over the grow -
ing season compared to soil fertilizer inputs (Fig.  2). 
Specifically, farmers reported applying foliar nutri-
ents to traditional cultivars throughout the growing 
season and to rust-resistant cultivars predominately 
from May to July (Fig.  2A). All farmers added foliar 
nutrients predominately as N or K or NPK mixtures 
(≈ 10–35% of farmers), but fewer farmers foliar B 
isolates or BZn or BCa mixtures to traditional (≈ 10% 
max) versus rust-resistant cultivars (≈ 35% max in 
May). Fewer farmers also reported applying foliar 
nutrients of unknown composition (e.g., locally fer -
mented plant debris, proprietary blends) to traditional 
cultivars (≈ 22% max in August) compared to rust-
resistant cultivars (≈ 35% max in mid-June). Nearly 
all farmers (95%) applied soil fertilizer as NP or NPK 
mixtures at the onset of the rainy season (May to 
June) (Fig.  2B). Farmers applied a second round of 
soil fertilizer during the peak growing season when 
growing traditional cultivars (September to Octo-
ber), and more flexibly over the growing season when 
growing rust-resistant cultivars (mid-July through 
October). Roughly 30% of farmers applied urea as an 
N fertilizer to soils shortly before harvest (October to 
November).

rust-resistant cultivars (Fig.  3). Traditional cultivar 
body and balance scores were negatively correlated 
with soil CEC (PCC ≈ − 0.70), whereas all cup quali-
ties (except aftertaste) and final cup scores for rust-
resistant cultivars were positively correlated with 
soil K concentrations (PCC = 0.62 to 0.80) (Fig.  3A).

Soil fertility does not inform coffee plant nutrition 
under current management practices
We found potentially soil nutrient deficiencies rarely 
corresponded with leaf concentrations sub-optimal 
for Arabica coffee production in Central America, 
regardless of cultivar-specific variation in the timing 
and formulation of soil and foliar nutrient additions. 
For example, soil inorganic N and K concentrations 
across coffee fields were below minimum thresh-
olds generally considered ideal for crop production, 
despite the dominance of soil NPK fertilizers applied 
by farmers in the region. Conversely, Zn deficiencies 
were more commonly observed in coffee leaves than 
in soils despite repeated co-application of foliar Zn 
and B mixtures—all fields had B deficiencient soils 
and leaves. These findings suggest soil fertility meas-
ures alone may not adquately reflect the nutritional 
status of mature Arabica trees, which is consistent 
with smallholder Robusta coffee production  sys-
tems in South America (Kasmerchak et  al. 2025). 
Although we cannot identify the exact mechanism(s) 
underpinning leaf vs soil nutrient discrepancies in 
our observational study, it is plausible that sampling 
at peak-harvest did not capture wet season nutrient 
status when total rainfall was nearly two-fold higher 
(4015  mm) compared the dry season (2382  mm) in 
the 2019/20 crop year when sampling occurred.
Our findings suggest that at rates currently used 
by farmers, applying NP and NPK fertilizer once 
before the wet season (May to June) does not appre-
ciably  impact mature Arabica tree nutritional status 
throughout the growing season. High total rainfall 
during the wet season (4015  mm) and low soil CEC 
(6.0  meq 100  g−1) favors fertilizer leaching in coarse 
textured Andisols with low nutrient holding capacity 
(Allen Asensio et al. 2024). Negligible to negative agro-
nomic N and K use efficiencies from on-farm coffee 
fertilization trials on these same fields (Allen Asensio 
et  al. 2026) and generally low N  fertilizer persistence 
(e.g., only 50% 15N-fertilizer recovery as soil NO3
−-N 
at 7 weeks after application) (Cannavo et al. 2013) fur-
ther suggest fertilizer efficiency for coffee may be lim-
ited in steeply sloped, humid volcanic landscapes. This 
may in part explain limited use of fertilizers on an abso-
lute basis (kg ha−1) on smallholder farm throughout the 
volcanic landscapes in the Central American Coffee 
Belt compared to intensified coffee plantations (Hag-
gar et al. 2011; Harvey et al. 2021), which in turn could 
reflect non-nutrient yield limitations for coffee (e.g., 
pathogens, sunlight) (Wang et al. 2015; Bhattarai et al. 
2017).
Peak harvest soil vs coffee leaf nutrient discrepancies 
may also partially reflect differences in locally-tailored 
foliar nutrient management practices. Repeated applica-
tion of foliar N and/or K is a low-cost, rapid approach 
to correct severe N and K deficiencies under conditions 
favoring fertilizer leaching losses (Fageria et al. 2009; 
Tay 2022) and minimize impacts to production from 
coffee leaf rust and American leaf spot (Mycenae citri-
color) infection (Montero et  al. 2018; Brown 2019). 
Unlike soil inorganic N and K deficiencies, widespread 
soil and leaf B deficiencies across fields in our study 
likely reflect the high B adsorption capacity of abun-
dant short-range order minerals (e.g., allophane) in vol-
canic soils in this landscape (Terraza Pira et al. 2018) 
and generally low B mobility in phloem sap (Hansch 
and Mendel 2009; Silva et al. 2021; Rengel et al. 2022). 
Most farmers (50%) co-applied foliar B with Zn at the 
start of the rainy season (April to May) or immediately 
after cherry harvest (≈ 35%), which suggests our sam-
pling at peak-harvest may not have captured transloca-
tion of micronutrients from leaves to flowers during a 
transient period of post-harvest plant stress (Silva et al. 
2021) or lower leaf adsorption of foliar B and Zn addi-
tons during the dry season (Clemente et al. 2018).

Cultivar-specific relationships between cup scores 
and soil extractable and total leaf nutrient concen-
trations demonstrates the role of plant genetics in 
modulating coffee cup quality in this landscape. The 
positive relationship traditional cultivar cup qualities 
and leaf Zn may reflect increased enzymatic oxida-

scores in rust-resistant cultivars, but Centroameri-
cano obtained the highest cup score (86.25 points). 
Other soil properties (e.g., microbial biomass) and/or 
edaphic factors (e.g., light availability) not examined 
in our study could also be stronger determinants of 
coffee cup quality than soil extractable and total leaf 
nutrients, which that warrants expanding evaluations 
across soil properties and climatic conditions over a 
larger network of sites. Notably, Central American 
farmers in our study area (Brown 2019) and other 
regions (Turreira-García 2022) have reported mini-
mal management guidance for F1 hybrids. Contrary 
to the common perception that rust-resistant cultivars 
are inferior to traditional cultivars with respect to cup 
quality (Tay 2025), we find that Arabica cultivars 
with rust-resistant genes can have similarly appealing 
organoleptic cup qualities as traditional cultivars 
regarded for their high cup quality (Fig. 6).
We recognize our harvesting approach may not 
necessarily represent the cup quality Arabica grown 
under regional management practices, as we sorted 
and processed individual cherries based on opti-
mal quality attributes (i.e., ripeness, minimal exter -
nal defects). This may partially explained markedly 
higher final coffee cup scores in our study compared 
to traditional (76.3 points) and rust-resistant cultivars 
(80.5 points) grown at comparable elevations in Nica-
ragua (710–1250 m asl) (Marie et al. 2020). However, 
the SCA standard Arabica Q-grading system assesses 
cup quality based on objective human perceptions 
subjected to the accuracy of individual graders (Mar -
cus et al. 2023). Although CQI certified Q-graders in 
our study assessed three cups per coffee rather than 
five cups used in the standard SCA protocol (SCA 
2020a), assigned cup scores for individual CQI grad-
ers varied from median scores by 2–5% in most cases 
(Table  S7) and we intentionally analyzed median 
cup scores to better constrain grader subjectivity. 
It is worth noting that human subjectivity and other 
extrinsic factors (e.g., origin, post-harvest process-
ing) are evaluated in the SCA’s new industry standard 
Coffee Value Assessment (CVA) framework (SCA 
2024), which in replaced the 100-point Q-grading 
system in 2024 as the market standard after cup qual-
ity was assessed for the present study. Thus, potential 
bias introduced by selective cherry harvesting prac-
tices and subjective human perception would still be 
expected for any sensory value-based assessment of 
coffee cup quality.
Conclusions
Identifying the impacts of soil fertility and plant 
nutrition on C. arabica cup quality across on-farm 
sites is needed to evaluate the suitability of rust-
resistant Arabica cultivars compared to traditional 
American regarded for high cup quality. Contrary to 
our hypothesis, traditional cultivars were not supe-
rior to rust-resistant cultivars with respect to cup 
score, regardless of appreciable within-cultivar var -
iation in management practices and soil extractable 
and total leaf nutrient concentrations. Soil inorganic 
N, K, Mg, B concentrations were below minimum 
thresholds generally considered optimal for crop

production in most fields, as were leaf S, Zn, Cu, 
and B concentrations considered optimal for Ara-
bica production in Central America. Most cup qual-
ities and final cup scores were correlated with leaf 
Zn concentrations for traditional cultivars and soil 
and/or leaf K concentrations for rust-resistant cul-
tivars. Although this could reflect cultivar-specific 
cup quality nutrient limitations, such simple rela-
tionships may not be reliable under variable man-
agement practices in this region of Central Amer -
ica. More broadly, our results demonstrate that 
resource-limited farmers in volcanic highlands of 
Guatemala currently produce high quality Arabica 
coffee with favorable qualities across variable man-
agement practices. Additional work is needed to 
constrain the relationship between yields and coffee 
cup quality across a larger network of Arabica pro-
duction systems with variable edaphic factors and 
management practices.
