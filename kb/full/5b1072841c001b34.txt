Efficient Photocatalytic Degradation of Textile 
Dyes in Industrial Wastewater using ZnO and CdO 
Nanoparticles
Shakeel Khan · Muhammad Sadiq · Niaz Muhammad  · Awal Noor · 
Sadaf Qayyum
Received: 23 September 2025 / Accepted: 8 September 2026 / Published online: 15 September 2026 
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature Switzerland AG 2026
Abstract The discharge of untreated dye effluents 
from textile industries poses a serious environmen-
tal challenge, necessitating efficient and sustain-
able remediation strategies. In this study, zinc oxide 
(ZnO) and cadmium oxide (CdO) nanoparticles (NPs) 
were synthesized via a simple precipitation route 
and applied as photocatalysts for the degradation of 
Congo Red, Methylene Blue, and Methyl Orange 
in real textile wastewater samples collected from 
Karachi, Pakistan. The nanomaterials were system-
atically characterized using XRD, SEM, EDX, TEM, 
BET, and UV–Vis spectroscopy. ZnO NPs (band 
gap 3.0  eV, crystallite size 20  nm) exhibited supe-
rior photocatalytic activity, achieving up to 95% dye 
degradation under alkaline conditions (pH 12), com-
pared to 75% for CdO (band gap 2.51 eV, crystallite 
size 50  nm). Kinetic analysis revealed pseudo-first-
order behavior with rate constants of 0.015, 0.010, 
and 0.008  min⁻ 1 for Congo Red, Methylene Blue, 
and Methyl Orange, respectively. Radical scaveng-
ing experiments confirmed •OH and •O₂⁻ as the 
dominant reactive species, while COD analysis indi-
cated > 85% reduction of the organic load. Further -
more, ZnO demonstrated excellent recyclability with 
minimal loss of activity after four successive cycles. 
These findings highlight the practical potential of 
metal oxide photocatalysts for cost-effective and sus-
tainable treatment of complex dye mixtures in real 
textile wastewater.

1 Introduction
Organic dyes are frequently used in several indus-
tries, such as textiles (Topcu Sendogdular, 2023), 
rubber, dyestuffs, plastics, pulp, paints, cosmetics 
(Hadei et al., 2022), and leathers (Verma et al., 2022). 
Many organic dyes (Khan et al., 2023) are toxic, car -
cinogenic (Foroutan et  al., 2022), non-biodegradable 
(Mahmoodi & Mokhtari-Shourijeh, 2017) and highly 
stable molecules. Therefore, their release to aquatic 
systems as industrial effluents poses serious threats to

the living systems (Tichapondwa et al., 2020). Water 
contaminated with organic dyes like Congo red (CR), 
Methylene Blue (MB) and Methyl Orange (MO) cause 
serious health issues (Panwar et al., 2024). CR causes 
liver dysfunction, oxidative stress (Bilkan et  al., 
2023), and cancer (Karthik et al., 2019a, 2019b). MB 
and MO cause gastrointestinal irritation (Petrovičová 
et  al., 2021) of mucous membranes and urogeni-
tal disorder (Jin et al., 2023). Drinking MB and MO 
polluted water may cause tissue necrosis (Ren et  al., 
2022), irregular heart rate, vomiting (Al-Serwi et al., 
2022), and jaundice in humans (Pradeeba & Sampath, 
2018). Removal of these toxic, low bio-degradable 
and relatively stable dyes are possible through sev -
eral processes, including advanced oxidation pro-
cesses (AOPs) (Kulkarni et al., 2016), biodegradation 
(Hormozi Jangi, 2023), ozonization (Pekárek, 2008), 
electrochemical oxidation (Behara et  al., 2020), pre-
cipitation (Khan et  al., 2023), adsorption, chemical 
oxidation (Govindhan et  al., 2017), and coagulation/
flocculation (Shelke et al., 2022). Recent studies have 
demonstrated the effectiveness of nanostructured 
semiconductor materials for photocatalytic degrada-
tion of organic pollutants and wastewater remediation 
(Lianmawii et al., 2025a, 2025b). Significant progress 
has been reported in catalyst design, charge-carrier 
management, and reactive oxygen species genera-
tion, leading to enhanced photocatalytic performance. 
These developments highlight the growing impor -
tance of semiconductor-based photocatalysts for envi-
ronmental remediation applications and motivate the 
continued exploration of ZnO- and CdO-based nano-
materials for pollutant degradation (Lianmawii et al., 
2025a, 2025b).
Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) rely on gen-
erating reactive oxygen species (ROS) for the degra-
dation of organic dyes (Ferrari et al., 2013). Among 
AOPs, the photo-catalysis (Derkaoui et  al., 2023) is 
the most efficient, fast, cost-effective and eco-friendly 
method, used for the degradation of organic dyes/
mineralization at ambient temperature and pressure 
(Ruellas et al., 2019).
Transition metal oxides (Thambiliyagodage & 
Usgodaarachchi, 2021) owing to their stability, cost-
effectiveness, electronic structure, optical properties, 
photo-corrosion resistance (Naveed et al., 2022) and 
suitable band gap energy, have shown efficient photo-
catalytic mineralization of toxic organic dyes (Rajput 
et al., 2023). Semiconducting transition metal oxides 
such as TiO2 (Lelis et al., 2023), Fe3O4 (Nassar et al., 
2018), NiO (Asadi et al., 2020), Cu2O (Jeevarathinam 
& Asharani, 2024), CeO2 (Jeevarathinam & Asharani, 
2024), and WO3 (Akhlaghian & Najafi, 2018) are 
reported as photo-catalysts for the organic dyes deg-
radation (Anandakumar et al., 2013).
In this study, ZnO and CdO NPs were synthesized 
and employed as photocatalysts for the degradation of 
CR, MB, and MO dyes in real industrial wastewater. 
The selection of these metal oxide NPs is based on 
their excellent semiconducting properties (Khumphon 
et al., 2025), high surface reactivity (Georgiou et al., 
2015), and ability to generate reactive oxygen species 
(Deng et  al., 2023) under UV irradiation (Yayapao 
et al., 2015), which are essential for effective photoca-
talysis (Kumar et al., 2021). The novelty of this study 
lies in its comparative investigation of ZnO and CdO 
NPs synthesized via a cost-effective method, applied 
to the degradation of actual industrial dye effluents, 
rather than relying solely on model dye solutions. 
The primary objectives are to: (i) evaluate their deg-
radation efficiencies under optimized conditions, (ii) 
determine rate constants and assess model fitting 
parameters (R2, RMSE, residuals, AIC) for kinetic 
validation, and (iii) assess photocatalyst reusability to 
establish long-term viability. This work significantly 
contributes to the development of sustainable, metal 
oxide-based nanocatalysts for environmental reme-
diation by demonstrating their real-world applica-
bility and mechanistic insights into dye degradation 
pathways.

2.2 Synthesis of Zinc Oxide Nanoparticles
Zinc oxide NPs were synthesized via controlled pre-
cipitation route followed by thermal treatment, aimed 
at achieving uniform particle size and enhanced crys-
tallinity for optimal photocatalytic activity. In a typi-
cal procedure, zinc(II) chloride dihydrate (0.062 mol, 
8.4  g) was dissolved in 300  mL of deionized water 
under continuous magnetic stirring to yield a clear 
solution. Separately, 2 g of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) 
was dissolved in 50  mL of deionized water to form 
a homogeneous alkaline solution. The NaOH solu-
tion was added dropwise to the zinc precursor under 
vigorous stirring at ambient temperature, carefully 
maintaining the pH at 10 to promote the formation of 
Zn(OH)₂ precipitates. The resulting white suspension 
was stirred further for 1 h to ensure complete nuclea-
tion and precipitation.
The precipitates were isolated by vacuum filtra-
tion, thoroughly washed multiple times with absolute 
ethanol and deionized water to eliminate residual ions 
and organic contaminants, and subsequently dried 
at 80 °C overnight. The dried powder was then sub-
jected to thermal calcination in air at 500 °C for 3 h 
with a controlled heating rate of 1  °C  min⁻ 1. This 
thermal treatment facilitated the phase transition from 
Zn(OH)₂ to ZnO, enhancing the crystallinity and 
purity of the NPs. The resulting ZnO NPs exhibited 
a uniform particle size distribution (20 nm), high sur -
face area, and phase purity, as confirmed by XRD, 
SEM, and BET analyses. The controlled synthesis 
route employed here yielded ZnO NPs with tailored 
physicochemical properties optimized for photocata-
lytic degradation of organic pollutants in real indus-
trial wastewater.
2.3 Synthesis of Cadmium Oxide Nanoparticles
Cadmium oxide NPs were synthesized via controlled 
chemical precipitation method followed by thermal 
calcination, with emphasis on achieving phase-pure, 
nanostructured materials suitable for photocatalytic 
applications. In a typical synthesis, Cadmium(II) 
chloride hemipentahydrate (0.044 mol, 10 g) was dis-
solved in 300 mL of deionized water under continu-
ous magnetic stirring for 1 h at ambient temperature 
to form a clear cadmium precursor solution. Sepa-
rately, 2 g of NaOH was dissolved in 50 mL of deion-
ized water. The alkaline solution was added dropwise 
into the cadmium precursor under vigorous stirring, 
and the pH of the mixture was carefully adjusted to 
10 to facilitate uniform precipitation of Cd(OH)₂.
The suspension was aged under stirring for an 
additional hour to promote homogeneous nucleation. 
The resulting precipitates were collected via repeated 
centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min, washed thor -
oughly with deionized water and ethanol to remove 
residual ions and impurities, and dried in a hot air 
oven at 80 °C overnight. The dried powder was cal-
cined in air at 500 °C for 3 h with a controlled heating 
rate of 1 °C min⁻1. This thermal decomposition trans-
formed Cd(OH)₂ into nanocrystalline CdO, as con-
firmed by structural and morphological characteriza-
tions. The final product exhibited a characteristic dark 
red color, with average particle size of 50  nm, high 
crystallinity, and a direct band gap of 2.51 eV. These 
properties are critical for facilitating light absorption 
and charge carrier separation during photocatalysis in 
real textile effluents.
2.4 Radical Trapping Experiment
The photocatalytic degradation mechanism of CR, 
MB and MO, using ZnO and CdO NPs was con-
firmed through radical trapping experiments. The key 
reactive species involved in the degradation process 
were identified by introducing specific scavengers. 
Isopropanol (IPA), p-benzoquinone (BQ), and EDTA 
were employed to identify the contributions of •OH, 
•O₂⁻, and h⁺, respectively. Under optimized condi-
tions, three experimental runs were conducted to 
assess the role of hydroxyl radicals (•OH), superox-
ide radicals (•O₂⁻), and photo-generated holes (h⁺) in 
the degradation process.

2.7 Photo-degradation Process
The degradation of CR, MB and MO in real sam-
ples of industrial effluents were carried out using 
UV spectrophotometer in the presence of synthe-
sized ZnO and CdO NPs as photocatalysts. The 
photocatalysts were individually added to the 10 mL 
deionized water and were sonicated for 30 min, then 
10  mL of real wastewater samples containing CR, 
MB, and MO were aged into separate beakers and 
were sealed with colorless plastics. The photocata-
lytic experiments were initiated by sonicating the 
reaction mixtures for 30 min to ensure uniform dis-
persion of the catalyst. Subsequently, the suspen-

continuous magnetic stirring to maintain homo-

3 Results and Discussion
3.1 Morphological and Elemental Analyses
The photocatalytic performance of NPs is primar -
ily governed by their morphology, particle size, and 
structural purity. To evaluate these critical parame-
ters, the synthesized ZnO and CdO NPs were charac-
terized using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), 
Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX), and 
Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM). The SEM 
images of ZnO NPs, shown in Fig.  2(a–b), reveal a 
predominantly spherical morphology with notice-
able aggregation. The particle size distribution, pre-
sented in Fig. 2c for ZnO indicates an average size of 
approximately 20  nm. In contrast, the SEM images 
of CdO NPs in Fig.  2(d–e) display irregularly shaped 
particles, appearing in both dispersed and aggre-
gated states. The aggregation observed in both sam-
ples is influenced by particle size and surface energy; 
smaller NPs tend to aggregate more readily due to a 
higher nucleation rate that minimizes the system’s 
free energy. Figure  2f display average particle size of 
50 nm for CdO NPs. The elemental composition and 
purity of the synthesized ZnO and CdO nanoparticles 
were confirmed by EDX spectroscopy and elemen-
tal mapping (Fig.  3). The EDX spectra depicted in 
Fig. 3(a-b) exhibited characteristic peaks correspond-
ing to Zn and O in ZnO NPs and Cd and O in CdO 
NPs, with no detectable impurity peaks (Ruiz Peralta 
et al., 2012). The normalized EDX results in Table  1 
showed compositions of 67.60 wt% Zn and 32.40 
wt% O for ZnO NPs, and 73.41 wt% Cd and 26.59 
wt% O for CdO NPs. Furthermore, the elemental 
mapping images in Fig.  3(c–e) revealed a homogene-
ous distribution of Zn, Cd, and O throughout the sam-
ples, confirming the successful synthesis and com-
positional uniformity of the metal oxide NPs. These 
results verify the high purity and homogeneous ele-
mental distribution of the synthesized ZnO and CdO 
NPs.

3.4 BET Analysis
The N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms of ZnO and 
CdO NPs, as depicted in Fig.  7(a-b), were analyzed to 
determine their specific surface area, pore volume, and 
pore size distribution. Both materials exhibit a type-IV 
isotherm with a distinct hysteresis loop, characteris-
tic of mesoporous materials, as per the IUPAC clas-
sification. The presence of a hysteresis loop suggests 
capillary condensation within the mesopores, indica-
tive of a well-developed porous network. Addition-
ally, the gradual rise in nitrogen adsorption at higher 
relative pressures (P/P₀ ≈ 1.0) signifies the presence 
of macropores, which complement the mesoporous 
structure. The pronounced increase in nitrogen adsorp-
tion at high relative pressures (P/P₀ ≈ 1.0) is indica-
tive of the presence of large void spaces within the 
material. However, this behavior is more likely asso-
ciated with textural porosity and interparticle voids 
arising from nanoparticle aggregation and packing 
rather than confirmed intrinsic macroporosity. There-
fore, the high-pressure adsorption uptake should be 
interpreted as evidence of interparticle textural poros-
ity, while the presence of intrinsic macropores cannot 
be conclusively established based solely on nitrogen

3.7.1 Effect of Time Irradiation
Time optimization is a critical factor in achieving 
the complete mineralization of organic dyes during 
wastewater treatment. An optimal irradiation period 
ensures effective interaction between dye molecules 
and the active sites of the photocatalyst, enhancing 
degradation efficiency. Typically, photocatalytic dye 
degradation exhibits an increasing trend with pro-
longed irradiation. The UV–VIS absorption spectra 
of CR dye solutions subjected to varying irradiation 
times (0–60  min) in the presence of ZnO and CdO 
NPs are presented in Fig.  10(a-b), while the corre-
sponding %degradation is illustrated in Fig.  10c. The 
results indicate a significant enhancement in CR dye 
degradation with increasing UV exposure. ZnO NPs 
demonstrated superior photocatalytic efficiency com-
pared to CdO NPs under identical experimental con-
ditions. Within the first 10  min, ZnO NPs achieved 
approximately 60% degradation of CR dye, whereas 
CdO NPs exhibited only 45% degradation. Extending 
the irradiation time to 60  min further improved the 
degradation efficiency to 94% for ZnO and 75% for 
CdO. The enhanced photocatalytic performance of 
ZnO NPs is attributed to their lower band gap energy, 
which enables greater light absorption, and their abil-
ity to suppress the rapid recombination of photo-gen-
erated charge carriers, thereby facilitating more effi-
cient dye degradation.

3.7.8 Proposed Mechanism of Photo‑degradation
The interaction of the photocatalyst with UV light 
leads to the excitation of electrons from the valence 
band (VB) to the conduction band (CB), provided that 
the incident photon energy is equal to or greater than 
the band gap of the photocatalyst. This excitation gen-
erates electron–hole pairs, where positively charged 
holes (h+) are formed in the VB, and excited electrons 
 (e−) occupy the CB. The photocatalytic degradation 
of dye molecules occurs through the generation of 
reactive oxygen species (ROS). The holes (h+) in the 
VB interact with water (H2O) molecules, producing 
hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH), while the excited electrons 
in the CB reduce oxygen (O2) molecules, forming 
superoxide radicals (⋅O2−). These highly reactive spe-
cies effectively degrade dye molecules into smaller, 
non-toxic products such as H2O and CO2. However, 
if the excited electrons and holes recombine before 
participating in redox reactions, the photocatalytic 
efficiency decreases, leading to lower dye degrada-
tion. The superior photocatalytic performance of 
ZnO NPs may be attributed to their smaller particle 
size and higher effective surface area, which provide 
a greater number of active sites for photocatalytic 
reactions. Furthermore, scavenger and fluorescence 
analyses revealed efficient generation of reactive oxy -
gen species, particularly hydroxyl radicals (•OH), 
which contribute significantly to pollutant degrada-
tion. Although charge-carrier recombination behav -
ior may also influence photocatalytic performance, 
direct evidence for differences in recombination rates 
between ZnO and CdO was not investigated in the 
present study. The proposed mechanism of dye deg-
radation by the photocatalysts is illustrated in Fig. 17.

tetraacetic acid (EDTA) for h⁺. The degradation effi-
ciency was significantly reduced in the presence of 
TBA and BQ, indicating that hydroxyl and superoxide 
radicals played a dominant role in the photocatalytic

3.7.9 Scavenger Trapping and Fluorescence‑Based 
ROS Analysis
To identify the dominant reactive species involved in 
the photocatalytic degradation process, scavenger trap-
ping experiments were performed using benzoquinone 
(BQ), EDTA, and isopropanol (IPA) as scavengers for 
superoxide radicals (O₂ •⁻), photogenerated holes (h⁺ ), 
and hydroxyl radicals (•OH), respectively. As shown 
in Fig. 18a, the degradation efficiency decreased signif-
icantly in the presence of all scavengers, indicating the 
participation of multiple reactive species in the degra-
dation process. However, the most pronounced inhi-
bition was observed with IPA, where the degradation 
efficiency decreased from 100% to approximately 5%. 
In comparison, the degradation efficiencies decreased 
to approximately 25% and 18% in the presence of 
BQ and EDTA, respectively. These findings demon-
strate that hydroxyl radicals (•OH) are the dominant 
oxidative species, while superoxide radicals and pho-
togenerated holes also contribute to the photocatalytic 
degradation process. The formation of hydroxyl radi-
cals was further verified through fluorescence measure-
ments (Fig.  18b). The fluorescence intensity increased 
progressively with irradiation time for both ZnO and 
CdO NPs, indicating continuous generation of •OH 
radicals during photocatalysis. The higher fluorescence 
intensity observed for ZnO suggests more efficient pro-
duction of reactive oxygen species, which is consistent 
with its superior photocatalytic performance. Collec-
tively, the scavenger and fluorescence analyses confirm 
that hydroxyl radicals are the primary reactive species 
responsible for pollutant degradation.
Although IPA, BQ, and EDTA are commonly 
employed (Table  8) as scavengers for hydroxyl radi-
cals (•OH), superoxide radicals (•O₂⁻), and photogen-
erated holes (h⁺ ), respectively, their selectivity is not 
absolute and secondary interactions with other reac-
tive species or catalyst surface sites may occur. There-
fore, the scavenger experiments should be regarded as 
providing qualitative information regarding the rela-
tive contribution of reactive species rather than defini-
tive quantification. Nevertheless, the significantly 
greater suppression of photocatalytic activity observed 
in the presence of IPA indicates that hydroxyl radicals 
(•OH) play the dominant role in the degradation pro-
cess, while superoxide radicals and photogenerated 
holes also contribute to pollutant oxidation.

3.7.11 Different Pollutants Load
The photocatalytic efficiency of ZnO and CdO NPs 
for the degradation of CR, MB, and MO dyes at dif-
ferent pollutant loads (50, 100, and 150  ppm) are 
presented in Fig. 20(a–c). In all cases, ZnO exhibited 
superior catalytic activity compared to CdO, which 
can be attributed to its higher surface area, more effi-
cient charge separation, and suitable band gap for 
visible-light-driven photocatalysis. The efficiency 
decreased with increasing dye concentration due to 
reduced light penetration and active site saturation at 
higher pollutant loads. Among the dyes, CR showed 
the highest degradation efficiency, followed by MB 
and MO, indicating that molecular structure and dye 
catalyst interactions strongly influence photocatalytic 
performance. The rate constant trends in Fig.  20d 
further confirm this order (CR > MB > MO for both 
catalysts), with ZnO consistently achieving higher k 
values than CdO across all concentrations, validating 
its potential as a more effective photocatalyst for real 
wastewater treatment.
4 Conclusions
In this study, ZnO and CdO NPs were successfully 
synthesized via simple precipitation method and sys-
tematically characterized using XRD, SEM, EDX, 
TEM, BET, and UV–VIS spectroscopy. Their pho-
tocatalytic activities were evaluated for the degrada-
tion of CR, MB, and MO dyes present in real textile 
wastewater collected from industries in Karachi, Paki-
stan. ZnO NPs, with a particle size of 18  nm and a 
band gap of 3.0 eV, demonstrated superior photocata-
lytic performance, achieving degradation efficiencies 
of 95% CR, 86% MB, and 89% MO under optimal 
conditions (pH 12, 0.30  mg/mL catalyst dosage, 
10  ppm dye concentration). CdO NPs also exhib-
ited notable activity, with degradation efficiencies of 
76%, 75%, and 78% for CR, MB, and MO, respec-
tively. Scavenger studies confirmed that hydroxyl 
(•OH) and superoxide (•O₂ ⁻) radicals were the pre-
dominant reactive species driving the photocatalytic 
process. Furthermore, COD analysis revealed over 
85% mineralization, indicating effective breakdown 
of organic pollutants. The photocatalysts also dis-
played good recyclability, with a marginal decline 
in activity (~ 3.5%) after three consecutive cycles. 
These findings underscore the potential of ZnO and 
CdO NPs as robust, efficient, and reusable photo-
catalysts for the treatment of real industrial dye efflu-
ents, offering a promising strategy for sustainable 
wastewater remediation. Although CdO exhibited 
photocatalytic activity, the potential environmental 
risks associated with cadmium-containing materials 
should be considered before large-scale application. 
Future studies should evaluate metal-ion leaching 
and long-term environmental safety under practical 
operating conditions.
