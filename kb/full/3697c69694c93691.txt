High variability in carbon stable 
isotopes in the macroalga Ulva spp. 
and implications for eutrophication 
indicators
Javier Lloret
Ulva spp. are among the most commonly encountered macroalgal species forming algal blooms 
and are responsible for many Green Tide events worldwide. Numerous adaptations have provided 
this genus with broad plasticity in carbon acquisition and metabolism, allowing rapid responses to 
nutrient inputs and sustained growth during the limiting conditions that arise during large bloom 
events. Because of this plasticity, the carbon isotopic composition of Ulva spp. may be highly variable. 
A compilation of 1,382 observations of Ulva δ¹³C signatures from literature sources and systematic 
surveys revealed an unusually wide range of values, much broader than what is typically reported 
for other marine macroalgae, with a bimodal distribution. This pattern suggests two distinct growth 
modes in Ulva spp.: one characteristic of non-limiting conditions, and another associated with 
bloom conditions where rapid growth and carbon limitation drive higher δ¹³C signatures. Analysis 
of environmental drivers in New England sites further showed that enriched Ulva δ¹³C values arise 
under the limiting conditions that develop within dense macroalgal canopies in eutrophic estuaries. 
Excess nutrient inputs stimulate large macroalgal accumulations, which then alter the surrounding 
physicochemical environment and shift isotopic signatures. These results confirm that Ulva δ¹³C is a 
valuable tool for assessing the trophic status of coastal environments.
Keywords Ulva, Carbon stable isotopes, Eutrophication, Algal blooms, Indicators
In the coastal ocean, primary production is often limited by the availability of nutrients. The growing human 
population and increased settlement and use of coastal areas have caused a worldwide rise in nutrient inputs to 
coastal waters1,2. This increased nutrient availability often leads to the overgrowth of primary producers, which 
in turn can cause decreased water quality and eutrophication, a growing threat to many coastal ecosystems3.
In many shallow coastal areas, a common symptom of eutrophication is the occurrence of marine macroalgal 
blooms4–8. The effects of these blooms on receiving environments include drastic restructuring of natural 
communities and disruption of ecosystem function7,9–11. Accumulations of seaweed can physically damage other 
coastal life in valuable ecosystems such as seagrass beds 7,12,13, coral reefs 14,15, or salt marshes 16. The decay of 
excessive algal organic matter can also foster anoxic conditions that lead to fish and shellfish kills17–19, threatening 
commercial harvests. Other consequences of macroalgal blooms make coastal areas increasingly undesirable for 
human use, for example, by decreasing aesthetic value or affecting navigation20,21, impacts that can also result in 
economic losses22. Additionally, large amounts of decomposing algal material can accumulate along shorelines, 
producing bad smells or even more severe human health issues23.
Macroalgal blooms typically involve relatively few opportunistic taxa that are widely distributed along the 
world’s coasts7,24. Although various taxonomic groups of green, brown and red macroalgae can form blooms, 
in many cases the problem is characterized by large accumulations of green algae along the beaches of shallow 
coastal ecosystems, often referred to as “Green Tides” .
Among the proliferating green algae, species of the genus Ulva are the most commonly encountered24,25. Ulva 
spp. are responsible for many Green Tide events worldwide, including the massive free-floating blooms of Ulva 
prolifera in the Y ellow Sea, the largest Green Tides ever recorded26. Due to their particular morphological and 
physiological adaptations, Ulva spp. respond rapidly to nutrient inputs with high rates of nutrient uptake and 
growth27,28. The key to Ulva’s success lies in its ability to rapidly accumulate biomass under suitable environmental 
conditions, often outcompeting other fast-growing marine plants and algae 29–31. As bloom-forming species,

Ulva spp. frequently face strong inorganic carbon limitation due to rapid growth and high population densities. 
Additionally, when Ulva spp. grow in dense blooms, the formation of stagnant boundary layers around algal 
tissues can restrict inorganic carbon diffusion. The species of this genus must have evolved a variety of carbon 
acquisition strategies to overcome these limitations 32. These include the ability to access bicarbonate from 
seawater as an inorganic carbon source through carbon concentrating mechanisms that convert it to CO 2 for 
photosynthesis32–34, as well as plasticity in use of different photosynthetic carbon metabolic pathways35–37.
A common approach used to identify carbon sources and metabolic pathways in plants and algae is by 
analyzing the ratio of the two stable isotopes of carbon. The stable carbon isotope composition of plants and 
algae is a useful tool for understanding physiological processes and their responses to varying environmental 
conditions. Carbon has two naturally occurring stable isotopes, ¹²C and ¹³C. 98.9% of naturally occurring carbon 
is ¹²C, while ¹³C is much less abundant, accounting for only about 1.1% 38. Because biological and chemical 
processes typically prefer ¹²C but retain ¹³C, ecologists can examine the ratio of heavy to light isotopes to gain 
insights into carbon sources and metabolic processes within plant and algal tissues. The ratios are expressed 
in delta notation (δ), where δ 13C (‰) = [(R sample – R standard)/Rstandard] ∙ 1000, with R representing the ratio of 
heavy to light isotopes in a sample relative to a standard. Previous compilations of δ 13C values and ranges in 
marine macroalgae have shown that most genera exhibit very distinct isotopic values, typically constrained 
within narrow ranges of less than 10‰ within a species39.
Given the plasticity in carbon acquisition and metabolisms discussed above, it might be expected that the 
isotopic composition of Ulva spp. tissues spans a wide range of δ 13C values. By examining the variability in 
carbon isotopic signatures of Ulva spp., one of the most prominent and widespread algae causing Green Tides, 
we can gain insight into the traits that make this genus so successful at exploiting excess nutrients and forming 
large, often disruptive, blooms. In this paper, available literature data on stable carbon isotope ratios in Ulva spp. 
were reviewed, with particular emphasis on the factors relevant to observed variation in isotope signatures. Next, 
a comprehensive dataset from macroalgal surveys conducted at a subset of New England sites was analyzed to 
elucidate the relative influence of drivers responsible for variability in δ 13C signatures in Ulva spp. Finally, the 
potential use of stable carbon isotopes in Ulva spp. for studies of eutrophication and algal blooms was addressed.
Methods
Literature data collection
An extensive literature search using online tools and bibliographic databases, including Web of Science ( h 
t t p : / / i s i k n o w l e d g e . c o m ) , and Google Scholar ( http://scholar.google.com), was conducted by searching the 
keywords “stable isotope” AND “Ulva OR Enteromorpha” . Results from this search were carefully screened to 
ensure included data was relevant to this study. Several criteria were used to determine whether data could 
be included. Only full-tissue, naturally occurring δ 13C algal signatures were used. Data on compound-specific 
stable isotope analyses, experimental manipulations and isotope enrichment experiments were excluded. When 
available, unique individual observations were used, but if data were aggregated and reported as averages, they 
were included only when the number of samples was small and collected over a relatively limited spatial area. 
A total of 188 publications, including 173 scientific journal articles, 10 theses, and 5 technical reports published 
between 1964 and 2025, were selected. The complete list of literature sources is included as Supplementary 
Materials. Data presented in graphs were extracted by using the WebPlotDigitizer V5 software. Information on 
the sampling site location and typology was also collected.
Linking environmental variables to Ulva δ¹³C variability
To elucidate the relative influence of various environmental drivers responsible for variability in δ13C signatures 
in Ulva spp., a comprehensive dataset from macroalgal and water quality surveys conducted in a subset of 
New England sites was analyzed. A collection of sites within the Waquoit Bay estuarine system on Cape Cod, 
Massachusetts, were selected for this study. The shallow system, with most areas less than 1 m deep, regularly 
experiences the development of dense macroalgal canopies7. Various species of bloom forming algae, including 
Ulva lactuca, are common in the area40.
Interestingly, the various small sub-estuaries entering Waquoit Bay serve as a landscape-scale experiment on 
the effects of estuarine eutrophication. The surrounding sub-watersheds exhibit contrasting land cover and land 
use, ranging from protected parkland to densely developed suburban areas. These differences result in variable 
nitrogen discharges to the sub-estuaries40,41, with annual loads ranging from 5 to 580 kg N per hectare of estuary 
area, levels comparable to those experienced by over 90% of the world’s estuaries42. The wide range of nitrogen 
load magnitudes entering the various Waquoit Bay sub-estuaries help explain the dissimilarities in water quality 
and macroalgal biomass at different locations7,40,42.
Macroalgal samples were collected intermittently from sites within the Waquoit Bay estuarine system 
between 2015 and 2024. Samples were collected using a 152 × 152 mm Ekman grab, rinsed through a 1 mm 
mesh to remove excess mud and sediment, and stored on ice until further processing in the lab. In the laboratory, 
samples were sorted by species. All algal matter was then dried at 60 °C to constant weight for at least 24 h 
before being weighed. Algal biomass was expressed as grams of dry tissue per square meter of estuary bottom. 
Dried macroalgal samples were ground and analyzed for carbon stable isotope signatures. The stable isotopic 
values were determined using a Europa 20–20 Continuous-Flow Isotope Ratio Mass Spectrometer (Sercon Ltd., 
Cheshire, UK).
Water samples from the same sites were collected by submerging an acid-washed 1-L polypropylene bottle 
below the water surface. 60 mL of the samples was immediately filtered through a 25-mm diameter, pre-ashed 
GF/F Whatman microfiber filter and frozen for storage and transport to the laboratory. In the laboratory, nitrate 
+ nitrite concentrations were determined on a Lachat QuikChem 8000 AutoAnalyzer (PerkinElmer, Shelton, 
Connecticut) using a standard colorimetric assay 43. Ammonium concentrations were determined using the

Solórzano method44 and measured using a Varian Cary-50 UV-Visible Spectrophotometer (Varian Inc.,Palo 
Alto, California). In this study, the concentrations (µM) of nitrate, nitrite and ammonium in estuarine waters 
were summed and reported as Dissolved Inorganic Nitrogen (DIN).
Data on water temperature (°C) and pH, variables selected to be potential drivers responsible for variability 
in Ulva δ13C signatures, were obtained from the Waquoit Bay National Estuarine Research Reserve System-wide 
Monitoring Program. The Program collects continuous 15-min basic water quality data logging. Protocols are 
standardized and require frequent calibration and redeployment of YSI sondes45. For the purpose of this study, 
all available temperature and pH observations on the 15 days before each of the macroalgal sampling dates were 
averaged, assuming the conditions during those 15 days were representative of the environment in which algal 
tissues developed.
Additionally, recently published data on annual nitrogen loads (expressed as kg N ha −1) entering the 
various Waquoit Bay sub-estuaries were also compiled 40. Since DIN data only provide a snapshot of nutrient 
concentrations at the time of sampling, annual nitrogen loads offer a more accurate representation of total 
nutrient availability for algal growth throughout the entire growing season.
Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to explore the direct and indirect effects of water temperature, 
pH, nutrient availability, and algal biomass on Ulva δ¹³C signatures. The model was built based on hypothesized 
causal pathways that link nitrogen inputs from watersheds to changes in water chemistry and algal responses.
The SEM was fitted using the lavaan package46 in R. The model included several predictor variables: nitrogen 
loading, algal biomass, pH, and DIN. Log transformations of nitrogen loading and DIN data, and square-root of 
algal biomass were applied to improve linearity and meet model assumptions. Univariate normality was tested 
with Shapiro-Wilk tests. The model was fitted to 62 complete observations. Standardized path coefficients were 
used to assess the relative strength of relationships among variables, and the model fit was evaluated using several 
indices such as the Chi-square (χ²) test, Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean 
Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Residual 
normality of δ¹³C was assessed using a Shapiro-Wilk test.
Results and discussion
Global variability in Ulva δ13C signatures
The compilation of Ulva δ13C signatures done in this work contained a total of 1,382 observations. The dataset 
includes observations of at least 16 different Ulva species (Table 1) collected in 49 countries on the coasts of all 
continents and major regional seas (Fig. 1).
Compiled Ulva δ13C signatures revealed a remarkable wide range of values, from a minimum of −26.7‰ to a 
maximum of −3.9‰ (Fig. 2). The observed range is much broader than what is typically reported for most marine 
macroalgae, which usually show ranges of less than 10‰ 39. The δ13C values displayed a bimodal distribution, 
with a relatively more frequent group of observations centered around the most 13C-depleted isotopic signatures 
(mean ≈ −16.2‰), and a less frequent but still abundant group of 13C-enriched signatures (mean ≈ −10.9‰; 
Fig. 2).
Compiled δ13C values did not display any apparent relationship with latitude (Fig. 1 inset), and neither the 
width of the δ 13C range nor the existence of the two modes appeared to be associated with species-specific 
differences in signatures (Table 1). This suggests that the most likely explanation for the bimodal pattern is the 
existence of two distinct modes of carbon acquisition and/or metabolism in the genus.

Several factors could strongly influence the isotopic make up of Ulva spp. tissues. First, the formation of 
diffusive boundary layers around algal tissues, especially in areas with restricted water exchange or dense algal 
accumulations, can limit inorganic carbon diffusion. This can lead to the entrapment of 13C, which is otherwise 
more strongly discriminated against, resulting in elevated δ 13C values in algal tissue 47. Second, the ability of 
Ulva spp. to use bicarbonate instead of CO 2 as an inorganic carbon source 34,39, may also lead to higher δ 13C 
values, both because bicarbonate has a more enriched isotopic signature than dissolved CO 2, and because the 
processes associated with active transport of bicarbonate across algal cell membranes produce smaller isotopic 
fractionation than the diffusive transport of CO 2

Limited diffusion of inorganic carbon, the ability to use bicarbonate as a carbon source, and physiological 
adaptations that maintain high carbon uptake rates are all conditions commonly experienced by Ulva spp. during 
bloom events. It is therefore reasonable to infer that the observed bimodal distribution of δ¹³C values reflects two 
different modes of growth: one more typical under non-limiting conditions, and another associated with bloom 
conditions, where rapid growth and carbon limitation drive higher δ¹³C signatures in the algal tissues.
When information on the type of coastal environment where Ulva spp. specimens were collected was 
considered, an emergent pattern became evident. Although the ranges in Ulva δ¹³C values were similar across 
all coastal environments, higher values (i.e. those centered around −10.9‰) were more frequently observed 
in environments with more restricted water exchange such as shallow coastal lagoons, semi-enclosed bays 
and harbors, and estuaries. In contrast, samples from more exposed environments, including wave-exposed 
beaches and island shorelines, more consistently displayed lower δ¹³C values, with fewer occurrences of elevated 
signatures (Fig. 3). Coastal systems with intermediate levels of water exchange, such as rias, fjords, and open bays 
or gulfs, exhibited an intermediate range of δ¹³C values (Fig. 3). This pattern suggests that the environmental 
conditions conducive to elevated δ¹³C signatures in Ulva spp., such as carbon limitation and altered inorganic 
carbon availability, are more prevalent in settings where water exchange is restricted.
Interestingly, the variability in δ¹³C signatures of Ulva spp. differed significantly across thallus morphologies 
(blades vs. tubular forms; Mann–Whitney U = 149,267, p < 0.001). Although the range of δ¹³C values was 
similarly broad for both morphologies (Table 1), enriched δ¹³C signatures were more frequently observed in 
blade-like species. Despite the statistical significance of this difference, the effect size was small (rank-biserial 
r = 0.18; Cliff ’s δ = 0.30), indicating that the magnitude of this variation across morphologies was modest. Several 
factors may have limited the conclusiveness of these results. For instance, there was a substantial disparity in 
data availability between the two growth forms, with six times more data from blade-like species than from

Environmental drivers of Ulva δ¹³C variability
The sub-estuaries of Waquoit Bay harbor a complex and abundant macroalgal community. The macroalgal 
canopy is composed of various opportunistic seaweed species growing together on the estuary bottoms, 
although occasionally large mats of floating macroalgae can also be observed in some locations. The majority of 
the biomass in the collected samples was contributed by the red algae Gracilaria tikvahiae, Polysiphonia urceolata 
and Ceramium sp., as well as the green algae Cladophora vagabunda, Chaetomorpha sp. and Ulva lactuca.
For most species, δ¹³C analysis of algal tissues revealed distinct isotopic signatures with relatively narrow 
ranges (Fig. 4). In contrast, U. lactuca displayed a much wider range of δ¹³C values (Fig. 4), despite co-occurring 
with other species within the same canopy and being exposed to similar environmental conditions. This suggests 
that U. lactuca is more responsive to environmental variation and its physiology leads to greater isotopic 
variability than the other algal groups.
To explore the drivers of this variability, the relationships between Ulva δ¹³C values and a set of environmental 
variables measured across Waquoit Bay were examined. A positive correlation with temperature was initially 
expected, under the assumption that higher temperatures would promote faster growth, potentially leading to 
carbon limitation and enrichment in heavier carbon isotopes. However, the data did not support this hypothesis, 
and no clear relationship between temperature and δ¹³C values was observed (Fig. 5). Similarly, an increase in 
Ulva δ¹³C values at higher pH was anticipated, which could reflect decreased CO₂ availability and a greater 
reliance on bicarbonate as a carbon source. Y et again, no consistent trend emerged from the data (Fig. 5).
In contrast, nutrient availability appeared to have a clearer influence on Ulva δ¹³C. While relationships 
between δ¹³C and water column DIN at the time of sampling were weak, stronger patterns emerged when δ¹³C

The strongest and most consistent patterns in Ulva δ¹³C emerged when compared to algal biomass. 
Surprisingly, these patterns were more robust when total macroalgal biomass, including all canopy species, was 
considered, rather than U. lactuca biomass alone. At sites with low U. lactuca biomass, δ¹³C values ranged widely 
from depleted to enriched. However, at sites with high U. lactuca biomass, δ¹³C values were consistently higher 
(Fig. 5). This pattern was even more pronounced when total algal biomass (all species combined) was used: low 
biomass corresponded with depleted δ¹³C, while high biomass was associated with enriched and stable δ¹³C 
signatures (Fig. 5).
These results suggest that the broader macroalgal community, not just Ulva spp., plays an important role in 
shaping the biogeochemical conditions that influence Ulva δ¹³C. High overall algal biomass reflects eutrophic 
conditions that promote rapid growth and carbon limitation, ultimately driving the observed isotopic shifts.
To further explore the influence of environmental drivers on Ulva δ¹³C, and to assess the relative importance 
of different mechanisms underlying its variability, a SEM, including four endogenous variables: pH, DIN, total 
algal biomass, and Ulva δ¹³C; and two exogenous variables: temperature and nitrogen load, was constructed 
(Fig. 6). Overall, the SEM showed good fit: χ²(2) = 2.40, p = 0.302; CFI = 0.997; TLI = 0.982; RMSEA = 0.056; 
SRMR = 0.038. The model explained 51% of the variance in δ¹³C (R² = 0.511).
Path coefficients revealed several significant relationships consistent with the initial hypotheses. Nitrogen 
loading had strong positive effects on both algal biomass and DIN concentrations. In turn, algal biomass 
negatively affected pH, indicating that greater biomass reduces CO₂ availability in the system (Fig. 6). Algal 
biomass also had a positive effect on DIN, though this may reflect covariance between the two variables, as 
both increase with higher nitrogen inputs (Fig. 6). Water temperature had negative effects on both DIN and pH, 
suggesting that warmer periods lead to decreases in DIN concentrations due to increased nutrient demand by 
primary producers, as well as increases in ecosystem respiration and CO 2 production, which in turn drive pH 
values down (Fig. 6).
With respect to δ¹³C, algal biomass had a strong positive effect, supporting the hypothesis that higher biomass 
is associated with less depleted isotopic signatures (Fig. 6). This reinforces the idea that the development of dense 
macroalgal canopies influences biogeochemical conditions in ways that very markedly shape Ulva δ¹³C.
 Ulva δ¹³C also appeared to be negatively affected by pH (Fig. 6). Elevated pH is often associated with heavier 
δ¹³C signatures in marine primary producers due to reduced availability of dissolved CO 2 and greater reliance 
on bicarbonate48. Nevertheless, several factors could explain the absence of a clear relationship in this case. 
First, the pH measurements used here represent conditions in the upper part of the water column, which may 
not accurately reflect the microenvironment experienced by U. lactuca in the macroalgal mats near the estuary

DIN and temperature did not have significant direct effects on Ulva δ¹³C (Fig. 6). While both variables are 
known to influence algal growth rates, their variability alone was insufficient to explain the observed isotopic 
differences. This suggests that rapid growth, by itself, may not be enough to drive shifts in δ¹³C signatures. 
Instead, it appears that the development of bloom conditions, characterized by high algal biomass and associated 
changes in water chemistry, is necessary to significantly alter Ulva spp. ’s carbon isotopic composition.
Implications for eutrophication studies
Nitrogen stable isotopes are widely used to trace sources of excess nitrogen in eutrophication studies. Recent 
evidence suggests that δ¹³C signatures of phytoplankton can also serve as sensitive indicators of eutrophication48,51. 
The results presented here confirm that the same holds true for green macroalgae of the genus Ulva, a group 
of fast-growing opportunistic species that are among the most widespread contributors to Green Tide events 
globally.
The global data analysis highlights the broad applicability of using Ulva δ¹³C as an indicator of eutrophication 
and macroalgal blooms. Elevated δ¹³C values were more frequent in shallow, restricted coastal environments 
near human settlements—precisely the conditions where large nuisance macroalgal blooms are most frequent—
underscoring this metric’s potential for detecting eutrophic conditions.
Data from the Waquoit Bay sites further demonstrated that Ulva δ¹³C is a responsive indicator of nutrient 
enrichment and macroalgal blooms. Together, the results support a mechanistic pathway in which land-derived 
nutrient inputs promote excessive macroalgal biomass, which in turn alters within-canopy water chemistry and 
shifts the carbon isotopic composition of U. lactuca. The lack of a clear direct response to changes in pH or 
temperature suggests that δ¹³C signatures of Ulva spp. may not necessarily respond to conditions projected 
under future ocean acidification and warming scenarios. As observed in other algal species52, changes in seawater 
pH may not significantly affect the physiological processes of photosynthesis and nutrient uptake in Ulva spp. 
during bloom conditions. Instead, local conditions within dense algal canopies, shaped by factors such as 
nutrient enrichment and eutrophication, are likely to have a stronger influence on carbon isotope composition.
In many coastal systems, traditional indicators of eutrophication, such as nutrient concentrations or 
phytoplankton biomass, can be highly variable in space and time, often making the assessment of the trophic 
status of ecosystems difficult42. The results presented here confirm that Ulva δ¹³C is a valuable tool for assessing
