Abstract
Purpose Anxiety is associated with lower well-being in autistic children, yet little is known about how anxiety symptoms 
moderate the relationship between neurodivergent traits and Quality of Life (QoL).
Methods The present study analyzed data from 316 clinician-diagnosed autistic children aged 7–17 from the Province of 
Ontario Neurodevelopmental Disorders Network. QoL, depression (as a control variable), anxiety, autistic traits, and ADHD 
traits were assessed. We expected anxiety to interact with autistic and ADHD traits in their associations with QoL domains.
Results Regression analyses revealed that after controlling for depression, greater anxiety was associated with reduced 
school QoL. Additionally, greater ADHD hyperactive-impulsive (ADHD-HI) traits were associated with lower family QoL, 
while greater ADHD inattentive (ADHD-I) traits were associated with lower self-esteem QoL. Autistic traits were associated 
with lower friendship QoL but higher school QoL. There were interactions between ADHD-HI traits and anxiety. At high 
levels of anxiety, ADHD-HI traits were associated with higher school and self-esteem QoL. At low levels of anxiety, ADHD-
HI traits were associated with lower friendship QoL. No significant associations were found for the interaction between 
ADHD-I traits and anxiety, or for the interaction between autistic traits and anxiety.
Conclusions These results introduce novel insights into how autistic traits, ADHD traits and anxiety interact in their asso -
ciations with QoL in autistic children. Given that no corrections were made for multiple comparisons, this study should be 
considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, intended to inspire future research that adopts a trait-based approach in 
studying the QoL of neurodiverse children.

Introduction
Quality of Life (QoL) is defined by the World Health Orga-
nization (WHO) as “individuals’ perceptions of their posi -
tion in life in the context of the culture and value systems 
in which they live and in relation to their goals, expecta -
tions, standards and concerns” (WHO, 1995). Evaluating 
QoL often involves assessing both objective components, 
such as physical health, and subjective components, such 
as psychological wellbeing. QoL is increasingly used to 
understand outcomes for neurodivergent children, and in 
the present study, it serves as a framework for examining 
how neurodevelopmental and anxiety traits are related to 
the daily lives of autistic children.
The KINDL framework assesses children’s health-
related QoL across six domains: physical wellbeing, emo-
tional wellbeing , self-esteem, family, friends, and school

functioning. In the KINDL, physical wellbeing refers to 
somatic health indicators such as feeling ill, experiencing 
pain, and feeling tired or energetic (Ravens-Sieberer & Bull-
inger, 2000). Emotional wellbeing reflects experiences such 
as enjoyment, boredom, loneliness, and fear. Self-esteem 
captures feelings of pride, satisfaction, and perceived com -
petence. Family reflects the degree of comfort at home, the 
presence of conflict, and experiences of restriction. Friends 
assesses peer acceptance, shared activities, and feelings of 
similarity or difference relative to peers. School function -
ing includes the ease of schoolwork, interest in lessons, 
and worries about grades or the future (Ravens-Sieberer 
& Bullinger, 2000). Collectively, these domains represent 
distinct areas of children’s lives that vary in developmental 
demands and contextual influences. Higher scores in these 
domains reflect better QoL. This study aimed to examine 
how autistic and Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder 
(ADHD) traits are related to QoL outcomes with anxiety as 
a moderator.
Autistic traits, such as social communication challenges 
and sensory sensitivities, may influence emotional wellbe-
ing, peer relationships , and family dynamics , as they are 
often linked to greater social stress, reduced social par -
ticipation, and increased strain on families (Kojovic et al., 
2019; Smith et al., 2023; Leekam et al., 2011). ADHD inat-
tentive and hyperactive–impulsive traits may be related to 
differences in school engagement, behavioral regulation at 
home, and self-esteem, as these traits are linked to academic 
difficulties, executive functioning challenges, and behavior-
related conflicts that are associated with differences in a 
child’s self-perception (Danckaerts et al., 2010; Graetz et 
al., 2001; Peñuelas-Calvo et al., 2021). Work in the adult 
ADHD literature has noted that inattentive versus hyper -
active traits may be differentially associated with QoL; 
however, these findings are mixed (Agarwal et al., 2012). 
Moreover, these studies were conducted in adults with 
ADHD, not autistic children. There is still some evidence 
to suggest that these two types of traits should be studied 
separately (Wu et al., 2023). Anxiety has been associated 
with differences in physical wellbeing, emotional stability, 
and school participation , as elevated anxiety is linked to 
greater somatic complaints, heightened emotional distress, 
and increased avoidance of demanding or unpredictable 
environments (Adams et al. 2019a; Trembath et al. 2012). 
Evaluating these six KINDL QoL domains separately pro -
vides a detailed and theoretically grounded approach to 
understanding how autistic traits, ADHD traits and anxiety 
uniquely influence multiple aspects of children’s everyday 
functioning.
Although prior research has examined relationships 
among autistic traits, ADHD traits, anxiety, and wellbeing, 
much of this work has only examined these traits within 
the context of their associated diagnosis. Broadly, autistic 
traits and ADHD traits have been associated with lower 
QoL, including areas such as psychosocial functioning and 
academic achievement (Danckaerts et al., 2010; Kuhlthau 
et al., 2010; Tedla et al., 2024). Other work demonstrates 
that autistic and ADHD traits are linked with elevated anxi-
ety and depression (Garcha & Smith, 2024). Recent reviews 
highlight that research in this area is fragmented and that 
existing studies rarely integrate neurodevelopmental traits 
and mental health variables using a multidimensional or 
transdiagnostic approach (Garcha & Smith, 2025; Torices 
Callejo et al., 2025). Notably, despite often experiencing 
ADHD traits (Rong et al., 2021), little is known about how 
ADHD traits are related to the QoL of autistic children. 
Additionally, it is unclear whether anxiety modifies (i.e., 
moderates) the strength of associations between these traits 
and different QoL domains. The present study addresses 
this gap by considering these trait1 dimensions concurrently 
across six domains of QoL.
Autistic Traits and QoL
Autistic traits can critically affect the daily lives of autistic 
children. Challenges with social communication have been 
linked to difficulties with an individual’s ability to engage 
meaningfully in peer interactions and have been associated 
with strain on family dynamics (Benson, 2006; Chamber-
lain et al., 2007; Kelly et al., 2008; Schroeder et al., 2014; 
Smith et al., 2023). Sensory challenges are also associated 
with more social difficulties, lower adaptive functioning, 
and lower overall QoL (Ausderau et al., 2016; Kojovic et 
al., 2019). Disruptions in sensory processing can be associ-
ated with reduced QoL by affecting an individual’s ability to 
regulate emotions, engage socially, and manage daily func -
tioning (Thye et al., 2018; Zhai et al., 2023). Furthermore, 
restricted and repetitive behaviours (RRBs) interfere with 
daily activities and social connections by limiting behav -
ioural flexibility and openness to new experiences, which 
can in turn contribute to reduced QoL (Bishop et al., 2006; 
Leekam et al., 2011; Troyb et al., 2016).
Higher levels of autistic traits are consistently associated 
with lower QoL in child and adult populations (de Vries & 
Geurts, 2015; Kuhlthau et al., 2010; Mason et al., 2018; 
Tedla et al., 2024). In comparison to non-autistic peers, lower 
QoL has been reported in autistic children across physical, 
psychosocial, and school domains, with the strongest effects 
consistently seen in the social and emotional domains (de 
Vries & Geurts, 2015; Kuhlthau et al., 2010; Tedla et al.,

However, QoL is shaped not only by the presence or 
severity of autistic traits but also by the fit between the indi-
vidual and the environment. The association between trait 
expression and QoL has been related to the extent to which 
environments accommodate or constrain individual needs 
(Andrews et al., 2024; Poulsen et al., 2025). Many children 
navigate structured school and home environments orga -
nized around low child autonomy, rigid routines and social 
expectations that may not align with their neurodivergent 
profiles (Al Qutub et al., 2024; Kahveci et al., 2024). Thus, 
observed associations between trait levels and QoL likely 
reflect both intrinsic characteristics and environmental fit.
Autistic Traits, Anxiety and QoL
Co-occurring mental health challenges, such as anxiety, 
are associated with additional barriers to achieving a good 
QoL in autistic youth (MacKenzie et al., 2024; Myles et al., 
2001; Trembath et al., 2012). Anxiety is a persistent emo -
tional state marked by excessive worry and apprehension. 
It is associated with significant impairment in social, aca-
demic, and occupational functioning (American Psychiatric 
Association, 2022). A meta-analysis found that approxi -
mately 40% of autistic children had a co-occurring anxiety 
disorder, which is nearly double the estimated 15 to 20% 
prevalence in the general child and adolescent population 
(van Steensel et al., 2011). Anxiety is linked to challenges 
with social interactions, sensory sensitivities, sleep difficul-
ties, and intolerance of uncertainty, making autistic traits 
more difficult to manage (Mihai et al., 2026; Myles et al., 
2001; Richdale et al., 2023; Trembath et al., 2012; Wood 
& Gadow, 2010; Zaidman-Zait et al., 2025). While anxi -
ety is partly neurobiological, environmental factors, includ-
ing lower socioeconomic status, chronic exposure to stress, 
bullying, and academic pressure, can also influence anxiety 
in autistic youth (Mihai et al., 2026). Caregivers of autis -
tic children with anxiety frequently report elevated stress 
levels, disrupted sleep patterns, increased meltdowns, and 
heightened dependency, particularly when they are unable 
to effectively communicate their triggers (Cassidy et al., 
2008; Ozsivadjian et al., 2012). Anxiety is also associated 
with heightened physiological arousal to sensory sensitivi -
ties, leading to the avoidance of overstimulating environ -
ments (e.g., crowded spaces), and a greater vulnerability 
to panic attacks, emotional outbursts, and self-regulation 
struggles (Bellini, 2004, 2006; Mihai et al., 2026; Richdale 
et al., 2023; Trembath et al., 2012).
Some studies have found that higher anxiety can be 
associated with lower QoL for autistic children beyond the 
impact of autistic traits alone. Adams et al. ( 2019a) exam-
ined the association between anxiety and QoL in autistic 
children. Those above the cut-off for high anxiety had lower 
scores on the physical, emotional, social and school QoL 
domains. Similarly, other work noted that anxiety symp -
toms were more strongly related to emotional and school 
QoL than autistic traits (Adams et al., 2020). Addition -
ally, although limited research has explored these relation -
ships in children, studies involving autistic adults suggest 
that anxiety may serve as both a moderator and mediator 
in the relationship between autistic traits and QoL (Lin et 
al., 2025; Smith et al., 2019). Anxiety can become a pri -
mary driver of reduced well-being at higher levels. Anxiety 
may also partially account for the link between autistic traits 
and lower QoL across domains as anxiety can impact how 
autistic traits manifest (Lin et al., 2025; Myles et al., 2001; 
Smith et al., 2019; Trembath et al., 2012). Together, these 
findings also raise the possibility that anxiety may modify 
the strength of associations between autistic traits and QoL, 
rather than acting solely as an independent predictor.
ADHD Traits and QoL
While autistic traits and anxiety appear to play a sig -
nificant role in the QoL of autistic children, ADHD traits 
represent another important co-occurring factor that is 
under-researched. ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condi -
tion characterized by persistent inattention and/or hyper-
activity-impulsivity that interferes with daily functioning 
(American Psychiatric Association, 2022). ADHD is classi-
fied into three presentations: inattentive (ADHD-I), hyper-
active-impulsive (ADHD-HI), and combined (ADHD-C). 
The inattentive presentation includes traits such as difficulty 
sustaining attention, forgetfulness, and poor attention to 
detail. The hyperactive-impulsive presentation is defined by 
behaviours such as excessive movement, impulsive verbal 
responses, and difficulty waiting for turns. The combined 
presentation encompasses symptoms from both domains 
(American Psychiatric Association, 2022). ADHD affects a 
substantial proportion of autistic individuals, with a meta-
analysis reporting a current pooled prevalence of 38.5% and 
a lifetime prevalence of 40.2% (Rong et al., 2021). As such, 
it is crucial to understand how these traits may impact the 
lives of autistic children.
One study suggests that children with both autism and 
ADHD experience lower QoL than those with autism alone 
(Tedla et al., 2024); however, research on ADHD traits in 
autistic children in the context of QoL is limited. Studies

assessing ADHD traits in children with ADHD consistently 
suggest that greater overall ADHD trait levels are associ -
ated with lower QoL across psychosocial, family, and 
achievement-related domains, with different functional and 
behavioural outcomes that are unique to each trait subtype 
(Danckaerts et al., 2010; Garner et al., 2013; Gjervan et al., 
2014; Graetz et al., 2001; Wolraich et al., 1996). ADHD-I 
traits have been associated with academic difficulties, lower 
self-esteem, and internalizing problems such as anxiety 
and depression, whereas ADHD-HI traits are more often 
linked to disruptive classroom behaviours, oppositional -
ity, and rule-breaking (Garner et al., 2013; Gjervan et al., 
2014; Graetz et al., 2001; Wolraich et al., 1996). Addition-
ally, children with an ADHD diagnosis often have poorer 
psychosocial QoL compared to neurotypical peers (Escobar 
et al., 2005; Klassen et al., 2006). Nevertheless, it remains 
unclear how inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive ADHD 
traits uniquely relate to the QoL of autistic children across 
domains. Given research suggesting that inattentiveness 
and hyperactivity-impulsivity are related to different behav-
ioural and psychological outcomes, it is crucial to examine 
these trait clusters separately.
Current Study
Although research has identified how ADHD traits can be 
associated with the QoL in individuals with ADHD and in 
general population samples, it remains unclear how levels 
of ADHD-I and ADHD-HI traits may be associated with 
QoL in autistic children. It is also unclear how ADHD and 
autistic traits interact with anxiety severity across QoL 
domains. Investigating this gap is important. Autistic chil -
dren are often underrepresented in ADHD-focused research 
despite the high rate of co-occurrence (Rong et al., 2021). 
By examining ADHD traits within an autistic population, 
the present study aims to bring awareness to shared char -
acteristics across neurodevelopmental and mental health 
conditions, thereby informing more inclusive, trait-based 
research. Furthermore, the potential moderating role of anx-
iety on the association between autistic traits and QoL in 
autistic children is not yet well understood. Depression was 
included as a control variable in our analyses given the high 
co-occurrence between anxiety and depression in autistic 
youth (Hammond & Hoffman, 2014).
We hypothesized that (1) children with higher anxiety 
would experience lower QoL across all domains (Adams et 
al. 2019a, 2020; Trembath et al. 2012), (2) children with 
higher autistic and ADHD traits would experience lower 
QoL across all domains (Danckaerts et al., 2010; de Vries & 
Geurts, 2015), and (3) anxiety would moderate the relation-
ship between autistic traits and QoL, and ADHD traits and 
QoL, such that there would be a negative relationship in low 
anxiety, but that higher anxiety would weaken the strength 
of these relationships since anxiety may exert a dominant 
influence, diminishing the observable impact of neurodi-
verse traits on QoL (Lin et al., 2025; Smith et al., 2019).

need to be able to read and answer the KINDL. Thus, most 
were likely in the average range of intellectual ability and 
were able to read roughly at grade level. Additional demo -

Measures
Children’s Quality of Life Questionnaire (KINDL)
The Children’s Quality of Life Questionnaire (KINDL) 
assesses self-reported QoL across physical well-being, emo-
tional well-being, self-esteem, family, friends, and school 
functioning (Bullinger et al., 2008). Two versions were used 
based on the age of the child, including the Kid-KINDL 
(ages 7–13), and Kiddo-KINDL (ages 14–17). These two 
different versions ask the same basic questions, though with 
wording geared to the age of the child. There are 24 items 
which are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = all 
the time), where higher scores reflect better QoL (Ravens & 
Bullinger, 2000). Each subscale consists of 4 items, which 
were summed to generate a total score for each QoL dimen-
sion. Internal consistency for the KINDL in the present sam-
ple was acceptable (α = 0.70–0.74).
Revised Child Anxiety and Depression Scale, Parent Version 
(RCADS-P)
The Revised Child Anxiety and Depression Scale, Parent ver-
sion (RCADS-P) is a 47-item parent-reported questionnaire 
that assesses their child’s anxiety and depressive symptoms 
(Chorpita et al., 2000). Parents are asked how often their 
child felt each symptom in the past two weeks. Items are 
rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = never to 3 = always). The 
37 anxiety items were summed and converted to a t-score. 
Higher t-scores indicate greater anxiety severity. Similarly, 
the 10 depression items were summed and converted to a 
t-score, which was used as a control variable in our analy -
ses. Internal consistency for the subscales of the RCADS-P 
was considered acceptable to good (α = 0.68–0.84).
Social Communication Questionnaire: Lifetime (SCQ)
The Social Communication Questionnaire is a 40-item par-
ent-reported questionnaire that assesses the levels of autistic 
traits for children ages 4 and older (Berument et al., 1999). 
The items assess social responsiveness, verbal communica-
tion, and restricted and repetitive behaviors based on DSM-
IV criteria, using dichotomous “yes” or “no” responses, 
scored as 0 or 1. Items are summed to produce an overall 
score, with scores over 15 indicating a potential autism 
diagnosis (Berument et al., 1999). The SCQ showed good 
internal consistency (α = 0.86).
Strengths and Weaknesses of ADHD-Symptoms and 
Normal-Behaviours (SWAN)
The Strengths and Weaknesses of ADHD-Symptoms and 
Normal-Behavior Questionnaire (SWAN) is an 18-item 
parent-reported questionnaire that assesses strengths and 
weaknesses in attention and behavioral regulation using a 
dimensional rather than deficit-based framework (Swan-
son et al., 2012). The SWAN evaluates both ADHD-I and 
ADHD-HI traits using nine items for each (Swanson et al., 
2012). Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging 
from − 3 (Far Above Average) to + 3 (Far Below Average), 
with 0 representing average functioning compared to chil -
dren their age (Hay et al., 2007). Items were summed for 
each subscale. Higher scores represent higher ADHD trait 
levels. The SWAN demonstrated good internal consistency 
(α = 0.87).
Data Analysis
We conducted a moderated regression analysis for each 
QoL subscale/domain using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 
31.0.1.0 IBM Corp, 2025). Anxiety, autistic traits, ADHD-
HI traits, and ADHD-I traits were included as predictors. 
Depression was included as a control variable. The Kid-
KINDL and Kiddo-KINDL were combined to create one 
QoL variable. Items were summed for each subscale, and 
the appropriate items were reverse-coded. Higher scores 
reflect a higher QoL. All anxiety items from the RCADS-
P were summed and converted into t-scores to produce an 
anxiety score. Depression items from the R-CADS-P were 
summed and converted into t-scores to produce a depression 
score. Items from the SCQ were summed to create a score 
for autistic traits, with higher scores indicating higher autis-
tic trait levels. Items 1–9 from the SWAN were summed 
to produce an ADHD-I trait levels score, and items 10–18 
were summed to produce an ADHD-HI trait levels score. 
Higher scores indicated higher trait levels. All variables 
were mean centered prior to analysis. Assumptions of skew-
ness, normality and homoscedasticity were met. Only par -
ticipants with complete data were included in the analysis. 
Alpha was set at p<.05; given the exploratory nature of this 
study, corrections for multiple comparisons were not made.

Discussion
Anxiety
Findings from the present study indicate that elevated anxi-
ety levels are associated with reduced school-related QoL in 
autistic youth. This is consistent with past literature demon-
strating that autistic children above the clinical threshold for 
anxiety experience lower QoL across physical, emotional, 
social, and school functioning domains, and that anxiety 
is more strongly associated with emotional and school 
QoL than autistic traits alone (Adams et al. 2019b, 2020). 
Anxiety is negatively associated with school achievement 
for children with or without an autism diagnosis, how -
ever, school environments may have a unique influence 
on autistic students through increased social demands and 
reduced opportunities to express self-regulatory behav -
iours (e.g., restricted and repetitive behaviours; Adams et 
al. 2019b; Wood et al., 2010). However, these ideas would 
require further research to confirm that this is indeed the 
mechanism in question.
Autistic Traits
Higher autistic trait levels were associated with lower friend-
ship QoL, consistent with existing literature demonstrating 
that social communication challenges, sensory sensitivities 
and restricted and repetitive behaviours can be negatively 
associated with peer relationships (de Vries & Geurts, 2015; 
Mason et al., 2018). Higher autistic trait levels were also 
associated with better school QoL. This contradicts previous 
literature which suggests that children with higher levels of 
autistic traits often struggle in classroom environments that 
do not match their learning needs, and report lower school 
QoL (Tedla et al., 2024).
One possible explanation for the current finding is that 
children with more pronounced autistic traits may be identi-
fied earlier and receive more school-based support, possi-
bly corresponding with better school adjustment and QoL. 
Conversely, children with milder autistic traits may remain 
undiagnosed or unsupported during critical early school 
years, potentially resulting in unmet educational needs and/
or poorer school-related QoL outcomes. There is strong 
evidence that autistic children with milder trait presenta -
tions and those without co-occurring intellectual disabilities 
are diagnosed at later ages and experience greater barriers 
in accessing supports. These patterns have been linked to 
trait masking/misattributions, long waitlists, and lower

proactiveness from providers (Kentrou et al., 2019; Renty 
& Roeyers, 2006; Zuckerman et al., 2015). An alternative 
possibility to consider is that the school environment was 
not controlled for in the current study. As such, it is also 
possible that children with more pronounced autistic traits 
attended schools whose environments were more neurodi -
vergent-affirming and adaptive to diverse learning needs. As 
school environment data were not collected, this explana -
tion remains highly speculative, and future research should 
assess the role of school-level environmental factors on the 
experiences of autistic children.
ADHD-HI Traits
ADHD-HI traits were significantly associated with fam-
ily QoL, in which higher trait levels were associated with 
lower QoL. Literature in children with ADHD has shown 
that hyperactivity can lead to increased family conflict and 
stress due to disruptive behaviours (Peñuelas-Calvo et al., 
2021). Additionally, higher ADHD-I traits were associated 
with lower self-esteem QoL, which is consistent with the 
literature that higher inattentive traits are associated with 
lower self-esteem in children with ADHD (Graetz et al., 
2001). Less is known about how ADHD traits are associated 
with QoL among autistic youth, as most existing research 
has examined this relationship in ADHD populations, mak-
ing the current findings a novel contribution.
Higher ADHD-HI trait levels were also associated with 
significantly higher self-esteem and school QoL at high 
anxiety levels. However, this relationship was not signifi-
cant at mean and low anxiety levels. Additionally, higher 
ADHD-HI traits were associated with lower friendship QoL 
at low levels of anxiety. This relationship was not signifi-
cant for mean and high levels of anxiety. Although literature 
has found that anxiety can increase the disruptiveness of 
ADHD on daily functioning by impacting executive func -
tioning (e.g., cognitive functioning, attention and work -
ing memory), there is still some uncertainty as to whether 
anxiety may also serve as a protective factor against dis -
ruptive hyperactive-impulsive-related behaviours (Maric et 
al., 2018; Menghini et al., 2018; Schatz & Rostain, 2006). 
Further research is needed to investigate these relationships.
One possibility is that anxiety may be associated with 
response inhibition in those with ADHD traits (Maric et 
al., 2018). While this finding has not been established in 
autistic populations, it may offer a tentative explanation for 
the present results. It is possible that although children still 
have high hyperactive-impulsive tendencies, co-occurring 
anxiety may reduce impulsive behavior due to heightened 
self-monitoring and fear of negative consequences (Maric et 
al., 2018). Consequently, these children might receive more 
positive feedback from their environment than those with 
similarly high ADHD-HI traits but lower anxiety, poten -
tially resulting in a higher QoL. It is important to note that 
this explanation is exploratory and speculative and should 
be interpreted with caution given the modest interaction 
effects, low explained variance, and limited literature linking 
anxiety, response inhibition, and QoL in autistic children.
Another possible explanation for the present findings 
is that the positive illusory bias may impact self-reported 
ratings of QoL. The positive illusory bias is commonly 
observed in children with ADHD and refers to a child’s 
tendency to overestimate their abilities compared to per -
ceptions from others, such as parents or teachers (Crisci 
et al., 2022; Owens et al., 2007). Children with ADHD are 
more likely to overestimate their abilities in daily activities 
compared to neurotypical children, and these effects are the 
greatest in the domains in which they experience the great -
est challenges (Hoza et al., 2004). This has been attributed 
to a self-protective mechanism in which they deny problems 
that cause distress to protect their self-image, leading to an 
overly positive report of their daily functioning (Ohan & 
Johnston, 2002). Externalizing symptom severity has also 
been associated with a higher discrepancy between child 
and parent reports of activity limitations (Volz-Sidiropoulou 
et al., 2013). As the overestimation of ability is linked to 
externalizing challenges and greater domain-specific diffi-
culties, we may have a biased assessment of the sample’s 
QoL. However, further research would be needed to deter -
mine if this is a viable explanation.
Depression as a Control Variable
Depression emerged as a significant covariate in the mod-
els where physical and family QoL were the outcome vari -
ables, aligning with findings in the general population that 
depressive symptoms are linked to somatic complaints and 
disruptions in family functioning (e.g., Campo, 2012; Res-
tifo & Bögels, 2009). However, depressive symptoms were 
not significantly associated with emotional, self-esteem, 
friendship, or school QoL. In contrast, anxiety and its inter-
actions with autistic and ADHD traits were more relevant 
to self-esteem, friendship, and school QoL. Together, these 
findings suggest that the types of internalizing symptoms 
(i.e., depressive vs. anxious) may be differentially associ-
ated with the overall QoL of autistic children. Future work 
should continue to examine how internalizing symptoms 
and neurodevelopmental traits jointly shape autistic chil -
dren’s QoL. It is important to note that not all potential con-
trol variables were added here. Children’s cognitive ability, 
environment, sex and access to supports may all have an 
impact on QoL and should be investigated in further studies.

Limitations
The study provides valuable insights into QoL for autistic 
children, but some limitations should be noted. A key limi -
tation is the low explained variance in the models. Across 
models, the proportion of variance explained in QoL out -
comes was modest (approximately 3% to 11%). Although 
relatively small, such effects are not uncommon when 
examining complex, multidetermined constructs like QoL 
(Carey et al., 2023; Schäfer, 2025). The unexplained vari -
ance likely reflects the influence of a broader fit between 
neurodivergent traits and a child’s environment, including 
school environment, support access, family dynamics, and 
financial resources (Al Qutub et al., 2024; Baker & Donelly, 
2001; Mitchell et al., 2022; Poulsen et al., 2025). This lim-
ited insight is partially due to the individualized and quanti-
tative nature of the current study, which uses the KINDL to 
capture personal states of well-being but does not gain rich 
qualitative insights on the child’s environmental context. 
These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution, 
as small effects may reflect limited but potentially meaning-
ful variation in functioning in each QoL domain (Carey et 
al., 2023; Schäfer, 2025).
The next limitations are the measurement constraints of 
the KINDL and SCQ. Although the KINDL is a well-vali -
dated generic measure of health-related QoL, characteristics 
associated with autism (e.g., differences in social commu-
nication and emotional insight) may influence self-report. 
We acknowledge that the KINDL may not fully capture 
autism-specific domains and has not been validated for use 
in autistic populations. As such, findings should be inter-
preted within this context (Erhart et al., 2009; Ikeda et al., 
2014). The use of the SCQ to measure autistic traits pres -
ents another limitation. The SCQ is primarily a screening 
tool rather than a dimensional measure of autistic traits, and 
items on the SCQ may be influenced by language ability 
and IQ (Kniola et al., 2024). As a result, our models may 
underestimate the full range of autistic traits, and associa -
tions with QoL should be interpreted with these measure -
ment constraints in mind.
Another limitation is the potential discrepancies that 
may arise between child and parent reports. Anxiety, autism 
and ADHD trait data were based on parent reports, which 
may be biased, as parents tend to overestimate their child’s 
anxiety and report more traits and lower empathy than their 
children self-report (Kalvin et al., 2020). Additionally, the 
QoL measure was assessed using only the child’s perspec -
tive, which increases the possibility of the positive illusory 
bias impacting the results, as discussed previously. Thus, 
the interaction effects between anxiety and ADHD-HI traits 
may reflect differential method variance of parent versus 
child reports, rather than the underlying psychological 
mechanisms. However, this can also be seen as a strength of 
the present study in that the predictor variables and the out-
come variables were obtained from different reporters and 
thus are not subject to shared method variance issues.
A fourth limitation is the sample characteristics, which 
predominantly represent male autistic youth with subclini -
cal ADHD. Males often exhibit more externalizing behav -
iours (e.g., hyperactivity and aggression), whereas females 
exhibit more internalizing symptoms (e.g., anxiety and 
depression), and masking in social situations (Napolitano 
et al., 2022). When controlling for sex differences in QoL, 
one study on autistic adults reported that males had higher 
physical QoL while females had higher social QoL, mak -
ing it crucial to remain cautious of sex-based differences 
(Mason et al., 2018). The sample was also skewed towards 
lower ADHD trait levels, with the mean ADHD-I trait score 
being 10.40 and 8.69 for ADHD-HI. Given that these scores 
are well below the clinical concern level of 27, the restricted 
range may have limited our ability to detect stronger asso -
ciations at higher levels of trait expression.
In addition to these sample-related considerations, it is 
also important to acknowledge analytic limitations associ -
ated with the study’s design. Given that the study exam -
ined six QoL domains and included multiple predictors and 
interaction terms, the likelihood of Type I error is increased. 
Although multiple comparison corrections were not applied 
due to the exploratory nature of the analyses, this approach 
may still increase the risk of identifying spurious associa -
tions. As such, the significant findings reported here should 
be interpreted with caution, and future research using larger 
samples and confirmatory analytic approaches would ben-
efit from applying appropriate statistical corrections to 
reduce the risk of false positives.
Implications and Future Research
Despite its limitations, the present findings offer prelimi-
nary insights that may inform future work on QoL in autistic 
children. Notably, the positive association between autistic 
trait levels and school-related QoL raises questions about 
the role of early identification and school-based supports 
in shaping educational adjustment. Future research should 
explore how autistic trait levels influence access to services 
to better understand whether children with lower trait lev -
els are currently underserved, and possible contributions 
to lower QoL. Additionally, adopting a trait-based rather 
than diagnosis-based approach revealed potentially mean -
ingful patterns, particularly among autistic children whose 
neurodiverse characteristics may not align with traditional 
diagnostic categories. For instance, the findings that higher 
ADHD-HI traits were associated with lower family QoL, 
and higher ADHD-I traits with lower self-esteem QoL,

Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders
mirror patterns reported in the ADHD literature. However, 
in autistic youth, these associations may be influenced by 
additional factors, such as social communication differences 
and sensory challenges, highlighting a need for further 
investigation. The significant interaction effects indicated 
that higher ADHD-HI trait levels were associated with bet -
ter self-esteem and school-related QoL among autistic chil-
dren with high anxiety. In contrast, higher ADHD-HI trait 
levels were associated with poorer friendship QoL only 
among children with low levels of anxiety. Although specu-
lative, these findings may suggest that anxiety moderates 
the impact of hyperactive-impulsive behaviors on QoL. It is 
also possible that positive illusory bias contributed to these 
patterns, warranting further investigation. Future research 
should consider examining how anxiety impacts response 
inhibition and self-monitoring in children with high ADHD-
HI traits to better understand whether this relationship can 
be replicated. Future research should also compare child 
reports with parent reports to understand whether the posi -
tive illusory bias contributes to higher self-reported QoL in 
self-esteem and school domains.
Along with further examining the significant relation-
ships in the present study, future work could also aim to 
examine how environmental factors may impact each QoL 
domain utilizing methods beyond the KINDL alone. These 
methods should be suitable for capturing the dimensionality 
of autistic traits, as well as the QoL of autistic children with 
diverse IQ and language abilities. While the KINDL can 
identify relationships between trait levels and QoL, under -
standing the role of external influences can help gain deeper 
insight into why these effects may occur. Future work may 
also want to account for other limitations including (1) 
recruiting a more balanced sample of males and females 
to enhance generalizability to both sexes, (2) using multi-
informant designs that allow for comparisons on child and 
parent reports, and (3) recruiting a sample with a greater 
range of ADHD traits to increase the study’s power to detect 
an effect.
Together, these findings highlight the importance of 
researching how neurodivergent traits beyond those associ-
ated with an autism diagnosis impact QoL in autistic youth. 
To our knowledge, no studies have compared ADHD-I and 
ADHD-HI traits across physical, emotional, self-esteem, 
family, friendship, and school QoL domains in autistic 
children, along with their interaction with anxiety. This 
study provides a novel contribution to the QoL literature by 
acknowledging that many autistic children may experience 
co-occurring ADHD traits and anxiety, which may impact 
their well-being. The findings of the present study should be 
interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather 
than confirmatory. Given the low variance explained and 
potential differential method variance, these results should 
be interpreted with caution. A key contribution of the pres -
ent study is to encourage future work to further investigate 
these findings and potentially replicate these relationships 
with corrections made for multiple comparisons. Together, 
these findings lay a foundation for continued research into 
how neurodevelopmental traits and anxiety jointly shape 
QoL in autistic youth.
