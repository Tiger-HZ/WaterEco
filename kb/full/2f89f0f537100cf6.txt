Abstract
The present systematic literature review investigates the ethical integration of 
Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) in Islamic Religious Education (IRE). In this 
review, ethical integration extends beyond general AI ethics principles such as 
privacy, transparency, academic integrity, and accountability by requiring GenAI use 
to remain consistent with Islamic educational values, source authenticity, teacher 
mediation, and religious and epistemic authority. Using PRISMA 2020 guidelines, 
this systematic literature review searched peer-reviewed English-language journal 
articles published between 2022 and 2026 in Scopus and Web of Science. Of 124 
records initially identified, 13 studies were included in the synthesis. Thematic analysis 
using deductive and inductive coding revealed four main themes. First, GenAI in 
IRE is primarily discussed as a pedagogical assistant for lesson planning, teaching-
material development, assessment support, personalised learning, administrative 
activities, and Qur’anic learning rather than as a replacement for teachers or religious 
authorities. Second, the literature highlights ethical, epistemic, and value-alignment 
concerns, including plagiarism, hallucinations, privacy issues, bias, unreliable sources, 
overdependence, doctrinal inaccuracies, and confusion regarding religious authority. 
Third, responsible integration requires multilayered safeguards, including teacher 
supervision, verification of AI-generated information, institutional policies, AI literacy, 
academic-integrity guidance, culturally sensitive use, and alignment with Islamic 
educational values. Fourth, the evidence base remains limited, fragmented, and 
predominantly conceptual, exploratory, or perception-based, with few classroom-
based, longitudinal, comparative, or intervention studies. Accordingly, the findings 
should be interpreted as an emerging synthesis rather than conclusive evidence of 
the effects of GenAI in IRE. The review further identifies adab, amanah, and epistemic 
authority as complementary conceptual lenses because they respectively address 
appropriate conduct towards knowledge, responsible and trustworthy use of AI and 
knowledge, and the legitimate validation and interpretation of religious knowledge.

 1 Introduction
The rapid development of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) tools has altered 
contemporary discourse around education, particularly in content creation, personalised 
learning, feedback, assessment, instructional design, and administrative assistance. 
In educational settings, technologies such as ChatGPT cannot be considered solely in 
terms of their technological functions; they must also be understood in relation to the 
production, interpretation, access to, and assessment of knowledge. Previous research 
on artificial intelligence in education has demonstrated that AI-assisted technologies can 
support adaptive learning, assessment, intelligent tutoring, and academic services while 
also raising issues related to pedagogy, ethics, regulation, and the role of educators [ 45]. 
Recent literature on GenAI and large language models highlights their potential to assist 
teachers and students while emphasising critical thinking, fact-checking, human moni -
toring, and institution-specific strategies for responsible implementation [ 24, 27]. At the 
same time, the development of GenAI technologies has progressed more rapidly than 
the development of ethical guidelines and regulatory frameworks capable of ensuring 
responsible implementation across educational contexts. Consequently, contemporary 
scholarship identifies both the educational potential of GenAI and concerns related to 
misinformation, academic integrity, misuse, and governance [20, 30, 34]. These concerns 
become particularly significant when GenAI is introduced into educational domains in 
which knowledge is closely connected to religious belief, moral formation, and recog -
nised sources of authority.
In this paper, Islamic Religious Education (IRE) refers to structured educational pro -
cesses concerned with Islamic knowledge, Qur’anic learning, religious beliefs, worship, 
ethics, moral formation, and the education of Muslim learners. It includes formal and 
semi-formal processes in which Islamic teachings are mediated through teachers, insti -
tutions, curricula, and recognised religious sources. Islamic education has been concep -
tualised as a holistic educational process encompassing intellectual, moral, spiritual, and 
social dimensions of human development [ 19, 39]. IRE therefore differs not only from 
secular educational domains but also from religious education traditions grounded in 
other theological and epistemological traditions. While Christian, Jewish, and other 
forms of religious education may similarly involve moral formation, sacred texts, reli -
gious identity, and forms of religious authority, IRE is situated within an Islamic con -
ception of knowledge and education in which the relationship between knowledge, 
ethical conduct, religious sources, teacher mediation, and moral formation is shaped by 
the Islamic worldview. In this sense, the distinctive concern of IRE is not simply that 
AI-generated information should be accurate, but that religious knowledge should be 
approached, transmitted, interpreted, and evaluated in ways that remain consistent with 
Islamic educational purposes and recognised sources of knowledge. This distinction is 
important because the present review draws selectively on broader religious education 
literature while treating such evidence as contextual rather than automatically transfer -
able to IRE.

Recent studies indicate that AI and ChatGPT have been explored in Islamic education 
for lesson preparation, student-centred teaching, innovative pedagogy, self-inquiry, con-
tent creation, and educator assistance [ 4, 28, 40]. In IRE, reported or proposed applica -
tions include the preparation of teaching materials, development of learning activities 
and assessments, personalised learning, and support for Qur’anic learning. However, 
the evidence base remains emerging, and the contextual factors associated with GenAI 
implementation need to be distinguished according to their evidential source. Studies 
directly concerned with IRE or Islamic education indicate the importance of teacher 
preparedness, institutional readiness, cultural context, technological infrastructure, and 
ethical considerations [ 4, 28, 40]. Broader educational AI literature similarly identifies 
teacher competence, institutional support, infrastructure, governance, and AI literacy as 
important conditions for implementation [ 30]. Accordingly, these factors should not be 
treated as uniformly established IRE-specific findings; rather, they represent a combina -
tion of direct evidence from Islamic educational settings and contextual insights from 
the broader educational AI literature.
The importance of such distinctions becomes clearer because the consequences 
of inappropriate GenAI use in IRE may extend beyond conventional academic errors. 
Unchecked or insufficiently verified AI-generated content may contribute to misconcep-
tions about religious concepts, inadequate evaluation of sources, and confusion between 
machine-generated information and knowledge conveyed by qualified teachers, schol -
ars, or recognised religious sources. In disciplines where knowledge is also connected 
to moral and spiritual formation, the question is therefore not simply whether GenAI is 
useful or acceptable, but how its use can remain educationally responsible and consis -
tent with the purposes and epistemic conditions of the discipline.
Accordingly, this review conceptualises ethical integration as the responsible imple -
mentation of GenAI in a pedagogically meaningful, transparent, and educator-mon -
itored manner that addresses general AI ethics while also preserving the distinctive 
epistemic, moral, and religious requirements of IRE. General AI ethics remains relevant 
to IRE and includes concerns such as hallucination, bias, plagiarism, privacy, transpar -
ency, authorship, academic integrity, accountability, and excessive automation [4, 18, 24, 
25, 36]. However, these concerns acquire additional significance in IRE because inaccu -
rate, decontextualised, or unsourced information may affect learners’ understanding of 
religious concepts, their evaluation of religious sources, and their perceptions of legiti -
mate authority. Ethical integration in IRE therefore involves a layered approach in which 
general AI governance is supplemented by pedagogical safeguards and Islamic educa -
tional considerations, including religious accuracy, source authenticity, teacher media -
tion, and responsible engagement with religious knowledge.
Three conceptual lenses are particularly useful for articulating this Islamic dimension: 
adab, amanah, and epistemic authority. Their selection is not intended to suggest that 
they exhaust the range of Islamic ethical concepts relevant to GenAI. Rather, they were 
selected because they address three complementary dimensions of responsible engage -
ment with knowledge. First, adab concerns the proper disposition and conduct of the 
learner towards knowledge, teachers, sources, and the learning process. This is consis -
tent with Islamic educational scholarship that places adab and ta’dib at the centre of the 
educational process, linking intellectual development with appropriate conduct and the 
proper recognition of knowledge and its purposes [ 5]. Second, amanah concerns trust,

responsibility, and accountability. Within Islamic educational contexts, knowledge trans-
mission itself can be understood as a responsibility that requires trustworthy handling 
and responsible practice. Contemporary Islamic education scholarship likewise con -
nects amanah with the responsible acquisition and transmission of knowledge and with 
ethical conduct [1]. Third, epistemic authority concerns the legitimate basis for validat -
ing, interpreting, and relying upon knowledge claims. In IRE, this includes the roles of 
teachers, scholars, religious texts, and recognised educational institutions in guiding the 
interpretation and authentication of Islamic knowledge.
The distinction between religious authority and epistemic authority is important in 
this framework. Religious authority refers broadly to the recognised authority of reli -
gious persons, institutions, traditions, or sources in guiding religious belief, practice, 
interpretation, and communal understanding. Epistemic authority, by contrast, refers 
specifically to authority concerning knowledge: who or what is legitimately relied upon 
to determine, validate, interpret, or transmit knowledge claims. The two may overlap in 
IRE because teachers, scholars, texts, and institutions may possess both religious and 
epistemic functions; however, they are analytically distinct. This distinction is particu -
larly relevant to GenAI because a system may be perceived by learners as an epistemi -
cally authoritative source simply because it produces fluent and apparently credible 
answers, without possessing the scholarly, textual, institutional, or religious legitimacy 
required for authoritative interpretation. Scholarship on religious authority similarly 
distinguishes authority associated with religious expertise and knowledge from author -
ity understood primarily as power or command [ 10]. Thus, the concern in IRE is not 
only whether GenAI generates inaccurate information, but also whether its use changes 
learners’ perceptions of who or what can legitimately be trusted as a source of religious 
knowledge.
Taken together, adab, amanah, and epistemic authority provide complementary ana -
lytical lenses for ethical integration. Adab addresses how learners engage with knowl -
edge; amanah addresses how users act responsibly in producing, attributing, verifying, 
and sharing knowledge; and epistemic authority addresses how religious knowledge is 
validated and whose expertise is legitimately relied upon. These dimensions are partic -
ularly relevant to GenAI because the technology can simultaneously influence learner 
behaviour, responsibility for generated content, and perceptions of knowledge author -
ity. They therefore provide a more targeted framework for IRE than applying general AI 
governance principles alone. The framework is consequently intended as a conceptual 
starting point for evaluating GenAI integration rather than as a claim that these three 
concepts constitute an exhaustive Islamic ethical framework.
The available literature on AI and education supports human-centred approaches to 
GenAI implementation involving privacy protection, sound pedagogical design, respon -
sible policymaking, and institutional governance [ 30]. Similarly, literature concerning 
religious and Islamic education highlights teacher training, institutional support, cultur -
ally sensitive policy, verification of AI-generated information, and educator involvement 
in decision-making [ 4, 26, 28]. These considerations are particularly important in IRE 
because teachers are not merely facilitators of information but may also serve as moral 
educators and mediators of religious knowledge. Consequently, GenAI implementation 
in IRE requires a balance between technological usefulness, pedagogical value, religious 
authenticity, and moral responsibility.

Despite growing academic interest in AI and religion, the literature on GenAI in IRE 
remains limited, fragmented, and under-theorised. In this review, the term under-theo-
rised refers specifically to the limited development of integrated conceptual frameworks 
that connect the pedagogical use of GenAI with Islamic ethical values, epistemological 
questions concerning knowledge and authority, and the theological and moral purposes 
of IRE. Existing studies frequently examine opportunities, risks, acceptability, pedagogy, 
AI ethics, Qur’anic learning, or responsible technology use separately, while relatively 
few studies systematically connect these dimensions within a coherent framework for 
IRE. The literature also contains limited classroom-based empirical evidence and few 
theoretical or evaluative frameworks capable of operationalising Islamic concepts such 
as adab, amanah, source authenticity, teacher mediation, and epistemic authority [ 6, 
8, 36]. A systematic review is therefore warranted to synthesise the available evidence 
while distinguishing between direct IRE evidence and insights derived from adjacent 
fields. Accordingly, this systematic literature review aims to synthesise the emerging lit -
erature on the ethical integration of GenAI in IRE. The review is guided by the following 
research questions:
1. How is generative AI currently used in Islamic Religious Education?
2. What ethical concerns are reported regarding the use of generative AI in Islamic 
Religious Education?
3. What measures are recommended to support the ethical integration of generative AI 
in Islamic Religious Education?
4. What research gaps can be identified in the literature on generative AI in Islamic 
Religious Education?
Although this review is centred on IRE, the directly relevant literature remains limited. 
Therefore, adjacent studies from broader religious education and general educational 
AI ethics are included only when they contribute substantively to one or more of the 
review questions and address an issue that is conceptually relevant to IRE, such as reli -
gious knowledge and authority, teacher mediation, academic integrity, AI reliability, gov-
ernance, or responsible use. Evidence from broader religious education is used primarily 
to illuminate issues that arise from the religious and theological character of the educa -
tional context, whereas general educational AI ethics literature is used to contextualise 
common technological and governance concerns. Such evidence is explicitly classified 
as indirect or contextual evidence and is not treated as direct evidence of GenAI imple -
mentation or educational effects in IRE. This distinction is intended to prevent inappro -
priate transfer of findings while allowing the limited IRE evidence base to be interpreted 
within the broader scholarship on religious education and educational AI ethics.
2 Literature review
2.1 Generative AI in education: opportunities and ethical concerns
The literature on generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) in the educational context 
has grown exponentially in recent years. Generally, the broader body of literature sug -
gests that GenAI is a promising tool in education because of its ability to perform tasks 
such as content creation, feedback generation, assessment support, automated tutoring, 
adaptive learning, and personalised instruction. Prior research on artificial intelligence 
in higher education has indicated the rising popularity of AI applications utilised for 
Page 6 of 29
Mat Yusoff et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence (2026) 6:1162 
profiling, prediction, assessment, tutoring, and adaptive systems, although the pedagogi-
cal role of teachers in this line of research was rather weak [ 44, 45]. Recent studies on 
GenAI and large language models also demonstrate the potential of these technologies 
to help students and teachers explain ideas, write texts, provide formative feedback, gen-
erate ideas, and design instruction [24, 43].
At the same time, the literature shows that GenAI faces ethical and practical problems 
that cannot be ignored regarding its educational potential. Misinformation, hallucina -
tions, plagiarism, bias, opacity, privacy risks, lack of accountability, authorship issues, 
and the undermining of academic integrity are among the main problems associated 
with GenAI in the educational context [ 6, 30, 43]. Moreover, some researchers argue 
that GenAI can contribute to superficial learning if students use machine-generated 
answers without thinking, evaluating sources and critically engaging with the informa -
tion [24, 37]. Thus, the literature on GenAI in education is beginning to focus not on 
whether GenAI is useful but on how it should be integrated. Despite the important con -
tribution of this ethical literature, however, it is too broad to describe specific problems 
of integrating GenAI in educational settings where knowledge has religious, moral and 
theological aspects.
2.2 Generative AI in Islamic religious education
From the emerging body of literature on the use of GenAI in Islamic Religious Education 
(IRE), both opportunities and potential risks have been identified. Existing studies gen -
erally position AI tools such as ChatGPT as pedagogical resources that can assist teach -
ers rather than replace teachers, scholars, or recognised religious sources. Reported or 
proposed applications of GenAI in IRE include lesson planning, development of teach -
ing materials, assessment preparation, student-centred learning, independent inquiry, 
classroom engagement, personalised learning, and teacher support [ 4, 8, 28, 40]. For 
example, Suhendi et al., [ 40] examine the potential uses of ChatGPT in designing IRE 
lessons, while Ahmad Taha et al., [ 4] examine the opportunities and challenges of Chat -
GPT in Islamic education through the lens of maqasid al-shariah.
Maqasid al-shariah refers broadly to the higher objectives of Islamic law and provides 
a normative basis for evaluating whether an action, practice, or technological application 
contributes to or threatens recognised human and religious interests. Classical formula -
tions commonly identify the protection of religion ( hifz al-din), life (hifz al-nafs), intel-
lect (hifz al-‘aql), lineage ( hifz al-nasl), and property ( hifz al-mal) as core objectives. In 
the context of GenAI, this perspective shifts the ethical question from whether a tech -
nology is merely permissible or useful to whether its purposes and consequences are 
compatible with these broader objectives. For example, the protection of religion may 
be relevant when GenAI is used to produce or interpret religious content; the protec -
tion of intellect may be relevant to concerns about cognitive dependency, superficial 
learning, and the uncritical acceptance of AI-generated answers; and the protection of 
property may be relevant to questions of authorship, intellectual property, and misuse of 
educational resources. Recent scholarship applying maqasid al-shariah to AI similarly 
identifies its potential to evaluate AI according to the preservation of faith, life, intel -
lect, lineage, and wealth, while emphasising the need to balance technological benefits 
against possible harms.

However, maqasid al-shariah should not be treated in this review as a fully operational 
framework for evaluating every aspect of GenAI use in IRE. Rather, it provides an over -
arching normative orientation for asking whether GenAI use promotes legitimate edu -
cational and human benefits and avoids significant harms. The present review therefore 
distinguishes this broad normative level from more specific analytical concerns related 
to how knowledge is approached, how users act responsibly, and how religious knowl -
edge is validated. This distinction is important because the literature on GenAI in IRE 
has not yet consistently operationalised maqasid al-shariah into classroom-level criteria 
for evaluating AI use.
Moreover, Majeed [ 28] found that Islamic teachers in Southern Pakistan perceive 
GenAI, including ChatGPT, as a potential pedagogical resource and an aid to teacher-
student interaction. Similarly, Faizin et al., [ 16] provide evidence concerning Muslim 
learners’ acceptance of AI in IRE. Such findings indicate that GenAI has potential peda -
gogical value and that users may be willing to adopt it. Nevertheless, technology accep -
tance and perceived usefulness should not be equated with educational effectiveness 
or ethical appropriateness. Acceptance studies primarily indicate whether learners or 
teachers are willing to use a technology; they do not establish whether GenAI improves 
learning outcomes, strengthens religious understanding, supports moral development, 
or produces responsible learner behaviour.
The current literature therefore requires empirical investigation beyond technology 
acceptance. In particular, future studies should examine whether GenAI affects learn -
ers’ acquisition and retention of religious knowledge, their ability to distinguish authen -
tic from unreliable religious sources, their critical evaluation of AI-generated religious 
information, their moral and ethical reasoning, and their capacity to engage indepen -
dently with teachers and recognised religious sources. Classroom-based research should 
also investigate behavioural outcomes, including patterns of AI dependence, disclosure 
of AI use, academic integrity, source verification, and responsible use of AI-generated 
content. These dimensions are necessary because the educational value of GenAI in IRE 
cannot be adequately assessed through perceptions of usefulness or acceptance alone.
However, despite the potential uses of GenAI in IRE, the existing evidence remains 
limited. Much of the literature is conceptual, exploratory, or perception-based rather 
than based on observations of actual classroom implementation. Consequently, there 
is still insufficient empirical evidence concerning the effects of GenAI on learning pro -
cesses, student behaviour, religious understanding, moral formation, and teacher-stu -
dent relationships. This limitation is particularly important when interpreting findings 
from acceptance studies, as positive attitudes towards GenAI do not necessarily demon -
strate educational effectiveness or ethical suitability.
2.3 Ethical integration, religious authority, and Islamic educational values
Ethical concerns are prominent in discussions of GenAI in IRE. In the broader educa -
tional literature, common concerns include plagiarism, cheating, unreliable outputs, 
lack of transparency, privacy violations, bias, authorship problems, and overreliance 
on AI systems [ 6, 25, 43]. These concerns are also relevant to IRE, but their implica -
tions may extend beyond conventional academic and technological risks because IRE 
involves religious knowledge, moral development, respect for knowledge, and the trans -
mission and interpretation of religious teachings. Islamic education has consequently

been conceptualised as a holistic process encompassing intellectual, moral, spiritual, and 
social development [19, 39]. Ethical integration of GenAI in IRE therefore requires con -
sideration not only of general AI governance but also of the epistemic and moral pur -
poses of Islamic education.
At the broadest normative level, maqasid al-shariah provides a useful Islamic perspec-
tive for considering whether the purposes and consequences of GenAI use are consistent 
with the protection of fundamental religious and human interests. In IRE, hifz al-din 
is particularly relevant to the preservation of religious understanding and protection 
against doctrinal distortion, while hifz al-‘aql is relevant to the development and pro -
tection of learners’ intellectual capacities, including critical thinking and independent 
engagement with knowledge. Other objectives may become relevant depending on the 
application and context, including the protection of privacy, dignity, life, and property. 
Thus, maqasid al-shariah provides the review with a broad normative question: Does 
the use of GenAI in IRE contribute to legitimate educational and human benefit while 
avoiding harms that undermine the purposes of Islamic education?
Nevertheless, the maqasid perspective alone does not provide sufficient analytical 
specificity for the particular problems identified in the GenAI–IRE literature. It can 
establish broad objectives, but it does not by itself explain how learners should conduct 
themselves towards knowledge, how responsibility for AI-generated content should 
be exercised, or how the authority and authenticity of religious knowledge should be 
established. For this reason, the present review identifies adab, amanah, and epistemic 
authority as complementary analytical lenses rather than as competing alternatives to 
maqasid al-shariah . The relationship between these concepts is therefore hierarchical 
and complementary: maqasid al-shariah provides an overarching normative orientation, 
while adab, amanah, and epistemic authority provide more specific lenses for examining 
the conduct, responsibility, and knowledge-validation dimensions of GenAI use in IRE.
The selection of these three concepts is also not intended to suggest that they exhaust 
the Islamic ethical tradition or that concepts such as ihsan, taqwa, sidq, and ikhlas are 
irrelevant. Rather, they were selected because they directly correspond to three recur -
ring problems identified in the literature on GenAI in IRE: the learner’s relationship with 
knowledge and learning, responsibility in the production and use of information, and 
the validation and authority of religious knowledge. Adab is therefore used to examine 
appropriate conduct towards knowledge, teachers, sources, and the learning process. 
Amanah is used to examine responsibility and trustworthiness in handling AI-generated 
information, authorship, student data, educational decisions, and religious content. Epis-
temic authority is used to examine how religious knowledge is validated, interpreted, 
and attributed to legitimate sources and knowledgeable persons. Together, these con -
cepts provide a more focused analytical structure for examining the ethical integration 
of GenAI in IRE.
Within this framework, adab concerns the manner in which learners and educators 
engage with knowledge. It includes appropriate conduct towards teachers, sources, 
fellow learners, and the learning process. Amanah concerns responsibility and trust -
worthiness in the acquisition, production, attribution, verification, and dissemination 
of knowledge. In the context of GenAI, this includes disclosing AI assistance, verify -
ing generated information, avoiding plagiarism, protecting student data, and accepting 
responsibility for the final use of AI-generated content. Epistemic authority, meanwhile,

concerns the legitimate basis on which knowledge claims are validated and interpreted. 
In IRE, this includes the roles of religious texts, teachers, scholars, and recognised edu -
cational institutions in authenticating and contextualising Islamic knowledge.
These concepts should therefore be understood primarily as analytical categories 
with normative foundations, rather than as a complete set of practical implementation 
rules. Their normative dimension derives from Islamic conceptions of proper conduct, 
responsibility, and legitimate knowledge, while their analytical function allows the lit -
erature to be examined systematically according to recurring ethical issues. The practical 
implications of these categories can subsequently be expressed through implementation 
criteria such as source verification, teacher mediation, responsible disclosure, academic-
integrity procedures, protection of learner data, and restrictions on treating GenAI as an 
authoritative substitute for qualified religious guidance.
Under this approach, the ethical problem is not simply whether GenAI can produce 
inaccurate information, but also how its use may influence learners’ relationships with 
knowledge, teachers, sources, and religious authority. Several studies raise concerns 
about AI accuracy, misuse of generated content, plagiarism, overreliance, privacy, and 
misalignment with Islamic values [ 4, 18, 25, 36]. In broader religious education, Papak -
ostas [36], emphasises that AI should be examined not only from a pedagogical perspec -
tive but also in relation to theological interpretation, authority, and spiritual formation. 
Although such evidence does not constitute direct evidence of GenAI effects in IRE, it 
provides contextual insight into issues that may arise when AI is introduced into educa -
tional settings involving religious knowledge and formation. Similarly, studies of Islamic 
education emphasise the importance of teacher and institutional involvement in guid -
ing AI use, validating generated material, and ensuring that technological innovations 
remain compatible with religious and cultural values [4, 26, 28].
Another important ethical issue is integrity. Research on GenAI in general education 
identifies plagiarism, assisted cheating, unclear authorship, and misuse of assessment as 
important ethical challenges [ 23, 25, 43]. These concerns are particularly relevant to the 
concept of amanah in IRE because academic integrity can also be understood as part 
of responsible moral conduct. If students use GenAI to generate religious explanations, 
assignments, or reflections without disclosure, verification, or meaningful personal 
engagement, the problem is simultaneously pedagogical and ethical. Ethical integra -
tion therefore requires policies concerning plagiarism, disclosure of AI use, assessment 
design, teacher guidance, and student education in responsible AI use.
Finally, readiness, policy, and institutional support remain important conditions for 
responsible GenAI implementation. Broader GenAI literature highlights teacher train -
ing, AI literacy, transparency, stakeholder involvement, and institutional governance [ 6, 
30, 37]. Studies of Islamic education similarly indicate the importance of cultural readi -
ness, infrastructure, teacher preparation, and institutional support [ 16, 28]. These find -
ings reinforce the need for a multilayered approach in which individual users, teachers, 
institutions, and Islamic educational values jointly shape the responsible use of GenAI.
2.4 Gaps in the existing literature
Although the literature on GenAI in education is growing, several gaps remain regard -
ing IRE. First, the evidence base is still empirically limited. Many studies discuss possible 
benefits, risks, and ethical considerations, but fewer studies examine actual classroom

implementation, student learning processes, teacher decision-making, or long-term 
educational effects in IRE settings. Second, the geographical and institutional scope of 
existing research remains narrow, with much of the directly relevant literature concen -
trated in specific national or educational contexts. Third, although Islamic values are fre-
quently mentioned, concepts such as adab, amanah, religious authority, and epistemic 
authority are rarely operationalised as analytical categories for evaluating the use of 
GenAI in IRE. Fourth, existing reviews on GenAI ethics in education provide important 
background, but they do not sufficiently address the distinctive pedagogical, theological, 
epistemological, and cultural concerns of IRE [6, 8, 36].
These gaps highlight the need for a systematic literature review that specifically 
focuses on the ethical integration of GenAI in IRE. Such a review is necessary to distin -
guish between direct evidence from Islamic educational contexts and indirect insights 
drawn from broader religious education or general AI ethics literature. It is also needed 
to clarify how GenAI is currently discussed or used in IRE, what ethical concerns are 
reported, what safeguards are recommended, and what future research is required. In 
this study, adab, amanah, and epistemic authority are treated not merely as abstract 
Islamic concepts, but as conceptual lenses for interpreting ethical integration in relation 
to knowledge verification, teacher mediation, source authenticity, authorship, academic 
integrity, and responsible learner conduct.
3 Methodology
3.1 Review design
A systematic literature review (SLR) was conducted to synthesise the emerging literature 
on the ethical integration of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) in Islamic Religious 
Education (IRE). An SLR was considered appropriate because research on GenAI in 
IRE remains relatively recent, fragmented, and methodologically diverse. Unlike a tra -
ditional narrative review, an SLR provides a structured process for identifying, screen -
ing, appraising, and synthesising relevant literature according to predefined procedures 
[29, 32, 35, 41]. This approach was particularly appropriate because the topic intersects 
several fields, including Islamic education, religious education, educational technology, 
generative AI, and AI ethics.
The review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-
Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [35]. PRISMA was used to document the identifica -
tion, screening, eligibility, and inclusion stages of the review. Searches were conducted 
in two multidisciplinary academic databases, Scopus and Web of Science. A total of 124 
records were initially identified, comprising 78 records from Scopus and 46 from Web of 
Science. After the removal of 21 duplicate records and three records that did not meet 
the eligible publication-type requirements, 100 records remained for title and abstract 
screening.
To improve consistency in the screening process, study relevance was determined

3.4 Inclusion and exclusion criteria
The inclusion and exclusion criteria were established prior to the full-text eligibil -

PRISMA 2020 reporting framework [35].

3.8 Quality appraisal of included studies
The methodological and reporting quality of the included studies as shown in Table 
1 was assessed using an appraisal framework adapted from Abouzahra et al., [ 2]. This 
framework was selected because the review included heterogeneous evidence, com -
prising quantitative surveys, qualitative studies, mixed-methods research, conceptual 
papers, literature-based studies, technical evaluations, and application reviews. Design-
specific tools such as CASP and JBI are available for particular methodological designs, 
while MMAT is designed primarily for the appraisal of empirical qualitative, quantita -
tive, and mixed-methods studies. Because the present synthesis also included conceptual 
and literature-based evidence, the adapted Abouzahra et al., [ 2] framework provided a 
common set of criteria for assessing clarity, methodological transparency, conceptual 
adequacy, and reporting quality across different types of studies. This approach has also 
been adopted in subsequent systematic literature reviews across different applied fields 
[14, 33].
Six quality assessment criteria were applied. QA1 assessed whether the purpose of the 
study was clearly stated. QA2 assessed whether the relevance and usefulness of the work 
were clearly presented. QA3 assessed whether the methodology was clearly established. 
QA4 examined whether the key concepts, constructs, or theoretical ideas were clearly 
defined. QA5 assessed whether the study was appropriately positioned in relation to pre-
vious work. QA6 examined whether the study’s limitations were clearly reported. Each 
criterion was scored as Yes = 1, Partly = 0.5, or No = 0, giving a maximum score of 6.0. To 
promote consistency in scoring, the same operational definitions were applied across all 
studies, with “Yes” indicating that the criterion was clearly and sufficiently addressed, 
“Partly” indicating that it was addressed but incompletely or with limitations, and “No” 
indicating that the criterion was not adequately addressed.
Although the same six appraisal domains were applied across all included studies, the 
criteria were interpreted in relation to the methodological and evidential character -
istics of each study design. For empirical studies, appraisal focused on the clarity and 
adequacy of the research design, participants or data sources, procedures, and limita -
tions. For conceptual, literature-based, and technical studies, the criteria were applied 
to the clarity of the analytical approach, conceptual development, evidential basis, posi -
tioning within previous scholarship, and acknowledgement of limitations where appli -
cable. Consistent with the purpose of the adapted appraisal framework [ 2], the criteria 
were therefore used as a common assessment of reporting, methodological transpar -
ency, and conceptual adequacy rather than as a design-specific risk-of-bias instrument.

3.9 Data synthesis and thematic analysis
The included studies were analysed through thematic analysis as the body of evidence 
was made up of different types of studies, such as conceptual studies, empirical research, 
literature reviews, and more broadly, AI ethics studies. Synthesis involved both deduc -
tive and inductive coding. Codes were developed using ATLAS.ti, which was employed 
to manage extracted data, generate codes, compare coded units, and help form thematic 
categories. The initial deductive codes were created based on the four research questions 
and related to GenAI application, ethical issues, responsible integration actions, and 
research gaps. Inductive coding involved rereading all included studies to identify recur -
ring concerns and patterns, including doctrinal accuracy, source authenticity, respon -
sibility for AI-generated content, teacher supervision, academic integrity, privacy, bias, 
religious authority, value alignment, and AI literacy. These recurring concerns were sub-
sequently interpreted through the complementary analytical lenses of adab, amanah, and 
epistemic authority. The coded data were compared and grouped into categories, which 
were then clustered into four final themes: pedagogical uses of GenAI in IRE; ethical and 
epistemic risks associated with the use of GenAI in IRE; safeguards for the responsible 
integration of GenAI in IRE; and research gaps and future directions. Such an approach 
has helped achieve a balance between synthesising diverse types of evidence and differ -
entiating between direct and indirect evidence in the context of IRE and educational AI 
ethics [17, 42].
3.10 Trustworthiness of the review
Several techniques were used to enhance the credibility of the review. First, the review 
adopted the PRISMA 2020 approach, which supports transparent reporting of how 
studies were identified, screened, selected, and included [ 35]. Second, the inclusion and 
exclusion criteria were defined prior to full-text screening to enhance consistency in 
study selection. Trustworthiness was further strengthened through independent screen -
ing and quality appraisal by two reviewers, comparison of decisions and scores, and 
resolution of disagreements through consensus, with wider-team consultation where 
necessary. Third, a structured data-extraction matrix was used to organise information 
on context, design, data source, evidence type, relevance to IRE, ethical issues, recom -
mended measures, and research gaps. Finally, the classification of direct IRE, indirect 
religious education, and contextual GenAI evidence helped distinguish IRE-specific 
findings from evidence derived from adjacent literature.

4.1.1 Theme 1: pedagogical applications of GenAI in IRE
The first theme concerns the potential pedagogical applications of GenAI in IRE. Evi -
dence from direct IRE and Islamic education studies suggests that GenAI is currently 
viewed primarily as an auxiliary tool rather than a substitute for teachers. Reported or 
proposed applications include lesson planning, teaching-material preparation, assess -

student-centred activities, and teacher–student interaction [ 3, 16, 28, 40]. In Qur’anic 
learning, GenAI has also been explored for applications such as pronunciation assess -

supplementary to teacher-mediated instruction [ 11]. Similar possibilities have been 
identified in broader religious education, where AI may support teaching efficiency, stu -

suggest potential benefits for productivity, explanation, learning support, and motiva -
tion [ 31, 38]. However, these findings should be interpreted as emerging possibilities 
rather than established evidence of pedagogical effectiveness in IRE. Much of the avail -
able literature is exploratory, conceptual, or perception-based, and there is limited 
empirical evidence from actual IRE classroom implementation. Therefore, the current 
evidence supports the potential usefulness and proposed applications of GenAI in IRE, 
but does not yet establish their effects on learning outcomes, religious understanding, 
student behaviour, or teacher–student relationships.
4.1.2 Theme 2: ethical, epistemic, and value-alignment risks
The second theme concerns the ethical, epistemic, and value-alignment risks associated 
with GenAI in IRE. Some concerns identified in the direct IRE literature, including pla -
giarism, cheating, unreliable outputs, privacy, bias, transparency, and AI readiness, are 
shared with the broader educational AI literature [3, 16, 28, 40]. Contextual studies simi-
larly identify academic integrity, fabricated citations, hallucinations, and over-reliance 
on AI as broader GenAI-related concerns [ 7, 12, 13, 15, 31]. However, these concerns 
may acquire additional significance in IRE because GenAI is used in relation to reli -
gious knowledge, theological interpretation, and moral formation. Direct and indirect 
religious education studies highlight concerns regarding the accuracy and appropriate 
use of religious information, source authenticity, religious authority, spiritual formation, 
and potential misalignment with Islamic values [ 11, 36]. Thus, while issues such as pla -
giarism, privacy, and bias are not unique to IRE, inaccurate or fabricated information 
may have particular implications when it concerns Qur’anic knowledge, religious beliefs, 
or moral guidance. These concerns should nevertheless be understood as potential risks 
rather than established consequences of GenAI use in IRE, given the limited direct 
empirical evidence.
4.1.3 Theme 3: safeguards for responsible integration
Theme three focuses on the precautions that should be put in place to ensure responsi -
ble GenAI implementation. The direct IRE and Islamic education literature consistently 
emphasises the importance of teacher oversight, verification of AI outputs, alignment 
Page 22 of 29
Mat Yusoff et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence (2026) 6:1162 
with Islamic values, digital competence, cultural appropriateness, institutional sup -
port, and teacher training [ 3, 16, 28, 40]. The safeguards in Qur’anic education include 
ensuring that AI does not replace conventional instruction, especially when there is an 
emphasis on recitation, tajweed, the relational and spiritual transmission of Qur’anic 
knowledge, and teacher guidance [ 11]. Precautions suggested through indirect religious 
education literature also include oversight by educators, theological-ethical guidance, 
policy support, infrastructural enhancement, and educator training [ 26, 36]. The GenAI 
literature across contexts reinforces the need for academic integrity policies, the rethink-
ing of assessment, guidance for students, AI literacy, responsible disclosure procedures, 
and verification of AI-generated sources [7, 12, 13, 15]. In general, these studies indicate 
that responsible implementation necessitates multi-layered precautions which include 
teachers, students, institutions, and ethical standards rather than individual users’ per -
sonal judgment alone.
4.1.4 Theme 4: research gaps and future directions
The fourth major theme addresses the limited and evolving nature of the literature. 
Research on the integration of GenAI into IRE and Islamic education tends to focus on 
perceived usefulness, acceptability, conceptual opportunities, and general ethical issues 
rather than classroom implementation [16, 28, 40]. While the qualitative and applied lit-
erature provides valuable insights, there is still a need for classroom-based, longitudi -
nal, comparative, and intervention studies examining the effects of GenAI on teaching, 
learning, religious knowledge, academic integrity, and teacher–student relationships in 
IRE [ 3, 11]. Indirect religious education literature further indicates the need for disci -
pline-specific research on theological guidance, religious authority, and educator media -
tion [26, 36]. Contextual GenAI literature identifies additional gaps concerning student 
AI literacy, academic integrity policies, responsible-use guidelines, and verification of 
generated content [7, 12, 13, 15, 38].
Hence, future research should move beyond general discussions of opportunities 
and risks towards empirically grounded frameworks for GenAI implementation in IRE. 
The proposed emphasis on adab, amanah, source authenticity, teacher mediation, and 
epistemic authority follows directly from the gaps identified in the reviewed literature: 
these concepts address learners’ conduct towards knowledge, responsibility in using 
AI-generated information, the authenticity of religious sources, the role of educators, 
and the legitimate validation of religious knowledge. They therefore complement rather 
than replace broader AI-ethics frameworks, which primarily address concerns such as 
privacy, transparency, fairness, accountability, and academic integrity. Further research 
should examine how these Islamic educational principles can be operationalised and 
empirically evaluated in GenAI-supported IRE settings.
5 Discussion
5.1 GenAI as a bounded pedagogical assistant in IRE
The findings suggest that GenAI is best understood as a bounded pedagogical assis -
tant in IRE rather than as a replacement for teachers, scholars, or recognised religious 
sources. The literature primarily associates GenAI with practical pedagogical activi -
ties such as lesson planning, teaching-material development, assessment preparation, 
administrative support, personalised learning, and teacher–student interaction [ 3, 16, 
Page 23 of 29
Mat Yusoff et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence (2026) 6:1162 
28, 40]. Regarding Qur’anic learning, the possible uses of AI may be confined to its tech -
nical abilities to analyse pronunciation or detect tajweed; however, they would be lim -
ited compared with the spiritual, relational, and pedagogical importance of traditional 
instruction [ 11]. Such results correspond to the general picture drawn by the GenAI 
education literature, as the latter acknowledges the usefulness of GenAI for explanation, 
writing assistance, provision of feedback and learning assistance, but emphasises human 
supervision and critical evaluation [24, 31, 38].
The bounded role of GenAI is particularly important in IRE because religious educa -
tion involves not only the transmission of knowledge but also moral formation, inter -
pretation, respect for knowledge, and the cultivation of religious understanding. GenAI 
may therefore assist with specific pedagogical tasks, but the available evidence does not 
support its substitution for the religious educator, moral guide, or legitimate source of 
epistemic authority. There is currently no robust evidence that GenAI has had a trans -
formative impact on IRE classrooms. Instead, the literature demonstrates perceived use -
fulness alongside limited evidence of actual pedagogical effects [ 16, 28].
5.2 Ethical integration beyond general AI governance
The literature review indicates that ethical integration of GenAI in IRE extends beyond 
general AI governance. Concerns such as privacy, transparency, bias, hallucinations, 
accountability, academic integrity, authorship, and plagiarism remain relevant to IRE 
[6, 7, 12, 13, 25, 43]. However, these concerns are not unique to IRE. The distinctive 
issue is how such risks interact with the particular epistemic and educational purposes 
of Islamic education. In IRE, inaccurate, decontextualised, or unsourced AI-generated 
content may affect not only academic performance but also learners’ understanding of 
religious concepts, perceptions of source authenticity, and trust in legitimate religious 
authorities. Direct IRE research identifies concerns regarding plagiarism, misuse of 
sources, unreliable outputs, privacy, bias, cultural readiness, and alignment with Islamic 
values [ 3, 16, 40], while broader religious education literature raises concerns about 
theological interpretation, religious authority, and spiritual formation [ 36]. These latter 
concerns remain potential rather than empirically established consequences in IRE.
Accordingly, ethical integration in IRE can be understood at three complementary lev-
els. At the general AI-ethics level, it involves privacy, transparency, accountability, bias 
awareness, and academic integrity. At the pedagogical level, it involves teacher super -
vision, critical evaluation of AI outputs, assessment redesign, AI literacy, and respon -
sible disclosure. At the Islamic educational level, it additionally requires attention to 
religious accuracy, adab, amanah, source authenticity, teacher mediation, and epistemic 
authority. These principles have practical implications: adab may require learners to 
engage respectfully and critically with religious knowledge rather than treating AI as an 
unquestioned authority; amanah may require disclosure of AI use, verification of gener -
ated religious content, and responsible handling of data and authorship; source authen -
ticity may require checking AI-generated religious claims against recognised texts and 
reliable sources; and teacher mediation may require educators to verify, contextualise, 
and correct AI-generated religious information. Epistemic authority therefore remains 
with recognised religious sources, qualified teachers, and scholars rather than with the 
GenAI system itself. The distinctive contribution of the Islamic perspective is therefore 
not to replace general AI governance, but to extend it by addressing how GenAI should

Mat Yusoff et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence (2026) 6:1162 
be situated within the epistemic, moral, and pedagogical purposes of Islamic education. 
This perspective shifts the discussion from whether GenAI is simply useful or permis -
sible towards how it can be used responsibly without compromising the authenticity of 
religious knowledge, the role of educators, and the moral purposes of IRE.
5.3 Toward an Islamic ethical framework for GenAI integration
The findings of this systematic review suggest the need for an Islamic ethical framework 
for GenAI integration in IRE. However, the framework proposed here is not derived by 
selecting Islamic concepts a priori. Rather, it emerged from recurring concerns identi -
fied across the reviewed literature concerning the responsible use of knowledge, respon -
sibility for AI-generated content, the authenticity and validation of religious knowledge, 
the role of teachers, and alignment between technology and the purposes of Islamic edu-
cation. These recurring concerns were subsequently mapped onto relevant Islamic edu -
cational concepts. On this basis, adab, amanah, and epistemic authority are proposed as 
core analytical lenses, while source authenticity, teacher mediation, and value alignment 
represent practical dimensions through which these lenses can be examined.
 Adab addresses concerns regarding the learner’s relationship with knowledge, 
teachers, sources, and the learning process. It therefore provides a basis for examin -
ing whether GenAI is used to support learning and critical engagement rather than to 
replace diligent study or teacher guidance. Amanah addresses responsibility in the pro -
duction, use, and dissemination of knowledge. In GenAI-supported IRE, this includes 
disclosure of AI assistance, verification of generated information, avoidance of plagia -
rism, protection of learner data, and responsibility for the final use of AI-generated con -
tent. These dimensions are consistent with the broader conception of Islamic education 
as involving not only the acquisition of knowledge but also appropriate intellectual, 
moral, and social conduct [19, 39].
Epistemic authority addresses a further concern repeatedly identified in the review: 
how religious knowledge is validated and who is legitimately positioned to interpret it. 
GenAI may provide explanations, examples, or learning assistance, but it should not be 
treated as an independent authority on Islamic doctrine, jurisprudence, Qur’anic inter -
pretation, or theological questions. This concern gives rise to the operational dimension 
of source authenticity, whereby AI-generated religious claims require verification against 
recognised sources and, where appropriate, mediation by qualified teachers or scholars. 
Teacher mediation consequently represents the practical mechanism through which AI-
generated information can be contextualised, evaluated, and corrected.
These dimensions collectively distinguish the proposed framework from general AI-
ethics approaches. General AI governance addresses concerns such as privacy, trans -
parency, fairness, accountability, and academic integrity, whereas the proposed Islamic 
perspective additionally examines how GenAI-mediated knowledge is approached, who 
is responsible for it, how religious knowledge is authenticated, and how technology 
remains aligned with the moral and educational purposes of IRE. The framework should 
therefore be understood as a provisional, literature-derived analytical framework, rather 
than a validated model. Its concepts and practical dimensions require further empirical 
investigation to determine how they can be operationalised and evaluated in actual IRE 
settings.

5.4 Implications for practice and policy
These findings have several implications for teachers, institutions, and policymakers. For 
teachers, GenAI can support lesson planning, the generation of examples and formative 
tasks, and differentiated learning. However, teachers should verify the accuracy of AI-
generated materials, provide appropriate religious contextualisation, and educate stu -
dents about the responsible use of AI. In IRE settings, students should understand that 
GenAI is an assistive tool rather than a source of religious authority. They should also 
be encouraged to critically evaluate AI-generated responses, verify relevant information 
against recognised sources, and disclose their use of GenAI in accordance with institu -
tional requirements.
For institutions, the findings highlight the need for context-specific GenAI policies. 
Such policies should address academic integrity, disclosure of AI use, appropriate and 
inappropriate applications, privacy, assessment design, teacher responsibilities, and pro -
cedures for verifying AI-generated content. Although general AI policies may provide 
a useful foundation for IRE, they may not adequately address issues related to religious 
knowledge, source authenticity, teacher mediation, and Islamic educational values. Insti-
tutions providing Islamic education should therefore develop policies that integrate AI 
literacy with Islamic educational ethics. Teacher training is essential because responsible 
GenAI integration requires educators to understand its capabilities, limitations, risks, 
and pedagogical applications [3, 28, 37].
Policymakers and curriculum developers should also establish discipline-specific 
guidelines for the use of GenAI in Islamic education. Such guidelines should neither 
unconditionally accept nor reject the technology, but should promote supervised, trans -
parent, and pedagogically appropriate use. Curriculum frameworks should incorporate 
AI literacy that addresses hallucination, bias, authorship, privacy, plagiarism, source ver-
ification, and responsible inquiry. In IRE, these dimensions should be connected explic -
itly to Islamic values such as adab and amanah, so that responsible AI use is understood 
as both an academic and moral responsibility. Policymakers should also address existing 
gaps in infrastructure and preparedness, particularly uneven access to digital resources 
and disparities in teacher training [3, 26, 28].
5.5 Evidence boundaries and future research
Despite the insights provided by this review, the evidence base remains limited and 
should be considered when interpreting the findings and formulating practical impli -
cations. The most directly relevant evidence comes from studies of Islamic education, 
IRE, Islamic teachers, Muslim learners, and Qur’anic education; however, these studies 
are predominantly exploratory, conceptual, or perception-based. Studies from broader 
religious education and contextual GenAI ethics provide useful insights into theologi -
cal, academic-integrity, reliability, and governance issues, but they cannot be treated as 
direct evidence of GenAI effects in IRE. Accordingly, claims concerning doctrinal distor-
tion, reduced spirituality, or displacement of religious authority should be interpreted 
as reported concerns or theoretical risks rather than established classroom outcomes. 
Practical recommendations should similarly be regarded as provisional implications 
requiring empirical validation.
Further research should therefore move beyond general discussions of the oppor -
tunities and risks of GenAI in IRE. Classroom-based empirical studies are needed to 
Page 26 of 29
Mat Yusoff et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence (2026) 6:1162 
examine how teachers and students actually use these technologies and how their use 
relates to learning, religious understanding, academic integrity, and teacher–student 
relationships. Intervention and comparative studies could examine the effects of differ -
ent forms of teacher guidance and GenAI use across madrasahs, Islamic schools, uni -
versities, Qur’anic learning centres, and teacher education programmes. Longitudinal 
research could further investigate potential changes in religious knowledge, critical 
thinking, academic integrity, teacher–student relationships, and moral formation over 
time.
The review also provides a preliminary operationalisation of the proposed Islamic eth -
ical framework. Adab may be examined through practices such as critical and respect -
ful engagement with AI-generated religious information, continued engagement with 
teachers and recognised sources, and avoidance of treating AI output as unquestionable 
knowledge. Amanah may be operationalised through disclosure of AI use, verification of 
generated content, responsible attribution, protection of learner data, and acceptance of 
responsibility for AI-assisted work. Epistemic authority may be examined through the 
extent to which learners and educators verify religious claims against recognised sources 
and maintain the role of qualified teachers or scholars in interpreting doctrinal, legal, 
ethical, and theological matters. These dimensions provide starting points for future 
classroom research, assessment design, institutional guidelines, and GenAI literacy 
programmes, but require empirical validation before they can be treated as established 
implementation criteria.
6 Conclusion
This systematic literature review examined the ethical integration of generative artificial 
intelligence (GenAI) within Islamic Religious Education (IRE). The review found that the 
available evidence remains limited and is predominantly conceptual, exploratory, or per-
ception-based rather than grounded in classroom implementation. GenAI is generally 
discussed as an assistive pedagogical technology rather than a substitute for teachers, 
scholars, or recognised religious sources. The findings therefore support cautious and 
human-mediated integration rather than claims of established pedagogical effectiveness.
The review further demonstrates that ethical integration in IRE involves both general 
AI-ethical concerns and distinctive Islamic educational considerations. Issues such as 
privacy, transparency, bias, plagiarism, and academic integrity remain relevant, but their 
implications become particularly significant when GenAI is used to produce or mediate 
religious knowledge. Concerns regarding source authenticity, religious accuracy, teacher 
mediation, value alignment, adab, amanah, and epistemic authority therefore extend 
the discussion beyond conventional AI governance.
The principal theoretical contribution of the review is a preliminary, literature-derived 
Islamic ethical framework for evaluating GenAI integration in IRE. The framework pro -
poses adab, amanah, and epistemic authority as core ethical lenses, operationalised 
through attention to source authenticity, teacher mediation, responsible AI use, and 
alignment with the purposes of Islamic education. In this framework, adab concerns 
appropriate conduct towards knowledge and learning; amanah concerns responsibil -
ity for AI-assisted knowledge, authorship, verification, and data; and epistemic author -
ity concerns the legitimate validation and interpretation of religious knowledge. These 
Page 27 of 29

dimensions complement rather than replace broader AI-ethics principles by addressing 
the specific epistemic, moral, and educational purposes of IRE.
This framework should be regarded as provisional rather than a validated model, given 
the limited evidence base. Future research should therefore empirically operationalise 
and evaluate these principles through classroom-based, intervention, comparative, 
and longitudinal studies. Such research can establish whether and how an Islamic ethi -
cal approach can support responsible GenAI use while preserving religious knowledge 
authenticity, teacher mediation, academic integrity, and the moral purposes of IRE.
