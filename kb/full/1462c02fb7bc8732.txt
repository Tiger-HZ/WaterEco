ABSTRACT 
Nutritional deprivation among children residing in urban slums continues to be a major public health concern in 
India. Dietary inadequacies, infrequent meals, low dietary diversity, and limited access to nutrient-rich foods 
contribute significantly to undernutrition and micronutrient deficiencies. This study assessed food frequency 
patterns, dietary habits, dietary diversity, and associated nutrient deficits among school-age children living in 
urban slums in Varanasi, Uttar Pradesh. We conducted a community-based cross-sectional study in 350 children 
aged 5–12 years from six selected slums of Varanasi. We assessed dietary intake using a validated Food Frequency 
Questionnaire (FFQ) and the 24-hour dietary recall method. Nutrient intake was estimated using Indian Food 
Composition Tables and compared with Recommended Dietary Allowances (RDA) prescribed by the Indian 
Council of Medical Research (ICMR). Anthropometric assessment used WHO BMI-for-age standards, Gomez 
classification, and Waterlow classification. Results show that most children (95.7%) belonged to non-vegetarian 
households; however, 75.1% consumed only two meals daily. All children frequently consumed rice and 
roots/tubers (100%), followed by pulses (70.6%), vegetables (71.1%), and wheat (65.1%). Consumption of green 
leafy vegetables (1.7%), milk products (16.9%), and fruits (0%) was extremely low. Fast-food consumption was 
remarkably high, with frequent intake of biscuits/samosa (83.7%), namkeen (72%), Kurkure (63.4%), chips 
(58.9%), and panipuri (52.9%). This study concludes that slum children's diets are characterised by poor dietary 
diversity and inadequate consumption of nutrient-rich foods, increasing the risk of chronic undernutrition and 
micronutrient deficiencies. Strengthening nutrition-sensitive interventions targeting dietary diversification and 
meal quality is urgently needed.

INTRODUCTION 
Childhood nutrition is a critical determinant of 
growth, cognitive development, educational 
achievement, and long -term health outcomes. 
School-age children require adequate energy and 
nutrients to support rapid growth and increasing 
physiological demands. However, children residing 
in urban slums frequently experience nutritional 
disadvantages arising from poverty, overcrowding, 
food insecurity, inadequate sanitation, and poor 
access to health services. [1,11-13] 
India is still carrying one of the world's largest 
burdens of childhood undernutrition despite 
significant economic growth. According to national 
surveys, urban slum populations remain 
disproportionately affected by malnutrition because 
of limited dietary diversity and poor food purchasing 
power. Dietary quality is increasingly recognised as 
a stronger predictor of nutritional status than calorie 
intake alone. [3,4,13] 
Food Frequency Questionnaires (FFQs) are widely 
used to assess habitual food intake and dietary 
diversity in population-based nutritional studies. 
FFQs help identify food-consumption patterns that 
contribute to nutrient inadequacies and adverse 
health outcomes. [6-8] 
Urban slum households often rely heavily on 
inexpensive cereals and starchy foods while 
consuming limited amounts of fruits, vegetables, 
milk products, and animal -source foods. Such 
dietary patterns increase the risk of deficiencies of 
iron, calcium, folate, vitamin A, vitamin C, and 
other essential micronutrients. [5,13,15] 
Varanasi is most densely populated and one of the 
oldest cities in India and has witnessed substantial 
growth in urban slum settlements. Despite numerous 
government nutrition programs, evidence on food 
consumption patterns among school -age slum 
children remains limited. Therefore, this study 
assessed food frequency patterns and dietary 
nutrient deficits among children aged 5–12 years 
residing in selected slums of Varanasi. [3]

1. Materials and Methods 
1.1 Study Design 
This community-based cross-sectional study was 
conducted among urban slum children in Varanasi. 
The study formed part of a larger investigation 
assessing nutritional status and cognitive 
development among slum children. 
1.2 Study Area 
The study was conducted in six urban slums of 
Varanasi: 
• Durgakund 
• Sunderpur 
• Nagwa 
• Chhittupur 
• Bhawaniya Pokhari 
• Shankuldhara 
These slums were selected from Varanasi Municipal 
Corporation records. 
1.3 Sample Size and Participants 
The study included 350 children aged 5–12 years, 
following sample size estimation procedures that 
accounted for cluster design and expected attrition. 
Inclusion Criteria 
• Children aged 5–12 years 
• Residents of selected slum areas 
• Caregivers willing to provide informed 
consent 
Exclusion Criteria 
• Children below 5 years 
• Children with diagnosed neurological 
disorders 
• Children with congenital heart disease or 
chronic illnesses affecting nutritional status 
1.4 Dietary Assessment 
Dietary assessment was done by using a food 
frequency questionnaire and 24-hour diet recall. [6-
8] 
Food Frequency Questionnaire 
Food consumption frequency was recorded for 
major food groups, including Cereals, Pulses, Green 
leafy vegetables, other vegetables, Fruits, Milk and 
milk products, Meat and poultry, Roots and tubers, 
Oils and fats, and Sugars and fast foods. 
24-Hour Dietary Recall 
We used the dietary recall method to estimate 
average daily nutrient intake. Nutrient calculations 
were performed using Indian Food Composition 
Tables and DietSoft software. Nutrient adequacy 
was assessed against ICMR Recommended Dietary 
Allowances. [2,8] 
1.5 Anthropometric Assessment 
We measured weight, height, BMI, and MUAC 
according to standard procedures. We classified 
nutritional status using WHO BMI -for-age 
standards and the Waterlow classification. [1,16] 
1.6 Statistical Analysis 
Descriptive statistics summarised dietary patterns 
and nutritional outcomes. Results were presented as 
frequencies, percentages, and mean values. 
Parental Consent: Written informed consent was 
obtained from all mothers/caregivers before 
enrolling them in the study. 
3. Results

The food habit table shows that most respondents 
(95.7%) were non-vegetarian, while 2.6% were 
vegetarian and only 1.7% were eggetarian. Three-
fourths of children consumed only two meals daily, 
indicating widespread breakfast skipping and 
reduced meal frequency. Such practices may 
adversely affect daily nutrient adequacy and 
academic performance. [17,18]

4. Discussion 
The findings of the present study reveal a highly 
monotonous dietary pattern among urban slum 
children in Varanasi. Rice, wheat, and roots/tubers 
constituted the primary components of the daily diet. 
Such cereal-dominated diets are common among 
economically disadvantaged populations because 
cereals provide relatively inexpensive energy 
compared with fruits, vegetables, and animal-source 
foods. [13,17,18] 
The most concerning finding was the extremely low 
consumption of fruits and green leafy vegetables. 
These foods are major sources of vitamins, minerals, 
antioxidants, and dietary fiber. Their inadequate 
consumption increases susceptibility to 
micronutrient deficiencies, impaired immunity, poor 
growth, and reduced cognitive performance. 
[5,17,18] 
Milk and milk product consumption was also 
remarkably low. Dairy foods are important sources 
of calcium, high-quality protein, riboflavin, and 
vitamin B12. Low intake may contribute to poor 
bone mineralization and impaired skeletal growth 
among children. [2,18] 
Breakfast skipping emerged as another important 
nutritional concern. Approximately three-fourths of 
children consumed only two meals daily. Previous 
research has shown that breakfast omission is 
associated with lower micronutrient intake, poorer 
academic performance, and reduced concentration. 
[19] 
One of the most significant findings of the present 
study was the coexistence of poor dietary diversity 
and excessive fast-food consumption. While fruits, 
green leafy vegetables, and milk products were 
rarely consumed, processed snack foods such as 
biscuits, namkeen, Kurkure, chips, and panipuri 
were consumed frequently by more than half of the 
study population. [14,15,20] 
The dietary inadequacies observed through FFQ 
assessment were reflected in anthropometric 
outcomes. Nearly seven out of ten children were 
undernourished according to WHO BMI -for-age 
criteria, while stunting affected almost three-fourths 
of the study population. These findings indicate 
long-term nutritional deprivation and chronic 
dietary insufficiency. [1,11-13,16] 
5. Conclusion 
The present study demonstrates that urban slum 
children in Varanasi consume diets characterized by: 
• Excessive dependence on cereals and 
tubers 
• Poor dietary diversity 
• Extremely low fruit consumption 
• Very low intake of green leafy vegetables 
• Inadequate milk and dairy consumption 
• High prevalence of breakfast skipping 
These dietary patterns are associated with 
widespread undernutrition, stunting, and wasting. 
Community-based nutrition education, school 
feeding programs, dietary diversification strategies, 
and improved accessibility of affordable nutrient-
rich foods should be prioritized to improve the 
nutritional well-being of urban slum children. [5,20]
