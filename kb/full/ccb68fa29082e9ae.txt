Abstract
With the large-scale deployment of carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technologies, CO2-enhanced oil recov-
ery and storage (CO 2-EOR & Storage) has emerged as a core technical approach that improves crude oil recovery effi -
ciency and enables permanent carbon sequestration. The operational efficiency and long-term stability of this technology 
rely heavily on microscopic interactions occurring within reservoir oil-water-rock multiphase systems. This paper sys -
tematically investigates the physical interactions and chemical reaction mechanisms between injected CO 2 and reservoir 
crude oil, formation water, and rock media. After reservoir injection, CO 2 dissolves into crude oil and triggers oil swell -
ing, viscosity decline, light component extraction, and miscible phase formation, which modifies crude oil fluidity at the 
molecular scale. CO2 also dissolves in formation water to generate weak carbonic acid. The resultant acid reacts with rock 
minerals through dissolution, precipitation, and ion exchange and alters reservoir wettability, which dynamically reorga -
nizes pore-throat geometries and subsurface flow environments. Competitive adsorption at oil–water–rock triple-phase 
interfaces, combined with the balance between capillary and viscous forces as well as multicomponent mass transfer and 
diffusion, governs the entire spectrum of microscopic interactions within the studied system. Existing research has tran -
sitioned from macroscopic characterization to nanoscale mechanistic investigations. Unresolved issues remain regarding 
reaction pathways under multiphase and multi-field coupling conditions, dynamic interfacial behaviors, and quantitative 
relationships between microscopic mechanisms and macroscopic engineering performances. This review elaborates the 
microscopic interaction mechanisms of CO 2 with oil-water-rock systems, delivering universal theoretical implications for 
reservoir research worldwide. It provides fundamental theoretical support for the optimization of global CO 2-EOR and 
carbon sequestration projects and offers generalized theoretical guidance for international CCUS deployment.

Microscopic CO2–crude oil interactions are dominated by 
dissolution, oil swelling, viscosity reduction, light compo -
nent extraction, and miscible phase formation. These physi-
cal behaviors lower interfacial tension and enhance fluid 
mobility to achieve efficient residual oil displacement [ 2, 
3]. CO2 dissolution in formation water generates weak car -
bonic acid, which reacts with rock minerals via dissolution, 
precipitation, ion exchange, and wettability alteration. Such 
reactions restructure pore-throat geometries and modify 
subsurface flow conditions [4, 5]. Competitive adsorption at 
oil–water–rock triple-phase interfaces, dynamic equilibrium 
between capillary and viscous forces, and multicomponent 
interphase mass transfer and diffusion jointly shape the full 
evolutionary framework of in-reservoir CO 2 multiphase 
microscopic interactions [6, 7]. This framework can be cat-
egorized into three research dimensions. First, for oil phase 
systems, CO 2 dissolution restructures crude oil molecular 
configurations at the molecular scale. Variations in crude oil 
density, viscosity, and diffusion coefficient initiate convec -
tive mixing and density fingering. The microscopic kinetic 
features of these processes dominate oil displacement perfor-
mance [2]. Shifts in CO2–crude oil miscibility alter oil phase 
viscosity and interfacial tension, which modulates fluid flow 
resistance within pore throats [ 8]. Second, for water phase 
systems, CO 2–formation water interactions induce aque -
ous acidification and ionic rearrangement, which modulates 
rock surface wettability and pore architecture [9]. Carbonic 
acid generated from CO 2–water reactions drives water–
rock interfacial dissolution and precipitation. The resultant 
changes in rock pore structure and mineral composition can 
optimize flow conduits or trigger scale-induced reservoir 
damage [10]. Third, for rock phase systems, carbonic acid 
derived from dissolved CO 2 triggers mineral dissolution, 
precipitation, and ion exchange, reshaping the geometry and 
connectivity of reservoir pore throats [ 11]. Mineral chemi-
cal reactions, clay swelling and migration, and dynamic 
pore structure evolution affect long-term reservoir stability 
and determine the availability of flow pathways and carbon 
sequestration voids [ 12]. Research focus has transitioned 
from macroscopic displacement rules to nanoscale mecha -
nistic exploration. Systematic collation and integration are 
still required to clarify multiphase and multi-field coupled 
reaction pathways, dynamic interfacial evolution, and quan-
titative relationships between microscopic mechanisms and 
macroscopic engineering parameters. Microscopic interac -
tions between CO 2 and reservoir media persist throughout 
the full displacement and carbon sequestration lifecycle [2]. 
Reservoirs represent complex multiphase and multicompo -
nent geological systems. CO2 injection disturbs the inherent 
thermodynamic equilibrium of oil–water–rock systems and 
induces a series of microscale physicochemical variations 
in subsurface formations [13]. This paper reviews physico-
chemical coupling mechanisms in the CO 2–oil–water–rock 
multiphase multiphysics system. It elucidates the integrated 
microscale reaction framework encompassing oil-phase 
fluid modification, water-phase acidification, and dynamic 
evolution of rock pore throats, and examines how multiscale 
microscopic behavior controls oil displacement efficiency, 
reservoir properties, and geological sequestration safety. 
The work refines the fundamental theoretical framework 
for CO2-enhanced oil recovery (CO 2-EOR) and geological 
sequestration. It supports laboratory investigations includ -
ing molecular simulations and core-based physical model -
ing, and links microscopic mechanisms to field development 
practices. This linkage provides a theoretical basis for opti-
mizing injection strategies, mitigating reservoir damage, 
and designing long-term carbon sequestration schemes.
Existing research on these microscale mechanisms 
remains fragmented, with no unified theoretical framework 
or integrated schematic that intuitively captures multiphase 
and multi-field coupling relationships. This paper presents 
a conceptual diagram of microscale coupling mechanisms 
for CO 2 flooding and sequestration (Fig. 1). The diagram 
categorizes and integrates core reactions across three sub -

rock. It characterizes spatiotemporal evolution patterns of

visualized, integrated framework for theoretical analysis.

mechanism
2.1.1 Solubilization and viscosity reduction mechanism
During CO 2-enhanced oil recovery (CO 2-EOR), the dis -
solution of CO 2 in crude oil is the primary physicochemi -
cal mechanism that triggers favorable oil displacement 
effects. As an active fluid, CO2 dissolves extensively in the

hydrocarbon system upon contact with crude oil, induc -
ing changes in physical properties—including reduced 
oil viscosity, volume expansion, density adjustment, and 
decreased oil–gas interfacial tension—whose combined 
effect directly improves oil displacement efficiency [14, 15]. 
Many studies have examined the mechanisms and key con-
trolling factors governing CO 2 dissolution behavior from 
various perspectives.
Guo et al. (2018) performed PVT and slim-tube displace-
ment experiments to examine CO 2 dissolution behavior in 
tight oil reservoirs of the Changteng Oilfield. CO2 solubility 
rises with pressure and concentration, reflected in a higher 
bubble-point pressure. A higher bubble-point pressure sig -
nals greater CO 2 dissolution, which lowers crude-oil den -
sity and narrows the density contrast between the oil and 
gas phases. By the Laplace equation, this reduced density 
contrast decreases interfacial tension and thus the minimum 
miscibility pressure; a higher bubble-point pressure there -
fore enables miscibility at lower pressures, reducing resid -
ual oil saturation and improving oil displacement efficiency 
[16]. Gandomkar et al. (2024) showed experimentally that 
accurate determination of interfacial tension (IFT) relies on 
precise calibration of the oil–gas density difference (Δρ). Δρ 
is measured by the mass–volume method in a high-temper -
ature, high-pressure reactor, whereas gas-phase density can 
be predicted with the Peng–Robinson (PR) equation of state 
or obtained from property databases. When the gas phase 
contains polymer thickeners such as PFA, however, the 
mixture becomes complex, binary interaction coefficients 
are usually unavailable, and equation-of-state (EOS) devia-
tions grow; in such cases, regression against experimen -
tally measured data and rigorous correction for instrument 
volume expansion at high pressure are required to ensure 
reliable IFT fitting [17, 18]. Seyyedi (2018) compared CO2 
flooding with carbonated water flooding experimentally and 
analyzed differences in their microscale interactions with 
residual oil by tracking changes in its composition and vis -
cosity. Pure CO2 flooding raised residual oil viscosity from 
82 cp. to 130 cp. (a 58% increase) and increased density 
to 0.932 g/cm³, shifting the composition sharply toward 
heavier fractions; carbonated water injection, by contrast, 
kept residual oil viscosity within 82–95 cp. and density 
stable at 0.923–0.928 g/cm³, markedly reducing residual oil 
viscosity [19]. Babaei et al. (2018) used molecular dynam -
ics simulations to examine how molecular diffusion rates 
affect CO 2 flooding efficiency and carbon sequestration. 
Molecular diffusion can increase CO 2 solubility in crude 
oil and improve oil recovery; with diffusion enabled, CO 2 
solubility in the oil phase rises sharply, and solubility differ-
ences across diffusion coefficient configurations can reach 
approximately 13%. Higher diffusion coefficients are not 
always preferable; when the CO 2 diffusion coefficient is 
excessively high—causing a corresponding drop in the dif -
fusion coefficients of heavy components such as C 10H22—
reduced heavy-hydrocarbon mobility traps the CO2-bearing 
oil phase in the pores, lowering oil recovery from approxi -
mately 94% to approximately 90%, a decline of about 4% 
points [ 20]. Zhang et al. (2023) performed high-pressure 
phase-behavior and asphaltene experiments on crude oil 
from well S13-95 in the North China Oilfield. Various con-
centrations of CO 2 were injected at 108 °C, followed by a 
saturation–depletion cycle, and the interaction between CO2 
and the crude oil was examined at the molecular level. CO2 
dissolution weakens the intermolecular (van der Waals) 
forces in the crude oil, causing its volume to expand by 
45% and its viscosity and density to decrease by 64.2% and 
16.4%, respectively, which improves fluid mobility [ 21]. 
Li et al. (2023) combined microscale experiments with 
numerical simulations to reveal the synergistic effect of 
advection—a macroscopic flow process in which the fluid 
moves under an external driving force and carries dissolved 
or mixed components with it—and diffusion on the recov -
ery factor during CO2 flooding. In the early injection stage, 
advection dominates oil displacement and reduces the oil 
molar fraction by approximately 24%; in the later stage, dif-
fusion further reduces this fraction by more than 35%. The 
two mechanisms together raise the recovery factor. With -
out diffusion, over 70% of the residual oil would remain 
trapped in dead-end pores and become unrecoverable, 
which identifies diffusion as the key mechanism for enhanc-
ing CO 2-flooding recovery [ 22]. Sun et al. (2023) carried 
out high-pressure PVT experiments to examine how engi -
neered SiO2 nanoparticles, used as displacement-enhancing 
agents, strengthen the intrinsic interaction between CO2 and 
crude oil. The nanomaterials are SiO 2 particles with sizes 
of roughly 20–30 nm; in field practice they are not injected 
alone but are co-injected with CO 2, usually as a nanofluid 
(e.g., dispersed in water) that is mixed with CO 2 before 
being pumped into the reservoir. Brownian motion induced 
by the nanoparticles generates local microconvection that 
disrupts the concentration boundary layer at the oil-phase 
interface, accelerating rather than changing the intrinsic 
diffusion and dissolution of CO 2 in the crude oil. Adding 
0.1 wt% SiO 2 nanoparticles raised CO 2 solubility by 17% 
(enhancement coefficient for solubility, ES = 1.17), showing 
that the physical perturbation introduced by the nanomateri-
als improves CO2–oil miscibility, increases crude-oil mobil-
ity, and enlarges the sweep volume, offering a practical 
route to a higher CO 2-flooding recovery factor [ 23]. Wang 
et al. (2023) used molecular dynamics simulations to exam-
ine viscosity variation in the CO 2 + 24-hexadecane system 
at 423.15–473.15 K and 1–5 MPa. As the CO 2 mole frac -
tion rose from 0.07 to 0.32, the system viscosity decreased 
markedly. The system viscosity declined significantly when

While these investigations have explored CO2 dissolution 
behaviors in crude oil and the mechanisms whereby disso -
lution modulates fluid physical properties via macroscopic 
experiments and microscopic simulations alike, notable 
research gaps still exist. Most existing research addresses 
only individual CO2 dissolution effects (viscosity reduction, 
swelling, diffusion) but seldom quantifies or compares the 
coupled processes of dissolution, swelling, extraction, dif -
fusion, and viscosity reduction. The transition conditions 
between these effects remain unclear. Most experiments use 
idealized conditions and cannot represent the heterogeneity 
and pore-structure constraints of real reservoirs. Molecular 
dynamics simulations can reveal the microscopic origins 
of viscosity reduction, but effective methods for cross-
scale quantitative mapping of these mechanisms to macro -
scopic recovery rates remain lacking. Addressing these gaps 
requires clarifying the transition conditions among CO 2 
effects, strengthening experimental validation under real 
reservoir conditions, and developing cross-scale quantita -
tive mapping methods that link microscopic mechanisms 
to macroscopic recovery—steps that would help translate 
laboratory findings into field engineering practice.
2.1.2 Swelling-induced viscosity reduction mechanism
CO2 flooding is one of the physicochemical mechanisms 
used in enhanced oil recovery, drawing on the effects of 
CO2 dissolution—specifically, crude-oil volumetric expan -
sion and viscosity reduction. At the molecular level, small 
CO2 molecules intercalate between hydrocarbon macro -
molecules, progressively replacing the original hydrocar -
bon–hydrocarbon van der Waals interactions with weaker 
hydrocarbon–CO2 intermolecular forces; this process also 
widens the molecular spacing and induces volumetric 
expansion of the crude oil [ 26, 27]. The expansion directly 
lowers the internal frictional resistance encountered when 
hydrocarbon macromolecules move relative to one another, 
which appears macroscopically as a reduction in crude-oil 
viscosity [26, 28].

late CO2 solubility in crude oil and its effect on hydrocar -
bon expansion; drawing on phase-equilibrium theory and a

the microscale miscibility mechanism: mutual solubility of

migration of CO2 and the oil phase. Pore-surface wettability 
changes the migration pathways of CO 2 and the oil phase, 
whereas a semi-closed pore structure suppresses rapid CO 2 
diffusion and strengthens oil-phase expansion, improving 
microscopic crude-oil mobility and expansion. These find -

recovery via CO2 flooding [31].Yuan et al. (2023) employed 
molecular dynamics simulations to examine the distribution 
and flow behavior of the CO 2–n-octane system in a 6-nm 
SiO2 nanopore at 353 K. After CO 2 injection, CO 2 prefer-

n-octane, and, through swelling, pushes the n-octane toward 
the pore center, where it mixes uniformly with bulk CO 2. 
The combined effect of swelling and viscosity reduction 
raises the flow velocity at the pore center; over an oil satura-
tion range of 70%–100%, the n-octane mass flux increases 
continuously with CO 2 injection volume, confirming that 
CO2-induced swelling and viscosity reduction enhance 
crude-oil mobilization and recovery in nanopores [ 32]. 
Wang et al. (2024) found experimentally that the crude-oil 
expansion factor in nanopores rises as pore radius decreases. 
At a CO 2 mole fraction of about 40%, the bulk expansion 
factor is roughly 1.08; in 10-nm pores it reaches about 1.28, 
some 18.5% higher than in the bulk phase. The expansion 
factor for shale oil, which is rich in light components, is 
about 1.28, markedly higher than the value of about 1.16 for 
heavy-component oils, an increase of roughly 10.3%. Within 
nanopores, CO2 both expels residual oil through volumetric 
expansion and improves crude-oil mobility through disso -
lution-induced viscosity reduction; acting together, these 
two effects raise shale-oil recovery [ 33]. Zhou et al. (2025) 
used molecular dynamics simulations to show that CO 2 is 
miscible with crude oil and causes volume expansion, but 
sweep efficiency during single-phase CO 2 flooding is lim -
ited, leaving the crude oil in the pores difficult to mobilize 
fully. During CO 2–water alternating flooding, by contrast, 
CO2 first weakens the interaction between crude oil and the 
pore walls, after which the advancing water phase carries 
the CO2–oil mixture; this synergistic effect raises oil recov-
ery efficiency. Under this scheme the oil recovery factor 
increases most sharply, confirming that alternating injec -
tion offers clear advantages for improving recovery in CO 2 
flooding [34].
Existing research has elucidated the core mechanisms of 
enhanced oil recovery, including CO 2 dissolution-induced 
viscosity reduction and volume expansion, and has con -
firmed how injection methods and pressure conditions 
affect displacement efficiency; several gaps remain, how -
ever. First, most studies examine individual microscopic 
mechanisms in isolation while inadequately exploring the 
coupled control mechanisms involving pore structure, inter-
facial effects, and fluid–solid interactions; how these mul -
tiple factors interact synergistically or impose constraints 
on one another has yet to be fully resolved. Second, current 
research emphasizes the favorable aspects of CO2 swelling-
induced viscosity reduction but gives insufficient attention 
to its adverse effects—such as the formation of an interfa -
cial layer through adsorption on pore walls, which reduces 
effective permeability. Future research should quantitatively 
characterize these multi-factor coupled control mechanisms 
and the competitive relationship between adsorption and 
viscosity reduction, and develop cross-scale simulation and 
experimental verification frameworks to support accurate 
prediction of CO 2 flooding performance and rational opti -
mization of injection strategies.
2.2 Extraction miscibility mechanism
2.2.1 Extraction mechanism
In the multi-contact miscible CO2 flooding process, extrac-
tion is one of the key microscale mechanisms that drive the 
convergence of gas- and liquid-phase compositions. Super-
critical CO2, whose density approaches that of a liquid and 
whose gas diffusion coefficient remains high, selectively 
extracts light and intermediate hydrocarbon components 
from crude oil and carries them into the gas phase, leav -
ing the residual oil relatively enriched in heavy components. 
The extent of extraction determines whether a miscible zone 
can form and propagate; this process is controlled by the 
coupled action of several factors, including pressure, crude-
oil composition, and pore-wettability characteristics.
Li et al. (2006) carried out a microscale experimental 
study of high-pressure CO 2 extraction of crude oil dur -
ing supercritical CO 2 miscible flooding. At 20 MPa, CO 2

near-complete displacement of residual oil. Raising injec -

porosity conditions. Supercritical CO 2 permeates porous

light components below C15 from crude oil, lowering crude-

extraction efficiency is highest; increasing the CO2 injection 
volume not only enhances light-component recovery but

surfaces, improving crude-oil flowability [ 38]. Yang et al.

how pressure affects CO 2 extraction capacity and its con -

10, 20, 30, and 40 MPa. CO 2 extraction efficiency rises 
with pressure, but the gain from further pressure increases 
above 30 MPa gradually diminishes. At 20 MPa, the diffu -

CO2 mass transfer within the pores. At high pressure, CO 2 
more readily displaces alkane molecules adsorbed on the 
rock surface, weakening adsorption between crude oil and 
the mineral surface, lowering residual oil saturation, and 
improving microscopic oil displacement efficiency [39]. Liu 
et al. (2025) used molecular dynamics simulations to exam-
ine the microscopic mechanisms of crude-oil-component 
extraction by supercritical CO2. The distribution of various 
components of crude oil and the location of carbon diox -
ide were investigated at three time points (0, 1, and 2 ns) 
during the simulated extraction of crude oil using hydroxyl-

dioxide. Supercritical CO 2 extracts light components from 
crude oil, lowers residual-oil viscosity, improves mobility 
in the pore space, and promotes desorption of oil molecules, 
raising microscale displacement efficiency [40] (Fig. 3). Tan

At the molecular scale, studies have confirmed that super-
critical CO2 enhances oil displacement efficiency by extract-
ing light components and stripping oil molecules from pore 
surfaces; several research gaps nonetheless remain. First, 
most existing studies examine the forward displacement 
mechanism of CO2 while neglecting adverse effects induced 
by light-component extraction—specifically, asphaltene 
precipitation—and provide little insight into how such pre -
cipitation might be contained or suppressed. Second, cur -
rent studies describe the behavior of individual parameters 
such as pressure and porosity but do not define the optimal 
operating range under multi-parameter coupling; the para -
doxical observation that displacement enhancement slows 
at high pressure while low-pressure diffusion yields the 
best results also remains unexplained. Third, because exist-
ing work relies on conventional reservoir simulations and 
experiments, the displacement adaptability of fractured and 
complex-porosity reservoirs remains poorly characterized, 
which hinders the precise application of these mechanisms 
to diverse field development conditions. Future research 
should prioritize asphaltene-precipitation inhibition, the 
identification of optimal multi-parameter coupling condi -
tions, and an explanation of the high-pressure/low-pres -
sure contradiction, while extending adaptability studies to 
fractured reservoirs so that microscopic displacement pro -
cesses can be more closely linked to actual field operating 
conditions.
2.2.2 Emulsification mechanism
In CO2 flooding, miscibility is one of the mechanisms that 
markedly increase oil recovery. When injected CO 2 mixes 
with reservoir crude oil, the interfacial tension between the 
two phases approaches zero, capillary forces are eliminated, 
and a single fluid phase with uniform physical properties 
ultimately forms. Miscibility, however, is not simply a 
fluid-mixing process but a complex phenomenon involving 
multi-component mass transfer and phase evolution; the key 
to achieving miscibility is the minimum miscibility pressure 
(MMP), a parameter that directly determines the compat -
ibility of reservoir conditions with the injection process.
Chen et al. (2022) performed core displacement and 
slim-tube experiments. Based on the multi-contact miscibil-
ity mechanism, the CO2–crude-oil contact zone was divided 
into four dynamic phase zones. Although near-miscible 
flooding reached a final recovery factor of 74.26%, slightly 
below that of miscible flooding (86.50%), its capillary flow 
capacity and overall recovery factor were markedly higher 
than those of non-miscible flooding [ 42]. Xue et al. (2023) 
conducted molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to inves -
tigate CO2 miscible displacement in calcite nano-slits. Their 
simulation results reveal that CO 2 first displaces octane 
adsorbed on pore surfaces, followed by gradual diffusion 
and mixing with crude oil until full miscibility is achieved. 
Van der Waals interactions between the two phases domi -
nate the miscible mixing process. Increasing temperature 
and slit aperture both accelerate miscibility evolution, while 
an optimal miscible pressure exists for the system. Collec -
tively, these findings demonstrate that carbonate nano-pores 
provide favorable microscale conditions for CO 2 miscible 
flooding (Fig. 4)[43]. Gandomkar et al. (2024) performed 
miscible displacement experiments on carbonate rock

CO2-flooding recovery reached 88.7%, 26.5% points higher 
than pure CO 2 flooding (62.2%). The improvement arises 
because PDMS lowers the oil–gas interfacial tension nearly 
to zero and reduces crude-oil viscosity from 3.8 cp to 3.0 
cp, raising recovery from 36% to 50% and improving sweep 
efficiency. At 3,250 psi—still above the minimum miscibil-
ity pressure (MMP)—however, CO 2-flooding recovery fell 
to 66.9%, showing that higher pressure favors molecular 
miscibility and a higher degree of mixing between CO 2 
and crude oil, and therefore a higher overall recovery [ 44]. 
Torabi et al. (2024) examined how four small-molecule 
CO2-soluble polymers—PDMS, PFA, P-1-D, and PVEE—
enhance CO 2 miscible flooding. These polymers dissolve 
stably in CO 2 under gas-flooding conditions, lowering the 
CO2–oil interfacial tension and reducing the minimum mis-

system raised recovery by 26.5% points relative to pure CO2 
flooding under miscible displacement, indicating that small-
molecule CO 2-soluble polymers with oxygen-containing 
functional groups lower the miscibility threshold and 
improve oil displacement efficiency [45]. Zhao et al. (2025) 
examined the transition of CO 2 flooding from miscible to 
non-miscible regimes as formation pressure declines; using 
the reduction in minimum miscibility pressure (MMP) as the 
key indicator, they assessed how associated-gas composi -

and enrich the CO 2 mixture, strengthening extraction and 
reducing the pressure required for miscibility. A composite 
system of 0.2% ethylene glycol butyl ether + 0.4% etha -
nol reduces the MMP by 17.38%, as the two additives act 
together to lower interfacial tension and weaken the phase-
interface energy barrier (the barrier imposed by interfacial

proposed a method for enhancing CO 2–tight-oil miscibility

dynamic miscibility process driven by coupled vaporizing-

intermediate hydrocarbons from the CO2-rich gas phase into

converge, interfacial tension falls nearly to zero, capillary 
resistance is eliminated, and oil recovery reaches a very 
high theoretical level [49].
Although prior work has described the miscible-flood -

actual reservoir conditions. No quantitative method yet 
exists to characterize the trade-off between flow-resistance 
control and mass-transfer efficiency in foam-assisted mis -

between the microscopic mechanisms identified by molecu-
lar simulation and macroscopic fluid-flow behavior. Future 
work should develop a miscible-flooding evaluation frame-
work that couples multiple scales and mechanisms, support-
ing the shift of CO 2 miscible flooding from laboratory and 
simulation studies to field application.

kerogen while CO2 is stored in the kerogen in the adsorbed 
phase, producing a microscale synergistic effect of oil dis -

Zhang et al. (2021) used non-equilibrium molecular 
dynamics (NEMD) simulations with quartz nanochannels to 
explore micro-adsorption and oil-displacement mechanisms

weakens CO2-crude-oil interfacial interactions, yet strength-

molecular dynamics simulations to examine the microscale 
behavior of the CO 2–octane two-phase system in a 5 nm

fluid–structure interaction, the fluid near the pore wall shows 
distinct density stratification; CO 2 molecules, with their 
stronger adsorption affinity, preferentially occupy adsorp -

wall toward the pore center [55]. Wang et al. (2022) adopted 
molecular-dynamics simulations to study CO2-driven crude-

pore yields optimal displacement via stratified flow, while 
mutual obstruction in the 0.65 nm single-layer-adsorption 
pore leads to the slowest displacement. Owing to stron -

CO2 competitively displaces adsorbed oil films and pushes 
crude oil toward pore centers [ 56]. Du et al. (2022) used 
molecular dynamics simulations to examine the microscale 
competitive-adsorption mechanism of CO2 in organic nano-

surfaces is much stronger than that of crude-oil components,

diameter. CO2 adsorption first increases and then decreases 
with temperature, peaking at 393 K, which reflects the com-

equilibrium [57]. Dong et al. (2023) used non-equilibrium

examining how nanopore type and displacement velocity 
affect the microscale mechanisms of CO 2-driven shale-oil 
displacement. Inorganic nanopores generally outperform 
kerogen-based organic pores in displacement efficiency, 
with feldspar pores performing best, whereas calcite pores 
are more prone to CO2 channeling. CO2 also displaces light 
hydrocarbon components more effectively than heavy com-
ponents; lower displacement velocities extend the contact 
time between CO2 and crude oil, strengthening competitive 
adsorption and displacement and raising overall displace -
ment efficiency [58] (Fig. 6).

microscale resistance to crude-oil displacement. Upon CO 2 
injection, CO2 molecules possess superior adsorption affin-

phenomena on long-term recovery needs further evaluation.

accounts for multiple interacting factors, building an inte -

sequestration.

2.4 Mass transfer mechanism of CO2 in nanopores
Shale oil reservoirs contain abundant micro- and nanopore 
throats, so fluid migration in these confined spaces differs 
markedly from that in conventional oil reservoirs. Super -
critical CO2 has strong diffusion and dissolution capacity, 
allowing it to invade nanopores and drive shale-oil displace-
ment. This mobilization follows a distinct mass-transfer 
pathway from fractures to micropores: CO 2 migrates along 
the fractures and then diffuses into the nanopores, where 
confinement induces a nano-confinement effect that helps 
CO2 reach the supercritical state, strengthening its disso -
lution capacity and lowering interfacial tension. With its 
strong adsorption affinity for pore walls, CO2 also separates 
oil molecules from the wall surfaces and uses the combined 
effect of permeation–dissolution–separation to mobilize 
crude oil in the nanopores.

3 Research status of CO2–water microscopic 
reaction mechanisms
3.1 Transport behavior of CO2 and formation water
In CO 2-enhanced oil recovery (CO 2-EOR), the migration 
of CO2 and formation water in the restricted pores of the 
reservoir directly governs displacement efficiency. Under 
the coupled action of capillary forces and interfacial effects, 
two-phase flow patterns differ markedly from those in con -
ventional reservoirs. Raising the CO 2 injection rate lowers 
the interfacial tension between reservoir fluids and forma -
tion water, while pressure variations further affect interfacial 
properties and the flow regime by changing the two-phase 
density difference. A clearer understanding of migration 
mechanisms and interfacial evolution in CO 2–formation-
water systems under confinement offers useful theoretical 
support for optimizing CO2 injection strategies and improv-
ing crude-oil recovery.
Niu et al. (2017) performed high-density supercritical 
CO2 flooding and supercritical CO2/water alternating injec-
tion experiments using a high-temperature, high-pressure 
microscopic visualization model. A vertically oriented 
laser-etched microscopic model was used to reproduce 
formation temperature and pressure, and thus the super -
critical CO2 state, while the distribution of CO 2 and water 
in pores and throats, the Jamin effect, and residual-oil dis -
placement were observed. High-density supercritical CO 2 
diffuses and migrates toward the lower sections of the res -
ervoir, improving longitudinal sweep efficiency [67]. Cheng 
et al. (2017) combined nuclear magnetic resonance imag -
ing with long-core flooding experiments. Nuclear magnetic 
resonance T₂ spectroscopy was used to monitor changes in 
pore-fluid distribution during CO2 flooding and to examine 
the microscale interaction between CO 2 and residual oil in 
the presence of a water phase. CO 2 can penetrate the water 
film and contact isolated residual oil at pore and throat 
boundaries, causing dispersed oil droplets to coalesce and 
be displaced; CO 2 injection also induces precipitation of 
ions such as Ca 2+, Mg 2+, and Mn 2+ from highly mineral -
ized formation water, altering pore-fluid properties and the 
rock-surface environment at the microscale [ 68]. Luan et 
al. (2020) used molecular dynamics simulations to build a 
silica-based blind-end nanopore model with an aqueous film 
and to simulate supercritical CO2 displacement of n-octane. 
Based on intermolecular interactions and hydrogen-bond 
evolution, the spatiotemporal distribution of CO2, water, and 
oil molecules was tracked. Supercritical CO₂ first diffuses 
into the aqueous film, disrupts the hydrogen-bond network 
among water molecules, induces micropores within the film, 
and gradually connects them to form a continuous mass-
transfer pathway (the water-film rupture process observed 
at 1700–1706 ps in Fig. 9). CO2 also aggregates at the oil–
water interface and changes interfacial tension, causing the 
oil phase to expand and be displaced; this is the microscopic 
mechanism by which CO 2 displaces residual oil efficiently 
through water-film rupture [ 69] (Fig. 9). Liu et al. (2022) 
used molecular dynamics simulations to build a quartz shale 
nanopore model with coexisting oil and water bridges and 
to simulate supercritical CO 2 injection and displacement. 
Based on intermolecular interactions, hydrogen-bond evo -
lution, concentration gradients, and pressure-gradient driv -
ing, they analyzed how CO2 overcomes the water bridge at 
the microscale: ① water molecules at the center of the water 
bridge show a porous distribution, with few hydrogen bonds 
and weak stability, which provides a pathway for CO2 inva-
sion; ② supercritical CO 2 disrupts the central structure of 
the water bridge through a dual mechanism of high-speed 
scouring and hydrogen-bond dragging, causing the bridge 
to fracture and a fluid channel to form; ③ CO 2 does not 
compete strongly with hydroxyl groups on the rock surface; 
after the water bridge breaks, dissociated water molecules 
adsorb onto the hydrophilic rock wall and thicken the water 
film, while CO2 forms a gas cushion between the water film 
and the oil phase, lowering flow resistance; ④ under the 
combined action of pressure driving and mutual diffusion,

with crude oil. Among the displacement methods tested, 
WAG shows the best flow-control characteristics, improv -
ing fluid migration efficiency and oil recovery [ 73]. Xia et 
al. (2025) built a CO2–water film–crude oil–rock molecular 
system and used molecular dynamics simulations to observe 
fluid-molecule distribution, mineral-interface adsorption, 
and crude-oil diffusion under different water-film thick -
nesses, examining how the water film regulates CO 2 mass 
transfer and crude-oil properties at the molecular scale. A 
continuous water film on the rock wall occupies the pore-
edge space and compresses crude-oil molecules toward 
the narrow center of the pore channel; combined with the 
oil–water interfacial force and the restraining effect of the 
hydrogen-bonded water network, hydrocarbon molecules 
become more densely arranged, the number of molecules 
per unit volume rises, the equivalent density of crude oil 
increases by 86.9%, and the diffusion coefficient falls to 
72.30% of its original value. The inherent confinement of 
the nanopores further intensifies the aggregation and com -
pression of crude oil, lowering its flowability. The complete 
water film acts as a physical barrier and is the main obstacle 
to CO2 penetration into crude oil; thicker water films inhibit 
CO2 diffusion more strongly, and CO 2 adsorption density 
on the rock surface decreases markedly with film thickness. 
Compared with pressure, heating more effectively disrupts 
the hydrogen-bonded structure of the water film, enhances 
CO2 mass transfer and diffusion, and improves crude-oil 
flowability [ 74] (Fig. 11). Gandomkar et al. (2025) used 
interfacial-tension (IFT) measurements to show that a CO2-
soluble nonionic surfactant (2EH-PO 5-EO15) raises CO 2 
solubility by about 100% (from 1.25 to 2.51 mol%) through 
micelle-mediated solubilization. Through interface-directed 
arrangement, the IFT was lowered from 32.4 to 6.7 dyn/cm, 
allowing stable foam to form (> 24 h); through surfactant 
adsorption, the rock wettability shifted from strongly water-
wet (18°) to intermediate gas-wet (71°) [75].
Although current research on the microscale mechanisms 
of CO2–water interaction has advanced considerably, sev -
eral gaps remain. The functional role of the water film is 
disputed: one view treats its rupture as a prerequisite for 
CO2 to enter blind pores and displace oil efficiently, whereas 
the other regards an intact water film as the main barrier 
to CO 2 permeation. No unified quantitative criterion has 
yet been established for the critical conditions under which 
the water film shifts from a barrier to a channel. The domi -
nant controls on water-film rupture are also unresolved, and 
the relative importance and applicable conditions of three 
mechanisms—CO2 dissolution disrupting the hydrogen-
bond network, high-speed scouring and dragging, and tem -
perature-induced weakening of hydrogen bonds—remain 
unclear. Existing simulations rely mainly on idealized sys -
tems and overlook the effect of high-mineralization ions 
on water-film stability. Future work should establish uni -
fied criteria, develop multi-factor coupled and cross-scale 
methods, and broaden active intervention by surfactants, 
moving the water film from passive breakthrough to active 
regulation.
3.2 Microscopic Inhibition of CO2 displacement by 
the aqueous phase
CO2 flooding is prone to gas channeling, which leads to 
unstable displacement; the water phase in the reservoir can 
suppress gas channeling at the microscale by dissolving and 
consuming the gas phase, occupying pore channels, and 
changing interfacial properties, which stabilizes the dis -
placement front. Most existing studies, however, focus on 
single-phase dissolution, and the microscale mechanisms by 
which the water phase suppresses CO 2 channeling remain 
unclear. The intrinsic mechanisms of water-phase stabiliza-
tion of displacement therefore need to be examined on the 
basis of CO 2–oil–water phase equilibrium and dissolution 
behavior, providing theoretical support for optimizing CO 2 
flooding [76].
Zhang et al. (2019) used molecular dynamics simula -
tions to examine crude-oil diffusion in different rock pores 
under water-bearing conditions, considering both molecular 
diffusion and interfacial adsorption. The hydrophobicity of 
organic surfaces, together with strong hydrogen bonding 
among water molecules, promotes water aggregation at the 
centers of graphite-like pores and strongly inhibits crude-oil 
diffusion; this reveals the microscale mechanism by which 
the spatial distribution of the aqueous phase impedes oil–gas 
mass transfer [77]. Chowdhury et al. (2023) used microflu -

three-phase fluids during CO 2-WAG injection. The inter -

CO2 were observed in real time in a micromodel, clarifying

4.1 CO 2 interfacial adsorption and mineral 
acidification–dissolution effects
4.1.1 Microscopic confinement adsorption behavior of CO2 
in nanopores
In geological CO2 sequestration, spatial confinement alters 
the microscopic mechanisms of CO2 adsorption and strongly 
influences both sequestration capacity and safety [93]. Con-
fined CO 2 adsorption in nanopores directly determines 
physical sequestration efficiency and is closely coupled 
with mechanisms such as residual trapping and solubility 
trapping [ 85, 86]. Elucidating the microscopic behavior 
of confined adsorption provides a basis for assessing the 
sequestration potential of low-permeability reservoirs and 
optimizing injection–production parameters, which contrib-
utes to the long-term stability of geological CO 2 sequestra-
tion [85].
Wang et al. (2016) used molecular dynamics simula -
tions to examine crude-oil migration through inorganic 
nanopores in shale. Using a quartz slit as the model sys -
tem and the COMPASS force field to calculate solid–liquid 
molecular interactions, they analyzed adsorption, diffusion, 
and interfacial potential energy for CO2, oil, and water. CO2 
preferentially adsorbs onto the quartz surface and displaces 
crude oil; through van der Waals interactions, CO2 disrupts 
oil–rock adsorption, lowers viscosity and interfacial ten -
sion, alters wettability, and improves oil displacement effi -
ciency [ 86]. Yang et al. (2017) used molecular dynamics 
simulations to examine the adsorption of water and octane 
on pore surfaces of different rocks. Comparing the adsorp -
tion energies of water and octane on quartz and graphite 
pores, they found that octane adsorption on both graphite 
and quartz was much stronger than water adsorption, and 
that the graphite–octane interaction was stronger still. Rock-
surface wettability determines the adsorption distribution of 
both the water film and crude oil, providing an interfacial-
adsorption basis for understanding how the water film affects 
the CO2–rock–crude-oil interface [87]. Zhang et al. (2019) 
used molecular dynamics simulations to examine crude-oil 
diffusion in shale nanopores under water-bearing condi -
tions. Building a molecular model of water-containing shale 
nanopores, they analyzed how rock-surface wettability and 
hydrogen bonding among water molecules affect crude-oil 
diffusion. The hydrophobic nature of organic rock surfaces 
and strong hydrogen bonding among water molecules cause 
water to aggregate at the center of graphite-like pores, form-
ing a water-film barrier that strongly inhibits crude-oil diffu-
sion; this clarifies, from a microscale interfacial perspective, 
how the water film obstructs crude-oil migration [ 77]. Liu 
et al. (2020) performed experiments on rock wettability 
transition under low-salinity water flooding. Using interface 
characterization and core displacement, they examined how 
a shift in rock-surface wettability from oil-wet/hydrophilic 
to neutral affects the initiation and migration of residual oil. 
Neutralization of rock-surface wettability promotes desorp-
tion of crude oil from the rock surface and its subsequent 
migration in pore throats; wettability alteration is thus the 
core microscale mechanism by which water-based systems 
enhance oil recovery through interfacial interactions [ 88]. 
Zhang et al. (2022) performed high-temperature, high-pres-
sure microfluidic experiments using a microfluidic chip with 
rock-pore structures to simulate deep-reservoir conditions 
and observe CO2–oil miscible displacement in real time. By 
reproducing the microscale channel structures of rock pores 
and fractures, they found that CO 2 extracts light crude-oil 
components in a funnel-like pattern, penetrates water films 
in rock pores to reach blind zones containing residual oil, 
and is accompanied by asphaltene deposition; this clarifies 
the microscale mechanisms of CO 2 extraction and interfa -
cial breakthrough in rock pore channels [89].
Gandomkar et al. (2023), based on three-phase inter -
face experiments on carbonate rocks, state that prior to 
wettability characterization, cores must undergo constant-
temperature static aging at formation temperature and pres-
sure for 1–2 days to reach equilibrium. Sufficient aging 
allows competitive adsorption of asphaltenes, resins, and 
formation-water ions onto mineral surfaces, establishing an 
oil–water interface that reflects the true reservoir charac -
teristics. Inadequate aging can cause uneven fluid distribu -
tion within the pores, prevent asphaltene adsorption from 
reaching a steady state, reduce the accuracy of contact-angle 
measurements, and make it difficult to evaluate the regu -
latory effects of CO 2, toluene, and nanoscale additives on 
wettability reversal and asphaltene deposition. In laboratory 
experiments, graded aging protocols—24 h, 7 d, and 14–30 
d—are used under simulated operating conditions, with all 
core samples subjected to identical aging conditions; this 
approach eliminates experimental errors arising from differ-
ences in interfacial adsorption and ensures stable, reliable 
wettability-characterization results [ 90]. Lü et al. (2023) 
examined oil displacement efficiency in quartz, illite, and 
smectite cores using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) 
combined with CO2 displacement experiments. Quantifying 
oil recovery in cores with different mineral compositions 
and pore sizes, they found that illite-bearing cores gave

Existing research on CO 2–rock interface interactions 
still faces several controversies at both the mechanistic 
and engineering-applicability levels. The role of wetta -
bility is disputed: one view treats hydrophilic conditions 
as favorable for oil recovery, while the other emphasizes 
water-film interference; the former helps CO 2 displace oil 
films, whereas the latter inhibits oil–gas mass transfer, and 
no unified criterion exists for the pore-throat size or water 
saturation that governs this transition. Conclusions on the 
regulatory effect of mineral type also vary: the marked 
difference in displacement efficiency between illite and 
smectite underscores the influence of mineral composition 
on pore-scale utilization, but most current findings come 
from idealized single-mineral models and do not account 
for competitive adsorption and differential dissolution in 
multi-mineral systems, limiting their applicability to real 
reservoirs. Future work should establish a unified transition 
criterion based on the dual nature of wettability, examine 
competitive adsorption and cross-scale coupling in multi-
mineral systems more deeply, and move the understanding 
of CO2–rock interface interactions from qualitative insight 
toward quantitative design.
4.1.2 Microscopic mechanisms of multi-mineral 
acidification and dissolution
The interaction between acidic fluids and multi-mineral 
systems (igneous and sedimentary rocks) is a core scien -
tific issue in geological carbon sequestration and oil-and-
gas reservoir alteration, and its dissolution behavior directly 
determines mineral transformation pathways and reservoir 
porosity–permeability evolution. Fracture aperture and sur-
face roughness strongly affect the dissolution–precipitation 
balance by regulating interfacial mass transfer and local 
chemical fields; small apertures and high surface rough -
ness promote iron-oxide precipitation, intensify surface 
passivation, and lower dissolution efficiency. Most existing 
studies focus on single-mineral dynamics and overlook the 
coupled effects of micromorphology, interfacial mass trans-
fer, and passivation, leaving the microscale mechanisms of 
multi-mineral acid-induced dissolution incompletely under-
stood. Quantitative mechanistic studies are needed to sup -
port engineering applications [94].
Tang et al. (2016) performed experiments on mineral dis-

microchemical reactions between injected CO 2 and rock 
minerals and observing the migration of mineral elements 
and their morphological changes. CO 2 injection induces 
selective dissolution of rock minerals, with some minerals 
undergoing precipitation transformation; at the microscale 
this alters the pore structure and surface composition of the 
rock, affecting oil recovery in the reservoir [ 95]. Matter et 
al. (2016) conducted field-scale and laboratory simulation

project in Iceland. Based on field injection monitoring and 
14C tracer studies, combined with mineralogical analysis to 
characterize mineralized products and reaction rates, they 
confirmed that basalt can achieve over 95% CO 2 mineral-
ization and sequestration within two years. The reaction 
rate first peaks and then gradually decreases and stabilizes, 
consistent with the double-exponential kinetics observed 
in the laboratory; this reveals the microscopic mechanism 
of permanent sequestration, involving rapid dissolution of 
magnesium-rich ferrosilicates and stable precipitation of 
carbonates [ 96]. Lin et al. (2022) performed multi-phase 
interaction experiments between CO 2, water, and tight 
sandstone, controlling injection pressure and temperature, 
observing the evolution of porosity and permeability, and 
integrating microscopic mineral dissolution and precipita -
tion to analyze the microscale mechanisms behind changes 
in rock properties. When injection pressure increased from 
15 MPa to 25 MPa, the porosity and permeability of the 
tight sandstone rose by 3.2% and 9.9%, respectively; when 
temperature rose from 44 °C to 64 °C, the porosity increase

Despite existing research on CO 2-induced acidification 
and dissolution of multi-mineral rocks (Table 1), several 
controversies remain. First, dissolution response varies 
markedly among lithological reservoirs: basalt exhibits very 
high mineralization efficiency, whereas limestone undergoes 
considerable mechanical-strength reduction; yet the factors 
controlling these differences remain unclear. Second, stud -
ies disagree on the dual “permeability enhancement” and 
“confinement” effects of mineral dissolution, in which pore 
expansion from dissolution and secondary precipitation 
causing throat blockage occur simultaneously; the critical 
temperature–pressure conditions and mineral assemblages 
at which this dissolution–precipitation competition shifts 
from permeability-enhancement dominance to confinement 
dominance remain unquantified. Future work should iden -
tify the factors controlling differential dissolution among 
lithologies, quantify the critical temperature–pressure 
conditions and mineral assemblages that trigger the shift 
from “permeability enhancement” to “confinement” in the 
dissolution–precipitation competition, develop long-term 
evolution prediction models that incorporate multi-mineral 
coupling, and move CO2–rock acidification and dissolution 
studies from descriptive observation toward quantitative 
prediction.
4.2 Microscopic mechanisms of secondary mineral 
evolution under ion migration
The ion migration rate, interfacial mass transfer, and their 
coupled regulation of mineral nucleation, phase transfor -
mation, and morphological evolution directly determine 
sequestration stability and capacity. The microscale inter -
facial mechanisms behind ion-migration-driven mineral 
evolution and the associated kinetic competition remain 
unclear; in particular, the synergistic interaction between 
hydrate formation and carbonate mineralization has not 
been characterized quantitatively [ 109]. Clarifying the 
intrinsic links between ion migration and secondary-min -
eral evolution offers useful theoretical support for long-term 
safety assessment and engineering optimization of carbon 
sequestration systems.
Wang et al. (2013) performed CO 2–water–rock interac-
tion simulation experiments combined with SEM–EDS 
characterization to analyze the microstructure and elemental 
composition of new phases, identifying mineral-trapping 
intermediate products. At 55 °C, no new minerals formed, 
whereas at 70 °C, 85 °C, and 100 °C, needle-shaped, spheri-
cal, and flower-like new minerals appeared on the sample 
surfaces, respectively; these are key intermediate products 
in CO2 mineral sequestration [110] (Fig. 14). McGrail et al. 
(2017) performed both field and laboratory experiments on 
the basalt–supercritical CO 2–brine reaction. Based on the 
acidification principle whereby CO 2 dissolves in water to 
form carbonic acid, they conducted in situ injection and 
batch-reaction tests on a basalt reservoir in the Columbia 
Plateau. At the microscale, native minerals in the basalt—
such as glassy material, monobahirite, and plagioclase—
were dissolved by carbonic acid, releasing ions such as 
Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, and Fe 2⁺, which reacted rapidly with dissolved 
CO2 to form carbonate minerals like dolomite and calcite, 
achieving CO2 mineralization sequestration [ 111]. Li et al. 
(2020) performed multi-field coupled numerical simula -
tions of CO2–water–rock systems in saline aquifers. Using 
a thermal–fluid–mechanical coupling model, they simulated 
pore-fluid flow, heat transfer, and rock-stress evolution dur-
ing CO2 injection and production, examining the microscale 
mechanisms of water–rock chemical reactions triggered 
by CO 2 dissolution: CO 2 dissolves in formation water to 
form carbonic acid, which ionizes H⁺ and dissolves min -
erals such as feldspar and calcite, releasing ions like Ca 2⁺ 
and Mg2⁺; clay minerals also swell on contact with water, 
altering pore-throat structure and fluid-flow pathways at the 
microscale [ 112]. Ma et al. (2020) performed one-dimen -
sional reactive-transport simulations and lithofacies experi-
ments on deep sandstones in the Bohai Bay Basin, using 
mineral-kinetics principles to examine the microscale inter-
action mechanisms of CO 2, water, and rock. CO 2-acidified 
fluids promote dissolution of calcite and chlorite, releasing 
ions while facilitating their conversion to dolomite and sid-
eromelane; the slow dissolution of aluminosilicates is the 
rate-limiting step for carbonate precipitation, improving 
overall pore-throat connectivity without significant block -
age and confirming that this system can achieve stable CO2 
mineral sequestration [113].
Sun et al. (2021) performed comparative experiments 
on the interfacial rheology of nanoparticle foams and their 
retention in porous media, based on particle adsorption, 
pore-throat blockage, and interfacial-film strengthening, to 
quantify nanoparticle retention in the rock matrix. Nanopar-
ticle retention decreases as rock permeability increases; 
through interfacial-rheological enhancement of foam stabil-
ity, the nanoparticles form bridging and sealing structures 
at the pore-throat scale, selectively reducing CO 2 mobility 
in high-permeability pathways, improving sweep efficiency 
in heterogeneous reservoirs, and facilitating long-term CO2 
sequestration [114]. Song et al. (2023) performed microscale

process achieves high sequestration safety [ 116]. Dai et

XRD and SEM were used to observe mineral evolution. 
During the short-term reaction phase, calcite Ca–O bonds 
dissociate and Ca2⁺ is released, while dolomite, quartz, and 
feldspar remain stable. The process is dominated by selec -
tive dissolution by acidic fluids; supercritical CO 2–acidic 
fluids preferentially dissolve carbonate minerals, accompa -
nied by precipitation of secondary minerals, which modi -
fies the pore-throat structure at the microscale and affects 
sequestration stability [117].
The microscale mechanisms of CO 2–rock interaction 
and mineralization sequestration remain disputed, with no 
consensus on several core issues. Mineralization efficiency 
varies widely across rock types: basalt exceeds 95% min -
eralization within two years, whereas sandstone or shale 
shows an initial increase followed by a decrease in perme -
ability because of dissolution–precipitation competition. 
Systematic cross-rock-type comparisons are still lacking, 
however, so the fundamental controls on these differences 
remain unidentified and reservoir selection has no unified 
mineralogical criterion. Views also diverge on how tem -
perature regulates mineral-evolution pathways—different 
temperatures produce distinct mineral morphologies—but 
this finding comes from specific systems and does not gen -
eralize to multi-mineral coexistence. Future work should 
address three areas: building a unified mineralogical evalua-
tion system, clarifying multi-mineral coupled evolution, and 
developing multi-field coupled, multiscale prediction meth-
ods. These efforts would establish quantitative links among 
rock type, temperature, and evolution pathways, moving 
CO2 mineralization sequestration from descriptive observa-
tion to quantitative prediction and engineering-adaptability 
assessment.
5 Challenges and prospects
5.1 Challenges
Research on the microscopic oil-water-rock reaction mech-
anisms of CO 2 flooding and storage has advanced from 
macroscopic displacement characterization to nano- and 
micro-scale interfacial analysis. Nevertheless, several core 
scientific bottlenecks still restrict the large-scale, long-term 
and safe engineering application of CCUS technologies, 
which are summarized as follows.
1. Ambiguous coupling relationships of multiple micro -
scopic mechanisms and lack of quantitative criteria. Cur-
rent studies mostly focus on individual mechanisms such 
as CO 2 viscosity reduction, swelling extraction, com -
petitive adsorption, and mineral dissolution, while the 
synergistic and restrictive coupling relationships among 
these multiple mechanisms remain unclear. The coupling 
regulation laws of pore confinement, wettability, mineral 
properties and other factors are unspecified. In addition, 
there is a lack of quantitative criteria for the primary-
secondary transformation mechanisms and critical con -
ditions of various microscopic effects, making it difficult 
to accurately characterize the complex displacement pro-
cesses in reservoirs.
2. Controversial dual regulatory mechanism of formation 
water and unclear action principle. Formation water 
presents dual properties of blocking mass transfer and 
constructing seepage channels. Existing studies have not 
reached a unified conclusion on the critical transforma -
tion conditions and main controlling factors of the water 
film “barrier-channel” effect. The competitive relation -
ship between the positive effect of water-phase seepage 
enhancement and the negative effect of permeability 
resistance cannot be effectively decoupled, leading to 
insufficient understanding of the CO2 displacement regu-
lation mechanism in water-bearing reservoirs.
3. Strong working condition dependence of interfacial 
confinement effects and insufficient full-cycle dynamic 
mechanism research. The superiority of CO 2 displace -
ment and adsorption is significantly restricted by pore 
size, temperature, pressure, mineral type and other condi-
tions, and no universal evolutionary law has been estab -
lished. Most existing studies concentrate on the positive 
gas injection displacement process, while ignoring nega-
tive effects such as heavy component re-adsorption and 
pore throat blockage during the pressure reduction stage. 
Research on the oil recovery attenuation mechanism 
throughout the full injection-production lifecycle is defi-
cient, and the ideal models poorly match the actual het -
erogeneous reservoirs.
4. Cross-scale disconnection between microscopic mecha -
nisms and macroscopic engineering indicators. Most 
current microscopic simulations and physical experi -
ments are carried out under ideal conditions, divorced 
from the actual reservoir environments characterized by 
high temperature, high pressure, high salinity and strong 
heterogeneity. There is a lack of quantitative correlation

between microscopic reaction mechanisms and macro -
scopic development indicators including oil recovery 
factor, gas channeling risk and sequestration stability, 
which fails to guide the optimization of field injection-
production parameters effectively.
5.2 Prospects
Aiming at the aforementioned core scientific bottlenecks 
restricting the development of CO 2 flooding and carbon 
sequestration technology, the future research can be carried 
out from the following four key directions to complement 
and improve the theoretical system of CO2-EOR and carbon 
sequestration, and support the safe and large-scale engineer-
ing application of CCUS technology.
1. Quantitative characterization of multi-mechanism cou -
pling and improvement of microscopic theoretical sys -
tem. This direction focuses on the synergistic coupling 
of multiple microscopic effects. It quantifies the primary-
secondary relationships and transformation thresholds 
of core mechanisms including dissolution, extraction, 
adsorption and mass transfer, establishes quantitative 
criteria for microscopic displacement under multi-factor 
coupling, and improves the multi-field coupled theoreti -
cal system of CO2 flooding.
2. Improvement of full-cycle dynamic displacement mech-
anism oriented to real reservoir conditions. Based on 
actual reservoir geological characteristics, it establishes 
composite pore and multi-component fluid models. It 
systematically reveals the full-cycle dynamic evolution 
of adsorption-desorption behaviors, quantifies the dis -
placement boundary under diverse reservoir conditions, 
clarifies the long-term productivity attenuation mecha -
nism, and optimizes the interfacial displacement theory.
3. Quantification of competitive water-rock reactions and 
establishment of long-term reservoir evolution pre -
diction methods. It defines the critical transformation 
conditions of dissolution-precipitation competition in 
multi-mineral systems, reveals the evolutionary laws of 
reservoir pore-permeability and mechanical properties 
under different working conditions, and builds a long-
term prediction system balancing enhanced oil recovery 
and carbon sequestration safety.
4. Construction of cross-scale correlation models and pro -
motion of theoretical engineering application. Previous 
studies have systematically expounded the microscopic 
reaction mechanisms of carbon dioxide in the process 
of oil displacement, including its interactions with 
crude oil, formation water, and reservoir rocks. There 
is a quantitative correlation between these different 
microscopic reaction mechanisms and the macroscopic 
characteristics of reservoir development (Table 2), which 
has significant guiding significance for on-site engineer-
ing implementation. In the future, research should be 
conducted from multiple perspectives such as micro -
scopic simulation, physical experiments, and reservoir 
numerical simulation to establish a quantitative cross-
scale correlation between the microscopic mechanisms 
and macroscopic development indicators. A compre -
hensive technical system covering mechanism analysis, 
parameter optimization, and on-site application should 
be established to promote the large-scale on-site applica-
tion of the collaborative technology of carbon dioxide 
flooding and carbon sequestration.
6 Conclusions
CO2 flooding and geological sequestration are pivotal 
technologies that couple enhanced oil recovery with car -
bon emission reduction. Their application performance 
and long-term stability highly depend on the microscale 
physicochemical interactions of the oil-water-rock multi -
phase system. CO 2 improves crude oil fluidity and strips 
adsorbed oil films from rock surfaces through dissolution, 
swelling, viscosity reduction, light component extraction 
and miscible effects. Clarifying the microscopic conditions 
of CO2-induced viscosity reduction and miscible extraction 
provides a molecular-level basis for determining the mini -
mum miscibility pressure and optimizing injection param -
eters. CO 2 reacts with formation water to form a weak 
carbonic acid system, which provides an acidic environment 
for water-rock reactions and breaks water film barriers to 
mobilize residual oil in blind pores. Meanwhile, the aque -
ous phase suppresses CO2 gas channeling and stabilizes the 
displacement front via pore occupation, Jamin effect and 
mobility regulation, achieving full-pore oil mobilization 
and uniform seepage. In the coupled CO 2-water-rock sys-
tem, selective mineral dissolution, ion migration, second -
ary mineral precipitation and wettability alteration jointly 
reshape the reservoir pore-throat structure. The dissolution 
of feldspar and carbonate minerals expands pore space and 
enhances permeability, while the generation and migration 
of clay minerals may trigger pore-throat blockage. Sys -
tematic clarification of the competitive dissolution-precip -
itation kinetics and secondary mineral evolution provides 
critical references for predicting reservoir physical property 
evolution and caprock integrity during long-term carbon 
sequestration. Accurate revelation and precise regulation of 
microscale CO2-oil-water-rock reaction mechanisms serve 
as the core scientific foundation for improving CO 2-EOR 
efficiency and ensuring carbon sequestration safety, pos -
sessing important theoretical and engineering significance
