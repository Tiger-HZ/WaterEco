Island endemic species are highly sensitive to various pressures due to small population sizes and adaptations to specific island conditions. This makes them more threatened than mainland species. On the other hand, conservation actions aimed at improving their population status may be more effective because threats can be more readily controlled within the limited area of the island. If effective, population trends in species benefiting from conservation efforts should be more positive than in those not targeted by such actions. We tested this prediction using island-endemic avian species in the order Charadriiformes, a highly threatened group of waders, gulls and their relatives, drawing on literature information on their global population trends and conservation actions undertaken, recognising five main action types (area protection, predator control, species protection, translocation, resource enhancement). Our models showed a higher probability of negative population trends in species whose ranges are within protected areas than in those occurring outside them. This pattern may reflect the designation effect, i.e. the tendency for protected areas to be established specifically for species with an unfavourable conservation status. Alternatively, protected areas may be unable to secure these species due to limited capacity to mitigate the major threats they face.
