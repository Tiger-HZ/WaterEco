SrinuanpanAbstract
Microalgae 
and cyanobacteria are emerging as foundational biological resources 
for sustainable space exploration and long-duration human missions. Their high 
photosynthetic efficiency, rapid growth, resilience to diverse environmental stresses, 
and ability to simultaneously fix carbon dioxide (CO2), produce oxygen (O2), and 
generate biomass make them promising components of bioregenerative life-support 
systems (BLSS). This chapter provides an overview of historical and contemporary 
spaceflight experiments involving microalgae and cyanobacteria, highlighting their 
physiological responses to space conditions and their potential roles in closed-loop 
life-support systems. Recent advances in cultivation technologies, omics-based 
approaches, and integrated bioprocessing platforms are discussed, with emphasis on 
applications in air revitalization, wastewater treatment, nutrient recycling, food 
production, and biomanufacturing. The major challenges associated with 
extraterrestrial cultivation, including microgravity, radiation exposure, limited 
resources, environmental instability, and biosafety, are examined together with 
emerging strategies to improve system reliability and biological performance. 
Advances in genetic engineering, synthetic biology, artificial intelligence (AI)- 
assisted modeling, automation, and process optimization offer new opportunities to 
develop robust and multifunctional biological systems for space applications. The 
chapter further explores future prospects for engineered, highly resilient strains and 
integrated space biorefineries capable of converting waste streams and available 
resources into oxygen, food, fuels, biomaterials, and other valuable products. 
Potential applications beyond low-Earth orbit, including lunar and Martian habitats, 
in situ resource utilization, and long-term planetary ecosystem development, are also 
considered. Overall, microalgae and cyanobacteria represent versatile biological 
platforms with significant potential to support sustainable, resource-efficient, and 
increasingly autonomous human activities beyond Earth.

1.
IntroductionSpace 
exploration is shifting from short orbital missions to long-duration 
expeditions, lunar bases, and future human settlement on Mars [ 1]. As mission 
duration increases, dependence on frequent resupply from Earth becomes 
impractical and costly, especially for water, oxygen, food, and other consumables 
[2, 3]. Consequently, autonomous and regenerative life-support systems are essential 
for sustainable space habitation [4]. Bioregenerative life-support systems (BLSS) are 
considered key components of future long-term missions [5]. Unlike physico- 
chemical systems, which depend on consumables and high energy inputs, BLSS use 
living organisms to recycle resources and close material loops [6]. These systems can 
capture carbon dioxide, generate oxygen, recycle wastewater, recover nutrients, 
and produce edible biomass with minimal external inputs [ 7]. Such capabilities are 
particularly valuable for lunar habitats, Martian settlements, and deep-space 
travel [8].
Among biological candidates, microalgae and cyanobacteria are especially 
promising due to their high photosynthetic efficiency, rapid growth, and metabolic 
flexibility [9, 10]. They can tolerate environmental stresses relevant to spaceflight, 
including radiation, nutrient limitation, oxidative stress, and fluctuating light 
conditions [11]. Studies have also shown that several species can remain 
metabolically active under microgravity and survive extreme extraterrestrial 
conditions [12]. In addition to life-support roles, their biomass provides proteins, 
polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), vitamins, pigments, and antioxidants 
beneficial for astronaut nutrition and health [13]. Through metabolic engineering, 
they may also produce pharmaceuticals, nutraceuticals, biopolymers, and radiation- 
protective compounds, supporting a future space bioeconomy [14].
As illustrated in Figure 1, the conceptual diagram of an algal-based BLSS 
highlights CO2 capture from crew respiration, O2 generation via photosynthesis, 
nutrient recycling from wastewater and human waste streams, and biomass 
harvesting for food production, bioactive compounds, and biofuel applications. 
Therefore, this chapter reviews current advances and future prospects in space algal 
biotechnology. It summarizes the biological potential of microalgae and 
cyanobacteria, historical and recent spaceflight experiments, and their responses to 
microgravity, radiation, and extraterrestrial resource limitations. It also discusses 
innovations in photobioreactor (PBR) design, synthetic biology, and in situ resource 
utilization (ISRU), while addressing operational challenges such as biofilm formation 
and gas–liquid mass transfer limitations. Overall, the chapter highlights the role of 
algae-centered systems in enabling sustainable human exploration beyond Earth.2.
Microalgae as key organisms for space applications2.1
Biological features ideal for space environments
Microalgae and cyanobacteria possess traits that make them highly suitable for 
extraterrestrial deployment, offering advantages over higher plants in closed 
habitats [10]. Species such as Chlorella vulgaris, Arthrospira platensis (Spirulina), and 
Synechococcus elongatus can double their biomass within 12–24 h, enabling rapid and 
continuous production of food, oxygen, and useful bioproducts within limited Ecosystems

can recover quickly after contamination or failure. Many species are metabolically 
flexible and can grow mixotrophically using light plus organic carbon sources 
derived from waste streams, helping maintain productivity during low-light 
conditions such as lunar nights or Martian dust storms [ 16]. Extremophilic taxa, 
including Chroococcidiopsis, tolerate desiccation, radiation, and extreme 
temperatures, supporting their use in harsh planetary environments [ 17, 18]. 
Microalgae also exhibit high photosynthetic efficiency and convert most of their 
biomass into edible or usable material, unlike crops that invest heavily in roots and 
stems [19]. Their unicellular structure allows precise cultivation in PBRs, where 
light, nutrients, gas exchange, and harvesting can be tightly controlled with minimal 
crew labor [15]. Importantly, microalgae perform dual life-support roles by fixing 
CO2 and generating O2, helping stabilize cabin atmosphere while producing biomass 
for food and manufacturing [20]. This makes them valuable for regenerative systems 
where mass efficiency and reliability are essential.
2.2 Nutritional and biochemical advantages
Microalgae are highly nutrient-dense and well suited for in situ food production 
during deep-space missions [13]. Many species contain 40–70% protein by dry 
weight, exceeding common terrestrial crops, and often provide balanced amino acid 
profiles important for preserving muscle mass in microgravity [ 11]. They are also 
rich in beneficial lipids, especially omega-3 fatty acids such as EPA and DHA, which 
support cardiovascular, neurological, and immune health [21]. In addition, 
microalgae supply vitamins, provitamin A, and trace minerals, offering a fresh

2.3 Genetically engineered and synthetic biology approaches
Genetic engineering can greatly expand the utility of microalgae for space 
systems [25]. Tools such as CRISPR–Cas, TALENs, and zinc-finger nucleases enable 
precise modification of genes related to stress tolerance, photosynthesis, and 
metabolite production [26]. For example, truncated light-harvesting antenna 
mutants reduce shading in dense cultures, improving light penetration and biomass 
productivity in compact PBRs [27]. Other engineering targets include enhanced 
DNA repair, antioxidant defenses, and protective pigment production to improve 
radiation resistance [28, 29]. Synthetic biology also allows microalgae to function as 
biofactories, producing vaccines, antibodies, pharmaceuticals, nutraceuticals, and 
biopolymers using sunlight and recycled CO2 [30]. This could reduce dependence on 
Earth resupply while supporting crew health and material needs. Overall, engineered 
microalgae represent multifunctional platforms for food production, 
environmental control, and in situ biomanufacturing, making them key technologies 
for sustainable long-duration human exploration beyond Earth.3.

Support System Alternative (MELiSSA), which adopted a modular and 
compartmentalized design [35]. Instead of a single closed unit, MELiSSA used linked 
microbial and phototrophic compartments to convert waste into oxygen, nutrients, 
and edible biomass. Its algal module commonly employed Limnospira indica 
(formerly Spirulina) for oxygen production, carbon fixation, and biomass 
generation [38]. Together, BIOS-3 and MELiSSA confirmed that phototrophic 
microorganisms can perform essential life-support functions in closed systems. They 
also showed that compartmentalization improves resilience, process control, and 
system stability [36, 39]. These early programs laid the foundation for later 
experiments aboard the International Space Station (ISS) and for future lunar and 
Martian habitat design.
3.2 Experiments on the ISS
Research aboard the ISS has greatly improved understanding of microalgal 
physiology under microgravity and space radiation [40, 41]. Many species maintain 
active growth in orbit, although others show changes in cell morphology, pigment 
composition, and division rates, indicating strain-specific responses to the space 
environment [42]. A major challenge in microgravity is the absence of buoyancy- 
driven convection. On Earth, natural mixing helps distribute nutrients and remove 
waste products. In orbit, transport becomes diffusion-limited, creating localized 
nutrient depletion and metabolite accumulation around cells [43, 44]. These 
conditions can reduce culture performance and require specially designed PBRs. To 
address this, several microgravity-adapted PBR systems have been developed. 
Membrane-based reactors use diffusive gas exchange instead of bubbling, avoiding 
foam formation and gas separation problems in weightlessness [ 45]. Other systems 
use rotating vessels, microfluidic channels, or mechanical mixing to maintain 
uniform nutrient distribution and prevent stagnation. Such designs are important for 
sustaining dense cultures during long missions.
Microgravity also influences photosynthesis. Changes in chloroplast positioning, 
thylakoid structure, and carbon fixation have been reported when gravitational cues 
are removed [46]. Some species show reduced photosystem II efficiency, whereas 
others compensate by increasing pigment synthesis or protective non-photochemical 
quenching mechanisms [47]. In addition, space radiation causes molecular and 
metabolic stress. Transcriptomic and proteomic studies show activation of antioxidant Microalgae

3.3 Omics-level findings
High-throughput omics technologies have greatly improved understanding of 
how microalgae adapt to spaceflight conditions [25]. Transcriptomic studies 
commonly show upregulation of stress-response genes related to redox balance, lipid 
remodeling, protein folding, and chaperone activity [50]. This suggests that cells 
shift resources from growth toward protection and repair. Increased expression of 
heat shock proteins (HSPs) further indicates that microgravity induces proteotoxic 
stress requiring continuous protein stabilization [51].
Metabolomic and lipidomic analyses reveal accumulation of antioxidant and 
photoprotective compounds such as astaxanthin, β-carotene, and tocopherols, 
which help scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) and protect DNA, 
membranes, and photosynthetic machinery from radiation-induced damage 
[52]. Lipid remodeling also helps maintain membrane fluidity, nutrient 
transport, and cellular signaling under diffusion-limited microgravity 
conditions [53].
Proteomic studies provide further evidence of coordinated regulation of energy 
metabolism, photosystem stability, ROS detoxification, and stress-signaling 
pathways [50]. Together, these findings demonstrate the strong metabolic plasticity 
of microalgae under space stressors. Importantly, omics data also identify targets for 
engineering improved strains. Genes controlling antioxidant defenses, DNA 
repair, and stress tolerance may be exploited to develop space-hardened microalgae 
with higher resilience and productivity, strengthening future BLSS performance.

3.4
Limitations of current research
Despite substantial progress, important barriers still limit the translation of 
experimental results into fully operational BLSS [54]. Most spaceflight studies use 
milliliter-scale cuvettes, culture bags, or small PBRs that do not reflect the large 
volumes required for real missions [36, 44]. Supporting even a small crew would 
require hundreds to thousands of liters of culture, creating additional challenges in 
mixing, light penetration, gas–liquid mass transfer, and nutrient distribution. 
Environmental conditions aboard the ISS are also limited. Constraints in light 
quality, irradiance uniformity, gas exchange, power supply, and thermal control 
often prevent testing under fully optimized operating conditions [20, 55]. As a result, 
current studies may underestimate the true productivity of microalgae in future 
dedicated habitat systems [56].
Another major gap is long-term ecological stability. Many experiments are short- 
duration and conducted under near-sterile conditions to avoid contamination 
[48, 57]. Therefore, risks such as phage infection, bacterial overgrowth, community 
shifts, inhibitory metabolite buildup, and sudden culture collapse remain poorly 
understood. In a closed-loop BLSS, a culture crash could disrupt oxygen production, 
carbon dioxide removal, and food supply simultaneously, posing serious risks to 
crew safety [58]. To overcome these limitations, future research must focus on long- 
duration, integrated system trials that include automated harvesting, biomass 
processing, reinoculation strategies, and real-time monitoring [59]. Multi-year 
validation at realistic scales will be essential to demonstrate the reliability, 
controllability, and safety of algal-based BLSS for sustainable human presence 
beyond Earth.
3.5 Implications for future missions
Findings from BIOS-3, MELiSSA, and ISS experiments demonstrate both the 
strong potential and operational complexity of using microalgae in long-duration 
space missions [16, 40, 59]. These studies confirm that microalgae can provide key 
life-support functions, including oxygen production, carbon dioxide removal, and 
biomass generation, when environmental conditions are carefully controlled. 
However, scaling from laboratory cultures to systems capable of supporting human 
crews presents major engineering and biological challenges. These include 
maintaining efficient gas–liquid mass transfer, preventing contamination, ensuring 
uniform light delivery, and preserving metabolic stability under prolonged 
microgravity and radiation exposure [36].
Insights from multi-omics research are especially valuable for future applications, 
as they help identify traits linked to antioxidant capacity, photoprotection, stress 
resistance, and efficient resource utilization. Such knowledge can guide strain 
selection and genetic improvement for more reliable space cultivation systems 
[25, 50]. Future lunar bases, Martian habitats, and deep-space vehicles will likely 
require integrated and compartmentalized life-support systems rather than single 
biological units [60]. These systems must combine robust algal modules with 
advanced sensors, automation, and adaptive control to maintain long-term stability

Algal biotechnology for bioregenerative life-support systems4.1
Air revitalization
Microalgae are key components of BLSS because of their strong ability to 
regenerate cabin atmospheres by capturing CO2 and releasing O2 through 
photosynthesis [20, 39]. Unlike physicochemical systems used on the ISS, which 
require consumables or energy-intensive regeneration, algal systems are self- 
renewing and operate mainly using light and recycled nutrients [ 5]. Their high 
photosynthetic efficiency often exceeds that of higher plants when compared by 
biomass or illuminated area, largely because they lack non-productive tissues such as 
roots and stems [10]. Species such as Chlorella vulgaris and Arthrospira platensis 
(Spirulina) can maintain stable oxygen production under controlled conditions, even 
in microgravity [37, 44].
To overcome reduced natural mixing in space, cultivation systems use 
mechanical stirring, pneumatic circulation, or membrane-based gas exchange to 
distribute CO2 and remove O2 efficiently [61]. Photosynthetic activity can also be 
rapidly adjusted through light control, allowing microalgae to respond quickly to 
temporary increases in crew CO2 production [62]. In addition, dense liquid algal 
cultures can reduce equivalent system mass (ESM) compared with crop-based 
systems, saving launch mass, habitat volume, and crew time [63]. These advantages 
make microalgae highly effective for air revitalization in long-duration lunar, 
Martian, and deep-space missions.
4.2 Wastewater and nutrient recycling
In BLSS, microalgae play a dual role as waste-treatment agents and nutrient- 
recovery systems [20]. They efficiently absorb nitrogen and phosphorus from 
human waste streams such as treated urine and processed fecal matter, converting 
these wastes into new biomass [64]. Nitrogen recovery is especially important 
because urine contains high ammonium levels, which can become toxic to some 
species. This can be managed through bacterial nitrification, which converts 
ammonium to nitrate, or by using tolerant strains such as Chlorella spp. [ 64]. 
Microalgae also capture phosphorus, an essential but limited nutrient required for 
nucleic acids and energy metabolism. Recycling phosphorus within biomass is 
critical in closed-loop systems where external resupply is difficult [ 65].
Greywater treatment can be improved using algal–bacterial consortia. Bacteria 
break down organic compounds into CO2 and mineral nutrients, while microalgae 
supply oxygen through photosynthesis and assimilate the released nutrients into 
biomass [66]. These systems can remove pollutants and microbes while producing 
useful biomass for food or biomanufacturing. By converting waste into reusable 
resources, algal systems help close ecological loops, reduce dependence on Earth 
resupply, and improve the sustainability and resilience of future lunar, Martian, and 
deep-space habitats [59].

homogenization, or enzymatic hydrolysis are often needed [70]. Advanced food 
technologies, including algal protein isolates and 3D food printing, can create more 
familiar and customized meals, helping reduce menu fatigue and improve crew 
morale [71]. Beyond nutrition, microalgae may also function as “pharmaceutical 
foods” by delivering immunomodulatory peptides, anti-inflammatory carotenoids, 
and antioxidant compounds. These properties could help counter spaceflight-related 
immune dysfunction, bone loss, inflammation, and oxidative stress [ 72]. Thus, 
microalgae offer a multifunctional food platform for long-duration BLSS missions.
4.4 Biofuel and bioproduct generation
Beyond air revitalization and food production, microalgae are valuable platforms 
for producing renewable fuels and high-value materials, supporting sustainable long- 
duration missions and space settlements [16, 20]. Under stress conditions such as 
nitrogen limitation, salinity changes, or high light, many species accumulate lipids or 
carbohydrates that can be converted into biodiesel, bioethanol, and other 
biofuels [73]. These fuels could support surface vehicles, backup power systems, or 
habitat heating, reducing dependence on Earth-supplied resources [74]. Some species, 
including Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, can also produce biohydrogen under anaerobic 
or sulfur-limited conditions, offering a clean energy option for in situ use [75].
Algal biotechnology also supports ISRU through material production. Biomass 
can be processed into biodegradable polymers such as polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB), 
which may be used in additive manufacturing or 3D printing of tools, spare parts, 
and packaging materials [76]. This local production model can reduce payload mass, 
launch costs, and dependence on resupply missions [77]. In addition, engineered 
microalgae can produce valuable compounds such as antiviral agents, antibiotics, 
immunomodulatory peptides, enzymes, and diagnostic reagents using light, CO 2, 
and recycled nutrients as inputs [40, 50]. Together, these capabilities transform algal 
systems from simple life-support units into multifunctional platforms for energy 
generation, manufacturing, healthcare support, and scientific operations, making 
them central to self-sufficient space habitats.5.

passive fluid transport [79]. In microgravity, this allows uniform nutrient supply and

Light management is also critical because sunlight may be interrupted by orbital

pulsed-light strategies can improve energy conversion compared with continuous 
lighting [82, 83]. Optical fibers, reflective surfaces, and light guides can further 
improve internal light distribution in dense cultures.

reduce light penetration and mass transfer [84]. Automated cleaning systems using 
scraping, fluid jets, or compatible chemical rinses may help control fouling [85]. Gas 
management is equally important. Membrane-based PBRs using gas-permeable 
materials such as silicone or fluoropolymers allow efficient CO 2 delivery and O2 
removal by diffusion, avoiding foaming and gas separation problems common with 
bubble aeration in microgravity [86].

5.2 Biomaterials for ISRU
Microalgal biomass is a versatile feedstock for ISRU, offering a renewable 
alternative to Earth-supplied construction and consumable materials in space 
habitats [9]. Its main biopolymers, including starch, cellulose, alginate, and protein- 
rich fractions, can be processed into films, coatings, and biodegradable products 
[87]. Some algal taxa also produce hydrocolloids such as alginate and carrageenan, 
which can be molded or cross-linked into materials with tunable mechanical 
properties [13]. Material properties such as strength, flexibility, permeability, and 
degradation rate can be adjusted through processing conditions, enabling 
application-specific design for space habitats [88]. Further functionality is achieved 
through hybrid biocomposites that combine algal-derived polymers with in situ 
materials such as lunar or Martian regolith [89].
In these systems, algal extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) act as natural 
binders, and when combined with fungal or microbial networks, can form 
biologically based cement-like materials [90]. These composites can be 
manufactured using casting, extrusion, or 3D printing, producing structural 
elements with reduced mass and acceptable mechanical strength for habitat 
construction [91]. Because regolith can comprise most of the material volume, 
launch mass requirements are significantly reduced [92]. Beyond structural use, 
these biomaterials can also provide thermal insulation, water retention, and 
potentially self-healing properties when biologically active components are included 
[93, 94]. Overall, microalgal biomaterials support low-mass, sustainable, and 
adaptable construction strategies for future extraterrestrial habitats.
5.3 Radiation-shielding applications
Radiation is a major hazard for astronauts on the Moon, Mars, and deep-space 
missions because these environments lack strong atmospheric or magnetic 
protection from solar particle events (SPEs) and galactic cosmic rays (GCRs) 
[95, 96]. Microalgae produce photoprotective pigments such as scytonemin and 
mycosporine-like amino acids (MAAs), which strongly absorb UV radiation and 
possess antioxidant properties. These compounds can reduce ROS and help limit 
radiation-induced cellular damage [97]. As renewable biological products, they offer 
promising protection for both crew health and habitat materials.
Algal pigments and biomass have also been proposed as components of shielding 
materials. Layered systems made from living cultures or processed algal biomass may 
provide partial radiation attenuation while simultaneously supporting oxygen 
production, CO2 capture, and nutrient generation [59, 98]. The high water content and 
the abundance of light elements such as hydrogen, carbon, and oxygen in algal biomass 
can help slow and scatter high-energy charged particles, potentially offering a mass- 
efficient alternative to traditional shielding materials like aluminum [99]. This 
approach reflects a multifunctional habitat design, where biomass generated from food Microalgae

5.4 Challenges and constraints
5.4.1 Microgravity effects
Microgravity strongly influences microalgal cultivation by altering fluid behavior 
and mass transfer. Without buoyancy-driven convection, nutrient and gas 
movement depends mainly on molecular diffusion, which is slower and less efficient 
in PBRs [99, 100]. This can create localized nutrient depletion around cells and 
oxygen accumulation within cultures. High dissolved oxygen levels may cause 
photo-oxidative stress, inhibit photosystem II, and reduce photosynthetic efficiency, 
lowering biomass productivity [101]. Dead cells and suspended particles can also 
block or scatter light, limiting photon penetration in dense cultures.
Mechanical mixing can improve transport, but it may introduce shear stress from 
pumps or impellers that damages sensitive cells, membranes, or flagella depending on 
species' tolerance [102]. Therefore, reactor operation must balance improved mixing 
with cell integrity and long-term stability. Microgravity can also affect cell morphology 
and intracellular organization. Changes in chloroplast positioning, cytoplasmic 
streaming, and organelle orientation may reduce light-harvesting and carbon fixation 
[103]. These effects are often accompanied by shifts in gene expression, with more 
energy allocated to stress defense and repair rather than growth [104].
To address these challenges, specialized PBRs use capillary-driven flow, surface- 
tension effects, and microfluidic designs to improve nutrient and gas distribution 
under low-gravity [80, 105]. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) is also valuable 
for optimizing reactor geometry, identifying stagnant zones, and reducing energy 
demand [106]. With appropriate engineering and strain selection, productive and 
stable microalgal systems can be developed for BLSS and space biomanufacturing 
during long-duration missions.
5.4.2 Cosmic radiation
Microalgae cultivated during long-duration missions are continuously exposed to 
GCR and SPEs, both of which can threaten cell survival and culture stability [ 16]. 
High-energy radiation can cause DNA damage, especially double-strand breaks, and 
generate ROS that harm proteins, lipids, and cell membranes [ 107]. Long-term 
exposure may increase mutation rates, genetic drift, and epigenetic changes, 
potentially reducing biomass productivity, altering nutritional quality, or triggering 
unwanted metabolite production. Protection requires combined biological and 
engineering strategies. Synthetic biology can improve radiation tolerance by 
enhancing DNA repair systems, antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase 
and catalase, and protective pigments that absorb UV or neutralize ROS [ 25, 30].
Physical shielding is also important. Water-rich PBRs can help attenuate charged 
particles, while dense algal biomass layers may provide additional passive protection 
[36, 108]. Natural pigments such as scytonemin and MAAs further support 
radioprotection through UV absorption and antioxidant activity [109]. As illustrated

Operational monitoring is equally critical. High-throughput sequencing, 
transcriptomics, metabolomics, and radiation dosimetry can detect harmful 
mutations or metabolic shifts early [40, 50]. Combined with adaptive control of 
light, nutrients, and reactor conditions, these strategies can maintain stable 
productivity under chronic radiation exposure. Together, strain engineering, 
shielding, and real-time management can help ensure reliable microalgal life-support 
performance in lunar, Martian, and deep-space habitats.
5.4.3 Resource limitations
Space-based microalgal cultivation is strongly limited by available mass, volume, 
water, and energy. These constraints are commonly evaluated using ESM, which 
combines mass, power, volume, cooling needs, and crew time into one mission- 
planning metric [16, 68]. Energy demand is one of the greatest challenges because 
lighting, temperature control, fluid circulation, and gas exchange consume 
significant power in PBR systems [110]. In space, waste heat is difficult to remove 
because convective cooling is absent. Therefore, efficient solutions such as pulsed

exchange, and nutrient transport under microgravity [36]. Overall, sustainable 
microalgal BLSS will require integrated systems engineering that combines biological 
optimization, recycling efficiency, real-time control, and resource management to 
reduce dependence on Earth during long-duration missions.
5.4.4 Technical and biosafety issues
Maintaining contamination-free microalgal cultures in closed space habitats is 
a major challenge because nutrient-rich media can promote bacterial, fungal, viral, 
or phage growth that may destabilize cultures [115]. In BLSS, culture failure could 
disrupt oxygen production, CO2 removal, nutrient recycling, and food supply, while 
recovery is limited by restricted crew time, energy, and consumables [ 36, 59]. 
Risk mitigation requires multilayered biosafety strategies, including sterilization, 
aseptic handling, and continuous microbial monitoring [116]. Real-time tools such as 
qPCR, flow cytometry, fluorescence assays, and sequencing can detect 
contamination early and enable rapid intervention [117].
Long-term cultivation also raises concerns over genetic drift and phenotypic 
instability caused by radiation, microgravity, and closed-system selection pressures, 
potentially reducing productivity or altering biomass quality [118]. 
Countermeasures include periodic reinoculation from cryopreserved master stocks, 
genetic containment systems, and continuous monitoring for harmful mutations or 
metabolic shifts [119]. Additional risks such as phage persistence, horizontal gene 
transfer, and cross-contamination highlight the need for reactor redundancy and 
operational isolation [120]. Overall, robust biosafety management is essential to 
ensure reliable microalgal cultivation and long-term BLSS stability in space.6.
AI, automation, and modeling for space algal systems6.1
Predictive metabolic models
Predictive metabolic modeling is a key tool for optimizing microalgae under space 
conditions. Methods such as flux balance analysis (FBA) and flux variability analysis 
(FVA) simulate cellular mass and energy flows to predict growth and production 
of useful compounds such as lipids, astaxanthin, and phycocyanin [ 121, 122]. These 
models support strain selection and metabolic engineering by estimating Ecosystems 
and Biodiversity Annual Volume 202614
productivity, 
nutrient efficiency, and metabolite yields under constraints such as 
limited CO2, radiation, and microgravity-related mass transfer issues [123, 124]. At 
the cellular level, FBA helps identify bottlenecks in carbon fixation, redox balance, 
and nitrogen assimilation under high-light or high-CO2 cultivation conditions 
[25, 30, 121]. This information can guide synthetic biology strategies to redirect 
carbon toward lipids or antioxidant pigments, improve photoprotection, and reduce 
photorespiration, enabling high-performance strains for food, life-support, and 
biomanufacturing purposes. These models also improve cultivation strategies by 
optimizing batch, fed-batch, and continuous systems while minimizing 
nutrient inputs and maintaining gas-exchange efficiency [125]. When combined 
with real-time sensor data such as dissolved oxygen, CO 2 flux, nutrient levels, and 
biomass density, models can be continuously updated for adaptive control of PBR 
[126]. Overall, predictive metabolic modeling shifts algal cultivation from trial-and- 
error methods to intelligent, data-driven system design, supporting autonomous 
and resource-efficient BLSS for future long-duration space missions.
6.2 Digital twin systems for BLSS
Digital twin technology creates a high-fidelity virtual model of a BLSS that 
mirrors real algal cultivation systems in real time. By combining sensor data – such as 
pH, temperature, optical density, dissolved O2/CO2, and nutrient levels – with 
models including FBA, CFD, and growth kinetics, the system maintains an updated 
representation of reactor conditions [127–129]. This allows prediction of future 
culture states and early warning of issues such as oxygen oversaturation, CO 2 
limitation, or nutrient depletion. Digital twins also improve anomaly detection by 
comparing expected and observed behavior. Deviations may indicate contamination, 
sensor drift, biofouling, gas-transfer problems, or hardware faults, enhancing 
biosafety and operational reliability where crew intervention is limited [ 130, 131].
In addition, digital twins enable virtual optimization and scenario testing. 
Algorithms such as genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, and reinforcement 
learning can optimize lighting spectra, photoperiods, mixing intensity, nutrient 
dosing, and harvest timing before changes are applied to the real system [ 132]. This 
reduces experimentation, saves energy and consumables, and lowers crew 
workload. Overall, digital twins transform BLSS from reactive systems into 
autonomous, self-regulating ecosystems capable of predictive maintenance, adaptive 
control, and stable oxygen generation, nutrient recycling, and biomass production 
for long-duration space missions.
6.3 Robotic maintenance
Automation and robotics are vital for maintaining long-term microalgal cultures 
in space while reducing crew workload and contamination risk [ 133]. Robotic 
systems integrated with PBRs can perform repetitive, high-precision tasks that are 
difficult or inefficient for astronauts [5]. These include automated harvesting and 
biomass separation through centrifugation or membrane filtration, allowing efficient 
biomass recovery while preserving optimal culture density. Robots can also handle 
cleaning and sterilization using UV treatment, ultrasonic cleaning, or controlled 
chemical wash cycles. These methods reduce biofouling, maintain light penetration, 
and preserve efficient heat and mass transfer, which is especially important in 
microgravity where manual cleaning is difficult [134].Microalgae

Advanced robotic units equipped with manipulators, machine vision, and diagnostic

systems. When combined with artificial intelligence (AI), real-time sensing, and 
digital twins, robotics enables highly autonomous and resilient BLSS for future 
missions to the Moon, Mars, and deep space.
6.4 Machine learning in PBR optimization
Machine learning (ML) is a powerful tool for optimizing PBR operation by 
handling the complex, nonlinear interactions that control microalgal growth [ 137]. 
Key variables such as light intensity and spectrum, temperature, pH, nutrient 
supply, hydrodynamic shear, and gas transfer interact in ways that often exceed the 
capabilities of conventional rule-based or PID controllers [138, 139]. The ML 
methods, especially artificial neural networks (ANNs) and deep learning models, can 
analyze large sensor datasets to identify patterns and predict culture performance 
[140]. A major application is dynamic light optimization, where ML models 
continuously adjust light intensity, photoperiod, and spectral quality to maximize 
photosynthesis while minimizing energy use [141]. This is especially valuable in 
space habitats, where lighting is a major contributor to ESM. Reinforcement learning 
can also optimize cultivation decisions over time under uncertain conditions while 
respecting safety constraints.
Machine learning further improves biosafety by enabling early contamination 
detection. Supervised and unsupervised models can recognize subtle changes in pH, 
dissolved oxygen, nutrient uptake, fluorescence, or cell morphology associated with 
bacterial, fungal, or phage invasion before culture collapse occurs [ 142]. ML can 
also distinguish true biological problems from sensor drift or equipment faults, 
reducing false alarms and unnecessary crew intervention. When integrated with 
digital twins, metabolic models, and robotic automation, ML enables adaptive and 
autonomous PBR management with stable biomass production, efficient nutrient 
recycling, lower energy demand, and rapid response to disturbances. These AI- 
driven systems are expected to be essential for reliable BLSS operation on lunar, 
Martian, and deep-space missions.7.
Future prospects and applications7.1
Terraforming possibilities
Cyanobacteria and other phototrophic microorganisms may contribute to future 
planetary bioengineering or early terraforming strategies [15]. A natural precedent is 
Earth’s Great Oxygenation Event, when ancestral cyanobacteria transformed the 
atmosphere through oxygenic photosynthesis over geological timescales [143]. 
Because some cyanobacteria tolerate low pressure, intense UV radiation, desiccation, 
and temperature extremes, they are considered potential candidates for early 
biological deployment on Mars or other worlds. In practice, initial use would likely Ecosystems 
and Biodiversity Annual Volume 202616
occur 
inside localized, pressurized, or semi-contained habitats rather than on open 
planetary surfaces. These controlled systems could optimize light, temperature, 
humidity, and nutrients to maximize photosynthesis and oxygen generation while 
protecting cells from harsh environmental conditions. Over long periods, such 
systems might gradually influence local atmospheres and serve as testbeds for 
planetary bioengineering concepts [144].
Microorganisms may also assist regolith transformation into biologically useful 
proto-soils. Cyanobacteria and microbial consortia release organic acids, 
siderophores, and chelating compounds that mobilize minerals such as phosphorus, 
potassium, magnesium, iron, and calcium [145]. Studies with Martian regolith 
simulants show that cyanobacteria can grow on these materials while enriching them 
with organic matter and bioavailable nutrients, helping prepare substrates for future 
plants or microbial communities [146]. Although planet-wide terraforming 
remains highly speculative and far beyond current capabilities, localized microbial 
systems could support oxygen production, nutrient cycling, and soil development. 
These approaches may help establish self-sustaining biospheres and reduce 
dependence on Earth resupply for long-term human settlement beyond Earth.
7.2 In situ resource utilization
Microalgae can become a key component of ISRU by helping produce and recycle 
essential resources directly on the Moon, Mars, or other planetary bodies [98]. Algal 
systems can be linked with local water extraction technologies that use subsurface 
ice or hydrated minerals, while also treating greywater and human waste through 
nutrient uptake and microbial remediation. This process can generate reusable water 
for crop production and life-support systems, reducing dependence on Earth- 
supplied water [1, 9, 147]. Microalgae may also support bioleaching, a low-energy 
method for recovering useful metals and minerals from planetary regolith [ 148]. In 
algal–bacterial consortia, microorganisms can solubilize elements such as iron, 
aluminum, magnesium, titanium, and rare-earth elements from lunar or Martian 
materials [149]. These recovered resources could be used for construction, radiation- 
shielding materials, 3D printing, electronics, and replacement parts.
When combined with nutrient recycling and carbon fixation, algal ISRU systems 
convert regolith into a productive resource base. Habitat CO2 can be fixed into 
biomass, nitrogen and phosphorus can be recovered from wastes, and minerals can be 
mobilized from local substrates [10]. Together, these functions create an integrated 
closed-loop bioproduction platform. By embedding microalgae into ISRU strategies, 
future missions could lower resupply needs, reduce ESM, and increase operational 
autonomy. Such biologically driven systems offer sustainable pathways toward long- 
duration habitation and eventually self-sufficient extraterrestrial settlements.

ConclusionsMicroalgae 
and cyanobacteria have emerged as key organisms for sustainable 
space exploration because they can simultaneously support air regeneration, waste 
recycling, food production, and bioproduct synthesis. Advances in PBR design, 
AI-based control systems, and engineered resilient strains have improved their 
readiness for integration into BLSS. Results from historical programs, ISS 
experiments, and omics studies confirm that these microorganisms can function 
under space-relevant conditions and may play major roles in long-duration missions 
and extraterrestrial settlements. However, important challenges remain. 
Microgravity and cosmic radiation create complex physiological stresses that are still 
not fully understood, especially during long missions. Uncertainty also remains 
regarding reactor scalability, large-volume cultivation, contamination control, and 
long-term ecological stability. In addition, integrated multi-species systems, closed- 
loop nutrient cycling, and ISRU concepts require further validation under 
autonomous operating conditions. To overcome these limitations, future research 
should prioritize long-duration, fully closed-loop BLSS experiments in orbit and in 
planetary analog habitats. These studies should evaluate not only algal 
productivity but also ecological interactions, genetic stability, and whole-system 
resilience over mission-relevant timescales. Integration of omics monitoring, AI- 
assisted modeling, digital twins, and robotic maintenance will further improve 
understanding, automation, and resource efficiency. Research should also focus on 
ultra-resilient engineered strains able to tolerate radiation, UV stress, desiccation, 
and temperature extremes while maintaining high productivity. At the same time, 
development of space biorefineries capable of producing food, fuels, medicines, and 
biomaterials from algal biomass will greatly enhance mission autonomy. Overall, 
with continued interdisciplinary research and system-scale validation, microalgae- 
based biotechnology can progress from experimental demonstrations to operational 
deployment, becoming a fundamental component of human life-support 
infrastructure beyond Earth.Acknowledgments
