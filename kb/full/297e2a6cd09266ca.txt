Abstract Accurate measurement of cellulose crys-
tallinity is essential for understanding fiber develop-
ment, optimizing processing conditions, and improv -
ing material performance. However, the structural 
complexity of cellulose fibers presents significant 
challenges in determining crystallinity, or more 
precisely, the ratio of two discrete crystalline and 
amorphous model phases. As a result, many stud-
ies rely on empirical methods to estimate a crystal-
linity index (CrI). This study presents a cellulose-
specific workflow for quantifying crystallinity from 
one-dimensional X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) 
data using Rietveld refinement implemented in the 
open-source software MAUD. The step-by-step pro-
cedure—demonstrated using cotton fiber and tailored 
for non-specialists—guides users through refinement 
of ten key parameters, including a scale factor, back -
ground, unit cell geometry, crystallite size, preferred 
orientation, and phase composition, in four sequential 
refinement steps. The final refinement yielded strong 
agreement between calculated and observed diffrac-
tion patterns and demonstrated the utility of one-
dimensional full-pattern analysis in a workflow to 
quantify cellulose crystallinity.

Introduction
Cellulose fibers, in both naturally occurring and 
regenerated forms, play essential roles in industries 
ranging from textiles to composites and bio-based 
materials (Ramamoorthy et  al. 2015; Wang et  al. 
2021). Their performance characteristics, such as 
Cellulose 
Vol.: (0123456789)
strength, flexibility, chemical reactivity, and moisture 
management, are closely tied to their structural organ-
ization at the molecular level (Ward Jr 1950). Cellu-
lose is commonly described as a semi-crystalline pol-
ymer composed of crystalline and amorphous regions 
(Wakelyn et al. 2006). In crystalline domains, cellu-
lose chains are tightly packed and aligned in parallel, 
whereas in amorphous regions, they are disordered. 
Molecular modeling studies, on the other hand, sug-
gest that the nanoscale organization of cellulose may 
also include intermediate, partially ordered regions 
that are not cleanly classified as either fully crystal-
line or fully amorphous (Haigler et al. 2014; Kulasin-
ski et  al. 2014). These paracrystalline regions were 
found to exhibit intermediate mechanical properties 
between crystalline and amorphous phases (Kulasin-
ski et  al. 2014). From a practical viewpoint, a two-
phase model is possibly the closest approximation 
to the real situation that can be obtained. Fink et al. 
(1995) showed for example that existing lattice strains 
have minimal effect on crystallite size measurements. 
Nishiyama et  al. (2012) showed that larger strains 
resulting from molecular dynamics-based distortion 
had small effects on calculated diffraction patterns.
During biosynthesis in the developing fiber, cel-
lulose crystallizes predominantly in cellulose I allo-
morph (Fang and Percy 2020), though it can be 
converted into cellulose II, III, or IV through post-
treatment processes, such as mercerization or ther -
mal/chemical modification (Colom and Carrillo 2002; 
Szabó et al. 2023). Crystallite size, which varies with 
fiber type, development stage, and treatment history, 
influences the specific surface area of cellulose crys-
tallites, thereby affecting their chemical reactivity 
and interaction with other molecules. Meanwhile, the 
amorphous phase contributes to diffusion properties 
and flexibility, often serving as a target for chemical 
and enzymatic modifications (Emenike et  al. 2023). 
Because the balance between these two phases gov -
erns many of the fiber’s functional properties, the 
degree of crystallization, or crystallinity, is an impor -
tant metric in cellulose fiber science, widely used to 
evaluate fiber development, processing conditions, 
and application performance.
The crystallinity of cellulose can be assessed using 
diffraction techniques that employ sub-nanometer 
wavelength radiation, such as X-rays, neutrons, or 
electrons in electron microscopy. Among these, neu-
tron and electron-based techniques are less commonly 
employed: neutron sources are typically limited to 
national laboratories, and electron beams can rapidly 
degrade cellulose samples. In contrast, X-ray diffrac-
tion (XRD) using powder diffractometers is widely 
accessible and offers a relatively low-cost, non-
destructive approach for examining cellulose crystal-
linity. While diffraction remains the primary method 
for evaluating crystallinity, alternative techniques 
such as infrared (IR) and nuclear magnetic resonance 
(NMR) spectroscopy are frequently employed to dis-
tinguish between cellulose polymorphs (Lee et  al. 
2016). However, spectroscopic methods do not detect 
the long-range atomic order required for diffraction-
based analysis. Instead, they imply crystallinity and 
allomorph type from the orientations or interactions 
of specific functional groups associated with a given 
crystalline form of cellulose.
Most traditional XRD studies on cellulosic materi-
als have focused on determining a crystallinity index 
(CrI), which estimates the relative proportion of 
crystalline cellulose, with the remainder considered 
amorphous. Among these empirical approaches, the 
method of Segal et al. (1959), which is based on peak 
height measurements, is the most widely used due to 
its simplicity. However, the foundation of this method 
is flawed: it presumes that the intensity minimum near 
18° 2θ in a cellulose Iβ pattern (recorded using CuKα 
radiation) is the result of amorphous cellulose. Later 
studies on the shapes of diffraction peaks revealed that 
much of the intensity in this region is the result of the 
overlap of large adjacent peaks, together with contri-
butions from smaller peaks (French and Santiago Cin-
trón 2013; Nam et al. 2016). This effect can be readily 
demonstrated by simulating diffraction patterns of cel-
lulose Iβ from a crystal information file (cif), which 
contains published atomic coordinates of its asym-
metric unit (Nishiyama et  al. 2002; French and San-
tiago Cintrón 2013) (Fig. 1A). Varying the full width 
at half maximum (FWHM) in the simulations high-
lights the extent of peak overlap (Fig.  1B). The Segal 
method fails to account for the full diffraction pattern 
and important factors such as crystallite orientation 
and crystallite shape variations, leading to inaccurate 
assessments of cellulose crystallinity (French 2022).
In the present study, we introduce a method to 
quantify crystallinity through Rietveld refinement 
with modifications specifically adapted for cellulose 
materials. Rietveld refinement is a full-pattern fitting 
approach that calculates XRD diffraction intensities 
 Cellulose
Vol:. (1234567890)
from known crystal structures and iteratively adjusts 
structural and microstructural parameters of the 
known model structure to minimize the difference 
between the calculated and measured diffraction pat-
terns. The method enables extraction of structural 
information from overlapping peaks, incorporates 
physically meaningful parameters, and can model 
complex diffraction behavior. For cellulose materi-
als, however, several intrinsic features complicate 
the full application of conventional Rietveld analysis. 
Cellulose exhibits small crystallite sizes, extensive 
peak overlap, and a substantial amorphous contri-
bution, and its unit cell dimensions, a, b, and γ vary 
considerably. Under such conditions, the effective-
ness of refinement becomes influenced by how the 
amorphous scattering is represented (Salem et  al. 
2023). In addition, some standard Rietveld imple-
mentations do not fully accommodate characteristic 
cellulose features, such as its needle-like morphology 
with an elongated c-axis along the molecular chain 
and shorter transverse dimensions.
The method introduced here is based on a two-
phase representation of cellulose consisting of crystal-
line and amorphous cellulose components. To model 
this amorphous contribution, we prepared amorphous 
cellulose through mechanical treatment by high-
impact ball-milling. The diffraction pattern from the 
ball-milled sample was then fitted with a broad-peak 
version of the cellulose II calculated structure. Cel-
lulose samples from different sources and treatment 
histories will have different unit cell dimensions. 
We chose an FWHM of 9° corresponding to a cellu-
lose II crystallite size of 12  Å as a representative of 
amorphous material. This pattern was a better fit than 
the wide peak versions of cellulose I. Apparently, the 
action of the impact ball mill destroyed the particu-
lar O2-H⋅⋅⋅O6 inter-residue intramolecular hydrogen 
bonds, allowing the O6 atoms to take the gt O6 ori-
entation that is characteristic of cellulose II and amor-
phous cellulose. Another point mentioned in Plaza 
et al. (2026) is that if the isotropic crystallite size of 
the amorphous contribution is refined, it can exceed 
the limits for amorphous material. If the crystallite 
size exceeds 20  Å, the sample may be considered to 
be a mixture that includes crystalline cellulose II. If 
the refined crystallite size is less than about 8 Å, the 
resulting calculated intensity is just compensating for 
a deficit in the background correction. In this work, 
the crystallite size is kept fixed at 12 Å. Therefore the 
procedure makes use of the usual Rietveld provisions 
to allow flexible unit cell dimensions. The approach 
also incorporates accounting for preferred orienta-
tion—a departure from random orientation that is 
assumed in powder diffraction but extremely difficult 
to avoid in cellulose samples.
Here we present a step-by-step protocol for a cel-
lulose-specific Rietveld refinement workflow using 
the freely available MAUD (Materials Analysis Using 
Diffraction) software. The procedure is demonstrated 
using cotton fibers as a representative example. This 
refinement approach has also been used effectively 
with other cellulosic materials including cellulose 
nanofibers (Easson et  al. 2024; Nam et  al. 2024a, 
b). The protocol outlines practical guidance for

MAUD Rietveld software has many power -
ful capabilities. One of its strengths is provision for 
studying texture, the degree of preferred orientation. 
Rietveld software in general, including MAUD, also 
can take the place of a classical single-crystal dif-
fraction study for determining the atomic coordinates 
of powdered samples, especially minerals of simple 
composition but unable to form crystals large enough 
for neutron diffraction. However, it is absolutely inap-
propriate to utilize any Rietveld software to attempt 
to carry out such a structure determination for cellu-
lose structure with powder diffraction data, as there is 
simply not enough data to support determination of 
so many variables.
Materials and methods
Materials
Cotton fibers were obtained from cotton plants 
grown at the Southern Regional Research Center, 
New Orleans, LA. Methyl methacrylate, butyl meth-
acrylate, benzoyl peroxide, and methyl ethyl ketone 
were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, 
USA) and used as received.
Sample preparation
To prepare samples for XRD measurement, har -
vested cotton fibers were mechanically ground using 
a sharp Wiley cutting mill (Arthur H. Thomas Co., 
Philadelphia, PA) fitted with a 40-mesh screen. The 
amorphous cellulose was prepared by further pro-
cessing the Wiley-milled powder (approximately 
5 g) in a custom-built vibratory ball mill operating at 
1,750 rpm, following established procedures (Forziati 
et al. 1950). The milling was performed in a cylindri-
cal steel jar of 1-L capacity, filled with approximately 
500  mL of stainless-steel balls (6.35  mm diameter) 
as the grinding medium, and the ball-milling dura-
tion was 2h (Nam et  al. 2020). Limitations include 
experimental handling of the background data as 
both a blank run and a secondary input data file in 
what Morales et  al. (2026) call combined analysis. 
The default parameters were used in MAUD with-
out specification of factors that vary from the stand-
ard setup such as variable slits instead of fixed slits 
and goniometer radius. Mentioned previously were 
the Caglioti parameters that were left in the default 
state. Advanced users should take these factors into 
consideration.
For cross-section preparation, the ground fibers 
were embedded in a mixture of methyl methacrylate, 
butyl methacrylate and benzoyl peroxide and polym-
erized using a UV cross-linker (UVP CL-1000, Ana-
lytik Jena) following published methods (Thibodeaux 
and Evans 1986; Boylston et  al. 1991). The polym-
erized blocks were sectioned into slices of approxi-
mately 300  nm thickness using a microtome (Pow -
erTome Ultramicrotome, Boeckeler Instruments). 
The sections were transferred to coverslips, and the 
embedding medium was removed with methyl ethyl 
ketone.
Image analysis
Optical microscopic images were captured in trans-
mission mode using a digital microscope (KH-8700, 
Hirox, Oradell, NJ, USA). Scanning electron micros-
copy (SEM) images were obtained using a Phenom 
G6 ProX SEM (Nanoscience Instruments, Phoenix, 
AZ, USA) operated at an accelerating voltage of 10 
kV. Prior to SEM imaging, samples were sputter-
coated with a 5 nm-thick gold layer using a LUXOR 
sputter coater (Aptco Technologies, Nazareth, 
Belgium).

using an Empyrean diffractometer (Malvern Panalyti-

wavelength = 1.5418  Å) and a nickel filter to reduce

Software
The open-source MAUD software (Materials Analy -
sis Using Diffraction, version 2.9999) was obtained 
from its official GitHub repository and installed on 
an HP desktop computer running Microsoft Windows 
11. MAUD is a Java-based program originally devel-
oped for macOS and is also compatible with other 
operating systems, including Windows and UNIX/
Linux. Several example datasets and tutorials, includ-
ing those distributed with the MAUD installation and 
available online, demonstrate Rietveld refinement 
procedure. However, they provide limited discus-
sion of the practical issues encountered when ana-
lyzing weak or poorly resolved cellulose diffraction 
patterns and in quantifying its crystallinity. For this 
tutorial, no separate calibration of the instrument was 
performed. Instrumental calibration can be achieved 
by measuring a standard reference material, such as 
NIST LaB6 or silicon, under the same XRD condi-

parameters are then saved in an instrument file that is 
used for the samples instead of the default.par file”.

Results and discussion
Main inputs to MAUD
Load data file
The XRD pattern was collected from cotton fibers 
ground into powder using a Wiley mill. Segal et  al. 
(1959) and Nelson and Conrad (1948) reported mini-
mal decrystallization resulting from milling with this 
type of mill. Optical microscopy shows fiber fragment 
lengths ranging from approximately 100 μm to 1 mm 
with random orientation (Fig.  2A). The correspond-
ing SEM image reveals the characteristic ribbon-like 
morphology with convolution twists typical of mature 
cotton fibers (Fig.  2B). Cross-sectional SEM images 
(Figs.  2C and 2D) show the expected kidney bean-
like profile with average diameters of 21.8 ± 2.7  μm 
and 9.2 ± 3.2 μm along the longest and shortest axes, 
respectively. This micrometer-scale structure is built 
from a hierarchical assembly of elementary fibrils 
measuring roughly 3–5 nm in diameter (Wakelyn et al. 
2006). The crystalline signal detected by XRD origi-
nates from regions of cellulose that maintain long-
range structural order within these nanoscale fibrils. 
Due to the small fibril diameter, a relatively large frac-
tion of cellulose chains is located at the fibril surface, 
where their positional coherence is reduced compared 
to the fibril core because of increased conformational 
freedom. Consequently, these surface chains adopt a 
partially ordered, paracrystalline arrangement rather 
than a fully crystalline state, contributing to the dif-
fuse scattering (Ioelovich et  al. 2010, Martínez-Sanz 
et al. 2017).
The collected XRD pattern was saved as “cotton.
txt”, a plain text file (provided in the Supplementary 
Information) for import into MAUD as a dataset. 
MAUD provides tools for loading datasets, comput-
ing theoretical diffraction patterns, and performing 
Rietveld refinement via its user-friendly interface 
(Fig. S1; all such supplementary figures are located in 
the RefinementProcessGuide.pdf). Input files should 
be formatted as two columns: 2θ (angle) and intensity. 
It is recommended to restrict the 2θ range to 10–50°, 
which provides a practical balance between signal

Vol.: (0123456789)
intensity and fitting stability for low-crystallinity cel-
lulose samples. Low-angle regions are often domi-
nated by background, whereas high-angle regions 
tend to show weaker intensities and higher sensitiv -
ity to instrumental and structural model discrepancies 
(Young 1993; Pecharsky and Zavalij 2003). When 
raw intensity values exported from the diffractometer 
are numerically large, the data can be rescaled (e.g., 
divided by 100) to facilitate data handling within 
MAUD without altering relative intensities.
Once the dataset is loaded, the diffraction pattern 
appears in MAUD’s main window. Current versions 
of MAUD also support direct import of Panalytical 
XRDML files and similar formats from Bruker and 
Rigaku instruments. To load data, select the “Data-
sets” tab and choose “Load datafile” from the File 
menu. For visualizing the pattern, users can access 
Plot options from the Graphic menu and select the 
“Linear” display mode. By default, MAUD displays 
diffraction patterns using square root intensity scaling 
to enhance the visibility of weak peaks. This visu-
alization helps users identify minor reflections that 
might otherwise be obscured by high-intensity peaks. 
Selecting “Linear” displays the raw intensities, which 
is useful for quantitative comparisons or absolute 
intensity analysis.

Manual input
Manual input in MAUD enables users to adjust 
model parameters before running a refinement. To 
adjust the scale factor, locate the “DataFileSet_x” in 
the lower region of the MAUD interface. Expand-
ing this node reveals sections including “Diffraction 
Instrument.” Within this section, locate the parameter 
“pd_proc_intensity_incident”, which sets the initial 
scaling of the calculated diffraction pattern relative 
to the measured data. Adjusting this value (to 2000 
for the example file cotton.txt with MAUD 2.9999 or 
1000 with MAUD 2.99993) brings the calculated pat-
tern into view for an initial comparison after clicking 
the calculator button. The cotton.txt file features quite 
high intensity values, with the consequent lack of vis-
ible noise in the data. On a practical note, such high 
counts are practical for diffractometers with "strip" 
or "diode array" detectors. Other data will require 
smaller or even larger values. There is no need for an 
exact fit because the next step makes a big change. 
The optimal value depends on the sample’s diffrac-
tion intensity. Once the change is made, click the cal-
culator icon to generate an initial comparison of the 
observed and calculated diffraction patterns shown in 
Fig. 3. Alternatively, the pattern can be computed by 
selecting “Compute spectra” from the Analysis menu.

to the observed pattern, two clear discrepancies are 
apparent. First, it is notable that the main observed

the calculated peaks are considerably narrower, sim-

First refinement step: linear background, scale factor, 
a-axis, and crystallite size
The first refinement step targets five parameters, 
including linear background, scale factor, a -axis, and 
crystallite size, to establish a stable starting point for 
subsequent steps (Fig. S4). The background is the con-
tinuous baseline intensity arising from non-sample, 
environmental sources such as instrument, air, and 
sample holder scattering. The scale factor adjusts the 
overall intensity of the calculated diffraction pattern to 
match the observed pattern. Refinement of the a -axis 
of the cellulose Iβ unit cell adjusts peak positions, 
while the crystallite size determines peak broadening. 
This sequence stabilizes the refinement and reduces 
parameter correlations, facilitating reliable adjust-
ments in later steps. To incorporate the linear back -
ground function, locate the parameters, “_riet_par_
background_pol0,” and “_riet_par_background_pol1” 
under the “DataFileSet_x,” corresponding to the zero- 
and first-order polynomial terms, respectively. Change 
their statuses from “Fixed” to “Refined.” For the scale 
factor, expand the “Diffraction Instrument” under the 
“DataFileSet_x” and change the status of “pd_proc_
intensity_incident” from “Fixed” to “Refined” with 
its value set to 1000. To refine the a-axis of cellulose 
Iβ, expand the “CelluloseI” and change the status 
of “_cell_length_a” from “Fixed” to “Refined.” For 
the crystallite size refinement, expand the “Isotropic” 
under the “CelluloseI” and change the status of “_riet_
par_cryst_size” from “Fixed” to “Refined” with its 
value set to 80. After these changes, press the “Launch 
parameters refinement” button to start the refinement.
To visualize the background, open the “Graphic” 
menu, select “Plot options”, and check the box labeled 
“Plot Background”. This displays the background line 
on the diffraction pattern. Finally, execute the refine-
ment by clicking the “Launch parameters refinement” 
button (represented by a slot machine or a hammer 
icon) on the toolbar. It may be necessary to click the 
"hammer" or "slot machine" button more than once to 
fully converge the refinement. The process should con-
verge within a few seconds. Figure 4 shows the diffrac-
tion pattern after the first refinement step. Overall, the 
calculated pattern closely matches the observed pattern, 
indicating the significance of the parameters chosen 
for refinement in the first refinement step. Some misfit 
remains, however, near the 110 , 110, and 004 reflections 
at approximately 14.7, 16.6, and 34.8° 2θ, respectively.

Second refinement step: quadratic background, 
b-axis, and γ-angle
In a conventional powder XRD 2θ scan, the environ-
mental background typically appears as a smoothly 
varying curve that is elevated at low angles and grad-
ually decreases with increasing 2θ. The noticeable 
misfit near the 110 and 110 reflections after the first 
refinement step indicates the need to refine the b-axis 
and γ-angle, which affect their spacing and relative 
intensities (Fernandes et al. 2011, Altaner et al. 2014). 
Accordingly, in the second refinement step, the quad-
ratic background, b-axis, γ-angle were added (Fig. 
S6). The c-axis was left fixed as it represents the chain 
repeat direction and is less influenced by microstruc-
tural changes. To refine the quadratic background, 
locate the parameter, “_riet_par_background_pol2” 
under the “DataFileSet_x,” which represents the 
second-order polynomial term, and change its status 
from “Fixed” to “Refined.” To refine the b-axis and 
γ-angle of cellulose Iβ, expand the “CelluloseI” and 
change the statuses of “_cell_length_b” and “_cell_
angle_gamma” from “Fixed” to “Refined.” After 
these changes, press the “Launch parameters refine-
ment” button to start the refinement. After the second 
refinement step, the statistical indicators improved: 
sig decreased from 7.05 to 6.54, Rwp from 6.47 to 
5.99, and Rb from 4.94 to 4.32. Figure  5 shows the 
diffraction pattern after the second refinement step. 
Consistent with these improvements, the fit near the

discrepancies persist near the 004 reflection, indicat-

Third refinement step: preferred orientation
The remaining misfit in the profile of the 004 reflec-
tion, which consists of several closely spaced sub-
peaks (Fig.  3), is typically influenced by the way 
fibrous samples settle on the sample holder so that 
many crystallites become aligned parallel to the 
sample surface. To account for these preferred ori-
entation effects, MAUD provides (among others) the 
March-Dollase function (Fig. S7). In MAUD, this is 
accessed by opening the “Phases” tab in the upper-
left window and selecting the “CelluloseI.” Next, 
click the “eye” icon labeled “Edit the special object 
of the visible list” located below the “Special” menu 
at the top. In the new window, go to the “Advanced 
models” tab, locate the “Texture:” dropdown menu, 
and select “March Dollase.” Then click “Options,” 
followed by “Add term” (plus sign) to enable the 
function. Confirm the changes by selecting OK in 
both dialog boxes. After this step, the lower portion 
of the MAUD interface will display “March-Dollase” 
under “CelluloseI.”

Fourth refinement step: phase composition
Based on the semi-crystalline description of cellulose, 
the amorphous volume fraction was incorporated in the 
fourth refinement step. The amorphous contribution 
was modeled using cellulose amorphized by vibratory 
ball milling. Under the established milling conditions, 
the process effectively removes crystalline structure 
and produces a reproducible amorphous scattering pro-
file (Ling et  al. 2019). The ball-milled cellulose pro-
vides an experimental approximation of the disordered 
signal from amorphous cellulose regions and therefore 
serves as a practical amorphous reference for quanti-
fying the crystalline contribution. The SEM image 
of the ball-milled material shows loss of the native 
fibrous morphology and the formation of aggregated 
particles (Fig.  7A). The corresponding XRD pattern 
exhibits a single broad halo centered around 21° 2θ, 
characteristic of amorphous cellulose (Fig.  7B). This 
distinctive scattering profile allows the amorphous 
contribution to be separated from the slowly varying 
background. To simulate this amorphous cellulose, 
the cellulose II crystal structure reported by Langan 
et  al. (2001), with unit cell parameters, a = 8.10  Å, 
b = 9.03  Å, c = 10.31  Å, and γ = 117.10° determined 
from synchrotron XRD of mercerized ramie cellulose 
II (Its.cif file is provided in the Supporting Informa-
tion). These values are consistent with those reported 
for cellulose II prepared by deacetylation of cellulose 
acetate (a = 8.076  Å, b = 9.144  Å, c = 10.386  Å, and 
γ= 117.100°). When the crystallite size of cellulose 
II was set to 12  Å, the calculated diffraction pattern 
closely matched the experimental diffraction of ball-
milled cotton (Nam et al. 2016). Morales et al. (2026) 
similarly used a small isotropic cellulose I as a model 
of amorphous. They mentioned that their amorphous 
model was used by Lutterotti (2012), aligned with that 
of Le Bail (1995). Our preference is to use the cellu-
lose II model because it better matches the experimen-
tal amorphous material (Fig. 7B).

Limitations and scope
Although this workflow provides a structured 
approach for quantifying cellulose crystallin-
ity using Rietveld refinement, several limitations 
should be considered. (a) Two-phase representation. 
The method models semi-crystalline cellulose as a 
two-component system consisting of crystalline and 
amorphous cellulose phases. This simplified repre-
sentation does not explicitly include paracrystalline 
domains. b) Choice of amorphous reference. The 
workflow assumes that the scattering profile of ball-
milled cellulose adequately represents the amorphous 
cellulose and does not include non-cellulosic amor -
phous components. (c) Background-amorphous sepa-
ration. The background signal is treated as a smooth 
function, whereas amorphous cellulose produces a 
broad, non-periodic halo. The separation of these dis-
tinct intensity profiles is not unique and depends on 
the characteristics of the background. (d) Material-
specific considerations. The protocol is optimized for 
typical cellulose-rich materials. Materials with mixed 
cellulose polymorphs, pronounced preferred orienta-
tion, significant non-cellulosic phases may require 
supplemental modeling. Further limitations are 
the lack of tailoring of the MAUD software for the 
explicit diffractometer. The crystal information file 
resulted in an Rw based on F 2 of 42% so there must 
be some error in the atomic positions in the model 
structure. Some indicators of the quality of the study 
have not been discussed, needed especially when 
more advanced variables are considered. The order 
of adding parameters has not been discussed beyond 
the selection of the first five. Perhaps the amorphous 
could be introduced third, instead of March-Dollase 
function. If the crystalline material has preferred 00 l 
orientation, then perhaps the amorphous material 
does also.
Conclusions
To overcome limitations in cellulose crystallinity 
measurement using traditional XRD methods and 
to encourage more comprehensive analysis of pow -
der diffraction data, we present a cellulose-specific 
workflow for quantifying true crystallinity of cellu-
lose using Rietveld refinement with the open-source 
MAUD software. The refinement process was struc-
tured into four sequential steps, each designed to 
account for cellulose-specific structural character -
istics. Ten key parameters—including scale factor, 
background function, unit cell geometry, crystallite 
size, preferred orientation, and phase composition—
were systematically refined to represent the two-
phase nature of cellulose fibers. The strong agreement

Designed for accessibility, this protocol lowers 
the barrier for researchers without prior experience

powder diffraction profile analysis. Future work 
could explore additional refinement procedures, such 
as introducing an additional March-Dollase term 
to account for more complex orientation distribu-
tions, refining anisotropic crystallite sizes to capture 
directional size effects, or expanding the amorphous 
model to include possible alignment contributions, 
and examining how these refinements influence crys-
tallinity measurements. Another potential direction 
is the application of this workflow to cellulose refer -
ence materials across different diffractometers and 
user groups to help establish more robust reference 
crystallinity values. A key outstanding question is 
whether the number of unique diffraction data points 
is sufficient to reliably support the number of refined 
variables. This challenge is particularly relevant 
for cellulose samples, where broad peaks, overlap-
ping reflections, and low crystallinity often limit the 
amount of independent structural information avail-
able. Indicators such as nonphysical parameter values 
or unit cell dimensions outside reasonable limits can 
provide a practical check on refinement reliability.
Supplementary information
Six individual files are provided to assist users with 
the cellulose-specific Rietveld refinement method:
(1) One .txt file: Supplementary Information file 
1.txt–XRD data of cotton fiber formatted for 
import into MAUD as a dataset.
(2) Two .cif files: Supplementary Information file 
2.cif and Supplementary Information file 3.cif–
crystal information files representing the cellu-
lose Iβ and cellulose II phases, respectively, for 
use in MAUD. The Supplementary Information 
file 2.cif file is based on the neutron diffraction 
study of Nishiyama et al. (2002) that includes the 
positions of the hydroxy hydrogen (deuterium) 
atoms, including the observed fractional occu-
pancies. Those atoms are not available from the 
synchrotron X-ray results. The Supplementary 
Information file 3.cif file was forwarded by Paul 
Langan to ADF. Although based on Langan et al. 
(2001), it also includes hydrogen atom positions 
that are not available in that work. Both files were 
part of the Supplementary Information in French 
(2014) in a combined file that is not directly use-
ful in MAUD.
(3) One .par file: Supplementary Information file 
4.par–an analysis file containing the complete 
analysis used in this study. It can be opened in the 
MAUD software by selecting “Open analysis” 
from the File menu.
(4) One .pdf file: Supplementary Information file 
5.pdf–a document containing screenshots of the 
MAUD interface, illustrating the sequential steps 
of the refinement procedure.
(5) One .xlsx file: Supplementary Information file 
6.xlsx–spreadsheet containing refinement results 
exported from MAUD (“Export experimental/
calculated data”).
The .txt, .par, and .cif files can be viewed with sim-
ple text-editing software such as Windows Notepad or 
Mac TextEdit.
