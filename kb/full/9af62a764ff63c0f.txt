Effect of organic manures and fertilizer sources on soil
nutrient availability and nutrient content in rice under
coastal saline soil
SR Kamble1, MR Wahane2, MC Kasture3, VG Chavan4, NA Meshram5, NH
Khobragade6, AD Kuwar7, SS Patil8, SB Abhale9
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/2618060X.2026.v9.i9s.6712
A BSTRACT
Field investigation was carried out during the Kharif season of 2025 at the Khar Land Research Station,
Panvel, Raigad, Maharashtra, to assess the influence of organic manures and fertilizer sources on soil
nutrient availability and nutrient content in rice (Oryza sativa L.) under coastal saline environments.
The experiment followed a factorial randomized block design with five organic manure treatments
(control, farmyard manure (FYM), Gliricidia, dhaincha, and bhend) and four fertilizer treatments (no
fertilizer, recommended dose of fertilizer (RDF), UB-DAP briquette, and Konkan Annapurna Briquette
(KAB)), replicated thrice. Soil samples were collected at the maximum tillering and harvest stages to
determine available nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K), along with their contents in grain
and straw. Organic manure treatments significantly influenced soil available N and P at both stages,
while fertilizer application significantly affected nutrient contents in grain and straw. Among the or-
ganic manures, Gliricidia recorded the highest available N, whereas dhaincha recorded the highest
available P. The combination of dhaincha + KAB produced the highest available P at harvest (78.27 kg
ha–1), while dhaincha + UB-DAP recorded the highest available P at maximum tillering (85.11 kg ha–
1) stage. In grain, the integration of FYM + UB-DAP caused in the highest N content (1.79%), and
Gliricidia + KAB recorded significantly highest P content (0.35%). In straw, KAB application showed
the highest K content (0.43%), whereas RDF displayed the maximum K content in grain (0.24%).
Correlation analysis showed positive associations between soil available nutrients at both sampling
stages and nutrient contents in rice grain and straw. In general, the findings indicate that the combined
application of organic manures and fertilizer sources enhanced soil nutrient availability and nutrient ac-
cumulation in rice under coastal saline conditions.
Keywords: Coastal saline soil, Briquettes, Fertilizers, Manures, Rice, Soil fertility
Introduction
Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is a major food crop in India, but its productivity in coastal regions is significantly de-
clined by soil salinity. Coastal saline soils of the Konkan region are impacted by seawater or creek-water in-
undation, saline groundwater, capillary rise of salts, and poor drainage. About 65,000 ha in Maharashtra are
affected by coastal salinity (Dodake et al., 2022[1]; Patil et al., 2024[2]) [1, 2]. These areas face further risks
from tidal effects related with sea-level rise, poor-quality saline groundwater, and the occurrence of saline
and acidic soils, all of which hamper crop establishment and decline productivity (Dodake et al., 2022[1],
2023[3]; Patil et al., 2024[2]; Khobragade et al., 2025[4]; Abhale et al., 2026[5]). Soil electrical conductivity
in the region varies from 1.69 to 31.29 dS m−1, representing substantial salinity stress (Patil et al., 2024[2];
Rakhonde et al., 2024[6], 2025[7]) [2, 6]. Salinity carries out osmotic and ionic stresses on rice, limiting water
uptake and disturb the ionic balance and uptake of critical nutrients to the crop. Subsequently, sustaining sat-
isfactory nutrient availability and enhancing nutrient-use efficiency are life-threatening for supporting rice
production in coastal saline soils (Munns and Tester, 2008[8]; Patil et al., 2014[2]; Rakhonde et al., 2024[6])
[8, 2, 6]. Seasonal variation in water-soluble ions has also been reported in these soils, indicating dynamic
changes in salinity during the monsoon period (Gaikwad et al., 2025)[9].
Organic manures can improve the fertility of salt-affected soils by delivering organic matter and nutrients
and by subsidiary microbial-mediated nutrient cycling (Abhale et al., 2024[10], 2026[5]; Khobragade et al.,
2025[4]; Vanave et al., 2026[11]) [5, 10, 4]. Farmyard manure and green manures such as Gliricidia and dhain-
cha increase nutrient mineralization and recover nutrient availability (Rathod et al., 2018[12]; Abhale et al.,
2024[10], 2026[5]) [12, 10]. In contrast, inorganic fertilizers offer readily available nutrients, but their efficacy
under flooded conditions depends on the source, timing, and method of application. Briquette-based fertil-
izer application has been shown to influence soil properties, nutrient concentration, and nutrient uptake in
rice (Bedse et al., 2021[13]; Wahane et al., 2022[14], 2023[15]) [13, 14]. Deep placement of fertilizer briquettes
progresses nutrient availability in the rice root zone and minimize nutrient losses. Earlier studies have re

However, information on the response of rice to different organic ma-
nure–fertilizer source combinations in the coastal saline soils of
Maharashtra remains limited. Therefore, the present study was under-
taken to evaluate the influence of farmyard manure (FYM),
Gliricidia, dhaincha, and bhend in combination with the recom-
mended dose of fertilizer (RDF), UB-DAP briquette, and Konkan
Annapurna Briquette (KAB) on soil nutrient availability and nitrogen
(N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) contents of rice under coastal
saline conditions at the Khar Land Research Station, Panvel, Raigad,
Maharashtra.
Materials and Methods
A field experiment was conducted during the Kharif season of 2025 at
the Khar Land Research Station, Panvel, District Raigad,
Maharashtra, on coastal saline soil. The experimental site is located at
18°59′ N latitude and 73°10′ E longitude, about 5 m above mean sea
level and about 50 m from the Arabian Sea. The region experiences a
hot and humid climate with annual rainfall exceeding 1,995 mm.
Before planting, a representative surface soil sample (0–22.5 cm
depth) was collected from the experimental field and analysed for ini-
tial fertility status. The soil was slightly acidic, with an electrical con-
ductivity of 4.0 dS m–1, available nitrogen of 118.20 kg ha–1, avail-
able phosphorus of 45.51 kg ha–1, and available potassium of
2,038.56 kg ha–1.
Rice (Oryza sativa L.) variety Panvel-1, developed by Dr. Balasaheb
Sawant Konkan Krishi Vidyapeeth, Dapoli from the cross IR 8 × BR
4-10, was used as the test crop. The crop was transplanted at a spac-
ing of 25 × 15 cm in plots (net plot) measuring 4 × 3 m. The experi-
ment was laid out in a factorial randomized block design (FRBD)
with five organic manure treatments (M0: control (no manuring), M1:
farmyard manure (FYM) @ 10 t ha–1, M2: Glyricidia (Gliricidia
sepium) green manure @ 10 t ha–1, M3: Dhaincha (Sesbania ac-
uleata) green manure @ 10 t ha–1 and M4: Bhend (Thespesia popul-
nea) green manure @ 10 t ha–1) and four chemical fertilizer treat-
ments (F0: control (no fertilizer/briquettes), F1: recommended dose of
fertilizer (RDF) @ 100:50:50 kg N: P2O5:K2O ha–1, F2: UB-DAP
briquette containing 34:18.4% N: P2O5 and F3: Konkan Annapurna
briquette (KAB) containing 34:14:06% N: P2O5: K2O) resulting in 20
treatment combinations, each replicated three times.
Representative surface soil samples (0–22.5 cm depth) were collected
from each treatment at 45 days after sowing (DAS) and again at crop
harvest. Composite samples were prepared by applying the quartering
method for soil sample collection. The samples were air-dried under
shade, gently crushed using a wooden mortar and pestle, and passed
through a 2 mm sieve for general analysis. The processed samples
were stored in plastic bags placed inside corrugated boxes until chem-
ical analyses. Available nitrogen was determined by the alkaline
potassium permanganate method (Subbiah et al., 1956)[16]. Available
phosphorus was estimated by the Olsen method using 0.5 M sodium
bicarbonate as the extracting solution (Olsen, 1954)[17]. Available
potassium was extracted with 1 N neutral ammonium acetate and
measured using a flame photometer (Jackson, 1973)[18]. For plant
analysis, total nitrogen was determined by the Micro-Kjeldahl
method, phosphorus by the vanadomolybdate yellow colour method,
and potassium by flame photometry after digestion of the plant mate-
rial, following the procedures described by Tandon (1993)[19].
Results and Discussion
Data presented in Table 1 showed that soil available nitrogen (N) var-
ied from 132.04 to 206.66 kg ha–1 at the maximum tillering stage and
from 125.40 to 180.66 kg ha–1 at harvest. A reduction in available N
towards harvest was observed owing to crop uptake and soil N trans-
formations under submerged conditions. These values were compara-
ble with those reported by Abhale et al. (2024)[10] for coastal saline
soils of Maharashtra. At maximum tillering, Gliricidia application
showed the highest mean available N (188.32 kg ha–1) compared to
dhaincha (184.83 kg ha–1) and bhend (180.89 kg ha–1), while the con-
trol recorded the lowest (151.36 kg ha–1). The higher N availability
under Gliricidia and dhaincha was related with their easily decompos-
able, N-rich residues and speedy mineralization (Bhatt, 2023[20];
Rakhonde et al., 2024[6]; Lyu et al., 2024[21]) [20, 6, 21]. At harvest,
bhend application observed the highest mean available N (169.38 kg
ha–1), followed by Gliricidia (162.60 kg ha–1) and dhaincha
(161.46 kg ha–1), while the control recorded the lowest (142.31 kg
ha–1). The higher residual N under bhend may have reflected because
of continued decomposition of its organic residues.

Soil available phosphorus (P) fluctuated from 51.98 to 78.27 kg ha–1
at the maximum tillering stage and from 52.26 to 85.11 kg ha–1 at
harvest, with overall increase towards harvest (Table 2). This differ-
ence may be related with P mineralization and redox-induced trans-
formations of Fe-associated P under submerged conditions (Abhale et
al., 2026)[5]. The observed values were similar with those reported by
Rakhonde et al. (2024)[6] and Abhale et al. (2024)[10] for coastal
saline soils of Maharashtra. Organic manure treatments significantly
enhanced available P at both stages. Dhaincha recorded significantly
highest mean available P at maximum tillering (71.21 kg ha–1) and at
harvest (78.20 kg ha–1), while the control recorded the lowest values
(56.84 and 63.69 kg ha–1, respectively). The higher P under dhaincha
was linked with the decomposition of nutrient-rich biomass and
greater P mineralization and mobilization (Gu et al., 2024) [25].[25]
Fertilizer application also significantly improved available P at both
stages. Konkan Annapurna Briquette (KAB) showed the highest mean
at maximum tillering (68.03 kg ha–1), while UB-DAP showed the
highest value at harvest (78.38 kg ha–1). The stage-dependent re-
sponse might have shown differences in P release and transformation
under flooded conditions. The organic manure × fertilizer interaction
was significant at both stages. At maximum tillering, dhaincha +
KAB recorded the highest available P (78.27 kg ha–1), while at har-
vest, dhaincha + UB-DAP recorded the highest value (85.11 kg ha–1).
The lowest values at both stages were recorded under the unfertilized
and unmanured control (51.98 and 52.26 kg ha–1). Significant higher
P under the integrated treatments designated corresponding effects of
organic residue decomposition and fertilizer-derived P supply. Similar
benefits of integrated nutrient management on P availability have
been reported by Hou et al. (2023)[23].

Potassium (K) concentration in rice straw and grain was influenced
primarily by fertilizer sources, while the effect of organic manures
was less pronounced. Straw K varied from 0.35% under the no-ma-
nure treatment to 0.41% under dhaincha, however grain K ranged
from 0.16% under the control to 0.23% under bhend (Table 6).
Among the fertilizer sources, KAB recorded the highest straw K
(0.43%), while the recommended dose of fertilizer (RDF) showed the
highest grain K (0.24%).

Correlation among soil available nutrients and content
Correlation analysis showed positive relationships between soil avail-
able nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) at the maximum
tillering and harvest stages, as well as with nutrient contents in rice
grain and straw (Table 7). Soil available N at maximum tillering had a
strong positive association with soil available P (r = 0.726**) and a
moderate positive relationship with soil available K (r = 0.513**).
Likewise, soil available P at harvest was positively associated with
soil available K at harvest (r = 0.446**), signifying interrelationships
among the available macronutrients during rice growth. Among the
relationships between soil nutrients and grain nutrient contents, soil
available P at maximum tillering shown the solid positive correlation
with grain N (r = 0.580**), followed by soil available P at harvest (r
= 0.288**). Grain K was positively related with soil available N at
harvest (r = 0.469**), soil available P at maximum tillering (r =
0.494**), and soil available K at maximum tillering (r = 0.316**).
Grain P had positive correlations with soil available N, P, and K at
both sampling stages, though the strength of these relationships
varied.
Nutrient concentrations in rice straw also had positive relationships
with soil nutrient availability. Straw N was strongly associated with
soil available N at maximum tillering (r = 0.589**) and positively
correlated with soil available P at harvest (r = 0.457**). Straw P had

Conclusion
The study confirmed that the integrated application of organic ma-
nures and fertilizer sources improved soil nutrient availability and nu-
trient concentration in rice under coastal saline conditions. Among the
organic manures, Gliricidia and dhaincha showed positive effect in
improving available nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P), respectively.
Fertilizer sources enhanced soil nutrient availability and plant nutrient
concentration, with responses varying according to the nutrient and
growth stage. Among the integrated treatments, farmyard manure
(FYM) + UB-DAP showed the highest grain N concentration
(1.79%), while Gliricidia + Konkan Annapurna Briquette (KAB) had
the highest grain P concentration (0.35%). KAB confirmed the high-
est straw potassium (K) concentration (0.43%), but the recommended
dose of fertilizer (RDF) showed the highest grain K concentration
(0.24%). Correlation analysis further discovered positive relationship
between soil nutrient availability and nutrient content in rice grain
and straw. In general, the findings showed that integrated nutrient
management can improve soil nutrient status and nutrient accumula-
tion in rice grown under coastal saline conditions. Nevertheless, as
the study was conducted for a single season, the findings are prelimi-
nary and require further confirmation through long-term and multi-
season experiments under varying coastal saline conditions.
