Abstract
Public health, infrastructural resilience, and drinking water quality are all persistently threatened by contaminant intrusion 
into Water Distribution Systems (WDSs). There is mounting evidence that herbicides and their by-products are intruding 
into Water Distribution Networks (WDNs) across the world. Consequently, owing to the intricate interactions that occur 
during the intrusion, their prediction and modelling are challenging. The research community has created reaction-trans -
port models and advection-dispersion equations in response to these problems. These typically try to capture part of the 
reaction kinetics. Furthermore, some of these mathematical models have been integrated into simulation tools, utilising 
the EPANET software and its EPANET-MSX extension. They have been used in scenario-based simulation studies for 
demonstrating various interactions between the dynamic states of the parent herbicide compounds and their transformation 
products. The goal of this review paper is to briefly review the occurrence, mechanism, fate, and significance of pesticide 
transformation products (PTPs). A review of over 2-decade-and-a-half-long (2000–2026) of journal articles, conference 
proceedings, technical reports, and experimental, analytical, numerical, and field-based research on contaminant intrusion 
is presented in this publication. Importantly, this review identifies persistent gaps in the precise fate of herbicides after 
incursion events into WDS, including pipe material interactions, the impact of residual disinfectants, biofilm-mediated 
transformations, and hydraulic mixing/dilution effects, with parent chemical transformation into more toxic and persistent 
metabolites. The paper provides an overview of herbicides and PTPs as contaminants of emerging concern and discusses 
the modes of degradation of herbicides and pesticides, their properties and associated risks.

Introduction
A water distribution system (WDS) is the interconnection 
and assemblage of pipes, hydraulic and flow-control ele -
ments, meters and fittings, reservoirs and tanks, and other 
water reticulation appurtenances for conveying water from 
treatment plants to end-users at sufficient pressure. WDSs 
are important in modern and rural communities and are con-
sidered critical infrastructure, with public health risks if not 
well protected against potential contaminant intrusion [1].
The ever-increasing challenges to WDS have long 
stemmed from growing urbanisation and climate change, 
which affect water resources [ 2], and, more recently, the 
intrusion of contaminants into Water Distribution Net -
works (WDNs) through potable water pipelines. Contami -
nant intrusion in a WDS is the incursion of wastewater, 
pathogens, and harmful industrial and agrochemicals into 
water supply lines. It is the entrance of contaminants after 
treatment (for example, through negative-pressure leaks, 
backflow/cross-connections or permeation). These compro-
mise water quality as it flows to end-users. Contamination 
between the Water Treatment Plant (WTP) and end users is 
common and occurs through the distribution pipeline. Water 
quality deterioration in WDN occurs due to inadequate 
treatment processes in the WTP [3], deterioration within the 
WDN [4], and contaminant intrusion [5].
It is crucial to understand that secondary disinfection 
in WDS is not intended to remove herbicides. Instead of 
eliminating chemical pollutants, its purpose is to preserve 
a residual disinfectant as a microbiological defence. Impor-
tantly, the distribution network itself does not contain any 
treatment units, such as membranes, sophisticated oxidation 
processes, or granular activated carbon (GAC). As a result, 
any herbicide that enters the network by intrusion or escapes 
treatment at the plant will remain there, only to be acciden-
tally transformed by residual chlorine or chloramine. These 
interactions may result in transformation products that are 
unregulated and unmonitored but may be more hazardous 
or persistent than the parent chemical.
Herbicide use in residential garden, urban green space 
maintenance forms some of the pathways for intrusion 
(non-point sources), which are aggravated by intense pre -
cipitation as a means of transportation to the main water 
source. These herbicides are mostly unremovable by con -
ventional water treatment processes [ 6]. Water utilities can 
better protect public health and handle the intricate, multi-
faceted problem of herbicide contamination in water distri -
bution systems by considering the complete process, from 
application to tap.
This contaminant intrusion can be malicious and deliber-
ate, such as those from a terrorist attack [ 7]. Others may be 
accidental due to a deficit or improper WDN operation [ 4], 
or pipe leaks that allow contaminants to enter the pipeline 
during periods of low pressure. This can be lethal. A typi -
cal example was the Walkerton outbreak, which claimed the 
lives of 7 people and made 2,300 people ill due to flood -
ing that introduced E. coli into the drinking water supply 
[4]. Two cases of waterborne illnesses caused by bacterial

can manage herbicides and their reactive metabolites. With-

WDS as Vulnerable Infrastructure
WDS are known to be extensive and exposed. This makes 
them susceptible to physical attack, ageing failure and oper-
ational factors like flow velocity, water pressure, cathodic 
protection, improper operations, and maintenance proce -
dures [11]. Failure of WDS pipelines can happen even when 
they are properly designed. A backflow event or a direct 
attack on a reservoir could introduce biological, chemical 
or radiological agents that can affect a large population long 
before detection [12]. Any attack or failure of WDS disrupts 
conveniences, threatens public health, endangers firefight -
ing ability and limits economic activities.
The Growing Threat of Herbicide Contamination in 
WDS
Herbicides are becoming more frequently found in catch -
ments and plumbing, posing new threats to ecosystems and 
the safety of drinking water [ 13, 14]. Atrazine, simazine, 
metolachlor, glyphosate, bentazone, 2-methyl-4-chloro -
phenoxyacetic acid (MCPA), and other contemporary hydro-
philic herbicides are frequently detected at low amounts 
(ng/L to µg/L). The ingredient with the highest concentra -
tion was glyphosate, followed by 2,4-D and MCPA, and to 
a lesser extent by dimethomorph, fluopicolide, prothiocon -
azole, and metolachlor (-S), according to [15]. The rationale 
for focusing on herbicide intrusion in WDS is due to its per-
sistence, health concerns, and ubiquity [ 16]. The key gaps 
in WDS herbicide intrusion control are the lack of regular 
monitoring of herbicide transformation products at low 
chronic doses despite their greater persistence and detection 
frequency, as well as our limited knowledge of combination 
toxicity and sensitive life stage exposures (newborns, chil -
dren and adults) [15, 17].
The Evolving Threat Landscape: From Microbial to 
Chemical Contaminants
Historically, microbial contamination has always been the 
primary concern in water safety and security, with patho -
gens causing rapid and visible outbreaks of diseases [ 18, 
19]. Recently, the threat has evolved significantly, with 
water utilities facing complex and expanding chemical 
contaminants with chronic and often invisible health risks 
to our communities. A recent study defines Emerging Con -
taminants (EC) as newly identified natural or synthetic 
chemicals or biological agents that are present in the envi -
ronment and are thought to be potentially dangerous. The 
emerging chemicals of great concern, such as industrial 
chemicals, pesticides, herbicides, pharmaceuticals, heavy 
metals, disinfection by-products, per- and polyfluoroalkyl 
substances (PFAS), microplastics, etc., are many, unregu -
lated, unmonitored, non-point, and ultimately persistent 
and bioaccumulative [ 20]. Long-term exposure typically 
results in neurological damage, cancer, reproductive harm, 
endocrine disruption, and chronic health problems. These 
require advanced analytics and treatment. The evolving 
threats from microbial to chemical contaminants are driven 
by industrialisation, pharmaceutical consumption, agri -
cultural intensification, ageing network infrastructures, 
climate change, etc. The implications for water distribu -
tion systems include an increase in intrusion risks through 
backflow, cross-connection and permeation, persistence in 
the network, reaction with disinfectants, and monitoring 
challenges. A common example is Glyphosate (Herbicide), 
which comes from agricultural runoff and urban weed-con -
trol herbicide use, resulting in surface-water contamination 
before the water gets to the water treatment plants. Another 
serious risk to the WDS is pipeline infiltration by cross-
connection during backflow events. Contaminants can be 
drawn or forced into the WDS when there is a physical con-
nection between the potable water supply and a possible 
source of contamination, together with a reversal of nor -
mal water flow [ 21]. Water distribution concerns include 
chemical persistence in pipes, reactions with chlorine, and 
difficulty detecting contaminants early and in real time in 
WDS [22].

Historical Perspectives and Recent High-Profile 
Contamination Events
Water quality often declines between treatment plants and 
end-users because of contaminant intrusion along the pipe -
line, according to earlier water work experts [ 23]. One of 
the biggest instances of environmental contamination was 
the American Flint water disaster of 2014. Flint, Michi -
gan, changed its drinking water supply from Lake Huron 
water treated and supplied by the Detroit Water and Sewer-
age Department to the Flint River in April 2014. Numerous 
issues with water quality and public health resulted from 
the Flint Water Treatment Plant’s improper water treatment. 
A historic outbreak of Legionnaires’ disease occurred in 
the summer of 2014–2015 due to low chlorine circulation, 
which encouraged bacterial growth. Also, corrosive water 
flow degraded the lead pipes of the infrastructure, leading 
to systemic lead pollution [ 24]. A devastating waterborne 
outbreak of the pathogenic germs Campylobacter jejuni and 
E. coli O157:H7 occurred in May 2000 in the tiny Ontario 
village of Walkerton, due to the washing of spread manure 
from the nearby farm. The spread of the biological contami-
nant was through the municipal water distribution system, 
as the chlorinator #5 for one of the wells did not operate 
properly, leading to a deadly sickness that impacted over 
2,300 people and claimed the lives of at least seven citizens 
[25].
The 4-methylcyclohexylmethanol (MCHM) spill that 
contaminated the Elk River in West Virginia, USA, on Janu-
ary 9, 2014, is another recent high-profile incident. For four 
days, 300,000 people’s access to drinking water was cut off 
due to this accident. More than a month after the incident, 
the distribution network was still being recovered, and the 
stench of MCHM, which has a very low odour threshold, 
was still present [ 26]. There is a compelling argument for 
ongoing water quality monitoring across the distribution 
network due to the possible hazards and financial repercus -
sions. For instance, the water in Addis Ababa was found 
to contain excessive levels of lead, with an average of 
17.8 µg/L (above WHO’s 10 µg/L guideline), from sources 
that serve schools. This suggests that not all tap water may 
be considered “safe” [27].
A study on arsenic contamination in Bangladesh dis -
covered that the mobilisation and release of arsenic into 
drinking water lines is likely caused by the oxidation of 
mineral deposits, the release of arsenic from sediments due 
to reduction, and the release of arsenic from fertilisers and 
pesticides [ 28]. According to another study conducted in 
Algeria, metals in surface water samples most likely came 
from a combination of urban and industrial wastes and 
agricultural practices [ 29]. A conventional drinking water 
treatment plant that draws its drinking water from the Tengi 
River became contaminated due to ongoing and growing 
use of pesticides in Tanjung Karang, a popular rice-growing 
area in Kuala Selangor, Malaysia [ 30]. To guarantee safe 
drinking water, it is vital to investigate how well the treat -
ment plant removes pesticide contaminants from the water. 
Advanced treatment methods are required. Water bodies 
serving treatment plants need regular testing as safety pre -
cautions, whereas potable water should undergo advanced 
water treatment processes as needed.
The Critical role of Modelling in Prevention, 
Preparedness and Response
Contaminant intrusion into the WDS is frequently localised, 
fleeting and challenging to identify by traditional monitor -
ing, in contrast to source water pollution. Recent research 
typically combines modelling and monitoring to obtain a 
trustworthy representation of the systems and reduce the 
costs of real-time monitoring [ 31]. To forecast the prob -
ability of water pipe failures and contaminant intrusion fate, 
a variety of models have been created, which are roughly 
grouped into statistical, physical models and machine learn-
ing [32]. An essential tool for comprehending, forecasting 
and eventually averting these occurrences is mathematical 
modelling. Numerous methods for simulating contamina -
tion entry are found in the literature. Most methods consider 
both simulation results and laboratory data.
Optimization studies are typically used to choose moni -
toring stations, sensor locations, and contaminant source 
determination [3]. It’s critical to identify the best parameters 
for contamination intrusion modelling. Modelling frame -
works were developed by combining underlying network 
simulation with statistical modelling, classical optimiza -
tion techniques, evolutionary computation, graph theory, 
heuristic approaches, greedy approaches, machine learning, 
and other computational techniques. Three factors, such as 
conduit characteristics, the driving force, and a contami -
nation source, are necessary for the complicated but com -
monly occurring contaminant incursion in a WDN. Often, 
this information is typically incomplete, non-specific and 
uncertain.
Four major mechanisms for the contaminant’s intru -
sion can be identified. These are leakage pathways, nega -
tive pressure events, permeation through the pipe walls and 
backflow and cross-connection [ 3, 33]. The role of model -

 Physical and Hydraulic Pathways of Contamination 
Intrusion in WDS
When external contaminants enter WDS through hydraulic 
or structural weaknesses, usually during low or negative 
pressure occurrences, this is known as physical intrusion. 
The majority of research bases intrusion on three con -
current factors: the driving force (pressure differential, 
typically negative main pressure), the pathway (physical 
opening), and the contaminant source close to the pipe 
(such as sewage or contaminated soil) [ 1, 37]. According 
to Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) and experimental 
research, intrusion starts when the internal head falls below 
the exterior head, and the rate of intrusion is influenced 
by surrounding media, opening shape, and pressure [ 38]. 
Reviews highlight how pressure dynamics and physical 
channels combine to generate a complex intrusion process; 
even little leaks can pose a serious health concern when 
they coincide with transitory occurrences and adjacent 
sewage sources.
Contaminants outside the pipe infiltrate through the pipe 
wall or gaskets into the drinking water by permeation, a 
material-driven incursion pathway, even when the pipe is not 
obviously leaking. It primarily affects plastic and polymeric 
pipes that are exposed to wildfires or placed in contaminated 
soils. Diffusion-partition models imply that PE/PVC pipes 
are at the highest risk in contaminated soils. The partition 
(solubility) coefficient, external concentration/activity, pipe 
wall thickness and diameter, and contaminant diffusivity 
in polymer are important factors [ 39]. One of the distribu -
tion system’s biggest threats to public health and a frequent 
cause of epidemics is cross-connections and backflows 
[40]. Only when hydraulic conditions momentarily “pull” 
or “push” impurities into the pipe may they enter through 
leaks, cracks, valves, or cross-connections. These occur -
rences are primarily associated with transitory (unsteady) 
flow behaviour and low or negative hydraulic conditions, 
particularly during transients brought on by pump trips, 
valve operations, or pipe breaks acting on pre-existing leaks 
or openings, which allow WDS infiltration. Higher baseline 
pressures, rapid pressure stabilisation, and effective surge/
leak control significantly lower risk, whereas the depth and 
length of negative pressure, the network zones impacted, 
and leak/valve characteristics determine the severity of 
incursion [41].

The EU value of 0.1 µg/L for individual pesticides and 
0.5 µg/L for total pesticides is a precautionary, policy-based 
compliance standard which is a deliberately stringent value 
designed to minimize exposure to synthetic pesticides irre -
spective of their individual toxicity [ 44] (London et al., 
2000). This is different from health-based guideline val -
ues derived from toxicological data and incorporate safety 
margins to take care of uncertainty and vulnerable popula -
tions. While the US EPA and WHO use compound-specific 
health-based limits that vary widely by agent toxicity, the 
EU/UK approach sets a consistent, low “compliance” value 
for all pesticides. The WHO/EPA regulations seek to specify 
a level at which no significant health risk is anticipated, but 
the EU/UK standard is purposefully conservative. It has 
been said that South Africa’s drinking water pesticide regu-
lations are inadequate, with few monitoring data, few stan -
dards, capacity constraints, and no transformation product 
coverage [45]. Studies indicate that pesticide detections in 
South African water sources are frequently above the EU 
0.1 µg/L parametric value but remain below WHO/EPA 
health-based limits, underscoring the disparate outcomes of 
implementing different regulatory philosophies [44].
Analytical target lists for common herbicides vary from 
the EU to US EPA and South Africa. EU covered over 100 
pesticide parent compounds, with limited coverage of pes -
ticide transformation products (PTPs) [46]; the US EPA has 
an Unregulated Contaminant Monitoring Rule (UCMR) but 
includes some pesticides and PTPs with other evolving lists 
[47], while South Africa has no comprehensive list and lim-
ited routine monitoring and minimal coverage.
Due to their chemical and physical properties, polar, 
non-volatile herbicides like glyphosate and AMPA are 
particularly difficult to analyse [ 48]. Some of the key 
analytical parameters for herbicides include the Limit of 
detection(LOD)- lowest concentration distinguishable from 
blank (e.g.,˂ 0.1 µg/L for EU compliance; Limit of Quanti-
fication (LOQ)- lowest concentration that can be quantified 
with acceptable precision (typically 2–5 x LOD); recovery 
( percentage of analyte recovered from spiked samples; 
matrix effects (interference from water matrix like ions and 
organic matter; and sample preparation (methods to concen-
trate/purify analytes). These parameters suggest analytical 
techniques like LC-MS/MS(triple quadrupole), applicable 
in routine quantification of polar herbicides and PTPs [ 49] 
or HRMS (Q-TOF, Orbitrap) for Non-target screening and 
identification of PTPs and degradation products [ 48] and 
other analytical methods.
One modelling study uses EPANET/EPANET-MSX to 
explicitly simulate pesticide intrusion into water distribution 
systems and its interaction with chlorine. It then uses machine 
learning (kNN, random forest) to locate the source of con -
tamination, achieving high localisation accuracy in scenarios 
involving synthetic pesticide intrusion [26]. The source-path-
way-driving force triad with nearby chemical sources (such 
as contaminated groundwater or agricultural drains), negative 
pressure, leaks or breaks in the main is highlighted in broader 
intrusion reviews [50]. This is because true “intrusion” into 
distribution mains is primarily addressed via modelling and 
detection algorithms rather than documented field cases.
 Key Findings from the Herbicides’ Intrusion in WDS 
Studies and Knowledge gaps
There are comparatively few studies on actual pipe network 
“intrusion,” with most concentrating on herbicides travelling 
from catchments into sources and finished drinking water. 
When combined, they exhibit a few recurring patterns.
a. From source to tap, herbicides are commonly used.
b. Pesticide transformation products (PTPs) are often more 
problematic than the parent compound or its active 
ingredients.
c. Intrusion is often controlled by factors like sources, 
concentration at the sources, proximity of the source to 
the point of entry, pipeline materials and hydraulic flow 
within the WDS.
d. There is a serious need to create a model that will predict 
the fate of herbicides in WDS.
Overall, research clearly documents herbicides in catch -
ments and drinking water sources. Still, there is a major gap 
in empirical and modelling work that follows herbicides 
through treatment and real distribution system intrusions 
to the consumer’s tap. Most studies stop at the catchment 
and source water scale; true intrusion into distribution pipes 
and tap-level exposure is poorly investigated. Particularly 
for episodic peaks and “shock loads” of mobile, polar 
herbicides, there is insufficient data to connect catchment 
concentrations to what enters treatment plants and then the 
network. Although commonly known, transformation prod-
ucts (such as those of flufenacet, metazachlor, and glypho -
sate) are nevertheless inadequately tracked along the entire 
source-treatment-distribution cycle and are not monitored 
in many drinking-water systems. There are very few field 
case studies that validate such models in actual incursions;

 Transport Dynamics of Contaminants in WDN
The velocity field from hydraulic analysis drives most mod-
els, which use 1-D advection–dispersion–reaction equations 
along pipes to depict contaminant flow [ 58]. Advection is 
bulk flow transfer that is managed by network hydraulics, 
demand trends, and operational modifications (valve/pump 
operations) [ 58, 59]. Diffusion and dispersion: Molecular 
diffusion and velocity gradients disperse pollutant plumes, 
particularly in laminar or transitional regimes and at low 
velocities; ignoring these effects can distort the structure 
and persistence of plumes [ 60, 61]. Reactions and wall 
interactions: single- or multi-species reactive transport for -
mulations are needed because pollutants may decay, react 
(e.g., disinfectant–DBP formation), or sorb/desorb at pipe 
walls [60].

 Role of Demand Variability and System Dynamics
Patterns of consumer demand influence the velocity field, 
which in turn influences the placement and dilution of 
plumes. Changes in consumer behaviour during an event 
can dramatically influence flow directions, plume trajecto -
ries, and exposure, according to stochastic demand models 
and agent-based frameworks [59]. Long residence times and 
increased dispersion cause significant spatial variability in 
concentrations in dead ends and low-flow zones [60].

To simulate the fate of contaminants, including emerg -

show a tightly coupled system in which human behaviour, 
hydraulics, dispersion, and reactions all work together to

 Advection and Dispersion in a pipe Network
In WDS, advection and dispersion are important mecha -
nisms controlling the movement of solutes, including her -
bicides. The bulk movement of water carrying dissolved 
materials is referred to as advection, whereas the spread -
ing of these materials because of changes in velocity and 
molecular diffusion within pipes is referred to as dispersion. 
Because empirical methods frequently underestimate disper-
sion coefficients under various flow regimes, which affects 
predictions of contaminant concentration profiles, accurate 
modelling of longitudinal dispersion in WDS is difficult 
[1]. Herbicide spread and persistence in water networks are 
thus influenced by the interaction between advection and 
dispersion, which may result in localised contamination or 
dilution depending on flow conditions. Understanding these 
pathways is important for controlling the hazards of herbi -
cide incursion in WDS and enhancing water quality models 
to more accurately forecast contaminant behaviour [1].
 Herbicide Transformation During Treatment Stage
Herbicides may change during drinking water treatment 
before water reaches the distribution network. Polar herbi -
cides can only be partially removed by processes such as 
coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, and filtration [ 65, 
66]. Through reactive oxygen species (ROS) like hydroxyl 
radicals (OH −) and sulfate radicals (SO₄ 2⁻), advanced 
oxidation processes (AOPs) such as UV/H₂O₂, UV/TiO₂ 
photocatalysis, ozonation, electro-Fenton, and activated 
persulfate/peroxymonosulfate can degrade herbicides. 
However, unless built explicitly as in-network booster sys -
tems, these procedures are typically missing from distribu -
tion pipes and are purposefully designed as treatment units. 
The consequences for WDS are that the designed systems’ 
rate constants, (e.g., k OH = 108–1010 M− 1 s− 1 kOH ≈ 10 8–10 
10 M − 1 s − 1) are not transferable to distribution networks 
due to significant differences in pH, oxidant dosage, water 
matrix, and contact time. These processes don’t happen in 
pipes unless a utility deliberately doses an oxidant into the 
network.

the concentration of parent herbicide chemicals, but this 
does not translate into a decrease in danger. Oxidation of 
glyphosate produces AMPA, a metabolite that is more per -

of evaluating the parent alone, toxicity endpoints should 
be evaluated for the total of the parent and transformation 
products. Without proving similar pH, dosage, matrix, 
and contact circumstances, rate constants from laboratory 
research (such as UV/H2O₂, electro-Fenton, and high 
chlorine doses) shouldn’t be immediately used in WDS 
models.
 Dark Reactions and Hydrolysis in WDS
Herbicides may experience dark reactions and hydroly -
sis (reaction with water) when there is no light and little 
oxidant residue. At pH > 8, several herbicides, includ -
ing atrazine and other triazines, undergo base-catalysed 
hydrolysis, which can happen in pipelines lined with 
cement. Although this is less frequent in drinking water, 
sulfonylureas hydrolyse more quickly at low pH [ 70]. In 
distribution networks with lengthy residence durations, 
these abiotic processes contribute to long-term modifi -
cation despite their modest pace (half-lives of weeks to 
months). Under dark conditions, biotic processes like 
microbial breakdown in biofilms can also take place [ 39]. 
Implication for WDS is that, although slow, hydroly -
sis and dark reactions are important for herbicides with 
extended hydraulic residence durations (such as dead 
ends and storage tanks). Only when their residence period 
is longer than the average decay half-lives can they be 
included in fate models.
 Herbicides Adsorption into pipe Walls and Biofilms
Scales (mineral deposits) and biofilms (microbial layers) 
inside pipes can both adsorb and subsequently release pol -
lutants, changing the water quality over time. Pipe bio -
films, which are complex microbial layers combined with 
extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), can interact 
significantly with herbicides that enter drinking water or 
distribution systems [ 79]. Particularly in old or corroded 
networks, biofilms and scales may exfoliate, releasing bac -
teria and sorbed pollutants. Biofilm formation and sorption 
behaviour are significantly influenced by pipe material, 
corrosion, stagnation, and disinfectant levels. Biofilm 
mass and its function as a contamination reservoir are both 
affected by control techniques (disinfection, flushing, cor -
rosion management) [ 80]. As a sink and delayed source, 
biofilms can hold onto herbicides even after they are no 
longer visible in the bulk water. Sorption frequently exhib -
its significant potential accumulation capacities in the 
biofilm matrix and is non-linear (e.g., Langmuir-type for 
diuron) [80].

 Modelling Approaches for Contaminant 
Intrusion and Transport Within WDS
Modelling approaches for contaminant intrusion in WDS 
have always sought to estimate where and how much pol -
lution enters, as well as anticipate its fate and travel across 
the network. This is done within a water quality modelling 
framework, which is a structured set of tools and methods 
used in the simulation of the way pollutants move, trans -
form and are removed in water distribution systems in space 
and time. To reduce numerical dispersion and computation 
time, transient hydraulic models (Eulerian) combined with 
Lagrangian water-quality solvers are frequently employed 
to simulate intrusion during pressure transients and subse -
quent transit [23]. Compared to constant flow assumptions, 
some models improve realism by directly linking intrusion 
rate to local pressure and surrounding soil properties [ 37]. 
Others consider terrain slope effects on spread and simulate 
intrusion from sewers or wastewater [5].
Risk frameworks describe intrusion potential or risk 
across networks by combining pathway, source, and driv -
ing force information using fuzzy logic, Dempster-Shafer 
theory, or fuzzy-rule systems. Critical nodes are ranked, 
and propagation risk is evaluated using EPANET simula -
tions and complex network theory. Modelling for “pre -
paredness–detection–management” is organised by broader 
reviews, which emphasize uncertainty in contaminant kind 
and source [81].
 EPANET and Extended Period Simulation
Hydraulic-water quality models, which monitor how a pol -
lutant enters, travels, mixes, and decays over time, are typi-
cally utilised to investigate the intrusion of contaminants 
into WDS. The main open-source program for this is called 
EPANET, and it uses extended period simulation (EPS) to 
reflect variations in demand, operation, and water quality. 
With inputs like source location, injection pattern, and reac-
tion coefficients, EPANET 2.2 simulates the movement of 
contaminants (such as chemicals, pesticides, and chlorine) 
under hydraulic conditions [61].
Standard EPANET tracks one chemical species (or 
water age/source tracing) at a time, while EPANET-MSX 
represents multiple interacting bulk and wall species. 
EPANET-MSX uses BULK and WALL phases; wall spe -
cies are surface-associated rather than simply ‘attached 
and mobile.’ Other EPANET tools described in previous 
works include EPANET-C, EPyT-C, EPANET-DD, and 
EPANET-PMX. They are not interchangeable elements of 
the main program; rather, they are unique, research-focused 
extensions or wrappers built around the standard EPANET 
hydraulic engine. An “umbrella simulation platform” called 
EPANET-C was created to make using EPANET-MSX to 
represent contamination occurrences in WDS easier [82]. Its 
main goal is to make multi-species water quality modelling 
more accessible to people without programming experience 
by overcoming the complicated user interface and program-
ming requirements usually associated with EPANET-MSX.
In order to do this, EPANET-C offers pre-built and edit -
able directories of conceptual and mathematical models 
that explain chemical, biological, and physical reactions. It 
combines EPANET and EPANET-MSX, simulating various 
contamination situations with a straightforward command-
line interface. In 2022, it was presented at the World Envi -
ronmental and Water Resources Congress.
A completely independent multi-species reactive trans -
port modelling plugin for EPyT (the EPANET-Python Tool-
kit) is called EPyT-C (where “C” refers to contaminant). 
It was created because the regular version of EPyT only 
allows for single-species water quality analysis.
To perform multi-species reactive transport modelling, 
EPyT-C uses an integrated water quality solver and invokes 
the hydraulic solver from EPANET 2.2 [ 62]. It offers pre-
configured modules that specify the governing equations for 
intricate water quality factors, like the “Bacterial regrowth” 
and “Chlorine decay and Trihalomethanes formation” mod-
ules. Numerical accuracy, response coefficients, and input 
modification are all possible with this tool. “EPANET-DD” 
is the name of EPANET’s Research Extension for Pressure-
Driven Demand. It might allude to an application of pres -
sure-driven demand (PDD) analysis tailored to a particular 
study. Other extensions, like WNTR (the US EPA’s Water 
Network Tool for Resilience), provide the option to perform 
hydraulics using PDD simulations. Additionally, it is used 
in the EPANET-PDX extension and EPANET-PMX [ 83]. 
An integrated network analysis model called EPANET-
PMX (Pressure-dependent Multi-species eXtension) was 
developed to overcome the shortcomings of current tools, 
mainly their incapacity to perform pressure-driven analysis 
in conjunction with multi-species water quality simulations. 
It expands on the work of EPANET-PDX, a pressure-driven 
extension of EPANET 2 that maintained water quality mod-
elling for a single species but was unable to simulate several 
chemical compounds simultaneously [55]. This is addressed 
by EPANET-PMX, which combines the multi-species 
modelling capabilities of EPANET-MSX with pressure-
dependent demand analysis. This enables more accurate 
low-pressure simulations, which are essential for evaluating

 Parameterization of Herbicide Reaction Kinetics
Parameterising herbicide kinetics means transforming 
observed degradation, sorption, and transport behaviour into 
numerical rate constants and model forms that can be used in 
fate and risk models. Rate laws (first- or second-order) and 
their reliance on pH, oxidant dose, light, and matrix compo-
nents are commonly used to characterize the kinetics of her-
bicide reactions in water distribution (or treatment) systems. 
Experts can forecast herbicide degradation and byproduct 
development along treatment trains or pipes using these 
factors. With apparent rate constants that change with pH 
and species (HClO vs. ClO⁻), direct reactions with disin -
fectants such as chlorine can exhibit second-order kinetics 
[70]. Sulfonylureas and chlorine are frequent examples of 
these reactions: Second-order k app = 2–288 M⁻¹ s⁻¹; half-
lives ~ 18–27 min at pH 7 and 4 mg/L Cl₂ [ 70]; kO₃ varies 
from ~ 2 to 2190 M⁻¹ s for phenylureas with ozone/OHUV/
PS promotion: pseudo-first-order k_obs ≈ 0.236 min⁻¹;

are crucial for forecasting the removal of herbicides and con-
trolling possibly more hazardous or permanent byproducts.
 Critical gaps and Research Challenges
To close the knowledge gaps about the precise fate of 
herbicides following incursion events into WDS, more 
research is required. These include pipe material inter -
actions, the impact of residual disinfectants, biofilm-
mediated transformations, and hydraulic mixing/dilution 
effects [ 67]. Most of the data that is currently accessible 
comes from laboratory simulations or open aquatic habi -
tats rather than actual distribution networks. Products of 
transformation provide additional difficulties: regulatory 
frameworks frequently lag scientific understanding of the 
dangers associated with these metabolites; some are per -
sistent or more toxic than the main active ingredients [ 77, 
78]. Given the short residence times typical of WDS in 
comparison to natural waters or treatment facilities, reme -
diation technologies exhibit promise but must be adapted 
to system-specific constraints [ 71].
 EPANET-MSX for Multi-Species Reactive Transport 
data Limitations
A flexible engine for modelling several chemical species 
that interact in WDNs is EPANET-MSX. Although it is a 
potent tool for multi-species reactive transport (MSRT), its 
application is severely constrained by performance, input 
complexity, and data availability. However, using general 
rate/equilibrium expressions allows users to design arbi -
trary reaction networks at pipe walls and in bulk water 
[77, 95]. Additionally, it allows for the simultaneous simu -
lation of numerous species in a single run, such as DBPs, 
chlorine, water age, haloacetic acids, arsenic species, and 
taste/odour compounds [ 89, 96]. For better comparison 
with experiments, the revised versions add dispersion and 
enhanced mass-balance reporting [ 90]. Table 8 shows Key

 Model Calibration, Validation, Sensitivity and mass 
Balance
Any WDS model’s quality assurance framework is made up 
of these four elements. A model is just an untested math -
ematical exercise without them. Calibration guarantees that 
the model accurately depicts reality, validation verifies the 
model’s predictive ability, Sensitivity indicates what to mea-
sure, identifiability indicates what you can accurately esti -
mate, and mass balance verifies the model’s numerical and 
physical consistency. When combined, they offer the assur-
ance required to make decisions using models [97]. Table 9 
summarises how these four elements can be used to improve 
the quality of water quality and hydraulic modelling.
 Conclusion
This paper included a cross-method evaluation, a critical 
assessment of methodological strengths and limitations, 
and a future research agenda in contrast to previous, more 
focused assessments. The review found convergent evi -
dence that the shape and location of leaks, the size and dura-
tion of negative pressure episodes, and the interaction with 
nearby soils and operational transients are the primary fac -
tors driving intrusion risk. A systematic technique is needed 
to develop efficient contamination intrusion, dynamics, and 
risk assessment methods for herbicide intrusion in water 
distribution systems. Significant differences still exist, 
though: estimates of infiltration volumes differ depending 
on the type of pipe material; health risk propagation through 
chemical or microbial contaminants is still not consistently 
quantified; and digital monitoring or early-warning tech -
niques are still not widely used. This entails integrating 
real-time monitoring systems, comprehending parameter 
sensitivities, and considering the most relevant specific pol-
lutants. Water utilities can improve public health protection 
and effectively handle contamination situations by tackling 
these issues.
A thorough and critical investigation of processes 
involved in the decay of herbicides and PTPs is important 
in the prediction of their behaviour and fate in the WDS 
whenever an intrusion event occurs. There is a wide and 
persistent gap in the precise fate of herbicides and pesticides 
after each intrusion event into WDS which includes pipe 
material interactions, the impact of residual disinfectants, 
biofilm-mediated transformations, and hydraulic mixing/
dilution effects, with parent chemical transformation into 
more toxic and persistent metabolites. Water conservation 
and herbicide infiltration are two sides of the same coin. 
Leaky, inadequately pressurized, and hydraulically slug -
gish water distribution systems are prone to contamination 
(allowing herbicide intrusion) and water inefficiency (los -
ing water). On the other hand, a hydraulically engineered, 
pressurized, and well-maintained network saves water and 
shields users from herbicide exposure. Moving beyond 
compartmentalized approaches to water quantity and water 
quality management, this dual advantage ought to be the 
cornerstone of utility investment, regulatory policy, and 
research agendas.
