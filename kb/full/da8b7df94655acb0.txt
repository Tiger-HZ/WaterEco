Estuaries are sentinel ecosystems for understanding the impacts of climate and land-use change, but
availability of in situ data for continental-scale comparative studies is rare. Here, we document the
status of water quality in 29 U.S. estuaries across multiple climate zones using in situ data to detail
decadal-scale trends in key environmental parameters and their drivers. Our analysis assembles more
than 250 million data points from over the past two decades (2002-2022), providing direct evidence of
widespread eutrophication and warming in estuaries, with signi ﬁcant increases in chlorophyll-a and
water temperature at approximately two-thirds of sites, and oxygen declines at half of sites. Changing
nutrient concentrations, not temperature, appear to be the primary driver of increases in chlorophyll-a
at the continental scale, while increasing temperature and chlorophyll-a are the best predictors of
declining oxygen concentrations. These analyses reinforce that changes in coastal ecosystem
dynamics are widespread and complex, driven by multiple interacting factors that require large-scale,
standardized data collection to disentangle.
Estuaries, situated at the critical int erface between terrestrial and aquatic
ecosystems, are ecologically and economically valuable environments that
are increasingly threatened by anthropogenic and climate-driven stressors1.
While estuary characteristics vary considerably across geographic settings,
they are consistent in their ecological role of serving as critical nursery
habitats, fostering high levels of pro ductivity, and facilitating active bio-
geochemical cycling largely driven by mixing of chemically-distinct water
masses as well as nutrient inputs from surrounding watersheds 2– 4.T h i s
productivity and habitat provision equates to billions of dollars in ecosystem
services to coastal economies, particularly for ﬁsheries, recreation, and
tourism industries5. However, the location of estuaries at the land-water
interface exposes them to both terrestrial (e.g., land use change, runoff) and
aquatic (e.g., sea level rise, ocean acidi ﬁcation, harmful algal blooms)
stressors6– 9.
Eutrophication is a common threa t to the water quality of coastal
ecosystems10,11. During eutrophication, increased nutrient inputs trigger
overgrowth of phytoplankton, in so me cases resulting in harmful algal
blooms (HABs), which some studies show have been increasing in extent
and frequency over previous decades12. Decomposition of phytoplankton
aggregations following these blooms can signiﬁcantly lower oxygen levels
through microbial uptake during decomposition, causing hypoxia (deﬁned
herein as dissolved oxygen [DO] <2 mg/L) and anoxia (complete oxygen
d e p l e t i o n ) .T h e s el o w - o x y g e ne v e n t sreduce estuarine nursery function and
negatively impact ﬁsh populations 13 and benthic macroinvertebrate
communities14. Further, hypoxia can alter sediment redox conditions,
leading to internal nutrient loading of dissolved nutrients back into the
water column, creating a self-sustaining mechanism for subsequent phy-
toplankton blooms7,12. Increased phytoplankton biomass also reduces light
p e n e t r a t i o ni nt h ew a t e rc o l u m na n dto the benthos, triggering negative
ecosystem-level impacts15,16. Coastal eutrophication has previously been
estimated to cost $100 million annually for the United States, and $1 billion
for European Union nations, due to public health concerns, lost recreational
opportunities, and degraded commercialﬁsheries17. Coastal eutrophication
and hypoxia are longstanding problems in the U.S. and despite regulatory
efforts dedicated to combating theseissues, they continue to pose signiﬁcant
ecological, economic, and public health risks.

The U.S. National Estuarine Research Reserve System (NERRS)
System-Wide Monitoring Program (SWMP) was designed to quantify
short-term variability and long-term ecosystem change within a network of
30 coastal locations (i.e., Reserves) around the United States, including
Hawaii, Alaska, and Puerto Rico6,32– 34. SWMP data have previously been
utilized to characterize spatial and temporal patterns in water quality35– 37,
investigate the importance of autotrophic and heterotrophic processes38,39,
understand mechanisms regulating phytoplankton biomass40,a n dq u a n t i f y
the effects of eutrophication13 and ocean acidiﬁcation41 on water quality.
Given the range of geomorphologies and climatic conditions of estuaries
within the NERRS, application of sta ndardized methods across all mon-
itored sites, and time span of sample collection (i.e., ~ 20 years included in
this analysis), the SWMP is uniquely positioned to address large-scale spatial
and temporal questions regarding the status and recent trajectory of
estuarine conditions in the United States33,42. Here, we document the status
of estuarine ecosystems across the NERRS using standardized in situ
monitoring data, detail decadal-scale trends in key environmental para-
meters, and investigate factors contributing to trends in eutrophication and
oxygen declines. Our analysis brings together > 250 million data points
collected from 129 stations from 2002 to 2022, providing a unique inves-
tigation of in situ estuarine conditions at a continental scale.

Trends in estuarine conditions
We analyzed trends for 99 study sites and found that the majority of sites
across 27 U.S. estuaries are experiencing signiﬁcant, increasing trends in chl-
a (70%) and water temperature (66%), whereas half of sites (49%) exhibited
signiﬁcant reductions in DO concentrations (Fig. 3 and Supplementary
Data 4). Ourﬁndings are consistent with evidence of widespread warming,
eutrophication, and hypoxia fro m other recent studies of coastal
environments11,44. Further, the overall increase in temperature exhibited by
most estuaries almost assuredly played a key role in reducing DO levels (as
documented in the section below). Despite the proportion of sites exhibiting
oxygen declines, only eight sites exhibited signiﬁcant increasing trends in the
proportion of time under hypoxic conditions (DO < 2 mg/L; APA-EB, SAP-
DC, TJR-BR, TJR-OS, ELK-PW, HUD-TN, OWC-DR, and SFB-GC; Slopes
are reported in Supplementary Data 4 and reserve/station codes are in
Supplementary Data 1), suggesting that while trends in DO are generally
negative, water quality conditions have not yet deteriorated to the degree to
which signi ﬁcant negative ecological impacts related to low DO con-
centrations would be observed.

The equal weight assigned to trends in chl-a and temperature for
predicting changes in DO concentrations could be tied to several complex
ecological processes. Temperature directly governs oxygen solubility and is
therefore inversely related to DO concentrations; however, solubility alone
does not account for changes in DO in this dataset (Supplementary Fig. 9).
The importance of chl-a trends in predicting changes in DO (negatively
related to DO trend) may result from increased oxygen consumption via
accelerated organic matter decomposition, which can in some cases reduce
oxygen sufﬁciently to alter redox conditions and reintroduce dissolved
nutrients into the water column tha t can fuel future phytoplankton
growth62; however, our analyses do not indicate that instances of hypoxia are
increasing over time. Still, there are some locations where the positive
feedback between eutrophication and hypoxia is likely being demonstrated.
For example, Old Woman Creek (OWC) NERR in Ohio is not experiencing
increasing temperature but is experiencing signiﬁcant increases in chl-a and
decreases in DO, as well as an increasing trend in hypoxia at the Darrow
Road site (OWC-DR, Supplementary Data 1). Interestingly, there are dec-
adal nutrient declines in this estuary, and warming trends are mild, which
are two main drivers of phytoplankton growth (i.e., chl-a increases) in this
study. Climate is expected to have a moderate effect on warming air tem-
perature throughout this region and increase the frequency and magnitude
of large, episodic precipitation events63. In the OWC estuary, storm events
drive nutrient inputs into the system, as opposed to longer-term increases in
nutrient loading64. Storm-driven nutrient pulses are not well-represented in
our analyses because the SWMP proto col purposely avoids anomalous
storm events to build a time series that is representative of average condi-
tions. Thus, in many instances, it is possible that the episodic processes (e.g.,
storms) that can contribute to long-term change are not well-
represented65,66.
The positive relationship between median SpCond and DO trends
(Fig.5) probably reﬂects the location of sites along the freshwater-to-marine
gradient. Lower SpCond sites experience more freshwater in ﬂuence, and
these sites are also the most productive, as indicated by median chl-a con-
centration (Fig.1), resulting in more oxygen depletion as described above.
However, chl-a is more directly link ed to phytoplankton biomass rather
than primary productivity, which can be controlled by myriad factors such
as resource limitation and species-speciﬁc responses. There is also a positive
relationship between median chl-a ( trophic status) and DO and chl-a
trends. That is, the highest rates of DO declines are occurring at sites with
lowest median chl-a (i.e, the least productive sites), and the highest rates of
increase in chl-a are the most productive sites (Fig. 6). This is surprising
given the inverse relationship between DO trends and chl-a trends; however,
sites with low median chl-a also have the highest rates of temperature
increase, suggesting that the linkage between median chl-a and DO trend is
actually another signal of temperature effects on DO, as opposed to a
relationship with trophic state.
We were unable to elucidate the effects of longer-term cycles such as
the Paciﬁc Decadal Oscillation (PDO) and Atlantic Multidecadal Oscilla-
tion (AMO), as our dataset is not lon g enough to capture these processes
spanning more than 30 years67. However, we acknowledge that these cycles
may have signiﬁcant implications for water quality in estuaries, especially for
temperature and salinity (via drought). The PDO is currently in a cool phase
since the mid to late 2000s, while the AMO is in a warm phase that began
around 1995 and typically lasts 60-80 years. The AMO warm phase is likely a
signiﬁcant contributor to the widespread increase in temperature on sites
located on the U.S. East Coast, while the PDO cool phase may be responsible
for the nonsigni ﬁcant trends in temperature in the Paci ﬁcN o r t h w e s t ;
however, we do still observe some high rates of warming along the West
Coast. These direct effects on temperature likely have subsequent effects on
DO, as oxygen solubility is strongly tied to temperature, which was also
identiﬁed as a key driver in our analysis. In addition to impacting SpCond
(salinity), drought conditions driven by these cycles may also in ﬂuence
nutrient loading to these systems through changes in runoff delivery as well
as biogeochemical cycling of nutrients, but we were not able to test this
directly.

We have shown that warming temperatures and altered nutrient
concentrations spurred a series of changes to multiple dynamically inter-
acting parameters in coastal waters (chl-a, nutrient, and DO concentrations)
across the U.S. that were captured by the NERRS SWMP dataset. Altered
nutrient concentrations, not temperature, appear to be the primary driver of
increases in chl-a in estuarine systems included in our dataset at this scale,
while rising temperatures and algal biomass (chl-a) contribute to declining
oxygen concentrations. While we were able to identify broad-scale drivers of
chl-a and DO trends, there is likely signi ﬁcant site-speciﬁc variability in
drivers that accounts for unexplained variance in our analyses. These ana-
lyses reinforce that coastal ecosystem dynamics are complex and driven by
numerous interacting factors. Future studies that contribute a more holistic
approach to nutrient dynamics in estuarine systems will be key. For
example, investigations of how shifting nutrient concentrations, forms, and
ratios affect plankton size classes, palatability, and toxin production, and
consequences for higher trophic levels are needed in a wide range of estu-
aries with varied biogeochemistry and plankton communities. In addition,
studies that speciﬁcally examine the role of depth (i.e., stratiﬁcation vs. well-
mixed and related factors including salinity and turbidity) on DO
concentration, nutrient cycling, and phytoplankton biomass are needed
because stratiﬁcation can play a key role in nutrient partitioning as well as
setting up potentially disparate abiotic (e.g., temperature, salinity) and biotic
(e.g., chl-a) conditions. Finally, our results also highlight the importance of

To assess how well environmental conditions and trends in environ-

the prioritized variables described above. No interaction terms were inclu-

interpreting results, coefﬁcients were back-calculated to either their original

been performed).
We used linear mixed effects models to identify possible drivers of chl-a

with ΔAICc<4. AIC is the Akaike Information Criterion and AICc is cor-
rected AIC, accounting for instances of small sample sizes (as the sample size
increases, AICc converges to AIC).ΔA I C ci st h ed i f f e r e n c ei nA I C cs c o r e s
between the model under consideration and the model with the lowest AICc
score (i.e., the “best” model). Global models were constructed with the
predictors described above plus a random intercept for reserve. Models were
ﬁt using Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) with the“lme” function
of the R nlme package79,80 (v. 3.1.160). To determine whether the random
effect (reserve) was necessary to include in our models, a simple linear model
without a random effect was constructed via nlme::gls(), also using REML,
which is required when using AIC to compare models with different ran-
dom effect structures81. The models with and without a random effect were
compared via AICc, a modiﬁcation of AIC that corrects for small sample
size. When the model without the random effect was within 2 AICc of the
model with it, the random effect wa s dropped from the global model
(ultimately, a random effect was only present for the chl-a model). When the
random effect was required (e.g., chl-a models), the global model was re-ﬁt
in “lme” using Maximum Likelihood (ML) for subsequent model selection.
When the random effect was not required (e.g., DO trend models), global
models were re-ﬁt as simple linear models, using stats::lm().
All model selection, averaging, and variable importance calculations
were performed using the R MuMIn package 82 (v. 1.47.5). All-subsets
selection was conducted using the dredge() function. This resulted in 65,536
candidate models for predicted DO trends and 32,768 candidate models for
predicting trends in chl-a. Due to varying recommendations on AIC
thresholds for top model sets77,83,84 and based on the number of models that
would be included using different thresholds77, top model sets were gener-
ated for each response based on ΔAICc for thresholds of 2, 4, and 6 AICc
units. Results using ΔAICc<4 are presented here, as this cutoff was quan-
titatively similar to t he commonly used threshold of 2 while including
models that are still plausible, increasing the pool of candidate models used
in model averaging. Final standardized coef ﬁcients were generated by
averaging the top model sets with model.avg(), using the full-model method,
which is appropriate for comparing relative effect sizes when there is high
model uncertainty78. Variable importance was assessed partly by comparing
standardized coefﬁcients but also by using the ‘relative importance’ metric
calculated by the sw() function. The relative importance is the sum of Akaike
weights of models in which a predictor appears. It ranges from 0-1 and can
be interpreted as the probability that the predictor is in the“best” model77,78.
We also report how many models from the top set a predictor appeared in.
Predictors that are included in highly weighted and/or a high number of
models will have higher weights than those in few and/or low-weighted
models. Graphics were generated using the ggplot2 package in R72.
