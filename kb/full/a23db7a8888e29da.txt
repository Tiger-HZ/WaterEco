The thesis aimed at understanding the effect of both biotic and abiotic factors on Festuca ovina and its adaptation to the edaphic microhabitats, in particular, to the stress of water deficit. The study focused on a wild population of F. ovina on the Baltic Island of Öland in southern Sweden where the diploid F. ovina populations were found to carry a second copy of cytosolic phosphoglucose isomerase gene (PgiC2). This second copy, PgiC2, was originally considered as a duplication of the native PgiC1 gene, but later proved to be natural transgene of acquired horizontally from Poa species. Therefore, we questioned whether the PgiC1 and PgiC2 are adaptive in the wild population of F. ovina. The high genetic diversity within the native and transgenic Pgi loci of the grass Festuca ovina might be important in allowing this species to persist across the full range of microhabitats defined by soil moisture and pH. However, there has been no molecular survey of allelic haplotypes at these loci at the population scale, partly, because of their large size and high genetic diversity, as well as their high similarity to each other. As a result, there has also never been a comprehensive test of the role of variants at these loci in mediating local adaptation. The full-length allele sequence of PgiC1 and PgiC2 were generated and the genetic diversity of PgiC1 and PgiC2 were explored. A high level of polymorphism was obtained for PgiC1, while little variation was found in PgiC2 allele sequences. We found no strong evidence to support PgiC1 or PgiC2 being under selection at the allele level, while a test at the nucleotide site level did identify loci of both PgiC1 and PgiC2 under possible directional selection. This project provided an insight into the complex molecular characteristics of PgiC1, and laid the foundation for further research on the association between PgiC allelic combinations and the adaptation to the changing environment in F. ovina.
