A BSTRACT
The present investigation entitled “Comparative Effect of Different Foliar Nutrient Formulations on
Growth rate of Mung Bean (Vigna radiata L.)” was conducted during 2025 at the PG Experimental
Farm, Department of Agronomy, Vasantrao Naik Marathwada Krishi Vidyapeeth (VNMKV), Parbhani,
to evaluate the effect of different foliar nutrient formulations on the growth of mung bean (Vigna radi-
ata L.). The experiment was laid out in a Randomized Block Design (RBD) with eight treatments. The
treatments comprised T1 – absolute control; T2 – RDF + urea @ 2% at flowering stage; T3 – RDF +
DAP @ 2% at flowering stage; T4 – RDF + KNO3 @ 2% at flowering stage; T5 – RDF + 19:19:19 @
2% at flowering stage; T6 – RDF + 0:52:34 @ 2% at flowering stage; T7 – RDF + 12:61:0 @ 2% at
flowering stage; and T8 – RDF + Grade II @ 0.2% at flowering stage. During the course of the experi-
ment, growth parameters, namely Absolute Growth Rate (AGR) and Relative Growth Rate (RGR), were
recorded at different stages of crop growth. The results revealed that the application of RDF + 19:19:19
@ 2% at the flowering stage (T5) recorded higher Absolute Growth Rate (AGR) for plant height and
dry matter accumulation at the different stages of crop growth. Similarly, the treatment T5 – RDF +
19:19:19 @ 2% at the flowering stage recorded higher Relative Growth Rate (RGR) for dry matter ac-
cumulation across the different stages of crop growth.
Keywords: Mung bean, Foliar fertilizer, Absolute Growth Rate, Relative Growth Rate
Introduction
Pulses, commonly referred to as food legumes, rank second to cereals in terms of their production and con-
sumption in India. The World Health Organization (WHO) has suggested a daily per capita pulse consump-
tion of approximately 80 g, whereas the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) recommends a mini-
mum intake of 47 g per day. India is the world’s leading producer and consumer of pulses, contributing
nearly 32 per cent of the global pulse-growing area and about 25 per cent of total production. Among the im-
portant pulse crops, mung bean (Vigna radiata L.) contains approximately 25 per cent protein, which is
nearly two-thirds of the protein content of soybean, twice that of wheat and three times that of rice.
Moreover, mung bean protein is relatively rich in lysine, an essential amino acid that is present in limited
quantities in cereal grains (Amutha et al., 2012) [1].
India is also one of the major mung bean-producing countries in Asia, with an area of about 8.7 million
hectares, production of 8.83 million tonnes and an average productivity of 1014 kg ha−1 (Nair et al., 2022)
[2]. However, the productivity of mung bean remains relatively low, particularly because it is frequently cul-
tivated on marginal and sub-marginal soils with poor fertility and inadequate fertilizer application. Although
summer green gram has considerable yield potential, its productivity at the farmers’ level is often below the
achievable level. In particular, insufficient fertilizer use has been associated with substantial reductions in
summer green gram yield.
Foliar nutrition is widely recognized as an effective and economical approach for correcting nutrient defi-
ciencies in crops (Dixit et al., 2007) [3]. Additional nutrient supplementation through foliar application can
play an important role in improving seed yield in pulse crops (Chandrasekhar et al., 2003) [4]. Applying nu-
trients directly to the foliage provides an efficient means of meeting the crop’s nutritional requirements and
can subsequently improve yield. Compared with soil application, foliar fertilization may offer advantages by
minimizing nutrient losses caused by leaching and fixation. It can also promote photosynthetic activity and
facilitate the movement of nutrients from leaves to developing seeds (Manonmani et al., 2009) [5].
Foliar spraying provides a rapid means of supplying nutrients to growing plants. This approach can be par-
ticularly useful under rainfed conditions, where limited soil moisture may restrict nutrient uptake through the
root system. Although foliar feeding cannot completely replace soil fertilization, it can serve as an effective
supplementary practice. Appropriate foliar nutrition may improve fertilizer-use efficiency, reduce unneces-
sary fertilizer losses and lower the overall cost of cultivation, thereby contributing to more economical crop
production (Amutha et al., 2012) [1].

Materials and Methods
“Comparative effect of different foliar nutrient formulation on growth
rate of mung bean (Vigna radiata L.)” was conducted during kharif
2025 at PG Experimental farm, Department of Agronomy, Vasantrao
Naik Marathwada Krishi Vidyapeeth, Parbhani in Marathwada region
of Maharashtra state. The experimental site is geographically situated
at an altitude of 409 m Above Mean Sea Level (AMSL) between
19°16 North latitude, 76°47' East longitude in Marathwada region of
Maharashtra state, encompassed by 17°35’ to 24°40' North latitude
and 74°49’ to 78°15' East longitude geographical boundaries. The site
of the experimentation comes under assured rain fall zone out of nine
agro-climatic zones of Maharashtra.
The experiment was laid out in a Randomized Block Design (RBD)
comprising eight treatments.. The treatments are T1- absolute control
T2- RDF + Urea @ 2% at flowering stage T3- RDF + DAP @ 2% at
flowering stage T4- RDF + KNO3 @ 2% at flowering stage T5- RDF
+ . 19:19:19 @ 2% at flowering stage T6- RDF + 0:52:34 @ 2% at
flowering stage T7- RDF + 12:61:0 @ 2% at flowering stage and T8-
RDF + Grade II @ 0.2% at flowering stage.

Conclusion
The foliar application of nutrients at flowering stage significantly in-
fluenced the growth and yield attributes of green gram. Among the
treatments, application of RDF + 19:19:19 @ 2% at flowering stage
recorded significantly higher plant height, number of functional
leaves, number of branches, leaf area, dry matter accumulation, and
growth indices like AGR, RGR, and LAI at early stages.
