Effect of non-conventional dietary insoluble fiber on 
growth, nutrient digestibility, intestinal morphology, 
and carcass traits of male broilers
Vahid Reza Parsa · Yahya Ebrahimnezhad · Naser Maheri-Sis · Seyed Abdullah Hosseini · 
Tohid Vahdatpour
Received: 19 November 2025 / Accepted: 27 August 2026
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract
The effects of including wheat bran (WB), corn bran (CB), and corncobs (CC) (as sources of insoluble fiber) 
at two levels (2 and 4%) in the diet on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, carcass traits, and intestinal 
morphology were examined in male broilers from1 to 42 days (d) of age. A total of 294 one-day-old male 
Cobb-500 chickens were assigned to seven dietary treatments (a control diet and six diets containing WB, 
CB, or CC at 2 or 4%) with six replicates per treatment in a completely randomized design. The six fiber-
containing diets were also analyzed as a 3 × 2 factorial arrangement to evaluate the effects of fiber source and 
inclusion level. From 1 to 42 days of age, FI for birds fed CC was statistically lower than for birds fed WB, 
CB, or control (P < 0.001). In the same period when CB was used as a source of insoluble fiber, growth traits, 
the ratio of villus height to crypt depth (VH: CD), apparent digestibility of the ether extract and crude protein 
improved significantly, which was more noticeable at the 4% level (P < 0.001). Birds that used fiber diets had 
more extended villus height than the control, and the highest VH: CD was observed at 4%WB, followed by 
4% CB diet (P < 0.001). Growth traits, nutrient digestibility, and VH: CD increased with increasing fiber levels 
from 2 to 4% (P < 0.05). The CB-containing diet showed superior performance to the other sources, and its 
moderate inclusion maintained bird performance comparable to the control.
Keywords Broilers performance · Dietary fiber · Corn bran · Corncob · Wheat bran
Introduction
As a vital component of global livestock production, poultry farming plays a key role in food security by supply-
ing affordable, nutrient-rich meat and eggs. Driven by population growth, rising incomes, and changing dietary 
preferences, the global demand for poultry products continues to steadily increase, particularly in developing 
nations1. However, domestic production in these regions remains limited, primarily due to the high cost of feed. 
In this situation, the use of low-cost feed ingredients in the poultry industry is economically important, and agri-
cultural and industrial by-products such as fiber sources may be used to reduce feed costs2.

In addition to economic challenges, diseases caused by pathogenic microorganisms and digestive disorders 
have increased with the high productivity characteristics of modern poultry. Consequently, antibiotics were 
widely used to prevent and treat the mentioned disorders as well as to promote growth, effects that are probably 
related to changes in the gastrointestinal microflora 3. However, the use of antibiotics in food animals has raised 
ethical concerns regarding animal and human health. Therefore, the World Health Organization (WHO) called for 
a ban on antibiotics in poultry feed and for finding safe and reliable alternative antibiotics4. To achieve this objec-
tive, certain types of fibers in poultry diets have been proposed as a dietary intervention to promote the health and 
microflora of the gastrointestinal tract5. In this respect, contrary to published results of the anti-nutritional effects 
of fiber as a diet diluents6, recent studies have demonstrated the potential effects of insoluble fiber on improving 
gastrointestinal tract (GIT) development, nutrient digestibility, growth performance 6,7, and intestinal morphol -
ogy7,8. Pronounced responses have been observed when moderate levels (2 to 3%) of insoluble fiber are included 
in the diet9,10. These effects also depend on the nature (physico-chemical properties) of the fiber source used 11. 
Particle size, solubility, water holding capacity (WHC), viscosity and fermentability of dietary fiber are key fac-
tors that affect feed intake, organ development, nutrient digestibility, and GIT microflora 12. Considering these 
features, the soluble fraction of dietary fiber increases the digesta viscosity, which may inhibit the diffusion and 
absorption of nutrients in the small intestine13.
Despite these insights, the most effective sources and optimal inclusion levels of insoluble fiber in poultry diets 
remain unclear. Most studies have focused on conventional insoluble fiber sources commonly used in broiler 
diets, such as oat hulls (OH), rice bran (RB), wheat bran (WB), or sunflower hulls (SFH), while less attention 
has been given to corn by-products, including corncobs (CC) and corn bran (CB). These materials differ in their 
physicochemical characteristics and fiber composition, particularly in the proportions of hemicellulose, cellulose, 
and lignin, which may influence gastrointestinal function, nutrient digestibility, and growth performance in broil-
ers. Corncobs are classified as lignocellulose, and there are efforts to use it as animal feed 14, with higher ADF 
(Acid detergent fiber) and NDF (Neutral detergent fiber) than wheat bran (WB), while WHC and fermentability 
for CC are lower14,15. Both wheat and corn bran are known as completely insoluble fiber sources; however, CB 
contains higher amounts of water-unextractable arabinoxylan and lower amounts of water-extractable arabinox-
ylan8,16,17. Therefore, evaluating these under-studied corn-based fiber sources may provide additional insight into 
the functional role of different insoluble fibers in broiler diets.
Given this background, the present study investigated the effects of the inclusion of various sources of insolu-
ble fiber (WB, CB, and CC) at two levels of 2 and 4% on productive performance, nutrient digestibility, carcass 
traits, and intestinal morphology. It was hypothesized that the source and inclusion level of dietary insoluble fiber 
could influence these parameters, and that certain non-conventional sources might provide comparable or even 
improved outcomes relative to conventional sources.
Materials and methods
Ethical statement
The Institutional Animal Care and Ethics Committee of the Animal Science Research Institute (ASRI), Karaj, 
Iran, approved all procedures used in this experiment. The research protocol advises animal rights and welfare 
by ensuring minimal stress to animals. All procedures were carried out by the relevant research protocol. The 
experiment was carried out in keeping with the ARRIVE guidelines.
Insoluble fiber sources and diets
Three batches of CB, CC (Faravari Fructose Nab, Alborze, Iran), and WB (were purchased from a commercial 
supplier), by-products of the starch industry, were ground using a hammer mill (2-mm screen) and analyzed for

chemical composition (Table 1). These three substances were used as insoluble fiber sources in this experiment. 
The control diet was based on corn and soybean meal, and the remaining diets were produced by diluting (w/w) 
the control diet with 2 or 4% insoluble fiber sources (WB, CB, or CC). All diets were formulated to meet or exceed 
the nutrient requirements for broiler chickens recommended in the Cobb-500 Broiler Performance and Nutrition 
Supplement (Cobb-Vantress, 2018)18 across the different rearing phases (1–10 d or starter, 11–20 d or grower, 
21–30 d or finisher 1,and 31–42 d or finisher 2) (Tables 2 and 3). Ingredient proportions were adjusted during 
formulation to ensure that all experimental diets were approximately isoenergetic and isonitrogenous despite the 
inclusion of the fiber sources. Diet formulation and ingredient balancing were performed using Microsoft Excel. 
All diets were provided in mash form. Chromium oxide (0.3%) was added to all diets as an indigestible marker.
Husbandry and experimental design
The experiment was conducted at the Animal Science Research Institute (ASRI), Karaj, Iran. A total of 294 one-
day-old male Cobb-500 chicks with an initial BW of 39.0 ± 2.10 g were obtained from a commercial hatchery 
(Sabzdasht, Abhar, Iran) and randomly allocated to seven treatments and six replicates of seven birds each in a 
completely randomized design. The treatments included a control diet and six fiber-treated diets. Chicks were 
housed in cemented floor pens (1.5 × 1 m) equipped with a drinker and one trough feeder and furnished with 
wood shaving as bedding material for 42 d. On day 35, two birds from each pen with BW close to the average 
BW per pen were transferred to individual cages (60 × 60 × 40 cm3) to measure nutrient digestibility. According 
to the Cobb-500 Manual (2018), the breeding room temperature was set at 33 °C during the first week. Then, the 
temperature was decreased according to the age of the chicks until reaching 24 °C atarate of 3 °C each week, and 
it was then kept constant until the end of the experiment. Chickens were reared on a 23 h/daylight program and 
had free access to feed and water during the experimental period.
Analytical evaluation of ingredients and excreta
Fiber sources were analyzed for moisture by oven-drying (method 934.01), total ash by muffle furnace (method 
942.05), crude protein (CP) by Kjeldahl method (method 984.13), crude fiber (CF) by sequential extraction with 
dilute acid and alkali (method 978.10) and ether extract (EE) was measured by Soxhlet extraction after 3 N HCl 
acid hydrolysis (method 920.39), as described by AOAC International 19. Gross energy was determined using 
an isoperibol bomb calorimeter (model 1356, Parr Instrument Company, US) according to standard procedures.

Apparent digestibility of nutrients
The procedure for excreta collection and sample preparation was conducted according to the method described 
previously8,21. From 40 to 42 days, plastic pans were used to collect representative excreta samples. The samples 
were collected twice a day on two consecutive days and stored (− 20 °C) as collected. At the time of testing, 
excreta samples were thawed, pooled by replicate (two birds per replicate pen), homogenized, and dried in an 
oven at 60 °C for 72 h, and then ground with a laboratory grinder equipped with a 1-mm screen. Using chromium 
oxide as an indicator, the indigestible marker method determined the total tract apparent digestibility of DM, EE, 
and CP. Nutrient digestibility was calculated using the following equation:
 Nutrient digestibility = 100 [1− ( MCD
MCX ) × ( NCX
NCD )]
MCD and MCX represent marker content in the diet, and excreta NCX and NCD are the nutrients in excreta and 
diet, respectively. It should be noted that the concentration of chromium oxide was determined according to a 
method described previously22.
Carcass traits
At 42 days of age, two birds from each replicate pen, with BW close to the mean BW of that pen, were individu-
ally weighed and slaughtered. The weights of the eviscerated carcass (without the liver, spleen, pancreas, and the 
GIT and its contents), liver, heart, pancreas, breast, and thigh of each bird were determined. The relative weight 
of the eviscerated carcass and organs was expressed relative to live BW (percentage of live body weight).
Intestinal morphology
Intestinal morphology was evaluated according to a procedure previously reported. Briefly 23, fixed intestinal 
tissue was collected from the jejunum (midway between the point of entry of the bile ducts and Meckel’s diver -
ticulum), washed, dehydrated, and cleared, and then, samples were embedded in paraffin. Serial sections with a 
thickness of 5 µm were cut and placed on a glass slide for staining. Finally, villus height (µm) and crypt depth 
(µm) were measured using a light microscope at 40× magnification (Olympus CX31, Japan), and using those 
data, the villus height to crypt depth ratio was calculated.
Statistical analysis
Data was evaluated for a normality test and analyzed as a completely randomized design with seven treatments 
(diet) by the general linear model (GLM) procedure of SAS 9.1 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Each pen was 
considered as an experimental unit for all traits. Differences were considered significant at the P < 0.05, and when 
a significant effect was detected, differences between means (diets) were separated using Tukey's test. In addition, 
the effect of fiber source (WB, CB, and CC), fiber inclusion level (2 and 4%), and the interaction between fiber 
source and level were subjected to a 3 × 2 factorial arrangement for further analysis.

Results
Productive performance
The effects of fiber sources and diets on BWG, FI and FCR were significant at all ages (Table 4). However, fiber 
levels only had significant effects on BWG and FCR. In addition, the interaction effects were detected on growth 
performance (BWG, FI, and FCR) between the source of fiber and inclusion level (P < 0.01). From 1 to 21 d of 
age, broilers fed the CB-treated diet had higher BWG than those fed the WB or CC-treated diet (P < 0.001), and 
regardless of the fiber source, BWG was higher when the fiber level increased from 2 to 4% (P < 0.001). The 
highest and lowest BWG belonged to the 4% CB and 4% CC diets, respectively (P < 0.001). Similar results were 
observed from 22 to 42 and 1 to 42 d of age, although BWG was not significantly different between the 4% CB 
and control. From 1 to 21 d and 1 to 42 d of age, broilers fed the CB or WB-containing diets had greater FI than 
those fed the CC-containing diet (P < 0.001), and this parameter was statistically similar between the 4% WB, 2% 
CB, 4% CB and control diets (P < 0.001). However, from 22 to 42 d of age, 4% CB had a lower FI compared to the 
control (P < 0.001). In addition, broilers fed the CB-containing diet in this period had the highest FI (P < 0.001).

From 1 to 21 d of age, FCR was better for broilers fed the CB diet than for broilers fed the CC diet, with those fed 
the WB being intermediate (P < 0.01) and birds fed the 4% CB diet had better FCR than birds fed other diets, but 
not compared to birds fed the control diet (P < 0.001). The results also showed that increasing the level of dietary 
fiber from low to moderate led to an improvement in FCR (P < 0.001). Similar results were observed from 22 to 
42 and 1 to 42 d of age, with the difference that CB had an even better FCR than WB (P < 0.001).
The analysis revealed significant effects of fiber source (P < 0.001), fiber level (P < 0.001), their interaction 
(P < 0.001), and diet (P < 0.001) on EER and EPEI (Table 5). Similar to what was observed for FCR, at all ages, 
broilers fed the 4% CB diet had better EER than those fed other diets, but not compared to broilers fed the control 
diet, and increasing dietary fiber up to a moderate leveled to an improvement in EER. From 1 to 21 d of age, EER 
was better for broilers fed the CB diet than for broilers fed the CC diet, with those fed the WB being intermediate 
(P < 0.05). However, from 22 to 42 and 1 to 42 d of age, broilers on the CB diet had the highest EER. At 42 d of 
age, the highest EPEI value was related to the CB diet, which increased (P < 0.001) with an increase in the level 
of insoluble fiber. In addition, EPEI in the CB diet was similar to that in the control diet but higher than in the 
other diets.
Apparent digestibility of nutrients
Overall, significant interactions were found in the apparent digestibility of nutrients (P < 0.001) (Table 6). ADDM, 
ADEE, and ADCP were highest for the CB-containing diet (P < 0.001). The lowest values of the ADDM and 
ADEE were related to the CC diets (P < 0.001), and broilers fed the WB diets had the lowest CP digestibility 
(P < 0.001). Moreover, ADDM (P < 0.01) and ADCP (P < 0.001) began to increase with an increasing fiber inclu -
sion level. A comparison of the means between the experimental diets showed that ADDM was the highest for 
broilers fed the control diet, but ADCP and ADEE were lower than the 4% CB diet (P < 0.001).

Intestinal morphology
According to the significant effects of experimental diets on villus height (VH), crypt depth (CD), and villus

Carcass traits
Data for carcass traits at 42 days of age are presented (Table 8). According to the results, broiler carcass traits 
were not affected by dietary fibers.
Discussion

showed that moderate amounts of insoluble fiber in the bird diets did not affect FI11. In agreement with these find-
ings, increasing the level of fiber included from low to moderate levels in the present study did not significantly 
affect FI; however, FI was lower in the CC diet than in the control diet and even lower than in the diets contain -
ing the other two fiber sources. This response could be related to the physicochemical characteristics of the fiber

which can reduce the feed passage rate and increase digesta retention time in the GIT 11. As a result, birds may 
reach physical satiety earlier and reduce voluntary feed intake. In contrast, CB mainly comprises hemicellulose 
and only a small amount of lignin 16, which may exert a less pronounced effect on gut fill and digesta retention. 
Therefore, the response of FI to dietary fiber may depend not only on the inclusion level but also on the fiber 
source, lignification degree, and palatability of the diet24,25. In addition, the effects of dietary fiber may vary with 
the age of broilers, as older birds have a more developed gastrointestinal tract and greater capacity to utilize 
structural components of the diet. In the present study, growth traits (BWG, FCR, EER, and EPEI) improved in 
birds fed the CB-containing diet compared with those fed other fiber sources, which may be partly explained by 
the relatively higher FI observed in this treatment. It is interesting to note in this context that fiber-containing diets 
tended to reduce the growth characteristics of birds compared to the control, while this effect was the opposite 
with 4% CB. Similarly, birds on the CB diet showed higher FI and consequently, better weight gain and FCR 
than those on the brewer’s dried grain and palm kernel meal diets; however, the studied fiber sources significantly 
reduced the mentioned traits compared to the control diet 26. Previous studies have also reported that neither the 
fiber source (OH, RH, or SFH) nor the level of inclusion (2.5% or 5%) affected FI and growth6. However, positive 
age-dependent effects of OH on bird growth were observed27. On the other hand, our findings showed an increase 
in DF level from 2 to 4% improved growth traits in chickens without affecting FI. This is consistent with earlier 
reports that stated that the inclusion of insoluble fiber (low to medium levels) in a basal diet did not affect average 
daily FI but improved average daily gain and FCR 28. In contrast, the inclusion of 10% CC was found to impair 
broiler growth and nutrient digestibility, but FI was not affected29. These findings suggest that the effects of fiber 
supplementation on the productive performance of broilers are associated with changes in nutrient digestibility12 
and the physicochemical characteristics of the fiber source.

increases nutrient availability for digestion. Improved gizzard function may also regulate digesta passage rate 
and provide more time for enzymatic digestion and nutrient absorption in the small intestine 11. Consequently, 
moderate levels of insoluble fiber may enhance the utilization of nutrients. In addition, the superior response 
observed with CB may be related to its specific fiber composition, which generally contains a higher proportion 
of hemicellulose and lower lignin content compared with more lignified materials such as CC. Such structural 
characteristics may promote beneficial mechanical stimulation of the gizzard without excessively limiting nutri-
ent accessibility, thereby improving digestibility. Previous studies have similarly reported improved nutrient 
digestibility in broilers when moderate amounts of insoluble fiber sources such as oat hulls, wheat bran, or soy -

lulose increased CP losses to the lumen at the end of the ileum in chickens, which may be related to the abrasive 
effect of dietary fiber on the intestinal wall11.
In the current assessment, almost all fiber-containing diets increased villus height without markedly affecting 
crypt depth, except for diets containing 2% WB or 2% CC. The ratio of villus height to crypt depth increased with 
CB supplementation compared to the control and two other fiber sources, which reflects the promotion of muco-
sal absorption area of the small intestine 34, and this effect was more dramatic with 4% CB. In agreement with 
previous reports7,11, the improved intestinal morphological circumstances observed in chickens fed a diet supple-
mented with CB were associated with higher growth performance and greater digestibility of nutrients. It should 
be noted that the intestinal morphological structure in this study was also improved in birds fed the 4% WB diet, 
which is not compatible with its adverse effects on nutrient digestibility and growth performance compared to 
birds fed the 4% CB diet. This discrepancy may indicate that the observed morphological changes represent an 
adaptive response of the intestine to dietary fiber rather than a direct improvement in nutrient utilization. A pos-
sible explanation could be related to the physicochemical characteristics of wheat bran fiber, which may influence 
digesta properties in the gastrointestinal tract, potentially including increased viscosity, that may adversely affect 
digestion and nutrient absorption17,28. However, these parameters were not directly measured in the present study. 
Regardless of the fiber source, increasing dietary fiber has significantly improved the mentioned parameters. Sev-
eral studies showed that the inclusion of large amounts of insoluble fiber (lignin or sunflower meal) in the diet had 
adverse effects on the structure and vitality of the mucosal surface of the GIT33,35, while low to moderate amounts 
had positive effects8,36 or were ineffective37,38. Insoluble fiber may mechanically activate the intestinal mucosal 
surface, and this effect probably depends on the type, source, and level of included fiber11.
Regarding carcass traits, the inclusion of insoluble fiber from different sources and levels did not affect carcass 
characteristics in the present study. Similar findings have been reported in previous literature 27,37. One possible 
explanation is that moderate levels of insoluble fiber mainly influence digestive physiology and gastrointestinal 
development rather than altering nutrient partitioning toward carcass tissues. In addition, when diets are formu -
lated to meet the nutrient requirements of broilers, moderate fiber inclusion is unlikely to significantly change 
the availability of energy and amino acids required for muscle deposition or organ development. Therefore, liver, 
heart, and other carcass components may remain relatively unaffected by moderate dietary fiber inclusion.
Overall, moderate amounts of insoluble fiber generally resulted in more favorable responses than lower 
amounts across several evaluated traits. However, these effects were not consistent across fiber sources, as indi -
cated by the significant source × level interaction effects. In particular, increasing corn bran inclusion to 4% was 
associated with improved responses, whereas the same increase in corncob resulted in less desirable outcomes. 
Therefore, the optimal inclusion level of insoluble fiber may depend on the physicochemical characteristics of 
the fiber source.
Conclusion
As a general result in this study, the source and level of insoluble fiber inclusion influenced the response of birds 
to dietary fiber. Still, the age of chickens had minor effects. In this experiment, broilers fed diets diluted with corn 
bran (compared to the wheat bran or corncob) showed improved productive performance, likely due to increased 
feed intake and nutrient use efficiency. These effects were more dramatic with medium amounts of corn bran, and 
interestingly, 4% of corn bran had no negative effects on the performance and production of birds compared to the 
control. Moderate amounts of insoluble fiber, compared to low amounts, positively affected most of the evaluated 
traits. However, the response to dietary fiber was also source-dependent, as the effects of inclusion level were not 
uniform across fiber sources.
