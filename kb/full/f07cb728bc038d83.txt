1. Introduction
Injury documentation is central to medico-legal investigations
andservesasthebasisforreconstructingfacts,determiningthe
cause and manner of death, and providing objective evidence
incourtproceedings.1Accuracyandreliabilityindocumenting
injuries are thus vital to forensic practice. Two-dimensional
(2D) photographs have traditionally been the gold standard in
documentinginjuriesinpostmortemandlivingexaminations.2
They are considered a reliable means of keeping a permanent
recordandarereadilyacceptedinmedico-legalinvestigations.
While they remain in common use, 2D photographs still
have certain shortcomings that limit their ability to document
injuries accurately and reliably.3 Some of these shortcomings
include perspective distortion, a lack of depth perception,
differences in camera angles, and the subjective nature of
operatorskill.4
In recent times, innovations in imaging techniques
have given rise to the use of 3D surface scanning and
photogrammetry in forensic sciences.5 Through 3D imaging,
one can achieve surface morphology in great detail, in terms
ofdepthandcontours,aswellasspatialpositioningofinjuries,
whichcannotbeeffectivelyaccomplishedthrough2Dimaging.
The ability to form a 3D digital model makes morphological
analysis possible, along with repetitive examination, without
anylossofevidence.6
3D imaging in forensic medicine has been receiving
more attention around the world. Specifically, there has
been growing interest in virtual autopsies and digital forensic
reconstructions that make use of radiographic imaging and
surfacescanningtocreateanentirelynon-invasiveexamination
process for the deceased.7 3D surface scanning, for instance,
is capable of being used to document external injuries like
abrasions, lacerations, bruises, gunshot injuries, and ligature
marks. Thetechnologycanmeasuredimensions,surfaceareas,
anddepthstoenhanceobjectivityduringforensicanalysis.8
A critical benefit of 3D imaging is that it can reduce bias

With respect to gunshot injury wounds, it is critical
to document details such as entry and exit wound sites,
abrasion rings, soot distribution, and wound morphology to

Two-dimensional imaging does not provide sufficient detail

Evenwiththesestrengths,however,theuseof3Dscanning

nations. Some issues limit its extensive application, including
cost, technical knowledge, and a lack of protocols for 3D
scanning. Furthermore, a lot of research needs to be done
comparing 3D scanning with traditional procedures.13 Much
of the research done to date involves limited samples and
very specific cases, which points towards the need for more
expansiveresearchthatcoversallinjuries.
This current study seeks to fill this gap by performing a
comparison study on the effectiveness of 3D surface scanning
versus traditional 2D photography in recording and analyzing
forensic injuries through autopsies.14 The assessment will be
based on the precision and effectiveness of each technique
in achieving its purpose. The study makes use of advanced
statistical methods such as comparative analysis, agreement

Also, this study has important consequences for the
future development of forensic medicine. The use of 3D
imaging technology in practice is likely to ensure objective
record-keeping, improve the objectivity of findings, and thus
increase their evidence value. Moreover, this technology
corresponds to current developments in this field all around
the world and follows a tendency towards increasing use of
computers in science. In connection with the advent of
artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms, 3D
imagesmayalsobeusedforpatternrecognitionandprediction.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Study design and setting
This study was designed as a prospective, comparative
observational study conducted in the Department of Forensic
MedicineandToxicologyandMortuaryofKGMU.
2.2. Sample size and study population
Thesamplesizenecessarytodetectamorphometricdifference
between 2D and 3D imaging was estimated based on the
anticipated difference between the two groups, using a paired
design. Foreffectsize=0.5, power=80%, and α =0.05, the
minimum required sample size was determined as 64 cases.
To increase accuracy and take into account missing data, 96
autopsy cases were used in the final analysis. Cases with
firearminjuriesrepresentedaminoritygroup(n=5), andany
analysesincludingfirearm-relatedfindingsaretobetreatedas
exploratory.
2.3. Inclusion criteria
Medico-legal autopsy cases presenting with one or more
identifiableexternalinjuries.
Cases with a postmortem interval (PMI) of ≤24 hours,
ensuring minimal postmortem tissue changes and optimal
visualizationofexternalinjuries.
Bodies with intact anatomical landmarks suitable for
standardized2Dphotographyand3Dsurfacescanning.
Casesinwhichcompleteinjurydocumentationusingboth
imagingmodalitiescouldbeperformedbeforeroutineautopsy
procedures.
2.4. Exclusion criteria
Bodiesinadvanceddecomposition(PMI>24hoursorshowing
significantdecompositionchanges).
Extensive tissue destruction, burns, animal predation, or
severetraumapreventingaccurateinjuryassessment.
Cases with compromised anatomical landmarks affecting
morphometricmeasurements.
Cases in which complete documentation by either 2D
photographyor3Dsurfacescanningcouldnotbeobtained.
2.5. Data collection procedure
Autopsycaseselectionensuredthatinjuriesweredocumented
through standardized injury photography and 3D surface
scanning in the same environment. The injury images were
acquired before autopsy dissection to ensure no change in the
morphologyofinjurypost-dissection.
Inthecaseof2Dphotography,high-qualitypicturesofthe
injuries were taken using a Canon EOS 90D DSLR camera
witha100mmmacrolens. Thecamerawaskeptatastandard
distance of 40-50 cm from the injury and was kept at a 90°
angle to avoid any perspective distortion. For calibration
purposesandtakingaccuratemorphometricmeasurements,an
ABFO No. 2 forensic scale was kept at the same level as the
injury. Standard lighting conditions and standard settings of
thecamerawereusedduringtheprocessofimaging.
The injuries were imaged through a structured-light 3D
imaging system called Vectra H2 (Canfield Scientific, USA)
for 3D surface imaging. This equipment has an accuracy
measurement of ±0.2 mm with a spatial resolution of
0.09–1.2mmforfacialimagingandapproximately3.5mmfor
body surface imaging as per the manufacturer’s specification.
Calibration was done before each imaging process based
on the manufacturer’s recommended calibration procedure.
The injury was imaged in three overlapping projections to
reconstructtheentiresurfaceandminimizethemissingsurface
area. The images were processed in Vectra H2 proprietary
analysissoftwarethatwassuppliedbyCanfieldScientific.
2.6. Morphometric analysis
Morphometric measurements were conducted independently
from the calibrated 2D photos and the reconstructed 3D

surface models. The length, width, and surface area
of the injury were measured using the respective image
analysis software, and all measurements were recorded in
millimetres (mm) and surface area in square millimetres

from the 3D surface models as standard 2D photography

2.7. Diagnostic feature assessment
Allthemorphometricmeasurementsandfeaturesweredoneby

2.8. Reference standard (Gold Standard)
Reference standards for morphological and diagnostic
criteria were based on direct observations done via autopsy.
Measurements of injuries in terms of size (length and
width) were conducted directly on the cadaver by means
of a calibrated digital Vernier caliper (precision ±0.01
mm). Diagnostic criteria such as morphology of wounds,
collar of abrasion, soot/stippling, and ligature marks were
defined through consensus between the two forensic medicine
specialists conducting the autopsy. Disputes were resolved
duringajointreviewoffindings. Allthesedataobtainedfrom
theautopsyservedasreferencecriteriafor2Dand3Dimaging
methods.
2.9. Statistical analysis
Continuousdatawereexpressedintermsofmean ± SD,while
categorical data were expressed in terms of frequency and
percentage. The morphometric values derived from the two
modalities were statistically compared using the paired t-test
based on the assumption of normality. Categorical data were
statisticallyanalyzedusingtheChi-squaretestorFisher’sexact
test, wherever necessary. ROC curve analysis was done for
the diagnostic performance, and Bland–Altman analysis was
done for the agreement between the two modalities. The
significancelevelwastakenasp<0.05.

Statistically significant differences were detected between

measures. Even though the differences in terms of length

TheROCcurveindicatedthatthediscriminatorypowerof

The use of 3D scanning greatly enhanced the chances of

4. Discussion
The present study demonstrates that 3D surface scanning
significantly improves morphometric accuracy and diagnostic
performance compared to conventional 2D photography
in forensic injury documentation. These findings are
consistent with previous research highlighting the limitations
of traditional photographic methods and the increasing
adoptionofadvancedimagingtechnologiesinforensicscience.
Brüschweiler et al. demonstrated that forensic
photogrammetry enables accurate three-dimensional
reconstructionofpatternedinjuriesandfacilitatescomparison
with injury-causing instruments, thereby improving objective
forensic documentation.15 Thali et al. introduced the concept
of Virtopsy, highlighting the role of advanced postmortem
imaging modalities such as multislice computed tomography
(MSCT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in forensic
investigationsratherthanconventionalinvasiveexamination.16
These findings support the present study, where 3D imaging
demonstratedsuperiormorphometricprecision.
In the current study, statistically significant differences
were observed in injury measurements, particularly in
surface area estimation. This can be attributed to
the inherent limitations of 2D photography, including
perspective distortion and lack of depth perception. Bolliger
et al. emphasized that conventional methods reduce
complex three-dimensional injuries into two-dimensional
representations, thereby compromising measurement
accuracy and diagnostic interpretation.17 In contrast, 3D
imaging enables true spatial representation, enhancing
measurement reliability and objectivity. Building on these
developments, Urbanová et al. demonstrated the feasibility
and reliability of photogrammetry-based three-dimensional
surface documentation for external injuries in forensic
pathology.18 The findings of the present study are consistent
withthesereports,showingthat3Dsurfacescanningprovides
improved morphometric characterization and enhanced
visualization of external injuries compared with conventional
2Dphotography.
The higher diagnostic performance observed for 3D
surface scanning should be interpreted in relation to
the reference standard established during direct autopsy
examination. Becausebothimagingmodalitieswereevaluated
against standardized physical measurements and expert
consensus, the calculated sensitivity, specificity, and ROC
values represent their ability to identify injury characteristics
consistent with the reference diagnosis. The findings indicate
that 3D surface scanning more closely approximated the
referencestandardthanconventional2Dphotographyreported
byUrbanováetal.18
Improved detection of forensic features such as abrasion
collars, soot deposition, and ligature patterns in this study is
consistentwithfindingsfromBorniketal., whodemonstrated
that multimodal 3D data significantly enhance forensic case
analysisandthevisualizationofsubtleinjurycharacteristics.19
This is particularly relevant in firearm and asphyxial deaths,
whereaccurateinterpretationofinjurypatternsisessentialfor
medico-legalconclusions.
Agreement analysis using Bland–Altman plots showed
wide limits of agreement between 2D photography and 3D
surface scanning, suggesting systematic differences between
measurements obtained using the two imaging modalities.
However, as no independent reference standard was available,
these findings should not be taken to imply that one method
is intrinsically more accurate than the other. Instead,
the analysis indicates that the two techniques are not
directly interchangeable for the morphometric evaluation.
This observation is supported by findings of Hejna et al.,
who emphasized that conventional photography is prone to
observer-dependent errors and lacks reproducibility, whereas
digital imaging systems provide more standardized and
consistentdocumentation.20
Furthermore, 3D imaging allows digital preservation,
re-analysis, and reproducibility of forensic evidence, which
are critical in medico-legal investigations. Studies on virtual
autopsy techniques have highlighted that 3D datasets can
be archived and re-evaluated without loss of information,
thereby improving transparency and reliability in court
presentations.16,17
One of the major advantages of 3D surface scanning
demonstratedinthisstudyistheabilitytoobjectivelymeasure
the depth of an injury, a parameter that cannot be reliably
measured using conventional 2D photography. The depth
measurements provide additional morphological information
that improves injury characterization and may improve
forensicreconstructionandmedico-legalinterpretation.
Although 3D surface scanning requires higher initial
equipment costs and operator training, it provides digital
archiving, repeatable measurements, objective documentation,
and reduced observer variability. Conventional photography
remains less expensive and easier to implement but lacks
depthinformationandstandardizedmorphometricassessment.
Future cost-effectiveness studies are required before routine
adoption.
A direct statistical comparison to 2D photography was
not possible as traditional photographs do not have depth
information, but this unique ability offers a major practical
advantagefor3Dimaginginforensicdocumentation.
Despite these advantages, the widespread implementation
of 3D imaging remains limited due to cost and technical
expertise requirements. However, with ongoing technological
advancements and increasing accessibility, its integration into
routine forensic practice is expected to improve significantly.
The incorporation of artificial intelligence and machine
learning with 3D imaging may further enhance automated
injurydetectionandforensicinterpretation.
5. Conclusion
Our study showed that 3D surface scanning provides better
morphometric characterization and visualization of external
forensic injuries than conventional 2D photography. It
gave a better evaluation of surface area measurements
and improved detection of some forensic features, thus
supporting its potential role in forensic documentation.
However, results should be interpreted in the context of the
single-center design, limited number of firearm injury cases,
and absence of an independent reference standard. Further

large-scale multicenter studies with standardized imaging

6. Limitations
Study limitations: The study was conducted at one center,
and the sample size was relatively small, which may limit
the generalizability of the findings. The small number of
firearminjurieslimitedthepowerofsubgroupanalyses. There
is no independent reference standard available for validation
of the morphometric measurements, and therefore the
comparisonbetweenimagingmodalitiesshouldbeinterpreted
asagreementandnotabsoluteaccuracy.
Interrater reliability was not calculated, and bodies that
weredecomposedorseverelydamagedwereexcluded. Future
workisneededinlarger,multicenterstudiesusingstandardized
protocolsandvalidatedreferencestandards.
7. Recommendations
The results of the present study suggest that 3D surface
scanning should be regarded as an adjunct to conventional
2D photography for forensic injury documentation, especially
when an accurate morphometric assessment is required.
Standardised imaging protocols and adequate training are
needed to ensure consistency of forensic practice. Further
multicenter studies with larger and more heterogeneous
injury patterns, including firearm injuries and independent
reference standards, assessment of interobserver reliability,
and cost-effectiveness analysis are needed to establish the
clinicalandforensicutilityof3Dsurfacescanning.
