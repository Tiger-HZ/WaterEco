Growth and yield of hybrid Napier (Pennisetum
purpureum Schumach.) genotypes as influenced by
planting geometry and nutrient management
Vedavati N Purad1, M Shivaprasad2, HM Honnappa3, Ganapathi4, Basavarajappa Bhogi5
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/2618060X.2026.v9.i10a.6743
A BSTRACT
A field experiment entitled " Growth and Yield of Hybrid Napier (Pennisetum purpureum Schumach.)
Genotypes as Influenced by Planting Geometry and Nutrient Management" was conducted during year
2025-26 at Main Agricultural and Horticultural Research Station, Iruvakki, Shivamogga, Karnataka.
The results of the experiment showed that among the treatments, (T12) Suguna genotype at 60 x 60 cm
spacing with split application of nitrogen and potassium (180:120:80 kg NPK ha−1, N and K applied in
six equal splits) showed significantly higher values of number of tillers hill-1 (35.87), number of leaves
hill-1 (312.44), leaf:stem ratio (1.54), green fodder yield (296.69 t ha−1) and dry fodder yield (42.49 t
ha−1), respectively. However, it was on par with T11 (Suguna at 60 x 60 cm spacing with split applica-
tion of nitrogen alone), which recorded 34.02 tillers hill-1, 298.17 leaves hill-1, leaf:stem ratio of 1.50,
green fodder yield of 283.55 t ha−1 and dry fodder yield of 40.36 t ha−1. With respect to plant height,
significantly the tallest plants were recorded in Super Napier at 60 x 60 cm spacing with split applica-
tion of nitrogen and potassium (T4) with a mean of 258.59 cm, on par with Super Napier at 60 x 60 cm
spacing with nitrogen alone (T3) at 249.57 cm, while for per plant yield, significantly the highest value
was recorded in Suguna at 90 x 60 cm spacing with nitrogen and potassium (T10) at 2.33 kg plant-1.
Significantly the lowest values for tillers, leaves, leaf:stem ratio and fodder yield were recorded in Red
Napier at 90 x 60 cm spacing with split application of nitrogen alone, while the shortest plants were
recorded in Suguna at 90 x 60 cm spacing with split application of nitrogen.
Keywords: Hybrid Napier, Planting geometry, Nutrient management, Nitrogen and potassium, Green
fodder yield
Introduction
Green fodder is the main constraint on livestock productivity in India, which is short of green fodder, dry
fodder and concentrates (Krishnaveni, 2019) [1]. Since land for fodder cannot be expanded much, the need is
for high-yielding perennial grasses that supply green forage round the year. Hybrid Napier (Bajra Napier hy-
brid) suits this purpose because of its high yield, palatability and adaptability to varied soil and climatic con-
ditions (Velayudham et al., 2011) [2]. It can remain on the same field for three to four years, and repeated ra-
tooning gives a continuous supply of green forage (Biradar et al., 2020) [3].
Many hybrid Napier genotypes have been released, and they differ in plant height, tillering, leaf–stem ratio
and green fodder yield, so their suitability has to be judged under local conditions (Biradar et al., 2020) [3].
Super Napier, Red Napier and Suguna are now widely grown by dairy farmers, but there is little comparative
information on their performance in the Hilly Agro-Climatic Zone of Karnataka.
Yield in a multi-cut perennial grass also depends on crop management, especially plant spacing and nutrient
supply. Spacing determines plant population, tillering and light interception, and the optimum varies with lo-
cation and genotype (Velayudham et al., 2011; Khadda et al., 2016) [2, 4]. Nutrient supply is equally impor-
tant because every harvest removes large amounts of nutrients from the soil. Napier grass responds strongly
to nitrogen, and it also removes large quantities of potassium (Vicente-Chandler et al., 1959) [5]. Nitrogen is
normally top-dressed after each cut, but information on potassium application for Bajra Napier hybrids is
limited. Splitting the nitrogen and potassium dose across the crop's growth stages, rather than applying it all
at planting, tends to give the best returns, largely because the crop's continuous growth habit calls for a
steady nutrient supply and single large doses are prone to loss through leaching, runoff or volatilization
(Niranjana et al., 2018) [6]
There is little information on how genotype, spacing and the schedule of nutrient application interact in hy-
brid Napier under the conditions of Zone 9 of Karnataka. The present investigation was therefore carried out
to evaluate Super Napier, Red Napier and Suguna at two spacings under split application of nitrogen alone
and of nitrogen with potassium at each harvest.

Material and method
The study was carried out during 2025-26 at the Main Agricultural
and Horticultural Research Station, Iruvakki (KSNUHAS),
Shivamogga (14° 0' N Lat and 75° 42' E Long) at an altitude of 620 m
above mean sea level, situated in the Hilly Agro-Climatic Zone (Zone
9) of Karnataka. The experiment comprissed three hybrid napier
genotypes, namely Super Napier, Red Napier and Suguna, evaluated
under two spacings and two nutrient split-application schedules.
Planting was carried out on 1st August 2025 using healthy, three-
noded stem cuttings of uniform age, spaced at 90x60 cm and
60x60 cm. The experiment was laid out in Randomized Complete
Block Design with three replications. The following are the details of
treatments T1- Super Napier with 90x60 cm spacing and split applica-
tion of N at each harvest, T2- Super Napier with 90x60 cm spacing
and split application of N and K at each harvest, T3- Super Napier
with 60x60 cm spacing and split application of N at each harvest, T4-
Super Napier with 60x60 cm spacing and split application of N and K
at each harvest, T5- Red Napier with 90x60 cm spacing and split ap-
plication of N at each harvest, T6- Red Napier with 90x60 cm spacing
and split application of N and K at each harvest, T7- Red Napier with
60x60 cm spacing and split application of N at each harvest, T8- Red
Napier with 60x60 cm spacing and split application of N and K at
each harvest, T9- Suguna with 90x60 cm spacing and split application
of N at each harvest, T10- Suguna with 90x60 cm spacing and split
application of N and K at each harvest, T11- Suguna with 60x60 cm
spacing and split application of N at each harvest and T12- Suguna
with 60x60 cm spacing and split application of N and K at each har-
vest. Here, three genotypes of hybrid napier were combined with two
planting geometries and two nutrient scheduling patterns, giving
twelve treatment combinations, each replicated thrice, with a gross
plot size of 5.4 x 4.8 m and a net plot size of 3.6 x 3.6 m for
90x60 cm spacing and 4.2 x 3.6 m for 60x60 cm spacing.
Result and discussion
Plant height (Table 1), number of tillers per clump (Table 2), number
of leaves per clump (Table 3) and leaf:stem ratio (Table 4) differed
significantly among treatments under the different planting geome-
tries and nutrient management. Plant height, tillers and leaves in-
creased from the first to the sixth harvest, and the differences among
treatments were significant at every stages.
Plant height
T4 recorded the tallest plants (211.83 cm at the first harvest to
290.51 cm at the sixth; mean 258.59 cm), on par with T3 (218.29 to
281.29 cm; mean 249.57 cm). T10 was significantly the shortest
throughout (153.19 to 231.31 cm; mean 198.10 cm). The taller stature
of T4 can be explained by intra-row competition for light, where mu-
tual shading forces internode elongation (a shade-avoidance re-
sponse). Split N and K further supported cell elongation, chlorophyll
synthesis and turgor-driven growth. Nascimento et al. (2021) [7] re-
ported that synchronizing K with N through split fertilization en-
hanced forage biomass production. Dokbua et al. (2021) [8] found that
combined N and K significantly enhanced growth and biomass pro-
ductivity of hybrid napier. The short stature of T10 agrees with Seeja
et al. (2006) [9], who described Suguna as a profusely tillering but
comparatively short-statured Bajra-Napier hybrid.

No. of leaves per clump
Number of leaves per clump (Table 3), a co-component of tillering,
followed a similar trend. T9 recorded the most leaves at the first har-
vest (171.60 leaves/clump), on par with T10 (159.16). From the sec-
ond harvest onward T10 was highest (244.64 to 490.00), on par with
T9 (230.56 to 470.20). T8 was significantly the lowest throughout
(82.47 to 257.27). Since each new tiller carries its own leaves, re-
duced competition for light and space in T9 and T10, together with
better nutrient synchrony under the N + K schedule of T ₁₀ , accounts
for these results. Kaur et al. (2022) [12] reported that N improved
green fodder yield in Bajra-Napier hybrids partly through better
canopy development. Wijitphan et al. (2009) [13] reported that plant-
ing geometry significantly influenced tiller number and leaf develop-
ment in Napier grass under intensive N and irrigation. Both agree
with the present findings.

Leaf:stem ratio
Leaf:stem ratio (Table 4), a quality-linked attribute, showed the oppo-
site response, with the closer-spaced (60 × 60 cm) treatments record-
ing higher ratios. T11 was significantly the highest at the first harvest
(1.04), on par with T12 (1.00). From the second harvest onward T12
was highest (1.12 to 1.54), on par with T11 (1.02 to 1.50). T5 was sig-
nificantly the lowest throughout (0.51 to 0.79). The ratio depends on
how dry matter is partitioned between leaf and stem rather than on ab-
solute leaf production. Under closer spacing, competition for light
promotes elongation of leaf blades and sheaths relative to stem thick-
ening, because thin leaves give a better return in intercepted light per
unit of carbon than a thick lignified stem under shade. Khadda et al.
(2016) [4] similarly reported an improved leaf:stem ratio under closer
spacing in hybrid Napier in semi-arid Gujarat. Onjai-uea et al. (2023)
[14], working with purple Napier grass, similarly reported that
leaf:stem ratio was significantly influenced by the interaction of culti-
var, spacing and harvesting age, underlining that partitioning to leaf
versus stem cannot be predicted from tiller number alone. The higher
ratio of T11 and T12 is in line with Seeja et al. (2006) [9], who de-
scribed Suguna as having better leafiness and quality attributes than
conventional Bajra-Napier hybrids.

Green fodder yield (t/ha)
Green fodder yield, the most important economic trait of a fodder
crop, integrates plant height, tillering and leaf production (Table 5).
T12 recorded significantly the highest green fodder yield at every har-
vest and in total (296.69 t/ha), on par with T11 (283.55 t/ha), an im-
provement of about 4.6 per cent. T5 was significantly the lowest
(141.26 t/ha). T9 and T10 had the most tillers and leaves per plant, but
T11 and T12 gave the highest yield per unit area. The closer spacing
accommodates a larger plant population per hectare, which more than
compensates for the lower per-plant vigour. Islam et al. (2023) [15]
concluded that increasing planting density is among the most reliable
ways of raising green and dry matter yield per hectare in Napier grass.
Wijitphan et al. (2009) [13] reported that closer spacing under inten-
sive N management enhanced Napier grass productivity through im-
proved resource utilization. The additional yield of T12 over T11 is in
concurrence with Sathiya Bama (2017) [16], who reported that bal-
anced nutrient management improved green fodder yield and nutrient
uptake in Cumbu Napier hybrid. It also agrees with Tiwana et al.
(2004) [17], who reported that increasing N significantly increased
green fodder and dry matter yield of Napier-Bajra hybrids.

Per plant yield (kg /plant)
Per plant yield (Table 7) followed the growth attributes rather than the
population-driven area yield, since it reflects individual plant produc-
tivity independent of density. T10 recorded the highest (2.33 kg/plant
on average), on par with T9 (2.23 kg/plant), and T7 the lowest
(1.06 kg/plant). Wider spacing therefore gave higher per-plant yield,
while closer spacing gave higher yield per hectare. This shows that
plant population determines area-based productivity and that the two
measures are complementary. Islam et al. (2023) [15] noted a similar
dissociation in Napier grass. The higher per plant yield of T10 reflects
more efficient conversion of additional tillers and leaf area into forage
biomass, consistent with the improved nutrient availability and uti-
lization reported by Sathiya Bama (2017) [16].

Conclusion
The study revealed that Suguna genotype grown at 60 cm × 60 cm
spacing with split application of nitrogen and potassium (T12)
recorded higher growth and yield parameters of Hybrid Napier.
However, it was on par with Suguna genotype grown at 60 cm ×
60 cm spacing with split application of nitrogen alone (T11), and the
lowest was generally recorded in Red Napier genotype grown with
split application of nitrogen alone, at 60 cm × 60 cm spacing (T7) for
tillers, leaves and per plant yield, and at 90 cm × 60 cm spacing (T5)
for leaf:stem ratio and green fodder yield; plant height alone was low-
est in Suguna genotype grown at 90 cm × 60 cm spacing with nitro-
gen and potassium (T10).
