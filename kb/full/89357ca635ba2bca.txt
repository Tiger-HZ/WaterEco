Emotions are an ever-present aspect of human experience, with intense positive and negative events often leaving enduring memories. While much of the literature has focused on emotion as content, comparatively little attention has been given to emotion as context. Emotional context—the affective “hue” of an experience—persists as part of an event representation in memory and binds to neutral elements present during the episode. These neutral elements may later carry and reinstate the emotional context of the original experience. This dissertation pursued two overarching aims. First, it examined how negative emotional context is represented in the brain during memory retrieval. Chapter 2 presents a neuroimaging study in which participants encoded neutral objects paired with negative or neutral scenes and were later cued with the objects during functional magnetic resonance imaging. Multivariate analyses revealed a neural signature of negative emotional context in the ventral visual stream, reflecting reinstatement of encoding-related patterns, with more nuanced contributions from the hippocampus and ventromedial prefrontal cortex. Second, it investigated how the temporal position of neutral stimuli relative to an emotional event shapes the episodic details they carry forward. Accordingly, Chapter 3 presents a behavioural study in which two samples encoded a series of object–scene–object image sets and were later presented with one object from each set as a retrieval cue. Neutral objects encountered after a negative scene supported more specific and emotionally detailed episodic retrieval, demonstrating that emotional context preferentially binds to subsequently encountered information. Finally, these findings are situated within retrieved-context models of memory to clarify how emotional context structures episodic memory and the neural systems that support its reinstatement. Together, this work demonstrates that emotional context is a pervasive and enduring property of memory, shaping how experiences are represented, integrated, and recalled.
