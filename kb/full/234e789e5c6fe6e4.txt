Abstract
This study investigates the effect of nutrient composition and barley cultivars on saponarin production in young 
barley leaves (YBL) under low-intensity white LED light. Various treatments involving K 2HPO4, NaCl, and salicylic acid 
(SA) were evaluated to be under 6500 K light with 210 PPFD. Among different nutrient combinations, 600 mg L -1 
K2HPO4 with 0.1% NaCl significantly enhanced saponarin concentration (12.3 g kg dw
-1) and total saponarin amount 
per plant (150 µg plant -1). In addition, the effects of light intensity on saponarin production were also examined 
across six barley cultivars, and the high light intensity (500 PPFD) notably increased total saponarin amount (212 µg 
plant-1) in Keunal-bori than Hyenuri cultivars. Otherwise, Hyenuri cultivar showed the highest total saponarin 
amount (114 µg plant -1) at low light intensity (100 PPFD), as well as the highest electricity efficiency (201 mg 
kWh-1). These findings provide valuable insights into optimizing cultivation conditions for each cultivar in plant 
factories to maximize saponarin production while balancing energy consumption and metabolic efficiency.

 Introduction
Photosynthesis and carbon metabolism are greatly influ -
enced by the light quality; in particular, red light (600–
700  nm) and blue light (400–500  nm) are essential for 
these processes in plants [ 1–4]. Furthermore, the impor-
tance of full-spectrum light has recently been recognized 
due to its beneficial effects on metabolite production 
and plant growth [ 5–10]. However, when growing crops 
under artificial lighting conditions rather than sunlight, 
light energy efficiency should be considered. Therefore, 
precise controls of light quality and nutrient supplemen -
tation are a key strategy for optimizing plant growth and 
enhancing the biosynthesis of carbon-based metabolites 
in a plant factory under artificial light [ 11–17]. Recently, 
plant factories have been increasingly used to produce 
high-value phytochemicals, such as flavonoids, antho -
cyanins, and polyphenols. In particular, exposure to blue 
light and ultraviolet (UV) radiation was demonstrated 
to stimulate the biosynthesis of antioxidative secondary 
metabolites in plants [ 18–20]. However, excessive expo -
sure to short-wavelength (blue and UV) or high-intensity 
lights could elevate intracellular reactive oxygen species 
(ROS) levels in plant cells, thereby reducing photosyn -
thetic efficiency and ultimately suppressing plant growth

[21, 22]. Therefore, optimization studies of the cultivation 
environment are necessary for each crop and specific tar-
get metabolites.
Young barley leaves (YBL) are known to biosynthe -
size saponarin, a biologically active flavonoid glyco -
side, including anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective 
effects, and it is richly present in YBL [ 23–25]. In open-
field cultivation, the saponarin content was reported to 
be 11  g kg − 1 of dry weight [ 26], and similar saponarin 
levels were observed in plant factory cultivation under 
blue light or ultraviolet light treatment conditions; how -
ever, the growth suppression was observed in YBL under 
the blue or UV light conditions, and the biomass pro -
duction was not good in comparison with the open-field 
cultivation [ 27]. To determine the optimal light condi -
tion for saponarin production in a plant factory, Oh et al. 
(2021) investigated the changes in functional flavonoid 
content in response to correlated color temperature and 
cycle through a study on artificial light composition for 
saponarin biosynthesis in YBL [ 28]. Similarly, Lee et al. 
(2024) reported that saponarin biosynthesis flux in YBL 
was controlled by light-induced ROS [ 14]. Finally, they 
achieved saponarin production levels comparable to 
those observed in open-field cultivation under high-light 
intensity (≥ 500 PPFD). However, the proposed high-light 
intensity treatments were impractical for commercial 
application in plant factories due to excessive heat gen -
eration and elevated energy consumption. Therefore, 
practical conditions for reducing energy consumption 
during YBL cultivation need to be studied by optimizing 
nutrient composition, ROS-related hormones, saponarin 
precursor, and cultivar effects under low light intensity. 
Furthermore, comparative targeted gene-expression and 
metabolite analyses were conducted on the selected cul -
tivars to elucidate the key biosynthetic steps involved in 
saponarin accumulation in YBL.
Materials and methods
Chemicals and reagents
Standard chemicals for isovitexin, p-coumaric acid, 
saponarin, abscisic acid (ABA), cinnamic acid, sodium 
salicylate, and phenylalanine were purchased from 
Sigma-Aldrich (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). 
Lutonarin was obtained from the National Institute of 
Crop Science and Food Science (Wanju, Republic of 
Korea). Calcium chloride dihydrate, magnesium chloride 
dihydrate, sodium nitrate, ammonium chloride, sodium 
phosphate monobasic, potassium chloride, and potas -
sium phosphate dibasic were used for nutrient minerals 
with Daejung Chemicals & Metals Co., Ltd. (Siheung, 
Republic of Korea). Water and methanol were used 
HPLC grade from Burdick & Jackson ™ (Honeywell Inter -
national Inc., Morris Plains, NJ, USA).
YBL cultivation
The barley ( Hordeum vulgare L.) seeds for cultivation 
were supported by the National Institute of Crop Sci -
ence and Food Science (Wanju, Republic of Korea). The 
hydroponic cultivation conditions described in the previ -
ous report were followed [ 14]. Briefly, 50  g of seed was 
soaked in tap water for two days, then the sprouted seed 
was transferred to a tray (0.21 × 0.27 m) and cultivated in 
hydroponic conditions under white LED lights (3000  K 
and 6500  K lights, Kkumi Lighting Co., Ltd., Namy -
angju, Republic of Korea) at 20–22 ℃. Light intensity was 
adjusted to 100, 210, and 500 µmol m − 2 s− 1 (PPFD) and 
measured with a Portable Luxmeter (HD2102.1, Delta 
OHM S.r.l., Selvazzano Dentro, Italy). The light cycle 
was 12  h a day, and the primary leaf was harvested at 
15  cm. Each hydroponic cultivation was replicated with 
the three separate trays under each cultivation condition. 
The harvested leaf was dried at 45 ℃ in an air-convection 
dryer (PS-1000 A, Shinil Tech Co., Ltd., Gimhae, Repub -
lic of Korea), then ground and stored at −20 ℃ until 
instrumental analysis.
Analysis of saponarin and metabolites
Saponarin and the metabolites, including 4-coumaric 
acid, isovitexin, and lutonarin, were analyzed by the pre -
viously reported method [ 14]. Briefly, 1.0 g of dried YBL 
was added to 30 mL of 60% MeOH and shaken for 4  h 
at 4000 rpm. The extract was centrifuged for 10 min, and 
the supernatant was filtered with a 0.45 μm PVDF syringe 
filter (Biofact Co., Ltd., Daejeon, Republic of Korea). The 
filtrate was analyzed with HPLC-UVD (Agilent 1200, 
Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). The 
detailed instrumental condition was described in Table 1. 
Each tray sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the aver -
age was presented.

RNA expression analysis for CHI, CHS, OGT, and F3’M
Total RNA was extracted from YBL in different treat -
ment groups using IQeasy Plus Plant RNA Extraction 
Kit (iNtRON Biotechnology, Seongnam, Republic of 
Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The 
quantity and quality of the RNA was determined using 
a BioPhotometer D30 (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). 
Total RNA concentration was then adjusted to 50–100 ng 
µL− 1 before stored at −80℃ immediately after extraction. 
Extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into comple -
mentary DNA (cDNA) using an M-MLV cDNA Synthesis 
Kit (Enzynomics, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) according 
to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA expression level was 
determined by quantitative real-time polymerase chain 
reaction (qRT-PCR, QuantStudio 1, Applied Biosystems, 
MA, USA) with a TOPreal™ SYBR Green qPCR PreMIX 
(Enzynomics, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). GAPDH1 was 
used as a reference gene for quantitative gene expression 
analysis in YBL. The primers for qRT-PCR analysis are 
listed in Table  2. YBL for quantitative RNA expression 
analysis were sampled from three separate batches, and 
the expression data were presented as relative average 
values with error bars, normalized to qGAP1 expression. 
All the targeted gene expression data were presented in 
comparison with the non-nutrient treatment batch.
Statistical analysis
All cultivation experiments and instrumental analyses 
were performed with three replications, and statisti -
cal data were obtained using the R statistical program 
(version 4.2.3). Differences in treatment groups were 
explained using two-way ANOVA, and Duncan’s mul -
tiple range test (DMRT) was used for analysis when sig -
nificance was confirmed. For all analyses, the significance 
level was set at p < 0.05.
Results and discussion
Effect of nutrient treatments and light conditions on 
saponarin production in YBL
The effects of major nutrient supply (N, P , K, Ca, and 
Mg) on saponarin and biomass production in YBL were 
investigated under two different 6500  K and 3000  K at 
210 µmol m− 2 s− 1. At 6500 K, the P/K mixture treatment 
resulted in the highest saponarin concentration, reach -
ing 7.82 g kg dw
−1 (Table 3). This was followed closely by 
the PO4

concentrations of 7.29 g kgdw

tively. On the other hand, all treatments at 3000  K 
showed lower saponarin concentrations than those at 
6500  K; the P/K treatment showed the highest concen -
tration at 6.13 g kg dw
−1, although this was notably lower 
than the concentration observed at 6500 K. In addition, 
biomass production was observed to have no significant 
effect from the treatments. This demonstrated blue-rich 
white light (6500  K) had a key factor in saponarin bio -
synthesis in YBL, and it was a similar result to previous 
reports [ 14]. Finally, under 6500  K light, the P/K treat -
ment achieved the highest total saponarin amount at 
79.0  µg plant−1, followed by the PO 4
3- (70.7  µg plant −1) 
and the N/P/K (pH 6) (70.2 µg plant −1) treatments, while 
the P/K treatment under 3000 K light showed the highest 
total saponarin amount (57.6  µg plant −1). Thus, the P/K 
treatment consistently resulted in the highest saponarin 
concentration and total saponarin amount in both light 
conditions, and 6500 K light treatment was identified as 
more conducive to saponarin production in YBL. How -
ever, these values were not as high as those observed in 
YBL grown under high light intensity or in an open field 
condition.

Effect of the saponarin precursor supplement on saponarin 
in YBL
The precursor treatment effect on saponarin production 
was investigated using phenylalanine (PA), cinnamic acid 
(CNA), and coumaric acid (CMA) in YBL. However, no 
effects of precursor supplement up to 250 mg L − 1 were 
observed on saponarin accumulation and the biomass 
increment of YBL. In conclusion, SA, particularly at 
lower concentrations, stands out as a powerful tool for 
maximizing saponarin production, whereas ABA and 
precursor supplements in hydroponics would not posi -
tively affect total saponarin increment significantly.

saponarin content in YBL

Effect of barley cultivars on saponarin production under 
different light intensities
Six different barley cultivars, including Keunal-bori, 
Sakirang, Saknuri, Heimi, Heyang, and Hyenuri, were 
compared on saponarin production yield under low (100 
PPFD) and high (500 PPFD) light intensity to investigate 
the light intensity effects. Under high intensity, Hyenuri 
exhibited the highest saponarin concentration at 14.98 g 
kgdw
−1 (Table  6), but not the total saponarin amount 
(157  µg plant − 1). However, Keunal-bori exhibited the 
highest total saponarin amount (212 µg plant − 1), primar-
ily due to its relatively large biomass. Notably, Keunal-
bori also exhibited the greatest increase ratio in total 
saponarin increment (84%) by nutrient supplementation, 
indicating a robust response to the combined effects of 
high light and added nutrients. Other cultivars, such as 
Sakirang and Heimi, also showed significant increases in 
total saponarin amounts with the nutrient supplement 
under high light conditions, with amounts of 205  µg 
plant− 1 (50%) and 189  µg plant − 1 (39%), respectively. In 
the low light condition, a reduction in saponarin produc -
tion was observed across all cultivars compared to the 
high light, although the nutrient treatment effect was 
observed in total saponarin increment (39–82%). Under 
low-light intensity, Hyenuri exhibited the highest sapo -
narin concentration (12.03 g kg dw
−1) and total saponarin 
amounts (114 µg plant− 1).

Electricity energy use and saponarin production efficiency
The energy consumptions for 100, 210, and 500 PPFD 
were calculated to 1.8, 3.7, and 7.4 kWh m − 2 day− 1, and 
the total energy uses were calculated to be 16.2, 33.5, and 
67.0 kWh m − 2 until the harvest, respectively. Based on 
the total saponarin production, the energy efficiencies for 
saponarin production in YBL were compared in Table  7. 
Although Hyenuri showed a lower amount of saponarin 
than Keunal-bori, the energy efficiency for saponarin 
production of Hyenuri (201  mg kWh − 1) showed higher 
than that of Keunal-bori (144  mg kWh − 1) under 100 
PPFD. This was likely due to the seedling density of Hye -
nuri being 1.29 times higher than Keunal-bori. There -
fore, Hyenuri could be considered a suitable cultivar for 
energy-efficient saponarin production in plant factories.
Comparison of metabolites and gene expression levels 
related to saponarin biosynthesis
To understand the metabolic flow associated with sapo -
narin biosynthesis, the concentrations of its detectable 
precursors, like 4-coumaric acid and isovitexin, as well as 
its degradation product, lutonarin, were compared across 
different light intensities and between the two cultivars 
(Table 7).
The high-saponarin varieties, Keunal-bori under the 
high light intensity and Hyenuri under the low light 
intensity, exhibited higher saponarin and lutonarin con -
centrations under high light than under low light. The 
lutonarin concentration in YBL increased by 1.9-fold in 
Keunal-bori and by 2.6-fold in Hyenuri under the high 
light intensity compared to low light intensity. However, 
the lutonarin concentration was not drastically different 
between the two cultivars in the nutrient supplementa -
tion condition (Table  8), whereas the difference showed 
2.0-fold higher in Keunal-bori than in Hyenuri in the 
non-nutrient condition under 500 PPFD (Table S2 and 
S3). It was presumed that the nutrient supplementation 
might affect F3’M expression in YBL and the concentra -
tions of saponarin and lutonarin. In addition, the concen-
trations of precursors for saponarin, like 4-coumaric acid 
and isovitexin, decreased under the high light intensity in 
the nutrient supplement condition. Herein, the transcrip-
tion levels of CHS, CHI, OGT, and F3’M were compared 
for saponarin accumulation in YBL of two cultivars, 
Keunal-bori and Hyenuri. CHS, CHI, and OGT were 
reported to be a potential gene in the saponarin biosyn -
thesis, and F3’M involved in the oxidative degradation of 
saponarin [29].
After the treatment of K/P/Na under a high (500 PPFD) 
light intensity, OGT expression was increased at least 
two-fold, both in the cultivars (Keunal-bori and Hye -
nuri), while the other saponarin-related gene expression 
didn’t show a significant increment by K/P/Na treatment 
(Fig.  1). Interestingly, the OGT expression in Hyenuri 
was more sensitive to the higher light intensity following 
the K/P/Na treatment than that of Keunal-bori (Fig.  1D); 
it was 14-fold increased the gene expression under 500 
PPFD.
Otherwise, the expression of F3’M was drastically 
decreased up to 50-fold in Hyenuri by the treatment of 
K/P/Na under the high light intensity; F3’M1 expres -
sion was decreased to 50-fold, and F3’M2 decreased 
16-fold. The gene expression in Hyenuri decreased more 
than in Keunal-bori. This could explain the difference 
in the accumulation changes of lutonarin, as an oxida -
tion metabolite of saponarin between Keunal-bori and 
Hyenuri. Thus, Hyenuri exhibited higher F3’M expres -
sion reduction and lower lutonarin changes than Keu -
nal-bori by the nutrient treatment under the high light 
intensity (Fig.  1B and D). These results would explain 
that the nutrient treatment could lead to a more sensitive 
decrease in the oxidation of saponarin in YBLs of Hye -
nuri than in Keunal-bori by suppression of F3’M expres -
sion. Thus, the control of F3’M expression in YBL would 
be a key factor for the saponarin production.
In conclusion, the higher light intensity enhances 
saponarin production and yield in YBL across all barley 
cultivars, as well as K/P/Na supplements. In particular,

K/P/Na treatment than the others, resulting in increased 
saponarin accumulation under both high and low light 
conditions. Although Keunal-bori showed the highest 
total saponarin increment in response to the nutrient 
supplementation under high-light intensity conditions, 
Hyenuri could be more acceptable for saponarin produc -
tion under low-energy consumption conditions based on 
the efficiency of light energy consumption. In particular, 
the reduced expression of F3’M observed in Hyenuri in 
response to nutrient treatment could serve as a potential 
selection marker for enhancing saponarin accumulation 
and suppressing the oxidation product like lutonarin.
