In Sindh, whether a village has drinking water depends less on rainfall than on whether a canal happens to pass nearby. This project tried to measure that — for every village in the province — using nothing but freely available satellite data. The Sindh Water Access Index scores relative drinking-water accessibility for 5,159 villages. Five indicators go into it: distance to the nearest perennial water source, population within 2 km, dry-season rainfall, satellite-observed surface water, and elevation. Everything comes from open sources — HydroRIVERS and OpenStreetMap for water, WorldPop for population, CHIRPS for rainfall, Sentinel-2 for surface water, SRTM for terrain. No field visits, no proprietary data, no paid APIs. The full pipeline is included and reproducible. What this project actually found The interesting result isn't the index. It's that the standard way of building one is wrong for Sindh — and wrong in a way that would be easy to miss. Water-index studies routinely use annual rainfall as a proxy for water availability. Do that here and the model confidently ranks Tharparkar — the driest district in Pakistan, where drought regularly kills livestock and makes national news — as the best-served place in the province. The reason is that Sindh has two rainfall regimes that annual totals hide. The monsoon-fed southeast receives up to 600 mm a year, but almost all of it falls in roughly fifteen days of July and August; between November and May it gets about 10 mm. Western Sindh receives a quarter of that annually — around 150 mm — but 30 to 65 mm of it arrives spread through the dry months, carried by winter westerly disturbances. Annual rainfall is therefore inversely related to dry-season water availability. It doesn't just add noise; it ranks the province backwards. Replacing annual rainfall with dry-season rainfall (November–May) moves Mithi from first place to last, which matches what anyone in Tharparkar could have told you. District rankings hold up across six different weightings (Spearman rho = 0.906–0.958), so this isn't an artifact of arbitrary weights. A second finding worth stating: 88.6% of Sindh's villages have an irrigation canal, not a natural river, as their nearest perennial water source. Any index built on natural hydrography alone is measuring the wrong thing for nine villages in ten. Where it fails The index measures whether water is nearby. It cannot tell you whether that water is drinkable, because no open dataset provides groundwater salinity at this scale. That gap has a name: Thatta and Badin rank second and third best. These are the districts where seawater has pushed so far up the Indus delta that villages have been abandoned. The model sees water everywhere and coastal rain, and calls it good. It is confidently, verifiably wrong — and stays wrong under every weighting tested, which makes it a structural limit of the data rather than something a better formula could fix.
