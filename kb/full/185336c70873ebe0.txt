Abstract
This controlled experimental study examines bentonite, compost, and their combination (bentonite+compost) as bio -
geochemical drivers in the genesis of alkaline soda-lake water. Treatments affected mineral–water reactions (including 
hydrolysis), ion partitioning, and nutrient cycling processes, controlling brine evolution under non-flow-through, aerated 
bottle conditions in the laboratory. pH ranged from 8.18 to 10.35, highest in bentonite-only treatments, while electrical 
conductivity peaked in bentonite+compost at 995 µS cm⁻ 1. At the final sampling point (day 30), the bentonite+compost 
treatment showed the strongest Na enrichment and carbonate-system response, with Na⁺ reaching 61.64 mg L⁻ 1 and the 
carbonate-species signal 12.83 mg L⁻ 1 (HCO3
− + CO 3
2⁻), compared with 52.53 mg L⁻ 1 Na⁺ and 7.12 mg L⁻ 1 carbonate-
species signal in bentonite-only and 12.77 mg L⁻ 1 Na⁺ and 4.93 mg L⁻ 1 carbonate-species signal in compost-only. Com -
post-only produced the highest K⁺ (162.11 mg L⁻1), Ca2⁺ (46.80 mg L⁻1), and Mg2⁺ (20.39 mg L⁻1), whereas bentonite-only 
showed the lowest dissolved Ca2⁺ (3.30 mg L⁻1) and Mg2⁺ (2.26 mg L⁻1); bentonite+compost was intermediate for divalent 
cations (Ca 2⁺ 14.36 mg L⁻ 1, Mg 2⁺ 5.54 mg L⁻ 1) and K⁺ (67.38 mg L⁻ 1). For conservative anions, Cl⁻ increased across 
treatments from bentonite-only (241.02 mg L⁻ 1) to compost-only (329.20 mg L⁻ 1) to bentonite+compost (446.78 mg L⁻ 1), 
while SO4
2⁻ was lowest in bentonite+compost (5.87 mg L⁻ 1) relative to bentonite-only (23.71 mg L⁻ 1) and compost-only 
(29.30 mg L⁻1). Compost-treated samples showed nutrient enrichment, with TN increasing from 13.54 to 28.47 mg L⁻1, TP 
from 7334.33 to 13475.5 mg L⁻1, and TOC from 67.01 to 174.93 mg L⁻ 1, whereas bentonite contributed minimal nutrients 
but maintained higher and less variable pH. Evaporation rates differed among treatments (20.77, 18.39, and 17.95 mL 
day⁻1 for compost-only, bentonite-only, and bentonite+compost, respectively). The results are interpreted primarily from 
the solute chemistry dataset, with solid-phase and microbial effects inferred rather than directly measured. This deeper 
understanding of the biogeochemical genesis of alkaline soda waters provides crucial practical insights for the effective 
management and ecological restoration of these unique ecosystems.

Introduction
Soda lakes (also termed alkaline lakes) are unique aquatic 
ecosystems characterized by permanently high pH (typi -
cally > 9) and ionic dominance (on an equivalents basis) 
by Na⁺ together with carbonate-system species (HCO 3⁻ + 
CO3
2⁻) (Boros and Kolpakova 2018). These lakes occur 
predominantly in arid and semi-arid regions worldwide, 
including the East African Rift Valley, Eurasia, and parts of 
North and South America (Kempe and Kazmierczak 2011; 
Boros and Kolpakova 2018). Their formation reflects cou -
pled geological and climatic controls in endorheic basins 
where evaporation exceeds outflow and sodium enrichment

proceeds relative to Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ (Ross et al. 2014; Peco-
raino et al. 2015).
These systems are characterized by high salinity and 
alkalinity, with Na⁺ and carbonate-system species dominat-
ing the major-ion composition. Despite these extreme con -
ditions, adapted microbial communities occur and microbial 
metabolisms can generate or consume alkalinity in lake 
water and sediment pore water, influencing carbonate-sys -
tem chemistry and nutrient cycling (Grant 2004; Boros and 
Kolpakova 2018; Haines et al. 2023).
Understanding the chemical dynamics and ecologi -
cal roles of soda-lake waters requires considering solid–
water interactions at the sediment–water interface and on 
suspended particles, where surface reactions can modify 
dissolved-ion composition. Here, “distribution” refers to 
partitioning of ions between dissolved water and solid-asso-
ciated pools (sediments/particles), affecting nutrient and 
trace-element availability and water chemistry (Tóth 1999).
Conditions required for natural soda-lake development 
include an underfilled tectonic basin, where subsidence cre-
ates accommodation faster than sediment infill, and closed 
hydrography in which evaporation exceeds outflow. Under 
these conditions, progressive chemical evolution favors 
high Na⁺ relative to Ca 2⁺ and Mg 2⁺, and carbonate-system 
species can become dominant over sulfate and chloride in 
alkaline brines (Tosca and Tutolo 2023).
Bentonite is a clay material primarily rich in montmo -
rillonite. Montmorillonite-rich (smectite-rich) clay miner -
als commonly occur in the sediments and catchments of 
many alkaline soda lakes and closed-basin environments, 
including the soda pans of the Carpathian Basin, the East 
African Rift Valley, and other volcanic or weathered sedi -
mentary settings, where they play an important role in con -
trolling ion exchange and water chemistry (Deocampo and 
Jones 2013). Its high cation exchange capacity (CEC) and 
swelling characteristics allow bentonite to influence ion 
retention and solution chemistry through solid–water inter -
actions, including surface adsorption and exchange-related 
processes, in aqueous systems. In this study, bentonite is 
used as a model mineral phase in a bottle-water system. The 
composition and treatment of bentonite affect its exchange 
behavior; for example, sodium activation can increase CEC 
relative to raw bentonite (Bahranowski et al. 2021).
Compost can substantially alter the chemistry of saline–
alkaline aquatic systems by releasing dissolved organic 
matter and nutrients (particularly nitrogen- and phospho -
rus-containing compounds) during decomposition (Bernal 
et al. 2009). In natural soda-lake and saline-wetland set -
tings, comparable decomposable organic-matter inputs 
may occur through livestock droppings in grazed wetlands, 
decomposition of aquatic and shoreline vegetation, and run-
off from surrounding agricultural areas where manure- or 
compost-based fertilizers are applied. Compost can also 
provide a substrate and inoculum for microbial activity 
(Haines et al. 2023). In a non-flow-through aerated bottle 
system such as the present experiment, these processes are 
expected to increase dissolved nutrient and organic-carbon 
concentrations and to modify carbonate-system behavior, 
pH, and ion partitioning. Accordingly, in this study compost 
is treated as a model organic-matter input that drives chemi-
cal and microbially influenced changes in the solute phase 
(Bernal et al. 2009).
This experiment is an aqueous bottle model (water + 
solids with continuous aeration) rather than a soil study. 
Accordingly, interpretations are limited to water-column 
solute chemistry and solid–water interactions within the 
bottle system.
The genesis of alkaline brines in soda lakes is controlled 
by interacting geological, hydrological, climatic, physico -
chemical, and biological factors, including basin setting, 
evaporation–inflow balance, temperature, atmospheric CO2 
exchange, and water–sediment/mineral interactions (Tosca 
and Tutolo 2023). In this context, organic matter and reac -
tive clay minerals may influence carbonate-system chem -
istry, mineral precipitation/dissolution, and ion partitioning 
between dissolved and solid-associated phases, thereby 
contributing to the chemical evolution of lake water toward 
alkaline brine conditions (Kleber et al. 2021).
Alkaline soda lakes are globally unique and threatened 
ecosystems with high conservation priority, including 
within the Natura 2000 network of the European Union 
(Boros and Vörös 2024). In protected soda-lake wetlands, 
controlled grazing by domestic livestock is used to main -
tain open wetland surfaces (Boros et al. 2013); however, the 
biogeochemical effect of livestock droppings on soda-lake 
water chemistry remains insufficiently quantified (Boda et 
al. 2021).
Therefore, our goal was to compare how bentonite and 
compost affect solute chemistry and alkaline soda-lake 
water related brine evolution under controlled laboratory 
conditions using three treatments: bentonite-only (model 
mineral/clay phase), compost-only (model organic-matter 
input), and bentonite+compost (combined mineral–organic 
treatment). This comparison is important because it sepa -
rates mineral-driven, organic-matter-driven, and combined 
mineral–organic effects on soda-lake-relevant water chem -
istry, providing mechanistic insight into processes that are 
difficult to isolate directly in natural wetlands.
Working hypotheses were that (H1) bentonite increases 
and stabilizes alkaline conditions relative to compost-
only and the tap-water baseline, (H2) bentonite+compost 
produces stronger Na⁺ and carbonate-species dominance 
(HCO3
− + CO 3
2⁻) than either component alone, and 
(H3) compost increases dissolved divalent cations while

expected bentonite to yield higher and less variable pH, 
bentonite+compost to show the strongest alkaline soda-lake

highest nutrient and TOC release. Based on field observa -
tions from grazed wetlands, we further hypothesized that 
the combined bentonite+compost treatment would produce 
stronger alkaline soda-lake water related chemical responses 
than either single-component treatment.
Materials and methods
Experimental design and laboratory analysis
The experiment was conducted from 11 November 2024 to 
10 December 2024 (four-week incubation) at the Hungar -
ian University of Agriculture and Life Sciences, Gödöllő, 
Hungary. This duration was selected as a short-term incu -
bation window to capture early treatment-driven changes 
in solute chemistry relevant to brine evolution under con -
trolled laboratory conditions. Sodium bentonite powder 
(vendor: Unikén Kft., Kecel, Hungary) and a commercial, 
vendor-certified compost product (manufacturer/supplier: 
Profikomp Zrt., Gödöllő, Hungary) were commercially 
sourced. Bentonite was oven-dried at 200 °C for 3 h as a 
laboratory pre-treatment to reduce variability in initial 
water content and standardize the starting material across 
replicates. It was applied as a fine powder fraction (20–80 
µm) to provide reactive clay surfaces and reproducible min-
eral–water contact in the bottle system; the supplier label 
specifies an active SiO2 content of ≥ 65–70%. Compost was 
homogenized with a mortar and used at a coarser grain-size 
fraction (~ 0.5–3 mm) to preserve representative particulate 
organic matter while reducing sample heterogeneity. The 
different size fractions reflect the different physical nature 
and intended function of the two amendments (reactive 
mineral phase vs. particulate organic-matter input). Accord-
ing to the accredited analytical certificate (BÁLINT Anali -
tika Kft., Budapest; laboratory report for compost samples, 
client: Profikomp Zrt.), the compost (dry-weight basis) had 
pH(H2O) 7.29–7.45, organic matter 48.8–51.4%, total N 
1.34–1.42%, P2O₅ 1.24–1.60%, and K2O 2.07–2.25% (with 
Ca 5.30–5.56% and Mg 0.71–0.82%). The certificate also 
reported particle-size compliance (< 20 mm: 100%) for the 
submitted compost samples.
Bentonite and compost comprise multiple material frac -
tions (solid matrix, exchangeable ions, and water-extractable 
components). Accordingly, this study reports solute-phase 
chemistry in the 0.45 µm-filtered bottle water and starting 
material specifications are documented using supplier labels 
and the compost certificate. Exchange-phase composition 
was not directly measured; therefore, exchange-related 
interpretations are presented as inferences from solute pat -
terns and treated as mechanistic hypotheses rather than 
direct measurements.
A completely randomized design was employed with 
three treatments: (1) bentonite-only (65 g), (2) compost-
only (15 g), and (3) bentonite+compost (65 g bentonite + 
15 g compost). The bentonite and compost amounts were 
selected to generate measurable treatment-driven changes 
in solute chemistry within the four-week bottle experiment 
while preserving their different experimental functions 
(bentonite as the reactive mineral phase, and compost as the 
organic-matter input and microbial substrate). Each treat -
ment was replicated three times (nine experimental units 
total). Bentonite was used as a representative clay-rich min-
eral substrate relevant to clay-bearing, low-permeability 
layers in wetland settings, while compost was used as a 
representative organic-matter input. Tap water was selected 
as a reproducible electrolyte matrix with Ca–Mg–HCO 3-
type background chemistry common in many non-saline 
freshwaters.
All treatments were established in 5-L glass bottles filled 
with tap water and maintained in a dark environment at 
ambient laboratory temperature (~ 25 ± 2 °C). The same 
bottle type and filling volume were used for all replicates; 
therefore, exposed water surface and headspace condi -
tions were comparable among treatments. Each bottle was 
continuously aerated at a low rate to maintain oxic condi -
tions and to provide bubble-induced gentle mixing (weak 
circulation without vigorous turbulence or mechanical stir -
ring), sufficient to limit stratification while allowing set -
tling of the heavier solid fraction. Aeration was supplied 
using an aquarium air pump via open-ended tubing, with 
one air line per bottle operated at a low bubbling rate. The 
bottles were open to gas exchange with laboratory air but 
non-flow-through (no drainage/outflow). Room humid -
ity was not actively controlled; however, all bottles were 
incubated under the same laboratory conditions. Evapora -
tion was quantified from daily water-level measurements, 
and evaporative losses were replenished with deionized 
water to maintain constant volume. Initial (0 h) samples 
were collected immediately after treatment setup (addition 
of bentonite and/or compost to water), followed by 0.45 µm 
filtration and analysis; the same analyses were repeated at 
the end of the experiment.
Municipal tap water was used to provide a reproducible 
background electrolyte matrix for the bottle experiment. A 
composite sample of the same batch was analyzed before 
treatments (25 °C ± 2 °C): pH = 7.5; EC = 565 µS cm⁻ 1; 
Na⁺ = 13.5 mg L⁻1; K⁺ = 1.48 mg L⁻1; Ca2⁺ = 67.77 mg L⁻1; 
Mg2⁺ = 25.9 mg L⁻ 1; HCO3⁻ = 2.70 mg L⁻ 1; Cl⁻ = 28.2 mg 
L⁻1; SO4
2⁻ = 20.2 mg L⁻1.

Throughout the experimental period, pH and electri -
cal conductivity (EC) were measured daily using a pH/
EC meter (HANNA HI-9821). Major cations were ana -
lyzed using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry 
(ICP-MS; Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Anion 
concentrations were determined according to the standard 
procedures outlined by the American Public Health Asso -
ciation (APHA, 2005). Measurements were recorded on 
24 sampling days across the four-week incubation, with no 
measurements taken on weekends.
Carbonate-system species (HCO 3⁻ and CO 3
2⁻) were 
determined by acid–base titration (alkalinity titration) fol -
lowing APHA standard methods [15]. Briefly, filtered 
samples were titrated with standardized strong acid to the 
relevant alkalinity endpoints, and bicarbonate and carbonate 
contributions were quantified from alkalinity in combina -
tion with the measured pH. Species partitioning was cal -
culated using standard carbonate alkalinity relationships at 
~25 °C (± 2 °C). Chloride (Cl⁻) was determined by argento-
metric titration following APHA standard methods (APHA, 
2005), using standardized AgNO3 as titrant (with Cl⁻ quan-
tified from the formation of AgCl at the endpoint). Sulfate 
(SO4
2⁻) was measured by spectrophotometric determina -
tion following APHA standard methods (APHA (American 
Public Health Association) 2005), using a turbidity-based 
(BaSO4) procedure and quantification against appropriate 
standards.
Total nitrogen (TN) and total phosphorus (TP) concen -
trations were measured colorimetrically using a NANO -
COLOR spectrophotometer (Macherey–Nagel GmbH & 
Co. KG, Germany). Total organic carbon (TOC) in the water 
samples was measured using a TOC analyzer (Shimadzu 
TOC-L or equivalent).
For classification in this study (at ~ 25 ± 2 °C), a sam -
ple was considered alkaline soda-lake water if it met all 
of the following criteria: (i) pH ≥ 9.0; (ii) Na⁺ dominance 
among cations on an equivalents basis (≥ 50 e% of total 
measured cations); and (iii) dominance of carbonate-system 
species (HCO3⁻ + CO 3
2⁻) among anions on an equivalents 
basis (≥ 50 e% of total measured anions). Ion dominance 
was evaluated using equivalents-percent (e%), calculated 
after converting ion concentrations from mg L⁻ 1 to meq 
L⁻1 with ion-specific equivalent weights; thus, dominance 
was assessed on a charge-equivalent basis rather than mass 
concentration, consistent with soda-lake biogeochemical 
classification practice (Boros and Kolpakova 2018).
Data analysis
The statistical analysis was carried out in OriginPro 2024 
(OriginLab, Northampton, MA, http://www.originlab.com), 
and graphical data were visualized using GraphPad Prism 
version 10 (GraphPad Prism version 10.0.0 for Windows, 
GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA, www.
graphpad.com). The normality of variables was assessed 
using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and log transforma -
tion was applied where necessary. Treatment effects and 
temporal variations were evaluated using the measured 
water-chemistry dataset (daily pH and EC; major ions and 
nutrients at defined sampling points) and analyzed using a 
two-way multivariate analysis of variance (MANOV A), with 
treatment and experimental phase as fixed factors, including 
their interaction term. MANOV A was selected because mul-
tiple correlated response variables were evaluated simulta -
neously within this experimental design. To protect against 
inflating Type I error rates, significant multivariate effects 
were followed up with univariate two-way analyses of 
variance (ANOV As) for each dependent variable. Pairwise 
comparisons were subsequently performed using Tukey’s 
post-hoc tests where applicable. Statistical significance for 
all tests was established at α = 0.05. Exchange-related inter-
pretations are based on solute-phase patterns rather than 
direct measurements of the solid exchange phase.

and acidifying processes linked to compost decomposi -
tion. Smectite-rich clays can buffer pH through edge-site 
protonation/deprotonation and proton exchange reactions, 
which together can sustain higher pH and dampen short-term

drive pH downward, particularly in treatments where organic 
matter dominates the reactive pool (Bernal et al. 2009). For 
carbon stocks, this means that compost addition may both 
increase dissolved and gaseous carbon release during decom-
position and provide organic carbon that can potentially be 
retained through association with bentonite surfaces. How -
ever, this experiment did not directly quantify solid-phase 
carbon storage or CO 2 flux. The intermediate pH behavior 
in the combined bentonite+compost treatment is consistent 
with the co-occurrence of compost-derived acidification and 
bentonite-associated buffering/alkalinity generation in the 
same water column. Electrical conductivity (EC) primarily 
tracks total dissolved ionic strength; thus, higher EC indicates 
a larger dissolved-ion load arising from the combined contri-
bution of compost-derived solutes (major ions and nutrients 
released during decomposition) together with bentonite-
associated ion partitioning processes (exchange-related redis-
tribution of cations between solid-associated and dissolved 
pools). Although the observed pH values fall within the alka-
line range reported for many soda-lake waters, soda-lake clas-
sification is not based on pH alone and must be interpreted 
together with equivalents-based ion dominance derived from 
the measured major-ion suite (Boros and Kolpakova 2018). 
More broadly, these coupled pH–EC responses are consistent 
with “chemical divide” concepts in alkaline-lake develop -
ment, where mineralogy, solute acquisition, and hydrologic 
closure control trajectories toward carbonate-alkaline waters 
(Tosca and Tutolo 2023).
Treatment differences in evaporation rate likely reflect 
the contrasting physical behavior of the added solids at the 
water–solid interface. Bentonite is a swelling clay whose 
water-association behavior can alter the physical properties 
of clay–water mixtures, which is consistent with lower evap-
oration in bentonite-containing bottles (Wang et al. 2022). 
Compost introduces a coarser particulate matrix and soluble 
organic compounds that can modify the near-surface proper-
ties of the bottle mixture; together, these effects indicate that 
additive composition can influence evaporation behavior in 
the experimental system.
The cation patterns show that bentonite-containing treat-
ments were associated with stronger Na enrichment of the 
aqueous phase than compost-only. In soda-lake biogeochemi-
cal evolution, progressive removal of Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ through 
mineral controls and chemical divides favors Na dominance 
in the residual solution; this framework is widely used to 
interpret alkaline-lake development in closed basins (Gibbs 
1970; Tosca and Tutolo 2023). Consistent with this concep-
tual model, Na dominance (expressed as equivalents per -
cent) was achieved in the bentonite-only treatment and was 
approached in the combined bentonite+compost treatment by 
the final sampling point, while compost-only did not show Na 
dominance when evaluated on an equivalent’s basis.
Potassium was strongly associated with compost addition, 
reflecting the K content of compost and its rapid release to 
the dissolved phase during incubation. Lower dissolved K in 
the combined treatment compared with compost-only indi -
cates that adding bentonite altered K partitioning between 
the filtrate and the bentonite-containing solid fraction. Such 
treatment-dependent nutrient/ion transformations during co-
amendment with bentonite have been reported in compost -
ing and amendment studies, consistent with mineral–organic 
interactions affecting dissolved nutrient pools (Li et al. 2012).
Dissolved Ca2⁺ and Mg 2⁺ were highest in compost-only 
and substantially lower in bentonite-containing treatments, 
indicating that bentonite presence modified the dissolved 
divalent-cation fraction in the bottle water. Ca 2⁺ and Mg 2⁺ 
contribute disproportionately to charge balance relative to 
mass; therefore, separating these divalent cations and evalu-
ating their equivalent contributions is essential for robust 
dominance calculations, as required in soda-lake classifica-
tions (Boros and Kolpakova 2018).
Carbonate-system behavior also differed among treat -
ments. The carbonate-species signal was strongest in the com-
bined bentonite+compost treatment, consistent with a larger 
carbonate-system response under conditions where organic-
matter decomposition supplies inorganic carbon to solution 
and bentonite-containing bottles maintain more alkaline 
conditions. Interpreted within chemical-divide frameworks, 
increased carbonate-system dominance relative to sulfate and 
chloride is a defining feature of alkaline brines and soda-lake 
waters (Tosca and Tutolo 2023).
Measured chloride and sulfate provide the non-carbonate 
anion pool against which carbonate-system dominance was 
evaluated. Chloride typically behaves conservatively during 
evaporation–concentration and is commonly used as a tracer 
of evaporative enrichment in closed-basin waters (Gibbs 
1970). Sulfate can also behave conservatively over short tim-
escales in oxic waters, but in natural alkaline-lake settings its 
relative importance may additionally reflect mineral controls 
(e.g., gypsum/anhydrite) and redox-dependent microbial 
transformations. Accordingly, including Cl⁻ and SO4
2⁻ in the 
equivalents-based anion calculations provides a stricter test 
of carbonate-system dominance than relying on carbonate 
measurements alone, aligning the experiment with soda-lake 
classification practice (Boros and Kolpakova 2018).
Nutrient patterns were dominated by compost addition, 
consistent with nitrogen and phosphorus inputs from the 
compost material and release during decomposition. These 
observations align with the general behavior of organic 
fertilizers and composts, where mineralization governs the 
timing and magnitude of dissolved N release (Toner and

Catling 2020; Geisseler et al. 2021). The dissolved organic-
carbon signal (TOC) was highest in compost-only and lower 
in bentonite+compost, indicating that bentonite modified 
the dissolved/filtered organic-carbon fraction relative to 
compost alone. Such reductions in dissolved organic matter 
in the presence of reactive mineral surfaces are consistent 
with mineral–organic associations that shift organic carbon 
from the dissolved to solid-associated pool (Kleber et al. 
2021). This suggests a potential pathway by which clay–
organic matter interactions may contribute to carbon reten -
tion; however, direct evaluation of carbon sequestration 
would require measurements of solid-phase organic carbon, 
carbonate mineral formation, and CO2 exchange.
Overall, when evaluated against the operational alkaline 
soda-lake water criteria (pH threshold and equivalents-based 
dominance of Na⁺ among cations and carbonate-system spe-
cies among anions), treatments showed distinct responses. 
The bentonite-only treatment met the Na-dominance criterion 
and maintained alkaline pH, while the combined treatment 
was classified as transitional toward soda based on the equiv-
alents-based major-ion pattern (Na–(HCO3⁻+CO3
2⁻)) without 
sustaining alkaline pH continuously over the full incubation; 
compost-only promoted nutrient and organic-carbon release 
but did not produce the ionic dominance pattern characteristic 
of soda-lake waters on an equivalents basis. This separation 
among treatments is consistent with the broader understand-
ing that soda-lake water chemistry reflects coupled mineral 
controls, hydrologic closure, and carbon-system evolution 
rather than pH alone (Tosca and Tutolo 2023).
These bottle experiments are best interpreted as a mecha-
nistic analogue for early-stage brine evolution in closed or 
weakly flushed settings, where interactions among mineral 
surfaces, organic inputs, and carbonate-system chemistry 
can rapidly shift major-ion structure. In applied terms, under-
standing these short-timescale pathways is relevant for man-
agement and rehabilitation of threatened soda-lake wetlands, 
where hydrologic alteration and drying are increasing pres-
sures in arid and semi-arid regions (Tussupova et al. 2020; 
Boros and Vörös 2024). Replication was limited, so some 
differences among treatments may not have been detected.
Conclusions for future biology
This study provides a controlled bottle-scale demonstra -
tion that mineral and organic amendments generate distinct 
solute-chemistry trajectories relevant to the early-stage 
biogeochemical genesis of alkaline soda-lake water. Com -
post increased dissolved nutrients and organic carbon in 
the filtrate, whereas bentonite maintained higher and less 
variable pH and promoted a Na⁺-dominant cation structure. 
The combined bentonite+compost treatment showed the 
strongest genesis toward soda-lake-type chemistry and was 
classified as transitional toward soda when evaluated using 
an equivalents-based framework. These findings support the 
use of charge-based (meq L⁻ 1; e%) classification to inter -
pret early brine evolution and provide mechanistic insight 
into mineral–organic interactions that are difficult to isolate 
directly in natural wetlands.
The results are highly relevant not only for understanding 
the possible pathways involved in the long-term, historical 
development of pristine soda pans in clay-rich closed-basin 
landscapes—such as the Pannonian ecoregion—but also 
for modern, nature-conservation-oriented wetland manage -
ment. From an applied perspective, the experiment suggests 
that the interaction between clay-rich substrates and decom-
posable organic matter, such as plant-derived inputs or live-
stock droppings, actively can foster sodification processes 
and helps shift water chemistry toward Na⁺–carbonate-sys -
tem dominance.
Therefore, the practical conservation significance of this 
work is substantial. Saline–alkaline soda wetlands are cur -
rently highly threatened ecosystems with specialized and 
endangered biota, and their restoration is often limited by 
altered hydrology and missing ecological drivers. This study 
provides crucial baseline experimental evidence to over -
come these barriers. Our findings demonstrate that while 
the restoration of soda pans via river-water flooding is a fea-
sible conservation strategy, it requires careful consideration 
of both hydrological dynamics and site-management fac -
tors to be successful. For wetland rehabilitation, controlled 
water addition may help restore seasonal inundation and 
reduce excessive drying, but the biogeochemical outcome 
will depend strongly on the chemistry of the added water. 
Compared with the controlled tap-water matrix used here, 
river water is inherently more variable and may introduce 
suspended sediments, dissolved organic matter, nutrients, 
Ca2+, Mg 2+ and bicarbonate, thereby modifying pH, ion 
dominance, carbonate-system behavior, and the trajectory 
of brine evolution. Therefore, river-water-based rehabili -
tation must be evaluated carefully to restore hydrological 
function while preserving the characteristic Na-carbonate-
system chemistry of soda-lake wetlands, which depends on 
many other environmental factors as well.
Additionally, the observed reduction in dissolved organic 
carbon in the bentonite+compost treatment suggests that 
clay–organic matter interactions may support carbon reten -
tion by transferring part of the organic carbon from the dis-
solved phase toward solid-associated pools; however, direct 
carbon sequestration was not quantified in this experiment. 
Future research should build on these findings using lon -
ger-term mesocosm or field experiments with natural soda-
lake sediments and different water sources, including river 
water and groundwater, to evaluate how mineral–organic

habitat quality, and wetland rehabilitation outcomes under
