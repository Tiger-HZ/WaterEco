Long-term ecological quality dynamics and spatial 
associations in arid and semi-arid Northwest China 
using an adaptive PRSEI
Jiangmin Wu · Bin Lian · Qirui Zhang · Zhen Yan · Wei Zhao · Jiacheng Ya · Zaixin Chen · 
Yuxiang Lan
Received: 29 May 2026 / Accepted: 11 September 2026

Abstract
Long-term ecological monitoring in arid and semi-arid regions requires indices that capture region-specific 
stressors while remaining comparable through time. We developed an adaptive particular remote-sensing eco -
logical index (PRSEI) for Gansu Province, China, using growing-season kNDVI, wetness (WET), land surface 
temperature (LST), sandification index (SI), and a PM10-based particulate indicator (TI) from 2000 to 2024. 
A unified principal component analysis (PCA) with common scaling defined the primary time series, while 
annual PCA and fixed weights were used for sensitivity testing. Unified PC1 explained 79.12% of total vari -
ance, and mean PRSEI increased from 0.343 to 0.419 (slope = 0.00432 yr⁻¹; R² = 0.778). Unified PCA and 
fixed weights agreed on 99.90% of pixel-level trend directions, whereas annual and unified PCA agreed on 
only 39.73%, indicating strong methodological sensitivity. Adaptive PRSEI was highly correlated with original 
PRSEI (r = 0.991) and traditional RSEI ( r = 0.975), showing the strongest consistency with mapped restoration 
transitions (81.47%) but weaker consistency with degradation transitions (34.22%). Precipitation, soil type, 
and land use/land cover were the most stable spatial explanatory factors. Overall, ecological quality improved 
broadly but heterogeneously, supporting adaptive PRSEI as a complementary multi-stressor index for drylands 
rather than a universal replacement for RSEI.

Introduction
Ecological and environmental quality reflects multiple dimensions of ecosystem condition, including vegeta -
tion growth, surface moisture, thermal stress, land degradation, and human disturbance. Long-term ecological 
monitoring is particularly important in arid and semi-arid regions, where limited precipitation, high evaporative 
demand, sparse vegetation, and extensive bare ground increase ecosystem sensitivity to climatic variability and 
land-use change1,2. Gansu Province, located in inland north-western China, spans the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau,

the Inner Mongolian Plateau, and the Loess Plateau. Within a single administrative unit, it encompasses diverse 
landscapes, including desert–Gobi systems, irrigated oases, loess hills, alpine ecosystems, and the humid Qinba 
Mountains3. This pronounced environmental gradient provides a representative setting for examining spatial dif-
ferences in ecological quality across contrasting dryland and mountain environments.
Remote-sensing ecological assessment has increasingly relied on composite indices that integrate multiple 
land-surface properties. The remote sensing ecological index (RSEI) uses principal component analysis (PCA) to 
combine greenness, wetness, heat, and dryness, thereby reducing reliance on subjectively assigned weights4–9. Its 
broad application across different regions has demonstrated its effectiveness in characterising spatial variations in 
ecological quality. However, the conventional four-component framework may not fully capture several stressors 
that are particularly relevant in arid environments, including weak vegetation signals, extensive bare-ground 
exposure, sandification, and atmospheric particulate loading10–14.
Accordingly, a growing body of research has sought to adapt ecological indices to arid environments by 
modifying vegetation metrics or incorporating components that better reflect dryland-specific stressors 15–19. In 
this study, the kernel normalised difference vegetation index (kNDVI) was used to capture nonlinear green -
ness responses, while the sandification index (SI) and a PM10-based turbidity indicator (TI) were introduced to 
represent additional surface and atmospheric environmental pressures. Importantly, the inclusion of additional 
components or a high contribution from the first principal component (PC1) does not, by itself, demonstrate 
superior ecological performance. A more robust evaluation therefore requires direct comparison with traditional 
RSEI and the original PRSEI configuration under identical years, pixels, masks, and preprocessing procedures, 
together with external ecological evidence to clarify where the adaptive formulation provides added information 
and where its limitations remain.
Long-term ecological assessments also face a fundamental challenge of temporal comparability. Analyses 
based on representative years provide an efficient means of comparing broad ecological differences among peri-
ods, but they may overlook interannual responses to drought, ecological restoration, irrigation, and land-cover 
change. By contrast, annual PCA retains year-specific information, yet the centring of variables, principal-compo-
nent loadings, and score ranges may vary from year to year. Apparent temporal changes may therefore reflect not 
only genuine ecological variation but also changes in the index construction itself. Despite this potential source 
of uncertainty, few studies have directly compared annual PCA, unified PCA, and fixed-weight approaches using 
the same underlying dataset, leaving the sensitivity of long-term ecological trends to PCA construction insuf -
ficiently quantified.
A third methodological issue concerns the interpretation of ecological change and its explanatory factors. The 
Theil–Sen estimator and Mann–Kendall test are commonly used to characterise monotonic trends, the Hurst 
exponent describes tendencies toward persistence or reversal, and geographical detectors quantify spatial strati -
fication20–24. These methods address complementary but fundamentally different questions, and none can inde -
pendently establish causal effects. In particular, the q statistic derived from OPGD measures the extent to which 
factor stratification accounts for spatial variation in ecological quality; it does not demonstrate that a given factor 
caused the observed changes. Robust inference therefore requires a clear distinction among descriptive patterns, 
temporal trends, external consistency evidence, and spatial explanatory associations.
Three research gaps emerge from the limitations outlined above. First, the extent to which a dryland-adapted 
PRSEI provides additional ecological information beyond the traditional RSEI has not been sufficiently evaluated 
under a strictly comparable framework. Second, the sensitivity of long-term PRSEI trends to different PCA con-
struction strategies remains inadequately quantified. Third, province-wide ecological changes need to be linked 
to eco-regional contrasts and the robustness of spatial explanatory factors without overstating causal relation -
ships. To address these gaps, this study constructs a temporally comparable 25-year PRSEI series using a unified 
PCA loading vector and common scaling, and directly compares traditional RSEI, original-configuration PRSEI, 
and adaptive PRSEI across identical years and common pixels. External consistency is further assessed using 
transition information from the China Land Cover Dataset (CLCD), while the robustness of OPGD results is

examined with respect to sampling and soil-classification schemes. Together, these analyses define the principal

Accordingly, this study aims to: (1) construct a temporally comparable adaptive PRSEI for 2000–2024; (2) 
quantify the sensitivity of long-term ecological trends to annual PCA, unified PCA, and fixed-weight approaches; 
(3) compare traditional RSEI, original-configuration PRSEI, and adaptive PRSEI in terms of spatial patterns, 
trend responses, and consistency with external land-cover evidence; (4) characterise ecological dynamics, class 
transitions, and persistence tendencies at both provincial and eco-regional scales; and (5) evaluate the stability 
and interaction structure of climatic, topographic, soil, land-cover, and socio-economic explanatory factors using 
OPGD. Throughout the analysis, CLCD transitions are treated as external categorical consistency evidence rather 
than as field-validated ground truth, while OPGD results are interpreted as spatial explanatory associations rather 
than causal mechanisms.
Materials and methods
Overview of the study area
Gansu Province is located in inland north-western China (32°11′–42°57′ N, 92°13′–108°46′ E) and covers 
approximately 425,800 km². The province extends across the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, the Inner Mongolian Pla -
teau, and the Loess Plateau, encompassing diverse landscapes such as alpine mountains, loess hills, irrigated 
oases, desert–Gobi systems, and mountain forests. Precipitation generally decreases from the south-east to the 
north-west, whereas evaporative demand and aridity increase along the same gradient. This produces a pro -
nounced environmental transition from relatively humid mountain ecosystems in the south-east to arid oasis and 
desert environments in the north-west Fig. 1.
To examine regional variation in ecological quality across contrasting environmental settings, Gansu Province 
was divided into five eco-regions: the Hexi Desert–Gobi, Hexi Oasis–Corridor Agricultural, Longzhong–Long -
dong Loess Hilly, Gannan Alpine Mountain, and Longnan–Tianshui Qinba Humid Mountain regions. The region-
alisation considered climatic gradients, geomorphology, land-cover patterns, ecosystem characteristics, and the 
spatial continuity of administrative units. These eco-region boundaries were subsequently used as consistent 
spatial statistical units for comparing annual PRSEI values, trend classes, and persistence tendencies Fig. 2.
Data and preprocessing
The study covered the period from 2000 to 2024. PRSEI components were derived from the MODIS/Terra 
MOD09A1 Version 6.1 surface reflectance product (500 m) for calculating kNDVI, WET, and SI; the MODIS/
Terra MOD11A2 Version 6.1 land surface temperature product (1 km, converted from Kelvin to °C) for LST; and 
the Long-term Gap-free High-resolution Air Pollutants (LGHAP) v2 PM10 product (1 km, µg m⁻³) for construct-
ing TI25–28. June–September growing-season composites were used to reduce the influence of snow cover, veg -
etation senescence, and seasonal variation in bare-ground conditions. All continuous variables were converted 
to their specified physical units before spatial resampling to ensure consistency among datasets. Because the 
publicly available documentation for LGHAP v2 covers 2000–2021, the archived TI rasters for 2022–2024 used 
in this study extend beyond the documented period and are therefore explicitly acknowledged as a limitation of 
the data source Fig. 3.
The explanatory variables comprised annual precipitation (PRE; mm yr⁻¹), annual mean temperature (TEM; 
°C), potential evapotranspiration (PET; mm yr⁻¹), population density (PD; persons km⁻²), night-time lights (NTL; 
nW cm⁻² sr⁻¹), and annual land use/land cover (LULC), together with static layers of the digital elevation model 
(DEM; m), slope (SLO; °), and soil type (ST; categorical)29–39. Fractional vegetation cover (FVC) was excluded 
from the main model because no independent annual archive was available and kNDVI was already included as

All rasters were clipped to the boundary of Gansu Province, projected to the CGCS2000 3-degree Gauss–

474.751 m. Continuous variables were resampled using bilinear interpolation, whereas categorical variables were 
resampled using the nearest-neighbour method. A pixel-year observation was included in the PCA only when all 
required component values were finite and exceeded the NoData threshold (− 9998). The primary 25-year trend 
and persistence analyses were conducted using a common intersection mask comprising 1,906,388 pixels. The 
strict three-index comparison was restricted to 24 common years (2000–2008 and 2010–2024) and 1,906,376 
common pixels. The year 2009 was excluded because the data required for the three-index comparison were 
unavailable and was not interpolated.

Temporal, spatial and class statistics
Annual PRSEI statistics, including the mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum, were calculated for the 
province and each eco-region. Following the conventional RSEI classification and subsequent applications8,11,14, 
PRSEI values were divided into five fixed 0.2 intervals: very poor [0, 0.2), poor [0.2, 0.4), moderate [0.4, 0.6), 
good [0.6, 0.8), and excellent [0.8, 1.0]. Fixed thresholds ensured direct interannual comparability and avoided 
artificial changes caused by year-specific quantiles or natural breaks. These classes represent relative positions 
along the ecological-quality gradient rather than field-calibrated ecological thresholds or regulatory standards.
Annual mean PRSEI time series for the five eco-regions were derived by overlaying the annual PRSEI rasters 
with the corresponding regional boundaries. These series were used to compare baseline ecological quality, the 
magnitude of change, and interannual variability among regions.
After applying a common valid-data mask, area statistics were derived from pixel counts using the stan -
dardised grid-cell area. For each year and eco-region, pixel counts were converted to areas and proportions 
for PRSEI classes, trend categories, and persistence categories. Proportional statistics were used to facilitate 
comparison among eco-regions of different sizes, while the underlying raster and area outputs were retained for 
reproducibility.

Eco-regional statistics
Using a common valid-data mask, annual PRSEI values, trend categories, and persistence tendencies were sum-
marised for each eco-region. The main text reports key regional metrics, including initial and final values, the 
25-year mean, trend slope, R², and residual degradation proportion, while detailed annual statistics, class areas, 
and transition matrices are provided in the Supplementary Data for reproducibility without overloading the main

OPGD analysis of spatial explanatory associations
OPGD was used to quantify spatial associations between PRSEI and nine explanatory factors: PRE, TEM, PET, 
DEM, SLO, PD, NTL, LULC, and ST.
For annual OPGD analysis, unified-PCA PRSEI values were matched with the corresponding annual dynamic 
variables and the static DEM, SLO, and ST layers. Continuous variables were discretised using equal-interval, 
quantile, geometric-interval, K-means, and natural-breaks methods with 5–10 classes, and the scheme yielding 
the highest q value was retained. LULC and ST were treated directly as categorical variables.

Here, h denotes the stratum, L the number of strata, Nh and N the numbers of pixels within stratum h and the full 
sample, and σh² and σ² the corresponding PRSEI variances. A higher q value indicates a greater proportion of 
PRSEI spatial variance explained by factor stratification. Accordingly, q is interpreted here as a measure of spatial 
explanatory association rather than a regression coefficient, causal effect, or test of causality.
Interaction detection was performed by overlaying pairs of factor strata and comparing q(X1 ∩ X2) with the 
corresponding single-factor q values. A higher interaction q value indicates stronger joint explanatory associa -
tion than either factor alone, but does not identify the underlying mechanism or quantify the ecological effect of 
changes in individual factors.
For reproducibility, the selected discretisation method and class number for each continuous variable were 
recorded, while LULC and ST retained their original categories. To assess sensitivity to soil classification, the 
original 112 ST classes were progressively consolidated into the 80, 40, 20, and 10 most frequent classes, with 
the remaining classes grouped as “Other”, and q values were recalculated for each scheme.
Robustness of the multi-year OPGD results was further evaluated across random seeds (42, 512, and 2024), 
sample sizes (25,000, 50,000, and 100,000), four discretisation methods, and 5–10 classes. Stability was assessed 
by comparing factor rankings, overlap among the top three factors, and q values of key interactions across set -
tings. These tests quantify sensitivity to sampling and discretisation choices rather than causal effects.

Annual PCA showed substantial temporal variation in index structure. PC1 explained 78.37–85.70% of the

Sensitivity analysis revealed strong dependence of long-term trend interpretation on PCA construction. Annual 
PCA with year-specific scaling classified 39.21% of pixels as improving, 3.34% as stable, and 57.45% as degrad-
ing, compared with 99.42%, 0.07%, and 0.52% under unified PCA and 99.36%, 0.07%, and 0.57% under the 
fixed-weight approach, respectively. Pixel-level trend-direction agreement reached 99.90% between unified PCA 
and fixed weights but only 39.73% between annual and unified PCA. Thus, the apparent widespread degradation 
inferred from annual PCA was strongly method-sensitive, and subsequent analyses were based on the unified-
PCA series.
Provincial annual dynamics and ecological-quality classes
Based on the unified-PCA series, the mean PRSEI for Gansu Province increased from 0.343 in 2000 to 0.419 in 
2024, with a linear slope of 0.00432 yr⁻¹ (R² = 0.778), indicating a clear long-term improvement despite interan-
nual variability. Figure 4 presents the provincial mean PRSEI derived from unified PCA together with the corre-
sponding ecological-quality class composition. Annual-PCA results are used only for sensitivity assessment and 
are not used for primary temporal inference.
Class transitions based on fixed thresholds provide a more conservative measure of structural change than 
trend direction alone. From 2000 to 2024, 37.57% of pixels increased by at least one 0.2 class, 60.73% remained 
unchanged, and 1.71% decreased, indicating that widespread positive trends did not necessarily translate into 
shifts across ecological-quality classes. Provincial trends should therefore be interpreted alongside baseline con-
ditions and class transitions. Common scaling ensured temporally consistent thresholds, while the five classes 
were treated as relative mapping intervals rather than field-calibrated ecological-status thresholds.

Persistence tendencies from Hurst coupling
Coupling unified-PCA trends with the Hurst exponent showed that 99.415% of the area exhibited persistent 
improvement, 0.517% persistent degradation, and 0.066% stable or uncertain behaviour, while reversal catego -
ries together accounted for < 0.002%. These results indicate persistence tendencies in historical trajectories rather 
than deterministic predictions of future ecological change Fig. 8.
Residual degradation was highest in the Hexi Oasis–Corridor Agricultural Region (2.062%), followed by 
the Longnan–Tianshui Qinba Humid Mountain Region (0.328%), Longzhong–Longdong Loess Hilly Region 
(0.187%), Gannan Alpine Mountain Region (0.139%), and Hexi Desert–Gobi Region (0.127%). Although these 
proportions were small, their occurrence across eco-regions with contrasting baseline conditions warrants tar -
geted monitoring. Accordingly, the Hurst analysis is used here as a monitoring indicator of historical persistence 
rather than a predictive tool; a positive trend combined with H > 0.5 indicates persistence in past trajectories but 
does not guarantee continued improvement under future climatic, water-use, or land-cover change.

Temporal comparability reshapes long-term trend interpretation
The contrast between annual and unified PCA demonstrates that long-term ecological trend inference is highly 
sensitive to index construction. Because annual PCA allows component loadings, centring parameters, and scal-
ing ranges to vary among years, apparent temporal changes may partly reflect changes in the composite index 
itself. The high trend-direction agreement between unified PCA and fixed weights (99.90%), compared with only 
39.73% between annual and unified PCA, supports the use of a fixed index framework for long-term temporal 
inference. Under this framework, positive trends were widespread, although only 37.57% of pixels crossed at 
least one fixed PRSEI class. This distinction indicates that trend direction and class transition capture different 
aspects of ecological change and should therefore be interpreted jointly.
Eco-regional divergence in ecological quality and trends
Eco-regional analysis further identified areas where positive trends coexist with persistent ecological constraints. 
The Hexi Desert–Gobi Region had the lowest 25-year mean PRSEI (0.225) and the slowest improvement rate 
(0.00270 yr⁻¹), while the Hexi Oasis–Corridor Agricultural Region retained the highest proportion of residual 
negative trends (2.07%). These patterns are consistent with persistent constraints related to water limitation, high 
evaporative demand, wind erosion, sandification, and intensive water use, indicating that positive trends do not 
necessarily imply the removal of ecological risks. By contrast, the Longnan–Tianshui Qinba Humid Mountain, 
Longzhong–Longdong Loess Hilly, and Gannan Alpine Mountain regions showed higher ecological baselines 
and/or faster improvement. Although these patterns are broadly consistent with more favourable hydrothermal 
conditions and previously reported restoration or soil-conservation processes, the available evidence does not 
permit attribution to specific policies or engineering interventions.
Management interpretation should jointly consider PRSEI baseline levels, class transitions, trend direction, and 
persistence tendencies. Low-value areas may still require continued protection despite positive trends, whereas 
small residual degradation patches are better suited to targeted monitoring and should not be interpreted as evi -
dence of province-wide ecological decline.
Coupled spatial associations among water availability, soil, and land cover
The most robust OPGD finding was the consistent hierarchy of spatial associations across annual models. PRE, 
ST, and LULC remained the top three factors, with their principal pairwise interactions yielding q values of 
0.889–0.907. This stable pattern supports a joint interpretation involving water availability, soil background, and 
land-cover configuration, while remaining insufficient to establish causal mechanisms.
A plausible ecological interpretation is that precipitation influences vegetation growth, surface moisture, and 
bare-ground exposure, while soil properties such as texture, water-holding capacity, salinity, and erodibility mod-
ulate responses to climatic inputs. LULC further differentiates major surface types, including cropland, grassland, 
forest, bare land, water bodies, and built-up areas 41–46. Although these pathways are consistent with previous 
studies, they should be regarded as mechanistic hypotheses because OPGD cannot resolve temporal mediation 
or intervention effects.
Interaction effects can be interpreted as context-dependent spatial associations: the relationship between PRE 
and PRSEI may vary across soil and LULC categories, and similar land-cover types may exhibit different PRSEI 
levels under contrasting moisture conditions. However, these interactions do not imply that changing a given 
factor will produce a specific PRSEI response. Soil-classification sensitivity further showed that ST q values 
remained similar after moderate aggregation but declined under coarser classifications. Thus, soil background is 
a robust contributor to spatial stratification, while the magnitude of its q value remains dependent on classifica -
tion resolution.

Implications for ecological management across contrasting eco-regions
Management priorities should integrate PRSEI baseline conditions, class transitions, trend direction, and per -
sistence tendencies. In the arid Hexi region, the results support continued protection of desert vegetation and 
oasis margins, targeted monitoring of residual degradation patches, and closer consideration of ecological water 
requirements in irrigation and groundwater management. Because this study did not quantify the effects of spe -
cific interventions, these measures should be viewed as evidence-based management priorities rather than pre -
scriptive recommendations for particular engineering actions.
In the Loess Hilly, alpine, and humid mountain regions, relatively rapid ecological improvement suggests that 
restoration and conservation gains should be consolidated rather than assumed complete. Continued priorities 
include soil and water conservation, grassland and wetland protection, forest conservation, and monitoring of 
localised degradation. However, project-specific recommendations require independent information on restora -
tion intensity, grazing pressure, water use, and protected-area management.
The enhanced joint associations among water availability, soil conditions, and LULC further suggest that 
ecological monitoring and intervention assessment should be stratified by environmental context. Such context-
specific evaluation is better supported by the present evidence than uniform province-wide recommendations and 
provides a practical basis for prioritising future field validation and process-based research.
Limitations and future work
First, the 474.751 m grid is appropriate for long-term provincial-scale monitoring but may not resolve narrow 
valley oases, urban fringes, or small restoration patches. Higher-resolution Landsat, Sentinel, or Chinese satellite 
data should therefore be used for local validation while retaining the temporal continuity of the MODIS-based 
analysis.
Second, OPGD q values and interaction terms represent spatial associations rather than causal effects and can-
not resolve temporal ordering, mediating pathways, or intervention responses. Future causal attribution should 
therefore integrate OPGD with process-based models, quasi-experimental designs, field observations, or matched 
restoration controls.
Third, the PM10-based TI is not dust-specific because PM10 includes both dust and non-dust sources. In addi-
tion, the archived TI grids for 2022–2024 extend beyond the period documented for LGHAP v2. Dust-specific 
interpretation therefore requires further validation using independent evidence such as AOD, dust-event fre -
quency, or deposition records.
In addition, annual data on ecological engineering intensity, grazing pressure, groundwater abstraction, and 
irrigation water use were unavailable. Consequently, the low q value of NTL should not be interpreted as evi -
dence that human activities exert little influence on ecological quality.
Finally, although unified PCA improves temporal comparability, it remains sensitive to global minimum–
maximum extremes. The five fixed 0.2 classes should be interpreted as comparable mapping intervals rather than 
universal ecological thresholds, and CLCD provides external categorical consistency rather than field validation. 
Future work should therefore assess more robust scaling approaches, field-calibrated thresholds, and independent 
ecological datasets.
Conclusion
This study constructed a temporally comparable adaptive PRSEI for Gansu Province (2000–2024) using uni -
fied PCA and common scaling. The unified PC1 explained 79.12% of the total variance. Sensitivity analysis 
showed that long-term trend inference was highly dependent on PCA construction: pixel-level trend-direction 
agreement was only 39.73% between annual and unified PCA, but reached 99.90% between unified PCA and the

fixed-weight approach. These results highlight the importance of maintaining a consistent index framework in 
long-term ecological time-series analysis.
Under the unified-PCA framework, mean provincial PRSEI increased from 0.343 to 0.419 (slope = 0.00432 
yr⁻¹; R² = 0.778). Positive trends covered 99.42% of the common-mask area, although only 37.57% of pixels 
increased by at least one fixed PRSEI class. Ecological improvement was therefore widespread but spatially het-
erogeneous: all five eco-regions showed positive trends, while the Hexi Desert–Gobi Region retained the lowest 
baseline and the Hexi Oasis–Corridor Agricultural Region the highest proportion of residual negative trends.
Index comparison showed that adaptive PRSEI remained highly consistent with original PRSEI spatially and 
showed greater consistency with mapped restoration transitions, but weaker consistency with degradation transi-
tions than traditional RSEI. It should therefore be viewed as a complementary multi-stressor index for drylands 
rather than a universally superior replacement for RSEI. OPGD further identified PRE, ST, and LULC as the most 
stable spatial explanatory factors, although their q values indicate association rather than causality. Remaining 
uncertainties in TI interpretation, degradation detection, class calibration, and process attribution require further 
validation using independent atmospheric, field, and management data.
