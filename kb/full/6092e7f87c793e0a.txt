Heavy metal exposure and source profiling based 
on leaf‑deposited particulate matter in Pune city
Akshay Paygude · Rajat Sharma · 
Latha Radhadevi  · Murthy Bandaru
Received: 23 January 2026 / Accepted: 3 September 2026 / Published online: 16 September 2026 
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature Switzerland AG 2026
Abstract This study investigates particulate matter 
deposited on plant leaves across 27 roadside, indus-
trial, peri-urban, highway, and rural sites in Pune 
city to quantify metal concentrations, identify prob-
able pollution sources, and evaluate inhalation-related 
non-carcinogenic health risk. Leaf-deposited dust 
offers a practical, low-cost approach for detecting 
metal-enriched urban microenvironments. Samples 
were analysed for ten elements, with concentrations 
following the order Ca > K > Fe > Na > Mg > Mn > Z
n > Cu > Cr > Pb, reflecting dominant contributions 
from crustal material, construction activity, and resus-
pended road dust. Strong correlations amongst Ca, 
Fe, Mg, Mn, Pb, Cu, and Cr pointed to combined nat-
ural and anthropogenic influences. Enrichment fac-
tor analysis identified Cu, Zn, and Cr as substantially 
enriched at select traffic- and industry-influenced 
locations. Principal component analysis distinguished 
source groups linked to construction and road dust, 
vehicular emissions, biomass or food-related activ -
ity, and localised industrial inputs. Spatial analysis 
showed central urban sites were mainly influenced 
by construction, manufacturing, and vehicular dust, 
whilst outer-zone sites reflected additional industrial, 
agricultural, and resuspended dust contributions. 
Health risk assessment showed hazard quotients fol-
lowing the order Cr > Pb > Cu > Zn, with hazard 
indices for adults and children remaining below the 
non-carcinogenic risk threshold. However, localised 
enrichment of Cu, Zn, and Cr indicates that metal 
exposure potential is spatially uneven, even when 
overall risk estimates remain low. This highlights the 
need for zone-specific monitoring and source-targeted 
control of road dust, construction activity, traffic 
emissions, and industrial inputs in rapidly urbanising, 
mixed-use urban environments.

Local activity patterns can therefore generate spa-

countries. The health relevance of particulate mat-

on chemical composition, especially the presence of 
toxic metals such as Pb, Cr, Cu, and Zn (Correa et al., 
2023).
Capturing this spatial unevenness, however, 
remains a persistent challenge. Conventional air qual-
ity monitoring relies on fixed-station networks that 
are costly to install and maintain, and are therefore 
too sparse to resolve the fine-scale, source-driven var-
iability typical of rapidly urbanising cities. This gap 
between where pollution actually varies and where 
it is actually measured has driven interest in passive 
biomonitoring as a complementary, spatially flexible 
alternative. Biological surfaces retain airborne parti-
cles over an exposure period and can therefore pro-
vide spatially resolved information on deposited par -
ticulate matter and associated metals.
The moss bag technique, first applied in Nordic 
countries, has been used since 1971 to assess the 
geographical distribution of heavy metal deposition 
(Rühling & Tyler, 1971), establishing the value of 
biological media in atmospheric pollution studies. 
Building on this concept, biomonitoring using plant 
and tree media has since been applied across diverse 
urban and industrial settings. Solgi et al. (2019) used 
coniferous trees in Pennsylvania, USA, whilst Zhang 
et al. (2017) assessed pollution levels in typical Chi-
nese urban areas, demonstrating that plant-based 
media can reflect spatial differences in atmospheric 
contamination across different geographies and cli-
mates. The effectiveness of leaves as passive collec-
tors, however, is not uniform: retention of particulate 
matter depends on leaf surface characteristics such 
as trichome density, wax content, surface roughness, 
leaf shape, and exposure angle, with higher trichome 
density and surface roughness generally increas-
ing dust deposition (Zhang et  al., 2021). Sharma 
et  al. (2022) further examined different plant parts 
to assess relative metal accumulation, underscoring 
that a sampling design influences the signal captured. 
Taken together, these studies show that live plants 
can provide an economical and spatially flexible 
approach for assessing local variation in atmospheric 
dust and metal contamination, precisely the kind of 
fine-grained, low-cost tool needed to resolve emission 
hotspots in fast-growing, resource-constrained urban 
environments such as Indian cities.
Other studies have also appraised the use of plants 
for removing particulate matter from the air (Han 
et  al., 2022; Liu et  al., 2017). Air pollution and its 
health effects in urban areas depend not only on par -
ticulate matter concentration but also on heavy metal 
composition. Studies of road dust in metropolitan 
areas show substantial contamination by Fe and Al 
(Jangirh et al., 2024). Exposure to toxic metals such 
as mercury, cadmium, and lead poses significant 
health risks, especially for vulnerable groups such 
as children and pregnant women (Okereafor et  al., 
2020; Rehman et al., 2018). Road dust contaminated 
by heavy metals can adversely affect human and eco-
logical health, including potential cancer risk (Taye 
& Chandravanshi, 2023, 2024). The enrichment fac-
tor of a metal indicates the degree of enrichment 
from anthropogenic sources relative to natural crus-
tal sources. Severely enriched metals and their spa-
tial variability can help identify exposure hotspots. 
Urban forests may help mitigate some of these threats 
(Livesley et  al., 2016; O’Brien et  al., 2022), and 
Miao et al., 2025) reviewed urban forest-management 
practices and their benefits. Despite this, most exist-
ing studies address particulate retention by vegeta-
tion, road dust contamination, source identification, 
or health-risk assessment separately. Fewer studies 
combine leaf-deposited metal analysis with source 
interpretation and site-level risk assessment across 
multiple urban microenvironments. This gap is par -
ticularly relevant for Indian cities such as Pune, where 
roadside, industrial, peri-urban, highway, and rural 
zones occur in close proximity because of mixed land 
use and rapid urban growth. Under such conditions, 
pollution sources and their relative contributions can 
vary sharply over short distances, making city-wide 
average air-quality estimates insufficient for identify -
ing local exposure hotspots.
This study addresses this gap by combining leaf-
deposited dust sampling, enrichment factor analysis, 
correlation analysis, PCA-based source interpretation, 
and inhalation-related non-carcinogenic risk assess-
ment within a single approach. The specific objec-
tives are to (i) quantify ten metals in leaf-deposited 
particulate matter across different microenvironments 
of Pune city, (ii) identify probable natural and anthro-
pogenic source contributions, (iii) examine spatial

Materials and methods
The study followed a sequential workflow linking 
site selection, controlled plant exposure, laboratory 
quantification, statistical source interpretation, and 
screening-level exposure assessment (Fig.  1). This 
structure was used to ensure that the sampling design, 
analytical treatment, and risk interpretation remained 
traceable.
Site selection
The present study was conducted in Pune, Maha-
rashtra, India, primarily in the area covered by the 
Pune Municipal Corporation. The sampling loca-
tions in Pune lie between 18.4475 and 18.6210° N 
and 73.7636 and 73.9430° E. Pune spans an area of 
approximately 516.2 km2. A semi-arid climate char -
acterises the city, and its weather fluctuates between 
rainy to cold and dry. The experimental locations 
exhibiting distinct land-use designs and particulate 
loads were selected for the study. Most of the loca-
tions were near the roadside, which were directly 
influenced by traffic emissions. Some were located at 
the periphery of industrial hubs, and the rest were sit-
uated along highways and in rural areas, which were 
influenced by biomass burning. Ease of permission 
and the availability of proper road dividers and foot-
paths were also required to place the sampling plant.

Plant selection
Total suspended particulate (TSP) deposition stud-
ies are usually conducted on pre-existing plants 
without a fixed deposition period. This method 
may produce incomparable results. Shrub plants 
are planted along road dividers to enhance road 
aesthetics and reduce glare from vehicles travelling 
in the opposite direction. Some of the commonly 
found plants on dividers are Euphorbia milii, 
Nerium oleander, Caesalpinia pulcherrima, The-
vetia peruviana, Tecoma stans, and Catharanthus 
roseus. Catharanthus roseus was selected for this 
study because it is hairless and has leaves without 
wax. Leaf arrangement, leaf shape and area, and 
the presence of hair or wax affect deposition on 
leaves (Viecco et  al., 2018). Placing fresh plants 
and using their leaves as a collection medium 
for economic purposes by the kerbside for a spe-
cific duration, followed by subsequent chemical

Sampling
Sampling sites were selected whilst ensuring maxi-
mum geographic coverage. Whilst ensuring maxi-
mum coverage, the number of sites could become 
very high. Hence, 27 sampling sites with differ -
ent micro-environments and perceived air qual-
ity conditions were considered for the study. The 
plants were healthy, young plants that had not 
started flowering, about 30- to 40-cm tall, with 
dense leaves, bought fresh from the nursery just 
a day before the sampling day. Plant leaves were 
sprayed thoroughly with distilled water to ensure 
no initial deposition of any sort was present on the 
leaves. Plants were labelled and placed at a prede-
fined site or as close to it as possible for a fixed 
duration of 2 days. The GPS location of each plant 
was recorded using a GARMIN 60 GPS. Deploy -
ment and collection dates and times (in the winter 
season) of plants were also recorded. Both primary 
and duplicate samples were collected for 27 sites 
and 20 sites, respectively. Duplicate samples were 
collected nearby to ensure that the area/point was 
well represented. A few plants went missing, and 
some samples that did not produce detectable ana-
lytes (i.e., metals) were ignored. A total of 35 sam-
ples were considered for this study. Areas of plant 
leaves were obtained by washing the leaves, plac-
ing them on graph paper, and summing the areas.
The campaign was conducted over a limited 
period (2nd half of January–1st half of February) 
at multiple sites for 48 h, primarily on the kerbside, 
which may provide a reasonable minimum value of 
exposure for people travelling through these roads 
or residents in the area.
Sample preparation for lab analysis
A plant blank solution was prepared using ten 
leaves from an unexposed plant. The leaves were 
washed with 10  mL of distilled water by keep-
ing them immersed in a beaker for approximately 
2  h before removal. To 5  mL of this blank solu-
tion, 3  mL of nitric acid (HNO3), 1.5  mL of 
hydrochloric acid (HCl), and 0.5  mL of hydro-
fluoric acid (HF) were added, and the solution was 
made up to 15 mL. The remaining 5 mL was pre-
pared in the same way for duplicate analysis and 
served as the reference solution. Similarly, ten 
leaves from each exposed plant were placed in a 
beaker with 10  mL of distilled water for the pri-
mary sample and 20 mL for the duplicate sample. 
From the 20  mL duplicate solution, 10  mL was 
taken, and 5  mL of HNO3, 2.5  mL of HCl, and 
1 mL of HF were added. A larger sample volume 
was used for the duplicate to meet the require-
ments for ionic analysis. The water in the beaker 
was stirred for 30  s using a magnetic stirrer, left 
undisturbed for 1  min, and stirred again for 30  s. 
The blank and sample solutions, containing the 
washed deposition from the leaves, were trans-
ferred to vials, digested for 24 h, and analysed for 
metal content. A test analysis of site 25, which had 
the highest deposition, indicated the presence of 13 
metals. However, the final analysis was limited to 
ten metals. Cobalt (Co), cadmium (Cd), and nickel 
(Ni) were present at very low concentrations, as 
inferred from the wavelength peaks. Since stand-
ards for these metals were not available, calibration 
was not possible; hence, they were excluded.
Chemical analysis
Analysis method and quality control
Atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS; Analy -
tik Jena ContrAA 800 D) was carried out accord-
ing to the standard procedure (Sergio et al., 2018). 
The ContrAA 800 D is a high-resolution contin-
uum-source atomic absorption spectrometer (HR-
CS AAS) that uses a single source (high-intensity 
xenon short-arc lamp) for all elements. It provides 
superior background correction using a reference 
spectrum and has a high-resolution double-echelle 
monochromator and a CCD array detector. The 
method is based on the absorption of light by free 
metallic ions. Standards with known analyte con-
tent are required to establish the relation between 
measured absorbance and analyte concentration, 
following the Beer–Lambert law. Each metal has a 
specific atomisation temperature and a characteristic 
absorption wavelength. Depending on the required 
temperature, a C2H2/air gas stream was used for

Statistical analysis
Correlation analysis
Pearson correlation analysis was performed to iden-
tify the possible sources of elements associated with 
TSP. It measures the degree of the linear relation-
ship between metal variables in TSP (Atiemo et  al., 
2011; Fu et al., 2020; Pipal et al., 2011). The statisti-
cally significant levels of the p-values are as follows: 
0.01 < p < 0.05 (“*”), 0.001 < p < 0.01 (“**”), and 
p < 0.001 (“***”). A correlation is considered to be 
statistically significant if the p-value < 0.05. Homo-
geneity and normal distribution were tested for the 
analysis using the “R” statistical package.

emissions in the crustal and non-crustal origin of the 
metal, the enrichment factor (EF), which measures 
the enrichment of metals in the dust compared to 
the upper continental crust, was used. The following

Validity of principal component analysis (PCA)
PCA is a technique that identifies strong patterns in a 
dataset by grouping variables into uncorrelated prin-
cipal components. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) 
test was used to assess sampling adequacy for fac-
tor analysis. The KMO value for Ring 2 was 0.818, 
which indicates strong adequacy for factor analysis 
with 22 samples. The KMO value for Ring 1 was 
0.551, which is marginal but acceptable for explora-
tory factor analysis with 13 samples; therefore, Ring 
1 PCA results should be interpreted cautiously. Vari-
ous methods have been used by the scientific com-
munity to study source apportionment and ecological 
and health risks of trace pollutants; details are avail-
able elsewhere (Onjia et al., 2022).
Exposure risk assessment
Humans can be exposed to deposited particulate mat-
ter through inhalation after resuspension. Because 
respiratory exposure is relevant to public health, 
USEPA-based inhalation risk equations were used 
for screening-level assessment. Amongst the metals 
examined, inhalation-related non-carcinogenic risk 
was evaluated for Zn, Cr, Pb, and Cu because rele-
vant reference values were available and these metals 
are associated with chronic health effects (He et  al., 
2025). At the individual level, substantial uncertainty 
remains because actual exposure depends on recep-
tor activity, proximity, resuspension, and time spent 
at each site. The hazard quotient (HQ) for long-term 
inhalation exposure, following the USEPA-based

Enrichment factor analysis for locations
EF analysis was done only for the toxic/heavy met-
als, i.e., Cu, Zn, Mn, Pb, and Cr. The EF analysis for 
the dust on the leaves at all sampling sites is shown in 
Fig.  4. Cu and Zn are relatively higher than the car -
cinogenic metals Pb and Cr. The EF of Cr is 25, and 
Pb has an EF of 2, indicating the least enrichment and 
suggesting that lead contamination is minimal or likely 
due to natural sources, but Cr is present due to human 
activities. Zn and Cu values are greater than 20, indicat-
ing excessive enrichment and a significant contribution 
from non-crustal and anthropogenic sources. Mn, how -
ever, lies at a moderate enrichment level (EF = 8.8).
The individual EF analysis for all sampling loca-
tions provides in-depth elemental characterisation. 
Figure 4 represents EF for all the sampling locations. 
The sites with extremely high enrichment for Cu are 
1, 5, 13, 16, 21, 28, and 24; for Zn, they are sites 2, 
5, 16, 21, 24, 28, 32, and 35; and for Mn, it is site 
28. These sites are in the same category, suggest-
ing major anthropogenic hotspots in these locations. 
Carcinogenic metals Pb and Cr were not present in 
every area; however, together, they exhibit significant 
enrichment at sites 1, 5, 21 and 26.
Principal component analysis (PCA)
Five principal components (PC1, PC2, PC3, PC4, 
and PC5) were extracted for the study, representing 
97.14% of the total variance. Table  6 represents the 
percentages of variance and cumulative percentages 
of each PC. The rotated factor loadings for the five 
PCs are shown in Fig. 5a.
PC1 accounted for 38.05% of the total variance. 
Fe, Mg, Ca and Mn heavily weighted it. The contri-
bution of these metals to PC1 can be considered as 
sourced from crustal and road dust. Hence, PC1 is 
attributed to metal generation and contamination 
through construction, road and crustal dust.
The PC2 accounted for 25.02% of the total vari-
ance. K and Zn mainly weighted it. As seen in Fig. 5a 
and b, it is clear that the enrichment of K in road dust 
is due to distinct sources of particulate matter, such 
as biomass burning (Sharma et  al., 2022). K was 
observed in areas where hospitals (refuse incinera-
tion), food processing (biomass as fuel), and logistics 
units are present. Zn in the PC2 category, which is 
dominant after K, is primarily sourced from road-
way activity and engine wear (Sharma et  al., 2014), 
underscoring the impact of vehicular movement (Rai 
et al., 2020). Hence, PC2 is considered to be “metal 
contamination” due to mixed sources, including point 
sources and vehicular movement (Ewen et al., 2009; 
Kabir et al., 2022).
The PC3 accounted for 24.59% of the total vari-
ance; however, no specific element can be attributed 
to the source. PC3 contains some Zn, Pb, Mn, and 
Cr, indicating a mix of elemental concentrations. 
Automobiles, engine wear, crustal dust and indus-
trial point sources indicate no significant contribu-
tion but impact the overall elemental concentration 
of PC3.
PC4 accounted for 7.35% of the total variance, and 
Na is the dominating element with a very low enrich-
ment factor. Na is rarely detected in crustal dust and 
is primarily sourced from sea salt (Wong et al., 2022; 
Kumar & Raman, 2020). In this case, the major cause 
of Na is restaurants and food chains, as the number of 
restaurants in the PMC region exceeds 5000. Na was 
not significantly enriched but was present in every 
sampling site.
The PC5 accounted for 2.11% of the total vari-
ance. The parameters of PC5 and their high cor -
relation with each other indicate the necessity to 
consider all five PC values for source apportion-
ment. Cu, Pb and Cr were the dominant parameters 
governing PC5. According to the correlation matrix 
analysis, the high positive correlations (r > 0.8) 
between all three elements are exactly sourced due 
to anthropogenic processes. Figure  4 illustrates 
the heavy enrichment of elements from industrial 
sources. Industrial emissions result in certain hot-
spots, causing these harmful metals to increase, 
along with moderate vehicular exhaust. The eigen-
values after PC2 are less than 1; this observation 
explicitly indicates a significant percentage of metal 
concentration, which cannot be ignored based solely 
on the eigenvalue.

Ring 1 (Fig.  5a), and Ring 2 (Fig.  5b) to compare 
the elemental distribution in various areas from dif-
ferent sources.

Exposure risk assessment
The atmospheric deposition exposure for both 
children and adults is shown in Table  7. The 
order of exposure in terms of hazard quotient is 
Cr > Pb > Cu > Zn for children and adults. The expo-
sure in children is significantly larger than that seen 
in adults. The hazard index (HI) is equal to the sum 
of HQ values.
In their study of road dust in Dhanbad, Kumari 
et al. (2021) assessed the hazard index for Cr, a car -
cinogenic element. It was found to be three orders of 
magnitude less than that in the current study.
The health risk assessment of students in Belgrade 
from inhalation exposure inside schoolrooms found 
that elements bound to PM2.5 showed Ni, Cr, and Mn 
had a more dominant non-carcinogenic risk; however, 
the hazard index values for all elements were within 
the safety threshold (Obradović et al., 2025). Higher 
cancer risk was found to be associated with Cr. The 
study also finds that traffic and resuspended dust were 
the predominant sources of elements in PM2.5 in an 
urban area.
Zupančič et  al. (2024) underscore in their study 
that most bioavailable metals are inhaled through 
traffic-related emissions. A recent study in South 
China indicates that road dust is a significant source 
of heavy metals, and it also finds that amongst these, 
Zn and Mn exhibit the highest presence in the gastro-
intestinal region (Han et al., 2023).
Limitations of the study
It is to be noted that sampling of TSP deposited on 
plant leaves by exposing them to the ambient environ-
ment for 48 h may not capture temporal variability or 
that leaf sampling has limitations compared to active 
air sampling. The campaign was done for a limited 
period (2nd half of January–1st half of February, i.e., 
Jan 15–19; Feb 01–05; 07–09; 16–19) at multiple 
sites, and it is not meant to study seasonality and tem-
poral variability, but to get a baseline. Furthermore, 
activities continue to change around the area, such as 
metro constructions and flyovers, causing repeatabil-
ity issues.
The deposited dust comes mainly from resus-
pended road dust due to traffic movement (sampling 
sites are near road junctions and dividers) and from 
nearby sources (such as industries within a 3-km 
buffer zone around the sampling site). The deposition 
is a function of atmospheric turbulence, leaf physi-
ology (such as shape, angle of exposure, plant type, 
hairs, and wax), and ambient pollution levels. Routine 
ambient air pollution monitoring using CAQMS (con-
tinuous air quality monitoring systems) is being con-
ducted in Pune; however, these systems are generally

Conclusion
This study assessed metal deposition on plant leaves 
across Pune city to identify spatial variability, prob-
able sources, and inhalation-related non-carcinogenic 
health risk. The mean metal deposition followed the 
order Ca > K > Fe > Na > Mg > Mn > Zn > Cu > Cr 
> Pb, indicating that crustal material, construction 
activity, and resuspended road dust were the domi-
nant contributors to deposited particulate matter. The 
wide site-to-site variability, especially for Pb, Cr, K, 
Zn, Mn, and Cu, shows that metal deposition in Pune 
is not spatially uniform but is strongly influenced by 
local land use and nearby activities.
Correlation and enrichment-factor analyses indi-
cated mixed natural and anthropogenic sources. Fe, 
Mg, Ca, and Mn were mainly associated with crustal, 
construction-related, and road-dust sources, whereas 
Cu, Zn, Pb, and Cr showed stronger anthropogenic 
influence linked to traffic, vehicle wear, industrial 
activity, and localised point sources. Enrichment-factor 
results identified Cu, Zn, and Cr as the most important 
enriched metals at selected locations, confirming the 
presence of metal-enriched microenvironments rather 
than uniform city-wide contamination. PCA further 
separated the major source groups, including construc-
tion and road dust, vehicular emissions, biomass or 
food-related activities, and localised industrial inputs.
Spatial grouping showed clear differences between 
central and outer urban zones. Central locations were 
more strongly influenced by construction, manu-
facturing, chemical activities, and vehicular dust, 
whereas outer-zone locations reflected additional 
influence from agriculture, agro-industries, industrial 
areas, and resuspended dust. This spatial pattern 
demonstrates that urban metal pollution is controlled 
by short-distance source variability and cannot be 
adequately represented by broad city-level average 
air-quality indicators alone.
The health-risk assessment showed that hazard 
quotients followed the order Cr > Pb > Cu > Zn for 
both adults and children. The hazard indices for adults 
and children were 3.25 × 10−5 and 1.04 × 10−4, respec-
tively, remaining below the non-carcinogenic risk 
threshold of 1. However, children showed higher rela-
tive exposure than adults, and the localised enrichment 
of Cu, Zn, and Cr indicates that low overall risk esti-
mates should not be interpreted as the absence of site-
specific concern. From an environmental-health per -
spective, these enriched locations should be treated as 
priority zones for further monitoring and mitigation.
The findings have practical relevance for urban air-
quality management. Dust suppression at construc-
tion sites, regular removal of road dust near junctions 
and dividers, control of traffic-related resuspension, 
inspection of industrial and chemical activity zones, 
and improved management of agro-industrial and 
roadside emissions should be prioritised in locations 
where enrichment is observed. The study also sup-
ports the use of leaf-deposited dust analysis as a low-
cost screening approach for identifying metal-enriched 
urban microenvironments and guiding the targeted 
placement of conventional monitoring instruments.
