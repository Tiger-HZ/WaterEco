 Abstract 
 Perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), a prevalent and environmentally persistent compounds in the per- and polyfluoroalkyl substance (PFAS) family, is ubiquitously detected in the food chain and has been epidemiologically linked to inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). However, the actions and mechanisms by which PFOA exposure influences colonic inflammation under colitis conditions remain incompletely understood. In this study, we investigated the impact of dietary PFOA exposure on colitis severity using a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis model. Our results suggest that dietary PFOA exposure exacerbated colitis severity, as reflected by increased loss in body weight, colon shortening, and histopathological damage. Notably, PFOA exposure promoted colonic neutrophil infiltration and activation, characterized by increased expression of neutrophil markers, upregulation of neutrophil-recruiting chemokines, and enhanced expression of oxidative enzymes. PFOA also led to goblet cell depletion and reduced E-cadherin expression in colitis mice, indicating compromised integrity of epithelial barrier. Furthermore, fecal microbiome and metabolomic analyses revealed that PFOA exposure altered gut microbial composition and fecal metabolic profiles, which could be associated with the enhanced inflammatory outcomes observed during acute colitis. Collectively, these findings indicate that dietary PFOA exposure aggravates experimental colitis through coordinated neutrophil-mediated inflammation, epithelial barrier disruption, and microbiome-associated alterations. Our results highlight the potential role of PFOA as an environmental exacerbator of IBD and provide mechanistic insight into how its exposure influences host–microbiome interactions.

 1. Introduction 
 Perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), one of the most prevalent synthetic fluorinated compounds within the per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) family, is now recognized as a pervasive environmental contaminant and a major public health concern. PFOA has been widely used since the mid-20th century in numerous industrial and consumer applications, including non-stick cookware coatings, food-contact materials, stain-resistant textiles, firefighting foams, and surface treatment agents 1 – 2 . Given its extensive historical use and remarkable chemical stability, PFOA contamination is ubiquitously detected in diverse aquatic environments, including drinking water supplies, groundwater, rivers, and oceans 3 – 5 . Moreover, PFOA bioaccumulates along the food chain, with particularly elevated concentrations reported in fish and seafood 6 – 7 . Human exposure to PFOA primarily occurs through the consumption of contaminated drinking water and via dietary intake 8 . Following ingestion, PFOA undergoes enterohepatic recirculation, whereby a substantial fraction excreted in bile is reabsorbed in the intestine 9 – 10 . This recycling process prolongs its residence within the gastrointestinal tract leading to enhanced local tissue exposure, raising concerns regarding its potential impact on gastrointestinal diseases. Collectively, these characteristics highlight PFOA as a persistent environmental toxicant with the potential to affect multiple physiological systems, including the gastrointestinal tract. 
 Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease, is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract with a rapidly increasing global incidence, affecting millions of individuals worldwide 11 . Although the precise etiology of IBD remains incompletely understood, the marked rise in disease prevalence over recent decades, particularly during rapid industrialization, strongly implicates industrial environmental exposures as critical contributors to disease onset and progression 12 – 13 . Emerging epidemiological evidence supports a link between PFOA exposure and IBD 14 – 15 . A large population-based cohort study conducted in highly exposed communities in the Mid-Ohio Valley reported increased incidences of ulcerative colitis among individuals and industry workers with elevated serum PFOA levels 14 – 15 . Beyond epidemiological associations, emerging studies have demonstrated that PFOA exposure induces intestinal injury and inflammation across multiple animal models. For example, in rodents, chronic PFOA exposure via drinking water results in IBD-like colonic pathology, including impaired function in epithelial barrier and activation of inflammatory signaling pathways 16 – 18 . Additionally, PFOA exposure can trigger intestinal epithelial apoptosis and alter immune cell homeostasis in non-mammalian species, including honeybees 19 and broiler chickens 20 , indicating conserved enterotoxic effects across taxa. Furthermore, accumulating evidence supports that PFOA exposure has been associated with gut microbiota perturbations 21 – 22 . Together, these findings suggest that PFOA exposure may represent an environmental risk factor for IBD. 
 Despite the growing body of evidence, the mechanisms by which PFOA promotes colonic inflammation and compromises mucosal integrity under colitis conditions remain incompletely understood. Moreover, while most prior experimental studies have relied predominantly on drinking water exposure paradigms, little is known about the biological consequences of chronic dietary exposure, which represents another primary route of human PFOA intake. To address these gaps, the present study aims to investigate the impact of dietary PFOA exposure on the susceptibility and severity of colitis using a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis model in C57BL/6 mice. In addition, we aim to examine alterations in gut microbial composition and metabolic profiles through fecal microbiome and metabolomic analyses. A better understanding of the impact of dietary PFOA exposure on host inflammatory responses and gut microbiota dynamics could help clarify the potential role of PFOA as an environmental factor that exacerbates colonic inflammation and provide insight into mechanisms by which chronic dietary exposure influence colitis pathogenesis. 2. Methods and Materials 
 2.1. Animal Experiments 
 All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Purdue University (Protocol #1123002447). PFOA (Sigma-Aldrich) are dissolved in soybean oil and incorporated into a standard AIN-93G diet (Dyets Inc.). Control diets will contain equivalent amounts of soybean oil without PFOA. The PFOA concentration of 15 ppm (~2.4 mg/kg/day, calculated assuming a 25 g mouse consuming 4 g of diet per day) was selected based on prior reports showing gastrointestinal toxicity of PFOA within an exposure range of 1–3 mg/kg/day in mouse models 18 , 21 , 23 – 24 . Moreover, previous research has shown that oral exposure to 2 mg/L of PFOA results in plasma accumulation of approximately 8.83 mg/L 17 . In addition, a previous study reported that 28-day oral gavage exposure to PFOA at 3 mg/kg/day resulted in mean plasma concentrations of 48.9 μg/mL in mice 25 . These exposure-associated concentrations are comparable to the range of serum PFOA levels reported in occupationally exposed human populations (0.20 to 47.04 μg/mL) 26 . Six-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and randomly assigned to receive either a standard AIN-93G diet or an AIN-93G diet incorporated with PFOA for 4 weeks (n = 6 mice per experimental group). Following this pretreatment period, colitis was induced by administering 1.5% dextran sulfate sodium (DSS; molecular weight 40 kDa, Thermo Scientific Chemicals, Waltham, MA, USA) in drinking water for 5 days, while dietary PFOA exposure was maintained. For the baseline (non-colitis) model, six-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were similarly assigned to either the standard AIN-93G diet or the PFOA-supplemented diet (n = 4 mice per group) for 4 weeks without DSS treatment. At study completion, animals were euthanized, and colon tissues were collected for subsequent analyses. 2.2. Histological Analysis 
 Distal colonic samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, processed through paraffin embedding, sectioned, and subsequently deparaffinized. To assess overall tissue morphology, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and examined using a brightfield microscope. Histopathological evaluation was performed using a scoring system that considered epithelial and crypt damage, inflammatory cell infiltration, muscularis changes, and goblet cell depletion following the established method 27 . Histological scoring was performed by a blinded investigator to minimize bias. 
 Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining were used to stain the PAS + goblet cells. After deparaffinization, sections were treated with periodic acid (Thermo Scientific Chemicals) for 10 min and then exposed to Schiff’s reagent for 5 min. PAS-positive cells were enumerated under a light microscope, and Goblet cell density was expressed as the number of PAS + goblet cells per crypt. 
 For immunofluorescence staining of E-cadherin, antigen retrieval was conducted by heating slides in citrate buffer (0.01 M, pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 10 minutes. Sections were incubated with an anti-E-cadherin antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #3195). A fluorescent secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor ® 555 (Cell Signaling Technology, #4413) was applied, and cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. Fluorescent images were captured using a Zeiss Imager.A2 AX10 fluorescent microscope. Signal intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). 
 For immunohistochemistry staining of Ly6G, antigen retrieval was conducted by heating slides in Tris–EDTA buffer (10 mM Tris base, 1 mM EDTA, pH 9.0) at 95 °C for 10 minutes. Immunohistochemical staining was carried out using an HRP/DAB detection kit (Abcam, #ab64264) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sections were incubated overnight with an anti-lymphocyte antigen 6 family member G (Ly6G) primary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #87048) and visualized using 3,3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen. To confirm staining specificity, the rabbit IgG isotype control antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #3900) was applied as a negative control for immunohistochemistry. Images were acquired using an Olympus BX43 microscope at 400X magnification, and Ly6G + cells were quantified by counting the number of positively stained cells per field using ImageJ software. Specifically, a total of 24 random fields per group (4 random fields per mouse) were analyzed. Each field had an area of 1600 × 1200 pixels. Fields were randomly selected, and only the lamina propria and submucosa regions of the colon were included for analysis to ensure consistency. 2.3. RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR) 
 Total RNA was extracted from distal colonic tissues using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was generated from purified RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted on a CFX96 real-time detection platform (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mouse-specific primers were obtained commercially (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and sequences were sourced from the PrimerBank database; complete primer information is listed in Table S1 ( Supporting Information ). Expression data were normalized to Gapdh ( glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase ) as the internal reference gene and calculated using the 2 −ΔΔCt method to determine relative fold changes compared with control. 2.4. Western Blotting 
 Total protein was extracted from distal colonic tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (Boston Bioproducts, #BP-115DG) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A32963). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #23227). Equal amounts of protein were separated on 10% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were then blocked with EveryBlot blocking buffer (Bio-Rad, #12010020) and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies following the manufacturer’s suggestion. After washing, membranes were incubated with IRDye ® 800CW (LI-COR Biosciences, #926–32211) or IRDye ® 680RD (LI-COR Biosciences, #926–68180) secondary antibodies. The primary antibodies used were Ly6G (Cell Signaling Technology, #87048), myeloperoxidase (Cell Signaling Technology, #15178), E-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology, #3195), and β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, #3700). Protein bands were visualized using a LI-COR Odyssey LX imaging system, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. 2.5. Gut Microbiota Composition Analysis 
 Fecal samples collected prior to DSS treatment were used for gut microbiota analysis. Total genomic DNA was extracted using a Quick-DNA Fecal/Soil Microbe Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, Tustin, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Amplicon library preparation and 16S rRNA gene sequencing were performed at the Purdue Genomics and Genome Editing Facility using an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) as previously described 28 . Briefly, the V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified by PCR using region-specific primers and subjected to paired-end sequencing (2 × 300 bp) on the Illumina MiSeq platform. Raw sequencing data were processed using the Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME2, version 2024.10) pipeline. Sequences were demultiplexed, and low-quality reads were removed during denoising using the DADA2 plugin, which also merged forward and reverse reads to generate a feature table of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and representative sequences. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the q2-phylogeny plugin in QIIME2 by aligning ASVs with multiple alignment using fast Fourier transform (MAFFT). For diversity analyses, sequencing reads were rarefied to a depth of 441,492 reads per sample, retaining all samples for downstream analysis. Alpha diversity metrics, including Shannon index and Chao1 richness, were calculated to evaluate within-sample microbial diversity. Beta diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity as well as unweighted and weighted UniFrac distance matrices, and visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Taxonomic classification of ASVs was performed using a pretrained naïve Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA database (release 138) for the V3–V4 region. Differentially abundant microbial taxa between groups were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) with a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score threshold of 2. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data visualization and statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.2) using the phyloseq, vegan, qiime2R, microeco, ggplot2, ggtree, magrittr, tidyverse, dplyr, and ggpubr packages. Microbial taxa with a relative abundance below 0.005% across all samples were excluded from downstream visualization analyses. Statistical comparisons of α-diversity were performed using Student’s t -test or the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA; 999 permutations) was used to assess differences in β-diversity. 2.6. Metabolome Analysis 
 Targeted fecal metabolomics was performed on the same batch of fecal samples that underwent 16S rRNA gene sequencing to enable integrative microbiome–metabolome analysis. Metabolome profiling was conducted using a multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM)-based platform as previously described 29 . This targeted panel detects >150 metabolites across major host and microbial metabolic pathways, including amino acid metabolism, glutathione and redox pathways, vitamin and cofactor metabolism, central carbon metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, and short-chain fatty acids. Briefly, frozen fecal material (25 mg) was homogenized using a bead beater at 4 °C and processed using the Bligh & Dyer protocol 30 . Following centrifugation, the aqueous phase was collected for metabolite profiling. Extracts were vacuum-dried and reconstituted in injection solvents (acetonitrile:methanol:ammonium acetate 300mM, 3: 6.65: 0.35, v/v/v) prior to mass spectrometry analysis. Avanti Equisplash Lipidomix solution (50 pg/mL) is used as a quality control (QC) sample injected periodically throughout the analytical run to monitor instrument performance and reproducibility. Metabolite data were acquired on an Agilent 6410 Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a micro-autosampler (G1377A) using previously reported methods 31 – 32 . Analyses were conducted in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes. A capillary pump was coupled to the autosampler and operated at a flow rate of 10 μL/min under a pressure of 150 bar. Instrument parameters were set as follows: capillary voltage, 4 kV; drying gas flow, 5.1 L/min; gas temperature, 300 °C. Optimized MRM transitions were used for metabolite detection and quantification. Raw data were processed using in-house scripts to calculate ion counts for each MRM. Data normalization, scaling, and statistical analyses were performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Metabolite ion intensities were normalized to sample weight and then auto-scaled. Principal component analysis was conducted to evaluate global metabolic differences between groups. Univariate statistical testing was applied to identify metabolites that were significantly altered. Volcano plots and hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated for data visualization. Metabolite set enrichment analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst based on the KEGG database. Enrichment was evaluated using over-representation analysis based on the hypergeometric test. 2.7. Detection of Plasma and Colonic PFOA Accumulation by DESI-TOF Mass Spectrometry 
 Plasma and colonic PFOA accumulation was evaluated using an XS desorption electrospray ionization (DESI) source coupled with a time-of-flight (TOF) mass spectrometer (Synapt XS, Waters Corporation). Colon tissue (15 mg) was homogenized, extracted using the Bligh & Dyer protocol 30 , vacuum-dried, and reconstituted in 50 μL methanol. 1.5 μL of plasma samples or colon extracts were deposited onto glass slides and air-dried prior to analysis. DESI-TOF mass spectrometric imaging was performed in negative ion mode, using 98% methanol as spray solvent. The solvent flow rate was 3 μL/min, sprayer voltage 0.35kV, cone voltage 20V, nitrogen gas pressure of 15psi, and scan rate of 0.235 sec. PFOA was detected based on its characteristic ion signal at m/z 412.9724, which was confirmed using a PFOA standard. Mass spectrometry images were acquired with a spatial resolution (pixel dimension) of 300 μm × 300 μm. Ion intensity maps were generated using HDI 1.5 (Waters Corporation) and analyzed to compare relative PFOA abundance among experimental groups. Blank/control samples showed a background ion intensity of approximately 1200 at m/z 412.9724, which was used as the baseline reference for signal interpretation. Quantification of ion intensity was performed using the total signal intensity across the analyzed regions of interest. This DESI-TOF approach mainly provides qualitative evidence of PFOA accumulation in plasma and colon tissue. It does not support the accurate absolute quantification of PFOA concentrations. 2.8. Statistical Analysis 
 Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Before statistical testing, the Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess whether the datasets were normally distributed, and Levene’s test was conducted to assess homogeneity of variances. Comparisons between two experimental groups were performed using either a Student’s t -test or a Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test. Student’s t -test was applied for comparisons between two groups when data were normally distributed, whereas the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test was used for non-normally distributed data. Statistical analyses were conducted in SigmaPlot (Grafiti LLC, Palo Alto, CA, USA). A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. 
 Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to identify associations between colitis-related phenotypic indicators or gut microbiota and fecal metabolites. Pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients and corresponding P values were calculated using the rcorr function from the Hmisc package in R. Correlations with an absolute correlation coefficient ≥ 0.6 and P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Significant correlations were visualized using a dot heatmap, where the color scale indicates the direction and magnitude of the correlation coefficient, and dot size represents the absolute value of the correlation coefficient. 3. Results 
 3.1. PFOA exposure exacerbated DSS-induced colonic inflammation and tissue damage 
 To investigate the impact of PFOA on colonic inflammation, we utilized a DSS-induced colitis model in C57BL/6 mice. Mice were pre-exposed for four weeks to either a standard AIN-93G diet or an AIN-93G diet supplemented with PFOA, followed by administration of 1.5% DSS (w/v) in drinking water for five days to induce acute colitis ( Fig. 1A ). The PFOA concentration of 15 ppm (~2.4 mg/kg/day, assuming a 25 g mouse consuming 4 g of diet per day) was selected based on prior reports showing GI toxicity of PFOA within an exposure range of 1–3 mg/kg/day in mouse models 18 , 21 , 23 . Following DSS challenge, PFOA-exposed colitis mice exhibited significantly greater body-weight loss relative to the colitis-only group ( Fig. 1B ). Moreover, colon shortening, a hallmark indicator of colonic inflammation, was significantly more pronounced in PFOA-exposed colitis mice ( Fig. 1C ). Histopathological evaluation of distal colon sections confirmed that PFOA exposure aggravated DSS-induced epithelial erosion, crypt destruction, and inflammatory cell infiltration ( Fig. 1D ). At the molecular level, PFOA exposure markedly elevated the colonic gene expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including Interleukin-1β (Il-1β) and Tumor necrosis factor-α (Tnf-α) ( Fig. 1E ).

In addition to the colitis model, we also conducted a 4-week dietary exposure study in mice fed either a standard diet or a PFOA (15 ppm)-containing diet to assess whether PFOA exposure influences basal colonic inflammation. However, we did not observe significant differences in colon length, histopathological architecture, and inflammatory cytokine expression under non-colitic conditions, indicating PFOA alone does not induce overt basal colonic inflammation within this exposure window ( Fig. S1 ). Moreover, desorption electrospray ionization (DESI) coupled with time-of-flight (TOF) mass spectrometry confirmed PFOA accumulation in both plasma and colonic tissues of mice exposed to dietary PFOA, indicating systemic absorption and tissue deposition following dietary exposure ( Fig. S2 ). Collectively, these findings indicate that dietary exposure to PFOA intensifies colitis severity by aggravating epithelial injury and amplifying inflammatory responses in the colon. Additionally, PFOA may only act as a disease-modifying environmental factor rather than a primary inflammatory trigger under steady-state conditions. 3.2. PFOA exposure enhanced neutrophil accumulation and activation in the colon 
 Neutrophil accumulation is a hallmark of acute colonic inflammation and a major contributor to epithelial injury and mucosal damage in colitis (19). To evaluate the effects of PFOA on neutrophil-associated markers, we quantified colonic expression of lymphocyte antigen 6 complex-locus G (Ly6G) and chemotactic mediators. Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated dense infiltration of Ly6G + neutrophils throughout the colonic lamina propria and submucosa of PFOA-exposed DSS mice ( Fig. 2A ), whereas negative control staining using rabbit IgG isotype antibody showed minimal background signal, confirming staining specificity ( Fig. S3 ). Consistently, quantitative PCR and Western blot analyses revealed that PFOA exposure elevated neutrophil markers lymphocyte antigen 6 family member G (Ly6G) and myeloperoxidase (MPO) at the mRNA or protein levels compared with DSS-only controls ( Fig. 2B - C ), supporting increased expression of neutrophil-associated markers under PFOA treatment at colitis conditions. We next examined the expression of chemokines that mediate neutrophil recruitment and activation. PFOA exposure significantly upregulated C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (Cxcl1) and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (Cxcl2) , two key CXC chemokines that orchestrate neutrophil chemotaxis toward inflamed mucosal sites ( Fig. 2D ). In addition, gene expressions of S100a8 and S100a9 , calcium-binding proteins abundantly produced by activated neutrophils and commonly used as markers of neutrophil-driven inflammation, were significantly elevated in the colons of PFOA-exposed mice ( Fig. 2E ). Furthermore, the gene expressions of pro-oxidative enzymes inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and NADPH oxidase 2 (Nox2) were upregulated ( Fig. 2F ), further supporting enhanced neutrophil activation and amplification of inflammatory oxidative responses. Besides neutrophils, other major immune cell populations, including T cells ( Cd4 and Cd8 ), B cells ( Cd19 ), and macrophages ( Emr1 ), were not significantly altered at the transcriptional level in response to PFOA exposure ( Fig. S4 ). Collectively, these findings indicate that chronic PFOA exposure intensifies neutrophil-driven inflammatory responses in the colon.

PFOA reduced colonic goblet cells and adherens junction components in colitis mice 
 Gut epithelial barrier integrity is a central determinant of mucosal homeostasis and strongly influences the severity of colitis. To test whether PFOA exposure exacerbates colonic barrier disruption during colitis, we assessed mucus-producing goblet cells and epithelial adherens junction integrity in DSS-challenged mice with or without prior PFOA exposure. Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining revealed a marked loss of PAS + goblet cells in the colonic epithelium of the PFOA exposure group compared with colitis controls ( Fig. 3A ), indicating compromised mucus layer maintenance under inflammatory stress. We next examined epithelial junction integrity by measuring E-cadherin, a key component of adherens junctions required for epithelial cohesion. Immunofluorescence staining showed E-cadherin localization along the epithelial lining in colitis controls. In contrast, PFOA exposure caused a pronounced attenuation of E-cadherin signal ( Fig. 3B ). Consistently, Western blot analysis of colonic lysates demonstrated reduced E-cadherin protein abundance in PFOA-exposed colitic mice ( Fig. 3C ). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PFOA exposure aggravates DSS-induced barrier injury by depleting goblet cell-associated mucus defenses and disrupting epithelial adherens junction integrity, consistent with a heightened vulnerability to mucosal damage during colitis.

PFOA exposure alters gut microbiota composition 
 The gut microbial community is highly sensitive to foodborne toxicants and can undergo compositional shifts that influence colonic inflammation and disease susceptibility 33 . To determine whether PFOA exposure reshapes the gut microbiota prior to DSS treatment, we characterized fecal microbial diversity and taxonomic composition in mice fed a standard diet or a PFOA-containing diet using 16S rRNA sequencing. Although PFOA exposure did not significantly alter α-diversity indices, β-diversity analysis revealed clear separation between the standard diet and PFOA-exposed groups, indicating substantial community-wide remodeling in response to PFOA exposure ( Fig. 4A - B ). Consistent with these shifts, taxonomic profiling demonstrated altered relative abundance patterns across dominant taxa at the phylum and family ( Fig. S5 ) as well as genus levels ( Fig. 4C , Table S2 ). Furthermore, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) revealed microbial lineages preferentially associated with the standard diet group, including Bifidobacterium -related taxa and Dubosiella , which are commonly associated with epithelial barrier support and immune homeostasis ( Fig. 4D - E ). On the contrary, dietary PFOA-exposed taxa were frequently linked to inflammatory states or opportunistic expansion, including Clostridium -related lineages, Romboutsia, and RF39 , which have been associated with intestinal inflammation and dysbiosis ( Fig. 4D - E ). To validate these sequencing-based observations, quantitative PCR analysis confirmed reduced abundance of Bifidobacterium, Akkermansia, and Enterococcus , along with increased abundance of Clostridium in fecal samples from PFOA-exposed mice ( Fig. S6 ). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PFOA exposure induces a shift in the gut microbiota, characterized by the loss of barrier-protective and immunoregulatory taxa and the enrichment of inflammation-associated bacteria.

PFOA exposure alters fecal metabolic profile 
 To further investigate metabolic alterations associated with PFOA-induced gut microbiota remodeling, we performed exploratory targeted metabolomic profiling of fecal samples from mice fed either a standard diet or a PFOA-containing diet prior to DSS treatment. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a separation between the metabolic profiles of the two groups ( Fig. 5A ), indicating that dietary PFOA exposure substantially altered the global fecal metabolite composition. Differential metabolite analysis further demonstrated distinct metabolic shifts associated with PFOA exposure. The volcano plot highlighted several metabolites that were significantly altered in the PFOA group compared with controls ( Fig. 5B ). Notably, multiple amino acid–related metabolites were elevated in fecal samples from PFOA-exposed mice, including arginine, lysine, cystine, serine, ornithine, and aspartic acid. In contrast, several metabolites were decreased under PFOA exposure, including creatinine, taurine, hypoxanthine, and proline ( Table S3 ). Consistent with these findings, hierarchical clustering analysis of differentially abundant metabolites revealed a distinct metabolic signature that separated the PFOA and control groups ( Fig. 5C ). To further explore pathway-level alterations, metabolite set enrichment analysis was performed. The results revealed that several metabolic pathways were significantly enriched in response to PFOA exposure ( Fig. 5D ), with the most prominent pathways related to amino acid metabolism, including arginine biosynthesis, D-amino acid metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, and histidine metabolism. Correlation analysis further demonstrated that several altered metabolites were significantly associated with inflammatory markers, physiological indicators, and representative altered bacterial taxa ( Fig. S7 ). In particular, amino acid–related metabolites, including arginine, aspartic acid, cystine, lysine, and ornithine, positively correlated with pro-inflammatory markers such as Il-1β, Tnf-α, Cxcl2, S100a8, and Nox2 . In contrast, taurine showed negative correlations with inflammatory gene expression. Moreover, several amino acid- and acylcarnitine-related metabolites were positively correlated with inflammation-associated taxa, including Romboutsia and RF39 , but negatively correlated with beneficial bacterial genera such as Bifidobacterium and Dubosiella . Collectively, these findings demonstrate that dietary PFOA exposure markedly reshapes the fecal metabolic landscape, particularly by perturbing amino acid–related metabolic pathways, which may reflect functional alterations in gut microbial metabolism under PFOA exposure.

 Increasing attention has focused on persistent environmental toxicants, particularly PFOA, and their impacts on human health, given their widespread distribution in drinking water and throughout the food chain 6 – 7 . A growing body of epidemiological and experimental evidence supports a link between PFOA exposure and IBD. However, the underlying mechanisms by which PFOA exacerbates colonic inflammation and disrupts mucosal integrity under colitis conditions remain poorly understood. In our study, we demonstrate that dietary PFOA exposure significantly aggravates IBD severity in an experimental colitis model. Notably, PFOA promoted neutrophil infiltration and activation, accompanied by increased expression of neutrophil-associated inflammatory mediators and oxidative enzymes in the colon. Moreover, PFOA exposure disrupted intestinal barrier integrity, characterized by goblet cell depletion and reduced expression of the epithelial adherens junction protein, E-cadherin. In parallel, PFOA induced substantial alterations in gut microbial composition and fecal metabolic profiles, suggesting microbiome–metabolome dysregulation under chronic exposure conditions. Together, these findings demonstrate that PFOA exacerbates intestinal inflammation through coordinated pro-inflammatory activation and epithelial barrier disruption, providing potential mechanistic insight into how persistent foodborne PFAS exposure may contribute to colitis pathogenesis. 
 Excessive neutrophil infiltration represents a central feature of the innate immune response during colitis and contributes substantially to mucosal damage 34 – 35 . Our findings demonstrated that PFOA exposure increased the accumulation and activation of colonic neutrophil under colitis conditions, suggesting a mechanism by which PFOA amplifies mucosal inflammation via neutrophil-driven responses. In particular, PFOA-treated colitis mice exhibited elevated expression of CXC chemokines that orchestrate neutrophil chemotaxis, along with increased levels of calcium-binding proteins, which are abundantly produced by activated neutrophils, indicating enhanced neutrophil recruitment and effector activity. These findings indicated that PFOA could act as an inflammatory “primer” that heightens mucosal chemokine gradients and boosts neutrophil effector responses once colitis is initiated. Consistent with these observations, PFOA has been reported to exert systemic immunotoxic effects 36 . Recent studies further demonstrate that PFOA promotes inflammatory cell infiltration and induces the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, reinforcing its inflammatory potential 18 . In addition to PFOA, exposure to PFOS has also been shown to induce immunotoxicity, particularly affecting B-cell responses 37 . However, whether additional major immune cell populations, especially macrophages or T cells, also contribute to PFOA-induced exacerbation of colitis remains unclear. Although our study provides preliminary insights into other immune cell populations, these analyses are limited to transcriptional profiling and do not fully capture changes at the protein or functional level. Future studies employing flow cytometry or single-cell approaches will be necessary to delineate the involvement of additional immune subsets under inflammatory conditions. In addition, only male mice were used in the present study; therefore, our findings should be interpreted within the context of male-specific responses. Previous research supported the sex-dependent differences in the toxicity of PFOA, particularly in its effects on immune cell function and responses. Indeed, exposure to PFOA has been reported to increase plasma IL-12 levels and reduce double-positive thymocytes in female mice, while increasing circulating T-cell populations in male mice 38 . Thus, future studies incorporating both sexes are needed to determine whether the observed pro-inflammatory effects are conserved in females and to better define sex-specific risk profiles. 
 Barrier dysfunction is another hallmark of colitis and may represent a critical mechanism underlying PFOA-associated gut toxicity 39 . In this study, we found that PFOA exposure disrupted epithelial junctional integrity, particularly by downregulating the major adherens junction protein E-cadherin. This finding is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that PFOA reduces junction-related protein expression and increases epithelial permeability. Indeed, long-term PFOA exposure has been shown to decrease the expression of key junctional proteins, including E-cadherin and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), in murine models 16 – 17 . Similar impairments in epithelial junction integrity have also been reported in other species, including honeybees and broiler chickens, where PFOA exposure reduced intestinal junctional protein expression and compromised barrier function 19 – 20 . In addition to junctional disruption, we observed that PFOA exposure also depleted mucus-producing goblet cells under colitis conditions. Goblet cells are essential for the synthesis and secretion of mucins that form the protective mucus layer overlying the intestinal epithelium; therefore, their loss weakens the first line of defense against luminal pathogens and inflammatory stimuli 40 . Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported reduced goblet cell abundance and compromised mucus function following PFOA exposure in multiple mouse models 16 – 18 . Such barrier impairment facilitates the translocation of bacterial antigens into the lamina propria, reinforcing inflammation and ultimately promoting endotoxemia and systemic immune activation 41 . Together, these findings support a detrimental effect of PFOA on epithelial integrity under inflammatory conditions and suggest that barrier disruption represents a key mechanism by which PFOA exacerbates colitis. 
 Microbiome perturbation is another factor that could influence colitis outcomes. In our study, PFOA exposure induces a shift toward a dysbiotic gut microbiota characterized by loss of barrier-protective and immunoregulatory taxa and enrichment of inflammation-associated bacteria. In particular, reductions in Bifidobacterium and Akkermansia following PFOA exposure could compromise mucus barrier integrity and epithelial protection. These taxa are known to support mucus layer maintenance, promote short-chain fatty acid production, and contribute to anti-inflammatory microbial signaling 42 – 43 ; thus, their depletion may weaken host barrier defenses and immune homeostasis. Moreover, the loss of Dubosiella could further remove a tolerance-promoting protective signal, since Dubosiella has been reported to modulate immune tolerance and protect against DSS-induced colitis in murine models 44 . In contrast, enrichment of Romboutsia and RF39 has been associated with inflammatory conditions and has been reported to correlate with ulcerative colitis disease activity 45 – 46 . Together, such microbial remodeling likely contributes to impaired epithelial integrity and heightened inflammatory responses. Similarly, other reports demonstrated that PFOA exposure perturbs gut microbial community structure and alters microbial metabolic outputs in vivo . Notably, PFOA exposure has been associated with reduced abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus and increased levels of potentially pathogenic taxa, including members of Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio 22 , 47 . Together, these observations support a role for PFOA-induced microbial dysbiosis in promoting colonic inflammation. However, a limitation of the current study is that only a single exposure dose of PFOA was used to establish a proof-of-concept for its impact on colonic inflammation and gut microbiota alterations. Future studies incorporating multiple exposure levels, particularly those reflecting human-relevant doses, are necessary to evaluate dose-response relationships, identify potential threshold effects, and better assess relevance to human exposure scenarios. In addition, whether PFOA influences gut microbiota and metabolome under inflammatory conditions was not directly addressed in the current study. Future studies using chronic spontaneous colitis models, such as IL-10 knockout mice, are needed to determine whether PFOA-induced microbiome and metabolome alterations persist, are amplified, or are reshaped during inflammatory disease progression. Moreover, to establish causality between microbiota alterations and inflammatory outcomes, future mechanistic studies employing fecal microbiota transplantation and germ-free models will be necessary to determine whether PFOA-induced microbial changes directly mediate colitis exacerbation and associated immune dysregulation. 
 In addition to changes in microbial composition, we observed that PFOA exposure also altered fecal metabolic profiles, particularly pathways related to amino acid metabolism, suggesting microbiota-driven metabolic reprogramming that favors intestinal inflammation. Notably, multiple amino acid-related metabolites were elevated in fecal samples from PFOA-exposed mice, especially arginine. Previous clinical studies have shown that increased serum arginine levels are positively associated with histopathological severity in patients with ulcerative colitis 48 . Furthermore, elevated fecal concentrations of amino acids and their derivatives have been proposed as metabolic biomarkers of IBD 49 – 50 . In addition, our metabolomic analysis revealed increased levels of acylcarnitine metabolites, including hydroxyhexanoylcarnitine (CAR 6:0;O) and methylmalonylcarnitine (CAR 5:1;O2), which are indicators of altered fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial metabolic stress and has been showed to contribute to pathogenesis of colitis 51 . Conversely, PFOA exposure resulted in reduced levels of several metabolites with reported tissue-protective or anti-inflammatory functions, particularly creatine, taurine, and hypoxanthine, which play important roles in epithelial energy metabolism, antioxidant defense, and mucosal repair processes 52 – 54 . Their depletion may therefore further impair intestinal barrier recovery and amplify inflammatory signaling during colitis. However, the detailed mechanisms through which PFOA exposure alters microbial metabolic pathways and how these metabolites changes contribute to colonic inflammation remain incompletely understood. Future studies will be necessary to delineate the specific metabolic pathways altered by PFOA exposure and to determine how these metabolite changes mechanistically contribute to intestinal inflammation. In particular, experimental validation will be required to test the functional roles of specific amino acids and related metabolic pathways in modulating colitis and gut microbiota, through targeted supplementation or depletion approaches. Additionally, although results from the baseline non-colitis model suggest that PFOA may prime the host to mount an exaggerated inflammatory response to a secondary insult (DSS), rather than acting as a direct inducer of intestinal injury under basal conditions, this priming effect and its underlying mechanisms require further validation. Future studies are needed to (i) include an appropriate control group maintained on the standard diet alone throughout the entire experimental period, (ii) monitor dietary intake to determine whether changes in food consumption contribute to the observed effects and quantitatively measure PFOA concentrations in plasma and tissues using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry with isotope-labeled PFOA standards, (iii) determine the causal role of the gut microbiota using fecal microbiota transplantation and germ-free models, and (iv) evaluate the impact of post-DSS induction with PFOA exposure on colitis progression, incorporating longitudinal analyses of inflammatory responses and microbiome alterations. 
 In conclusion, our study highlights that PFOA exposure aggravates colonic inflammation and disrupts epithelial barrier integrity in a DSS-induced colitis model. Moreover, PFOA reshapes both the gut microbial ecosystem and its metabolic outputs, promoting a pro-inflammatory intestinal environment. Taken together, these findings emphasize that persistent environmental contaminants can directly intensify intestinal inflammation and underscore the importance of environmental risk management for gastrointestinal health. Furthermore, this study provides mechanistic insight into PFOA in IBD pathogenesis and may inform future strategies aimed at targeting the gut microbiome to mitigate the adverse health effects associated with PFOA exposure.
