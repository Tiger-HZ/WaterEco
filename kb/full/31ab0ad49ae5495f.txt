Effects of maize straw-derived biochar on soil nutrient 
dynamics and nematode community structure in upland 
yellow soil
Yi Liu · Sanwei Yang · Yanzhen Lu · Jin He · Yan Zhang · Kaiyu Li
Received: 2 April 2026 / Accepted: 13 August 2026
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract
Biochar, a carbon-rich soil amendment, has considerable potential to enhance soil fertility, promote carbon 
sequestration, mitigate greenhouse gas emissions, and support sustainable agricultural development. However, 
in upland acidic yellow soil regions, the responses of soil nematode community structure, functional composi -
tion, and ecological indices to biochar application remain poorly understood, particularly at the field scale and 
in terms of dose–response relationships. Based on a three-year field experiment with five biochar application 
rates (0, 5, 10, 20, and 50 t·ha⁻¹), this study examined changes in soil physicochemical properties and enzyme 
activities, characterized shifts in nematode community composition, structure, and ecological indices, and fur -
ther explored the dose-dependent effects of biochar on nematode communities and the underlying mechanisms 
mediated by soil properties. The results showed that biochar application significantly increased soil pH, elec -
trical conductivity (EC), soil organic carbon (SOC), available nitrogen (AN), available phosphorus (AP), and 
urease activity (URE), with mean increases exceeding 10% across all variables. Soil enzyme activities and 
certain nutrient parameters exhibited nonlinear responses, with peak values observed at the 10 t·ha⁻¹ applica -
tion rate. Biochar markedly increased the relative abundance of bacterivorous nematodes (by 19.8%–35.2%) 
while decreasing the abundance of omnivorous–predatory nematodes (by 18.2%–31.9%). Moreover, herbivo -
rous nematodes tended to decrease under the 10 t·ha⁻¹ biochar treatment. Biochar application did not have a 
statistically significant effect on nematode diversity indices; however, both the Wasilewska index and the Basal 
index reached their highest values at the 10 t·ha⁻¹ treatment. Redundancy analysis and path analysis revealed 
that SOC, AN, AP, AK, and urease activity were key factors driving shifts in nematode trophic structure. Bio -
char indirectly regulated the nematode community by modifying these soil properties rather than exerting direct 
effects. In conclusion, biochar applied at 10 t·ha⁻¹ was most effective in enhancing upland yellow soil fertility 
and optimizing nematode community structure and function.

Introduction
Biochar, a carbon-rich solid produced via the pyrolysis of biomass under oxygen-limited conditions1, has attracted 
increasing attention in recent years due to its considerable potential for carbon sequestration, soil quality improve-
ment, and crop yield enhancement 2–4. Owing to these multifunctional benefits, biochar has been recognized by 
international organizations such as the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) as a promising car -
bon management strategy for terrestrial ecosystems5, highlighting its broad applicability as a soil amendment in 
agricultural systems. Biochar can improve soil environments through multiple pathways, thereby contributing 
to the stability and functioning of soil ecosystems 6. Although research on the edaphic effects of biochar has 
gradually expanded to include soil biota, most studies have primarily focused on changes in soil physicochemi -
cal properties and crop productivity. In contrast, a systematic understanding of the structure and function of soil 
biological communities—particularly key bioindicator groups within soil fauna—remains limited. Moreover, 
the underlying mechanisms governing these biological responses are insufficiently explored, revealing a notable 
imbalance in current research efforts7. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the effects of biochar on soil 
ecosystem composition and functioning, especially the response patterns of key biotic components, is essential 
for advancing the theoretical framework and practical application of biochar in agricultural systems8.
Soil nematodes are among the most abundant metazoan groups on Earth and constitute a critical component 
of soil ecosystems, accounting for approximately 80% of all terrestrial animals 9. Based on their feeding habits, 
soil nematodes are commonly classified into bacterivores (BF), fungivores (FF), plant parasites (PP), and omni -
vore–predators (OP)10. These trophic groups occupy multiple levels within the soil food web and play a central 
role in maintaining ecosystem functioning through their involvement in organic matter decomposition, nutrient 
mineralization, and energy flow11. Notably, nematodes are highly sensitive to environmental changes, and differ-
ent trophic groups exhibit distinct response patterns to environmental disturbances. Consequently, the structure of 
soil nematode communities has been widely used as a sensitive biological indicator of soil health12,13.
Initial evidence of biochar effects on soil nematodes was derived from studies of charcoal-enriched soils, 
where increased nematode abundances were observed in forest soils influenced by emissions from charcoal 
kilns14. Since then, research has expanded considerably; however, the reported effects of biochar on soil nema -
tode communities remain highly variable and often inconsistent across studies. For example, in stagnic anthro -
sols, biochar application did not significantly affect total nematode abundance, although application rates of 
12 and 48 t·ha⁻¹ significantly increased FF while reducing PP abundance 15. Similarly, in acidic soils (pH < 6), 
a single application of 160 t·ha⁻¹ biochar did not alter total nematode abundance but led to decreased PP and 
increased OP abundance16. In contrast, in a typical calcareic luvisol, pine gasification biochar applied at 5 and 30 
t·ha⁻¹ did not significantly influence soil properties, total nematode abundance, richness, or biomass; however, it 
altered community composition and trophic structure, with the lower rate favoring OP and PP and the higher rate 
promoting BF and FF17. Conversely, some studies have reported minimal or negligible effects. For instance, in a 
haplic podzol, biochar application at varying rates did not significantly affect trophic composition, group propor-
tions, or diversity indices of nematode communities 17. In contrast, negative effects have also been documented: 
in an arenosol, a single application of 126 t·ha⁻¹ biochar significantly reduced nematode abundance seven years 
after application18, while in red soils, continuous application at 60 t·ha⁻¹ significantly decreased the abundance 
of multiple trophic groups192021.
Collectively, these findings indicate that the effects of biochar on soil nematode communities are strongly 
dependent on application rate and soil type. Yellow soil, a representative zonal soil widely distributed across 
southern China, is characterized by inherently poor properties resulting from pedogenic processes, including 
heavy texture, strong acidity, and low water- and nutrient-holding capacity, which severely constrain regional 
agricultural sustainability22,23. Although the ameliorative effects of biochar on the physicochemical properties 
of yellow soil have been preliminarily demonstrated, its influence on key biotic components underpinning soil 
ecosystem functioning, particularly soil nematode communities, remains insufficiently understood. Therefore,

nematode community structure. The findings provide a reference for the rational application of biochar in upland 
yellow soil regions, with the aim of simultaneously enhancing soil productivity and ecological functioning, while 
advancing our understanding of biochar–soil biota interactions.
Materials and methods
Experimental materials
The experiment was conducted from April 2020 to April 2023 at the experimental base of Guizhou University, 
located in Huaxi District, Guiyang City, Guizhou Province, China (106°39′32″ E, 26°26′20″ N). The study area is 
characterized by a subtropical plateau monsoon climate, with a mean annual temperature of 14.9 °C, mean annual 
precipitation of approximately 1200 mm, and an elevation of 1149.3 m above sea level. The experimental soil 
was classified as acidic yellow soil, and its basic physicochemical properties prior to the experiment are presented 
in Table 1. The biochar used in this study was produced from maize straw via pyrolysis at 500 °C for 6 h under 
oxygen-limited conditions, followed by natural cooling. The resulting biochar had a pH of 7.98, a particle size of 
0.3–0.35 mm, and an ash content of 29.53%. Soil samples were collected with full permission from the relevant 
land manager and experimental station in Guizhou Province, China. All sampling procedures complied with local 
environmental regulations and institutional guidelines. No protected or endangered species were involved in this 
study.
Experimental design
Biochar treatments were established in April 2020, following the winter rapeseed harvest and prior to spring 
maize sowing. A randomized complete block design was employed, comprising five biochar application rates 
(Table 2), each with four replicates. Each plot measured 15 m² (5 m × 3 m). To minimize edge effects and pre -
vent interference among adjacent plots, a 0.8 m buffer zone was maintained between plots. The cropping system 
followed an annual maize–rapeseed rotation. After biochar application, the soil was plowed to incorporate the 
biochar thoroughly into the 0–20 cm topsoil layer. Fertilization was applied according to local conventional prac-
tices, with a compound fertilizer (N: P₂O₅:K₂O = 15:15:15) used as a basal application at 600 kg·ha⁻¹, followed

Soil sampling was conducted in April 2023, three years after a single biochar application. Sampling was per -

soil auger to avoid soil compaction and nematode mortality. Samples were transported to the laboratory, where 
visible stones and plant residues were removed. Each composite sample was divided into two subsamples: one 
was air-dried, homogenized, ground, and passed through a 20-mesh sieve for physicochemical analysis; the 
other was stored at − 80 °C as fresh soil for subsequent high-throughput sequencing analysis of the nematode 
community.
Soil nutrient and enzyme activity analyses
All soil properties were determined using standard methods as described below. Soil pH was measured potentio-
metrically using a pH meter (PHS-3 C, Shanghai, China) at a soil-to-water ratio of 2.5:1. Electrical conductivity 
(EC) was determined using a conductivity meter (DDS-307, Shanghai, China) at a soil-to-water ratio of 5:1. 
Soil organic carbon (SOC) was determined using the potassium dichromate–sulfuric acid oxidation method with 
external heating. Available nitrogen (AN) was measured using the alkaline hydrolysis diffusion method. Avail -
able phosphorus (AP) was extracted with 0.5 mol·L⁻¹ NaHCO₃ (pH 8.5) following the Olsen method and deter -
mined by the molybdenum–antimony–ascorbic acid colorimetric method using a spectrophotometer (UV-9000, 
Shanghai, China). Available potassium (AK) was extracted with 1 mol·L⁻¹ neutral ammonium acetate (NH₄OAc, 
pH 7.0) and measured using a flame photometer (AP1200, Shanghai, China).
Soil enzyme activities were determined as follows. Catalase (CAT) activity was measured by the potassium 
permanganate titration method and calculated based on residual H₂O₂. Invertase (INV) activity was determined 
using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid colorimetric method, with reducing sugars quantified using a spectrophotom -
eter (UV-9000, Shanghai, China). Urease (URE) activity was determined using the sodium phenolate–sodium 
hypochlorite colorimetric method, with NH₄⁺-N measured using a spectrophotometer (UV-9000, Shanghai, 
China). Phosphatase (PHO) activity was determined using the disodium phenyl phosphate colorimetric method, 
with phenol quantified using a spectrophotometer (UV-9000, Shanghai, China).

Data analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel 2016. One-way analysis of variance (ANOV A) 
followed by Tukey’s HSD test (α = 0.05) was used to evaluate differences in soil properties and nematode com -
munity indices among biochar application rates. Redundancy analysis (RDA) was conducted using CANOCO 
5.0 on z-score standardized data to examine the relationships between nematode trophic structure and soil prop -
erties. The relationships between soil properties and nematode communities were further assessed using Mantel 
tests implemented in the “vegan” package in R (version 3.6.1)25. All data are presented as mean ± standard error 
(SE). Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate relationships among (i) soil properties, (ii) nema -
tode trophic groups, and (iii) soil properties and nematode trophic groups. Variables showing significant pairwise 
linear relationships among biochar application rate, soil properties, and nematode community attributes were 
selected for path analysis. Path analysis was conducted based on linear regression using z-score standardized 
data to quantify the hypothesized relationships among variables, and model validity was evaluated using Pearson 
correlation coefficients. Detailed formulas for calculating soil nematode ecological indices are provided in the 
Supplementary information (Text S1).
Results
Effects of biochar on soil properties
As shown in Fig. 1, compared with the control (CK), biochar application significantly increased soil pH, EC, 
AN, and INV activity (P < 0.05), with increases of 4.2%–13.9%, 4.7%–23.1%, 8.8%–22.3%, and 16.5%–36.3%, 
respectively. Soil pH and EC exhibited significant linear increases with increasing biochar application rates 
(P < 0.05). SOC content increased with biochar application (by 2.5%–30.4%) but did not show a significant dose-
dependent response. AP content was significantly higher in the B10, B20, and B50 treatments, with increases 
of 10.5%, 20.9%, and 17.0%, respectively, compared with CK ( P < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was 
observed between B5 and CK. In addition, AN, AK, and the activities of CAT, INV , URE, and PHO exhibited 
a unimodal pattern, increasing initially and then decreasing with increasing biochar application rate, with peak 
values observed at 10 t·ha⁻¹. Overall, the 10 t·ha⁻¹ treatment showed the strongest improvement in soil nutrient 
availability and enzyme activities under the present experimental conditions.

Effects of biochar on soil nematode ecological indices
No significant differences were observed in the diversity index (Shannon–Wiener index, H′) or functional indices 
of the soil nematode community among biochar application rates (Table 3). However, numerical trends indi -
cated that both the Wasilewska index (WI) and Basal index (BI) exhibited a unimodal pattern across treatments, 
increasing initially and then decreasing, with peak values observed in the B10 treatment. The plant-parasitic 
index (PPI) and the PPI-to-maturity index ratio (PPI/MI) reached their lowest values in the B10 treatment. The 
Channel index (CI) remained below 50 across all treatments. Scatterplot analysis of the Structure index (SI) ver-
sus the Enrichment index (EI) (Fig. 3) showed that all data points were distributed in the second quadrant.
Relationships between soil properties and soil nematode community
To explore the relationships between soil environmental factors and nematode community structure, RDA was 
performed based on nematode trophic group composition. As shown in Fig. 4a, the first two RDA axes explained 
56.6% of the total variation, with axis 1 and axis 2 accounting for 35.5% and 21.1%, respectively. Mantel tests 
further identified SOC, AN, AP, AK, and URE as the main environmental factors significantly associated with 
nematode trophic group composition (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4b).
Path analysis was used to assess the relationships among biochar application rate, soil properties, and nema -
tode trophic groups (Fig. 5, Table S2). Biochar application rate showed no significant direct relationship with 
nematode trophic groups, but was significantly positively correlated with soil pH, SOC, and AP (R² = 0.7641, 
P < 0.001; R² = 0.7496, P < 0.001; R² = 0.3801, P < 0.01), and significantly negatively correlated with AK (R² 
= 0.3348, P < 0.01). These soil properties were further significantly associated with nematode trophic groups. 
Specifically, AK was negatively correlated with PP (R² = 0.3348, P < 0.01); SOC, AP, and pH were negatively 
correlated with OP (R² = 0.2248, P < 0.05; R² = 0.2889, P < 0.05; R² = 0.2464, P < 0.05); and AN was positively 
correlated with BF (R² = 0.2275, P < 0.05). In addition, a significant negative correlation was observed between 
OP and BF (R² = 0.5623, P < 0.001).

Effects of biochar on soil nematode community
The responses of different nematode trophic groups to biochar application varied markedly. Compared with CK, 
all biochar treatments increased the relative abundance of BF while decreasing that of OP (Fig. 2a), consistent 
with previous findings 40. This pattern can be explained by two main mechanisms. First, the porous structure 
of biochar provides favorable microhabitats for bacteria, reducing predation pressure and promoting bacterial 
proliferation. Second, biochar releases nutrients such as AN and AP (Fig. 1), which further stimulate bacterial 
growth. The resulting increase in bacterial biomass enhances food availability for BF, thereby increasing their 
relative abundance 41,42. In contrast, OP, which are mainly characterized by c–p values of 4–5 and represent 
K-strategists, are more sensitive to environmental changes. The shifts in soil pH and nutrient availability induced 
by biochar application are therefore likely responsible for the observed decline in OP43.
Although no significant differences in soil nematode ecological indices were observed among treatments, 
numerical trends still indicated the potential responses of the soil ecosystem to biochar application. MI showed 
a numerical decrease in biochar treatments relative to CK, which may suggest a slight increase in environmental 
disturbance intensity following biochar addition44. WI reached its highest numerical value in the B10 treatment, 
which was consistent with the increased proportion of microbivorous nematodes and implied a potential improve-
ment in soil biological functioning. Although differences in WI were not statistically significant, its trend was 
consistent with that of microbivorous nematodes, likely due to soil heterogeneity and experimental variability. 
This numerical pattern may be associated with the proliferation of r-strategists (c–p1 and c–p2), which could con-
tribute to accelerated nutrient turnover under moderate disturbance 45. BI showed an increasing numerical trend 
across treatments, which may imply enhanced resistance of the soil food web to environmental fluctuations 43. 
In addition, CI remained below 50 across all treatments, indicating that the bacterial decomposition pathway 
remained dominant, consistent with the increase in BF. The distribution of samples in the second quadrant of the 
SI–EI plot further indicates that the soil food web maintained a relatively structured and enriched state under bio-
char application43. Univariate nematode ecological indices including H′, MI, WI and BI have inherent limitations; 
these single indicators are commonly insensitive to subtle environmental modifications and prone to unstable 
outcomes under field conditions, which reasonably explains the non-significant inter-treatment differences of all 
indices observed in our study (Table 3). In contrast, multivariate RDA used in this research integrates the overall 
variation of nematode assemblage and multiple soil variables simultaneously and is superior for revealing the 
complex interactions between soil environment and nematode community structure45.
The B10 treatment exhibited more favorable numerical trends across multiple indicators, including soil nutri-
ents, enzyme activities (Fig. 1), nematode trophic composition, and ecological indices. Specifically, B10 showed 
a numerical reduction in PP, increased proportions of c–p1 and c–p2 groups, and higher numerical values of WI 
and BI. These trends suggest the existence of a potentially suitable application threshold. At this rate, biochar 
may simultaneously improve soil microhabitats, enhance nutrient availability, and avoid the potential inhibitory 
effects associated with excessive application 46–48. Overall, the 10 t·ha⁻¹ rate tended to balance soil improve -
ment and nematode community regulation, promoting beneficial trophic groups without disrupting soil food web 
stability.
Relationships between soil properties and soil nematode community
RDA and Mantel tests identified SOC, AN, AP, AK, and URE as the key environmental factors driving shifts in 
nematode community structure, collectively explaining 54.6% of the total variation. This result suggests that the 
combined modification of soil physicochemical properties and biological activity is a primary pathway through 
which biochar influences the nematode community. The regulatory effect of biochar appears to operate through 
soil property–biota interactions. Specifically, AK, which was significantly increased by biochar application, was 
negatively correlated with PP, suggesting a potential indirect suppressive effect mediated by enhanced plant 
resistance. Potassium is known to strengthen plant cell walls, enhance resistance to pathogens and parasitic

Scientific Reports
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-67286-3
nematodes, and stimulate the production of defensive compounds, thereby reducing PP infestation 46. Biochar 
application rate was significantly positively correlated with soil pH, SOC, and AP, reflecting its multifaceted 
effects on soil properties. These include increasing pH through alkaline ash inputs, enhancing SOC via stable 
carbon fractions, and improving AP availability by reducing phosphorus adsorption 47,48. Notably, increases in 
these soil properties were associated with a reduction in OP. This pattern is consistent with previous findings and 
may reflect shifts in resource availability and trophic interactions within the soil food web 49. Specifically, bio-
char may enhance microbial activity and root growth, thereby increasing resource availability for lower trophic 
groups such as BF and PP, which could place OP at a competitive disadvantage50,51. In addition, the negative cor-
relation between BF and OP suggests that increased bacterial resource utilization by BF may further restrict OP 
niche space. This negative relationship also reflects a top-down regulatory function, in which OP exert grazing 
pressure on BF, thereby controlling BF abundance. Such predation can alleviate potential resource competition 
between OP and BF and stabilize the soil micro-food web. Hence, the observed BF–OP negative correlation can 
be explained by the combined effects of resource competition of BF and top-down predation regulation of OP52. 
The increase in soil pH may also favor bacterial dominance, reinforcing the competitive advantage of BF and 
contributing to the decline in OP53.
The B10 treatment exhibited a relatively coordinated trend across soil properties and nematode community 
attributes. At this rate, AN and AK reached relatively high numerical levels, BF showed a numerical increase, and 
PP showed a numerical decrease, while moderate changes in pH may have mitigated excessive suppression of 
OP. This pattern highlights a dose-dependent interaction between soil properties and nematode trophic structure. 
Overall, the 10 t·ha⁻¹ application rate tended to balance soil improvement and food web regulation, contributing 
to favorable soil functioning without disrupting trophic structure stability.
Conclusions
Based on a three-year field experiment in upland yellow soil, this study elucidated the dose-dependent effects of 
maize straw-derived biochar on the soil nematode community and the underlying mechanisms. The main conclu-
sions are as follows: (1) Biochar application significantly increased soil pH, EC, SOC, AN, and AP, with increases 
of 4.2%–13.9%, 4.7%–23.1%, 2.5%–30.4%, 8.8%–22.3%, and 10.5%–20.9%, respectively. Soil enzyme activi-
ties exhibited non-linear responses, peaking at 10 t·ha⁻¹, indicating that this application rate produced the stron -
gest improvement in soil nutrient availability and biological activity. (2) Biochar application numerically altered 
nematode community structure, with increased BF (19.8%–35.2%) and decreased OP (18.2%–31.9%) across 
treatments. PP reached its lowest numerically level at 10 t·ha⁻¹, which also showed the highest proportion of 
r-strategists. WI and BI reached maximum numerical values at this rate, whereas no significant effect on the 
diversity index was observed. (3) RDA identified SOC, AN, AP, AK, and URE as the key factors influencing 
nematode community composition. Path analysis indicated that biochar regulated nematode trophic structure 
indirectly through modifications in soil properties rather than via direct effects. Overall, maize straw-derived 
biochar applied at 10 t·ha⁻¹ displayed relatively comprehensive advantages, improving soil fertility, reducing PP, 
enhancing BF, and promoting soil ecosystem functioning. These findings provide a reference for optimizing bio-
char application strategies in upland yellow soil. Future research should further evaluate extended-period effects 
and interactions with agronomic practices such as fertilization and mulching.
