Introduction 
One alternative to a suggestion for consistent fertiliser application 
over a rice production system inside a nation or area is site-specific 
nutrient management (SSNM). In general consumption of the 
fertiliser is more in cereals as compare to other crops. The most of 
south Asian soils particularly Indian soils are deficient in available 
nitrogen (N) (1). However, N present in soil cannot fulfil demand of 
rice crop during the critical growth stages (2). It is very important to 
match the optimal dose of nitrogenous fertiliser for improving the 
crop growth and development (3). Nitrogenous fertiliser affects the 
quick response of the crop in terms of the growth of plants, 
particularly urea. Excessive use of nitrogenous fertilisers adversely 
degrades the environment, particularly air and water (4). Nitrate 
(NO3-) content has increased in surface water bodies as well as 
ground water which are harmful for health purpose (5). 
 According to World Health Organization (WHO), NO3- content 
exceeding more than 10 mg is not suitable for drinking purposes (6). 
Thus, inputs of N fertiliser in agricultural land can be a key reason in 
eutrophication (‘‘improving nutrition’’). Due to eutrophication, too 
much growth of algae and macrophytes cause oxygen depletion, 
this unavailability of oxygen is toxic to aquatic organisms (7). 
 Improper application of fertilisers creates global warming 
problems which elevated the serious concern about the existing 
nutrient management practices. Denitrification is the process of 
microbial conversion of the NO3- into nitrous oxide (N2O) produces 
and accounts for approximately 5 % of the total greenhouse gas 
(CHG) (8). Nitrous oxide is also responsible for the depletion of the 
ozone layer. The N2O is also necessary for the atmosphere because it 
has maximum lifespan than other gases and the concentration is 
increased by 0.22 % per year (9). It is essential to manage the 
application of N fertiliser in farmers field. 
 Proper nutrient management through SSNM is vital to 
improve the productivity of the crops. Nitrogenous fertilisers are 
costly inputs and it is estimated that in agriculture more than $45 
billion per year is invested on nitrogenous fertilisers (6). Commonly, 
more than 50 % of the applied N is not utilised by the plants (10, 11).

Abstract 
Nitrogen (N) is an essential mineral nutrient for plant growth and the demand for nitrogenous fertilisers has increased significantly to achieve 
higher grain production per unit area. However, excessive application of N can lead to serious environmental problems, particularly water 
pollution (through nitrate leaching and runoff) and air pollution (through emissions of ammonia and nitrous oxide). To reduce the negative 
environmental impact of N while ensuring sustainable crop production, efficient N management is essential. The present study comprises N 
management site-specific nutrient management (SSNM) tools such as leaf colour chart (LCC), soil plant analyser development (SPAD) and rice 
crop manager (RCM) and certain strategies that have been introduced in agriculture to optimise fertiliser application rates, minimise N losses 
and supply adequate nutrients to crops according to their demand. Proper N management not only improves nutrient use efficiency but also 
enhances productivity and environmental sustainability. The SSNM is recognised as proven technology for the better management of N. We 
collected research papers published in reputed international journals on SSNM and precise N management during 2000–2025. The relevant 
data from the research papers was systematically tabulated and univariate analysis was performed to draw the interferences. The yield 
increment was 8.14 % in SSNM experiments over blanket application of N fertiliser. Nitrogen dose was drastically reduced by the application 
of N as per SSNM principle; it reduces around 12 kg N ha-1, which is 9.38 % lower N than the non-SSNM treatment. The partial factor efficiency, 
which is around 22.31 % higher in SSNM applied field than control treatment. While the agronomic efficiency was varied from 12.84–17.58 in 
farmers fertilisers practice (FFP) and SSNM, respectively, this is 36.9 % higher in SSNM than control treatment. Therefore, SSNM intervention 
needs to be popularised among the farmer’s for environmental sustainability and food security.

It is proven in many studies that N is a limiting nutrient, around 1 kg 
of N produces around 60 kg of rice (12). The demand of N was less 
before green revolution but after green revolution, the quick 
response of N over the crops makes it the most demanded fertiliser 
when compared to other fertilisers (13). Nitrogenous fertilisers can 
boost food production of wheat about 260 % (average growth of 
about 6.4 % per year). Presently for food production, half of the 
world’s population directly depends upon N containing fertilisers (6). 
Indian soils are deficient in organic N content, hence to fulfil the 
requirement of N to crops the farmer’s are depends on the chemical 
nitrogenous fertiliser. It is very challenging to sustainably manage 
the N management for better crop production. SSNM is a technique 
to supply the essential nutrients optimally as and when crop is 
needed. It was assessed that the yield of rice in Asia increase about 
14 % during 2000–2010 and by 25 % from 2000–2020. However, it 
was supposed that the average yields of irrigated rice must rise from 
5.3 t ha-1 in 1998 to 6.5–7.0 t ha-1 in 2020 (14). In this review our aim is 
to evaluate the impact of SSNM tools like Leaf Colour Chart (LCC) 
and Rice Crop Manager (RCM) on N footprint on environment and 
nutrient use efficiency indices like agronomic use efficiency and 
partial factor productivity.

Site-specific nutrient management tools for nutrient 
management in rice crop 
Leaf colour chart (LCC) 
LCC is an easy, low-cost way to use colour to determine a crop's N 
needs. There were six green LCC strips displayed, ranging in tint from 
light green to dark green as shown in Fig. 1. The presence of N 
content in the leaf can be determined by evaluating its greenness 
using these colour strips. The LCC reading of the standing rice crop 
can be started at peak N requirement stages that is starting of tillers 
to panicle formation stage, LCC is used to take readings in rice 
production fields. Research on nitrogenous fertiliser based on the 
LCC showed that it saved 51, 20 and 29 kg ha-1 of nitrogenous 
fertiliser compared to blanket N fertiliser, while producing same or 
better grain production (15). Fertiliser N management using 
inexpensive SSNM technologies like rice crop manager and LCC can 
reduce the extreme application of N by 10-50 % (14). This is because 
it is inexpensive, simple to use and provides a visual comparison of 
the greenness of crop leaves. The LCC is easily within reach of most 
farmer’s. 
 Under conditions of varying temperatures, planting times 
and other agronomic practices, N dosages can be modified in 
accordance with the readings of the leaf chlorophyll content chart, 
which measures the amount of green content in the leaf (16). 
Different leaf temperatures (15–35 °C) can cause SPAD values to 
fluctuate, even when the physiological chlorophyll level is the same. 
This indicates that temperature affects the accuracy of 
measurements. Ideally in the morning or late afternoon when the 
direct heat stress is at its lowest, take SPAD/LCC readings in a stable 
temperature environment and stay away from readings during the 
midday heat (17). SPAD readings are typically collected every 7–10 
days from the early vegetative phase through the time of panicle 
initiation, which is close to the reproductive phase. From the start of 
tillering (or early leaf growth) until after reproductive stage (e.g., 
panicle emergence in rice), LCC measurements should ideally be 
obtained every week. Meanwhile, there was a strong correlation 
between the N content in leaf tissue and chlorophyll index values.

Use of the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI) leaf colour chart 
(LCC) : In the first step, 14 days after transplanting (DAT) select 
randomly 10 plants across the field where they are uniformly 
distributed, healthy, fully expanded then topmost leaf of plant is 
compare from each of the 10 plants with the LCC. The leaf is placed 
on the middle part of strips for comparison and do not remove the 
leaf from the plants. Readings are taken in the morning time because 
in direct sunlight readings cannot be easily noted down. If the 
reading of 6 or 10 leaves of the plant is below the critical value 4, N 
fertiliser are needed to be applied to the crop. According to LCC 
critical value, applied N fertiliser dose is different for basmati and non
-basmati rice. 
Wet season (Kharif) 28 kg ha-1 urea is applied to non- basmati rice at 
LCC value < 4. 
Wet season (Kharif) 23 kg ha-1 urea is applied to basmati rice at LCC 
value < 3. 
Use of the Punjab Agriculture University (PAU) leaf colour chart 
(LCC): Select only representative plants and match colour of the 
intact leaves with the LCC under the shade. The plants selected for 
matching colour was normal and free from disease/insect incidence, 
water logging/stress other nutrient deficiencies. In transplanted rice, 
25 kg urea is required to be applied acre-1. Compare the colour of leaf 
to the LCC at 7 days interval, after 14 days of transplanting. 
Whenever, the greenness of 6 or more out of 10 leaves is lighter than 
LCC shade 4, apply 25 kg urea per acre. If leaf colour shade is darker 
than LCC value 4 then urea should not be applied in transplanted 
rice. After the initiation of flowers, discontinue the application of 
urea. The LCC reading is taken from the normal, disease/insect 
incidence free and nutrient deficient should not be visible on plants 
leave. 
 
Rice crop manager (RCM) 
Presently in this digital revolution, everyone is using smartphones to 
get rapid results for any query. The nutrient management of rice 
crop is not only depended upon the soil type but also depends upon 
the crop cultivation practices, duration of cultivars and other 
management factors, which varies among fields within a rice-
growing area. Rice crop manager is a web -based site-specific 
nutrient management tool developed by International Rice 
Research Institute (IRRI), Philippines as shown in Fig. 2. Study 
compared the nutrient management among RCM with the blanket 
application of nutrients state fertiliser recommendation (SFR) and 
farmer’s fertiliser practices (FFP) in eastern India due to its diverse 
soils, micronutrient deficiencies, management limitations and lower 
yields provide circumstances in which SSNM tool RCM offer 
significant benefits over farmer’s' fertiliser methods. The tool 
provides fertiliser recommendations determined by the local soil 
and crop conditions, climate and nutrient state (21). The grain yield 
was recorded maximum with RCM as compared to FFP (22). 
 Web tools based on SSNM are easily available and validated 
by the Indian Council for Agricultural Research (ICAR) institutes and 
universities, available for extension workers and farmer’s (22). Rice 
crop manager uses information collected from the farmer on the 
name of variety and age of transplanted seedlings and then assessed 
information on growth and duration of the selected variety, which 
was stored in a database, to calculate a recommendation with days 
after transplanting for each N fertiliser application. A set of 12–15 
questions asked by the web-based tool RCM, are relative to the 
history of the field, cultivation practice and weed management 
recommendations which provides the fertiliser recommendation for 
the crop field. The studies reveal that the RCM have an advantage to 
save the nitrogenous fertiliser over the blanket application by the 
farmer’s. The improved performance of RCM can be attributed to 
enhanced N uptake resulting from increased availability of both 
macro and micronutrients (22-24). Two year average significant 
increase in grain yield of puddled transplanted rice by RCM (57.6 % 
and 39.7 %) compared to FFP and SFR, respectively. In Northern 
Philippines, RCM yielded significantly higher than FFP in 2011 and 
2012 and was marginally significantly higher (P < 0.10) in 2013 as 
shown in Table 1.

Impact of site -specific nutrient management tools on 
greenhouse gas production, yields and efficiency indices 
Greenhouse gas emission 
The high requirement of water during cultivation of rice leads to a 
rise in temperature caused by the emission of GHGs such as carbon-
dioxide (CO2), N2O and methane (CH4 ) (25). Greenhouse gases from 
the rice fields can be mitigated through proper management of 
fertilisers, tillage practices and water. According to the past studies 
the total emission of GHG, CO2 is found to be a lower contributor in 
omission plot as fertiliser was not added. Therefore, N is one of the 
most important factors of GHGs emission, the growing of crop 
without N leads to substantial yield penalty. Hence, application of N is 
imperative for getting better yield of rice. Nitrogen management 
through SSNM tools had lower CO2 emission by 11.3 % than omission 
plot. Among the N management practices, the RCM had the lowest 
GHG emission. The CO₂ emission per kg of grain produced was 
highest in omission plots and lowest under N management using 
Punjab Agriculture University (PAU) LCC and IRRI LCC (23). 
 Management of fertilisers, weed, water and crop through 
SSNM tools have potential to obtain higher yields with increased N 
fertiliser use efficiency while maintaining low N2O emissions (26). 
Compared with control treatment, the two-year average ammonia 
(NH3), N2O and nitric oxide (NO) emissions under LCC + urea (SSNM1) 
were reduced by 45.2 %, 19.2 % and 22.0 %, respectively. 
Furthermore, over two years, the average NH3, N2O and NO emissions 
under LCC + controlled release of urea (SSNM2) were 70.4 %, 34.4 % 
and 36.7 % lower, respectively, than those under control treatment 
(27). The reason is that N supply and crop demand are now better 
aligned. By preventing the overuse and inappropriate timing 
of fertiliser application, SSNM lowers the buildup of mineral N 
(ammonium ion (NH₄+) and nitrate ion (NO₃-)) in the soil floodwater 
system. Efficient application of N fertilisers decrease N2O emission 
and global warming resulting in higher yields. The SSNM approach to 
fertiliser application doesn’t enhance the N2O emission per unit of 
grain yield (28). As same as nutrient expert, SSNM tool manage the 
fertiliser application in rice and wheat crops. More than 80 % of 
farmer’s have increased their yield and farm income by using the 
nutrient index tool. Nutrient index-based recommendation reduces 
the global warming potential (GWP) by about 2.5 % in rice crop and 12
–20 % in wheat as compared to FFP (29). 
Yield of rice 
Grain yield increased with N application are varied depending on the 
treatment compared to the control. The yield target was determined 
by taking the omission plot technique from grain yield in N fertilised 
plot with limited nutrients in another plot. Nitrogen management 
through SSNM tools i.e. PAU LCC, IRRI LCC and RCM directly 
influences the yield of rice. Yield recorded with IRRI LCC and PAU LCC 
are statistically similar and highest grain yield as compared to FFP 
and RCM (23). Study on farmer’s field, has been seen, SSNM practices 
produced 5 % higher yield than FFP. The reason behind it was 
associated with the reduction in insect and disease infestation and 
right application of optimal fertiliser dose at the right time improved 
lodging resistance (30). Improved N supply synchronisation with 
crop demand and the real time N application based on LCC 
enhances the N supply at the precise moment that the crop needs it 
the most, promoting N partitioning into yield components rather 
than excessive vegetative development that encourages pest 
outbreaks. Balanced N availability during critical development 
stages enhances photosynthesis, leaf area index, nutrient absorption 
and grain filling, which increases yield components while avoiding 
excessive vegetative biomass that might cause lodging (27). Site-
specific nutrient management has potential to fulfill the yields gap 
and nutrient efficiency in irrigated rice. According to research 
findings the average yield under SSNM was recorded as 7 % 
increased yield as compared to FFP and profitability by 12 % (31). 
Number of trials conducted under site-specific approach for nutrient 
management in irrigated transplanted rice crops in Northwest India 
shows that field-specific management of macronutrients increases 
yields of rice crop by 12 % and profitability by 14 % as shown in Fig. 3 
(32). Several studies reveals that higher yield was influenced by 
different SSNM tool as compared to FFP.

Nutrient use efficiency 
In this review study, different nutrient use efficiency like agronomic 
use efficiency, recovery efficiency and partial factor productivity of 
different research are used as comparison factor in variable 
treatment. Nutrient use efficiency is a term used for a ratio of an 
output (biological yield or economic yield) to the input (nutrient 
supply). The total aboveground plant biomass (plant dry matter) 
and total plant N differ from grain yield and grain N, which are 
components of the economic yield (6). The main objective of 
nutrient use efficiency (NUE) is to increase the crop production while 
minimising the nutrient loss from the field. Nutrient use efficiency 
can be increased by better agricultural management practices. 
Agronomic efficiency : Agronomic efficiency (AE) is calculated in units 
of yield increase per unit of nutrient applied. It directly reflects the 
production of a crop when fertiliser is applied and relates directly to 
economic return. On the basis of LCC the critical value based N 
management on farm experiment in Punjab revealed that the N 
saving was 9.4–54.2 kg N ha-1, with an average of about 25 % without 
any decrease in yield as compared to FFP of applied blanket N at 
fixed time intervals. By using the LCC, application of N fertiliser using 
LCC increased agronomic N use efficiency from 48 kg grain kg-1 N to 
65 kg grain kg-1 N (2). Real time N management through SSNM tools 
increases the AE in rice as it improves the growth parameters such as 
number of tillers, yield attributes and yield (33). Different research 
paper entries shows that, after the analysis 23 % improvement in AE 
exhibits better utilisation of N by the crop as shown in Fig. 4 (30). The 
performance of SSNM across 107 sites in China, evaluated over 10 
seasons, showed that the average N applied through SSNM (133 kg 
N ha-1) was lower than that under FFP (195 kg N ha-1). Experiments 
showed that an increase in yield of 5 % with SSNM, profit was 
observed with AE which is 61 % greater as compared to FFP (34). 
Nutrient management through SSNM was evaluated across 21 farm 
sites over four successive rice crops to assess its performance under 
field conditions. The study demonstrated that SSNM significantly 
improved fertiliser efficiency, with agronomic nitrogen use efficiency 
(AEₙ) being 80 % higher than under FFP. This increase was reflected 
in greater grain yield per kg of fertiliser applied, indicating superior 
synchronisation of N supply with crop demand under SSNM (35). 
Site-specific nutrient management tool LCC value is used in 
experiments of two fertilisation methods, SSNM and FFP were 
conducted considerably improved average tillers, leaf area index, dry 
matter and N uptake. Recovery efficiency and agronomic efficiency 
increased 38.5–133.4 % respectively, over the FFP. Site-specific 
nutrient management has likely increased yield and nutrient 
efficiency in irrigated and transplanted rice (36).

Partial factor productivity: Partial factor productivity (PFPN) is the 
easiest and simplest production efficiency in determining the units of 
crop yield per unit of applied nutrient and to maintain the data of 
inputs and yield. The total productivity of applied N is measured by 
the PFPN. The impact of N fertiliser, the contribution of indigenous soil 
N, the impact of management techniques and the influence of 
weather and crop type are all reflected in it. It is simple to compute

and an unfertilised control plot. Recovery efficiency (RE)

nutrient applied in crop field. With increase in PFPN (21 % in the 
Philippines and 26 % in Vietnam), SSNM reduces the N fertiliser 
application and increases yield. An increase in PFPN could reasonably 
be associated with increased plant use of N fertiliser and reduced loss

consistent with the measurements for N fertiliser use efficiency as 
PFPN (28). Same as with PFPN, the SSNM tool practices achieve 15.54 
% higher production as compared to FFP as shown in Fig. 5.

Conclusion 
The research papers highlighted the importance of site-specific 
nutrient management (SSNM) in sustainability of the rice production 
in South Asia. It is important to ensure the effective management of 
nitrogen (N). A reduction in the dose of N is possible through proper 
tools without yield penalty. The greenhouse gases (GHGs) footprint 
may also reduce from the rice field through the SSNM tools. 
Compared to the farmer fertiliser practice (FFP), SSNM in rice 
farming systems boosts rice production, profitability and N 
utilisation efficiency while lowering N losses. The farmer’s practice 
resulted in 23 % lower agronomic nitrogen use efficiency (AEN) and 
15.54 % lower partial factor productivity (PFPN) compared to SSNM 
despite utilising 14.3 % less N fertiliser. Balanced fertilisation 
combined with improved distribution and increased N fertiliser splits 
throughout the growing season are the main reasons for SSNM's 
superior performance over the farmer practice. Even though are 
several benefits for SSNM tools, the popularity of SSNM tools among 
the farming community is very less.
