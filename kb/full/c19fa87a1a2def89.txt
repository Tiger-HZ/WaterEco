1. Introduction 
Safe and reliable drinking water is fundamental to human health, 
social development and economic well -being. Water is required 
for drinking, food preparation, hygiene and sanitation, yet the 
presence of physical, chemical and microbiological hazards can 
turn an essential resource into an important route of human 
exposure to disease. The World Health Organization (WHO) 
emphasizes t hat drinking-water safety should be managed from 
the source through treatment and distribution to the point of 
consumption, with health protection based on risk assessment, 
preventive management and surveillance (World Health 
Organization [WHO], 2022). Mor e recently, WHO has further 
emphasized the importance of water -safety planning, sanitary 
inspection, microbial monitoring and independent surveillance, 
particularly for small and decentralized water supplies (WHO, 
2024, 2026). Globally, progress in access to drinking water has 
improved, but important inequalities remain, particularly in low - 
and middle -income settings where population growth, 
urbanization, inadequate infrastructure and environmental 
contamination continue to affect the reliability and safety of water 
supplies (WHO & UNICEF, 2023; WHO, 2022). 
 
Nigeria faces many of these challenges. Rapid population growth 
and urban expansion have increased demand for water while the 
availability and reliability of centralized water supply remain 
inadequate i n many communities. Consequently, households 
frequently depend on groundwater sources, including boreholes 
and hand -dug wells, as well as commercially packaged water. 
National evidence shows substantial variation in drinking -water 
quality across different sources and geographical settings, with 
contamination associated with sewage, waste disposal, 
hydrogeological processes, agricultural activities and other 
anthropogenic pressures (Ighalo & Adeniyi, 2020). A recent 
systematic review of groundwater -quality r esearch in Nigeria 
similarly identified continuing challenges in monitoring, data 
availability and the protection of groundwater resources as 
industrialization and population growth place increasing pressure 
on water systems (Omeka et al., 2024). At the ho usehold level,

Access to safe drinking water is essential for public health, particularly in rapidly growing urban 
communities where environmental and microbiological contamination may increase the risk of water -
related diseases. This study assessed drinki ng-water quality, climatic variability and selected water -
related disease patterns in Akure Metropolis, Ondo State, Nigeria, using secondary data from available 
water-quality, climate and disease -surveillance sources. Physicochemical and microbiological 
characteristics of drinking-water samples were assessed, while rainfall, temperature and reported cases 
of diarrhoea and typhoid were examined descriptively. The water -quality findings showed that pH 
ranged from 4.50 to 7.93, turbidity from 0.11 to 6.60 NTU and nitrate from 2.10 to 61.00 mg/L. 
Escherichia coli ranged from 0 to 57 CFU/100 mL, while total coliforms ranged from 0 to 99 CFU/100 
mL. Eleven of 14 pH observations (78.6%) fell outside the operational screening range of 6.5 –8.5, 
while four of 11 E. coli observations (36.4%) exceeded the non-detectable drinking-water criterion; one 
nitrate observation and one turbidity observation also exceeded their respective reference criteria. 
Climatic data showed considerable variation in rainfall and temperature d uring the study period. 
Disease-surveillance records showed reported cases of diarrhoea and typhoid in Akure North and Akure 
South during June and July 2023. Overall, the findings indicate variability in drinking -water quality 
and the presence of microbiol ogical contamination in some water sources, highlighting the need for 
regular water -quality monitoring, improved protection of water sources, appropriate treatment of 
contaminated water and strengthened environmental -health surveillance in Akure Metropolis .

The quality of groundwater is inf luenced by both natural 
processes and human activities. Geological characteristics, 
aquifer conditions, land use, waste disposal, sanitation practices 
and proximity to potential contamination sources can influence 
the physicochemical and microbiological pr operties of 
groundwater. Studies from different parts of Nigeria have 
documented variation in pH, electrical conductivity, total 
dissolved solids, turbidity, nitrate and other chemical 
characteristics of groundwater and domestic water sources 
(Egbueri et al., 2020; Gbadebo, 2021; Adesakin et al., 2020). In 
some locations, groundwater has been found to be acidic or to 
contain elevated concentrations of particular chemical 
constituents, while microbiological contamination has also been 
documented in groundwater sources (Afonne et al., 2020; Ganiyu 
et al ., 2021). Such findings are important because water that 
appears clear and acceptable based on physical characteristics 
may still contain microorganisms capable of causing 
gastrointestinal and other infections. 
 
Microbiological quality is particularly important in assessing 
drinking-water safety. Escherichia coli and other faecal indicator 
organisms are widely used to indicate contamination associated 
with faecal material and the possible presence of enteric 
pathogens. Research in Nigeria has repeatedly demonstrated the 
occurrence of coliform organisms and other bacteria in household 
and packaged drinking -water sources (Adesakin et al ., 2020; 
Udoh et al ., 2021). A systematic review and meta -analysis of 
packaged dr inking water in Nigeria found considerable 
contamination with total coliforms and faecal indicator bacteria, 
including E. coli, and identified treatment failure and inadequate 
regulatory oversight as important concerns (Udoh et al., 2021). 
Other Nigerian s tudies have also identified E. coli , Klebsiella, 
Enterobacter and other organisms in drinking -water sources, 
demonstrating that microbiological contamination is not limited 
to one type of water supply (Ganiyu et al ., 2021; Rabiu et al., 
2022). In southwest ern Nigeria, contamination has also been 
documented in water systems serving urban populations, 
emphasizing the continuing importance of microbiological 
surveillance (Oyewale et al., 2024). 
 
Packaged water is an important part of the Nigerian drinking -
water landscape. Sachet water is widely consumed because it is 
relatively affordable, portable and readily available, particularly 
in urban environments. However, its widespread availability does 
not necessarily guarantee consistent microbiological or chemical 
quality. A systematic review of sachet -water studies across 
Nigeria identified substantial variation in physicochemical and 
microbiological quality and reported the occurrence of organisms 
including E. coli, Salmonella, Enterobacter, Staphylococcus and 
Enterococcus species (Agbasi et al ., 2024). Udoh et al . (2021) 
similarly found evidence of microbial contamination in packaged 
drinking water and highlighted the need for stronger monitoring 
and regulatory oversight. Individual investigations have also 
reported microbiological or chemical concerns in sachet water in 
different Nigerian communities (Kusa & Joshua, 2023; Alabi et 
al., 2024). These findings do not mean that all packaged water is 
unsafe; rather, they demonstrate the importance of evaluating 
water quality using measurable microbiological and 
physicochemical indicators rather than relying solely on the 
appearance, packaging or presumed source of the water. 
 
Evidence from southwestern Nigeria provides further justification 
for examining drinking-water quality at the local level. Akure and 
its surrounding communities depend on a mixture of groundwater 
and commercially packaged water, and previous investigations 
have documented variability in both the physicochemical and 
microbiological characteristics o f local water resources. An 
earlier investigation of drinking -water sources in Akure reported 
microbiological contamination among several water sources 
(Uzuegbu et al., 1996), while more recent groundwater research 
in the metropolis has assessed multiple p hysicochemical 
characteristics and demonstrated spatial variation in groundwater 
quality (Afolabi et al ., 2022). Another investigation of 
groundwater sources in Akure identified enteric bacteria in both 
well and borehole water and reported potential microb ial health 
risks associated with consumption (Olalemi et al., 2021). These 
studies provide important local evidence, but their individual 
sampling designs, parameters and periods of observation differ. 
Consequently, bringing together available water -quality 
observations can provide a broader description of the 
environmental conditions affecting drinking -water safety in the 
metropolis. 
 
Water quality is also affected by environmental and climatic 
conditions. Rainfall can influence the movement of contaminants 
from surrounding land into water bodies and shallow 
groundwater, while heavy rainfall and flooding can overwhelm 
sanitation systems and increase opportunities for faecal 
contamination. Conversely, prolonged dry periods may influence 
pathogen concentrations and water availability. The relationship 
between rainfall and diarrhoeal disease is therefore not necessarily 
linear and may depend on the amount and timing of rainfall, 
antecedent environmental conditions, water infrastructure and 
sanitation practices. A systematic review and meta -analysis by 
Kraay et al. (2020) found evidence supporting a concentration –
dilution relationship in which rainfall following dry periods may 
flush pathogens into water systems, whereas rainfall following 
wetter periods may dilu te pathogen concentrations. Seasonal 
changes in water contamination have also been documented in 
Nigerian urban settings. In Port Harcourt, for example, 
thermotolerant-coliform contamination varied between dry and 
rainy seasons across different drinking-water sources (Kumpel et 
al., 2017). 
 
Climate variability is particularly relevant to public health in 
rapidly expanding urban environments. Changes in rainfall 
patterns, flooding, drought and temperature can affect water 
availability, water treatment systems, sanitation infrastructure and 
the movement of pathogens through the environment (WHO, 
2022). The relationship between environmental conditions and 
infectious disease is consequently influenced by several 
interacting factors, including water source, sani tation, hygiene, 
human behaviour and the resilience of local infrastructure. This 
makes local environmental monitoring important because broad 
regional climate patterns may not adequately describe the 
conditions experienced by individual communities. 
 
Water-related diseases remain an important public-health concern 
in Nigeria. Diarrhoeal disease can result from exposure to 
contaminated water, food and environments, and inadequate 
sanitation and hygiene can facilitate transmission. In a

community-based study in southeastern Nigeria, Nwokoro et al. 
(2020) identified sanitation and hygiene practices among factors 
associated with diarrhoeal morbidity. At the national level, 
Adamu et al . (2022) demonstrated an association between 
drinking-water source and diarrhoea among Nigerian households. 
Evidence from intervention studies also shows that improvements 
in drinking -water quality, sanitation and hygiene can reduce 
diarrhoeal disease risk (Wolf et al ., 2022). These findings 
reinforce the importance of considering d rinking-water quality 
within a broader environmental -health framework rather than 
treating water quality as an isolated laboratory issue. 
 
Other enteric infections, including typhoid fever, are also closely 
linked to environmental conditions and the qualit y of water and 
sanitation systems. The transmission of Salmonella enterica 
serovar Typhi is associated with faecal contamination and 
inadequate sanitation, making safe water, sanitation and hygiene 
important components of prevention. More broadly, studies of 
enteric microorganisms in Nigerian water sources continue to 
demonstrate the potential for contaminated water to contribute to 
gastrointestinal infection risks (Olalemi et al., 2021; Rabiu et al., 
2022). Viral pathogens also warrant consideration. For e xample, 
Ekundayo et al. (2021) reviewed human norovirus contamination 
of water sources and highlighted the importance of contaminated 
water in the environmental transmission of enteric viruses. More 
recent work on the epidemiology of norovirus in Nigeria f urther 
demonstrates the continuing relevance of enteric infections to 
public health surveillance (Chigor et al., 2024). 
 
The public-health significance of drinking-water quality therefore 
extends beyond whether individual physicochemical parameters 
fall within acceptable ranges. Microbial contamination, chemical 
contamination and environmental conditions can interact to 
influence exposure and health risk. For example, water may have 
acceptable pH, conductivity or total dissolved solids while still 
containing faecal indicator organisms. Conversely, elevated 
turbidity may indicate inadequate treatment or conditions that 
interfere with effective disinfection. Studies from Nigeria have 
documented this distinction between physicochemical 
acceptability and microbi ological safety (Adesakin et al., 2020; 
Udoh et al ., 2021). In addition, emerging contaminants have 
received increasing attention. Recent research in southwestern 
Nigeria has identified mycotoxins in different drinking -water 
sources, including groundwater and sachet water, illustrating the 
expanding range of contaminants that may require consideration 
in future water -safety programmes (Koko et al ., 2024). Other 
emerging contaminants, such as per - and polyfluoroalkyl 
substances, have also become an increasin g concern in African 
drinking-water research (Adewuyi & Li, 2024). 
 
Akure provides an important setting in which to examine these 
issues because of its growing urban population, dependence on 
multiple drinking -water sources and changing environmental 
conditions. Existing studies have generated valuable information 
on groundwater chemistry, microbiological contamination and 
packaged-water quality, but the evidence is distributed across 
different studies, sampling periods and water -source categories. 
This make s it difficult to obtain a broader public -health 
perspective from any single study. In addition, environmental and 
disease-surveillance information are often collected through 
separate systems, limiting opportunities to examine drinking -
water quality along side climatic and disease patterns. A more 
integrated assessment can therefore help identify recurring water-
quality concerns, describe environmental variability and place 
available disease -surveillance observations within their local 
context. 
 
The present study was designed to address this need by integrating 
available secondary data on drinking -water quality, climate and 
selected water-related disease surveillance in Akure Metropolis, 
Ondo State, Nigeria. The study evaluates physicochemical and 
microbiological characteristics of available drinking -water 
observations, compares selected parameters across water sources 
where the underlying data permit, assesses observations against 
relevant drinking -water criteria, describes rainfall and 
temperature patterns, and examines available diarrhoea and 
typhoid surveillance records alongside environmental conditions. 
By bringing these strands of evidence together, the study seeks to 
provide a clearer public -health perspective on drinking -water 
safety and environmental conditions in Akure Metropolis and to 
generate evidence that can support water -quality monitoring, 
environmental-health planning and future research in the area. 
 
2. Materials and Methods 
2.1 Study design 
A retrospective descriptive study design was used to integrate 
secondary data on drinking-water quality, climatic conditions and 
selected disease -surveillance indicators in Akure Metropolis, 
Ondo State, Nigeria. The study combined physicochemical and 
microbiological water -quality observations with meteoro logical 
and epidemiological data to provide an integrated assessment of 
environmental conditions relevant to public health. 
 
2.2 Study Area 
The study was conducted in Akure Metropolis, the capital of 
Ondo State in southwestern Nigeria. The metropolitan ar ea 
comprises Akure South and Akure North Local Government 
Areas. Akure has a tropical climate characterised by distinct wet 
and dry seasons, with rainfall occurring predominantly between 
April and October. The area is underlain mainly by Precambrian 
Basement Complex rocks, including migmatite, granite and 
charnockite, which influence groundwater occurrence and 
chemistry. 
 
2.3 Data Sources 
Three categories of secondary data were used: 
Water-quality data: Physicochemical and microbiological 
observations were compiled from available published drinking -
water and groundwater investigations in Akure Metropolis 
(Table1). The coded 2017 matched dataset was traced to 
Daramola and Akindureni (2017), which reported monthly results 
for April, June and August 2017 from BORBDA bo rehole water, 
TISCO borehole water and ODSWC (Ukere) tap water. For 
clarity, BORBDA denotes the Benin -Owena River Basin 
Development Authority; TISCO denotes TISCO Pharmaceutical 
Company; and ODSWC_Ukere denotes Ondo State Water 
Corporation (Ukere tap -water supply). The variables extracted 
across the compiled water -quality dataset included pH, 
temperature, turbidity, electrical conductivity (EC), total 
dissolved solids (TDS), nitrate, nitrite, chloride, calcium, 
magnesium, hardness, iron, sulphate, fluoride, chromium, 
potassium, Escherichia coli, total coliforms and faecal 
streptococci. 
 
Climate data: Monthly meteorological observations for Akure 
were incorporated into the study database. The variables included 
rainfall, mean temperature, minimum temperature, maximum 
temperature, rainy days, relative humidity and wind speed where 
available. The climate dataset covered January 2021 to June 2023.

2.4 Data Extraction and Management 
Relevant observations were extracted into a structured maste r 
database. For each water -quality observation, the water source, 
location, parameter, numerical value, measurement unit, sampling 
period and original source were recorded where available. The 
datasets were combined by aligning parameter names, units, 
water-source categories and observation periods while retaining 
the original source and sampling -period information. 
Measurements were harmonised into consistent units before 
analysis. Because the underlying studies did not report every 
parameter for every obs ervation, the analytical sample size (N) 
differs by parameter; N represents the number of non -missing 
observations available for that parameter after harmonisation. 
Missing observations were retained as missing values and were 
not replaced with zero or estimated values. 
 
2.5 Water-Quality Assessment 
Water-quality observations were assessed using physicochemical 
and microbiological indicators. Descriptive statistics were 
calculated for the available observations, including mean, 
standard deviation, median, minimum and maximum values. The 
results were compared with applicable drinking -water guideline 
criteria to identify observations exceeding reference values. 
 
2.6 Climate and Disease Data Analysis 
Monthly rainfall and temperature data were summarised to 
describe climatic patterns during the study period. Disease -
surveillance data were summarised according to disease, month 
and Local Government Area. Climate and disease observations 
were examined descriptively to characterise their temporal 
occurrence within the study period. 
 
2.7 Statistical Analysis 
Statistical analysis was performed using descriptive and non -
parametric methods appropriate to the structure of the datasets. 
For the matched 2017 water -quality subset, the three sampling 
months (April, June and Au gust) were treated as repeated blocks 
and the three water -source groups (BORBDA, TISCO and 
ODSWC_Ukere) as the related groups for each parameter. The 
Friedman test was applied to each parameter with complete 
matched observations, and Kendall's coefficient of concordance 
(W) was used to describe the strength of agreement in the 
rankings. Statistical significance was set at p < .05. The inferential 
analysis was restricted to this matched three -period dataset and 
was not applied to the heterogeneous compiled observations.

4. Discussion 
The present study provides an integrated assessment of drinking -
water quality, climatic conditions and selected disease -
surveillance observations in Akure Metropolis. The major finding 
was the presence of substantial variability in both 
physicochemical and microbiological water -quality 
characteristics, with microbiological contamination representing 
an important public-health concern. In particular, Escherichia coli 
was detected in some of the assessed water observations, while 
total coliforms and faecal streptococci also occurred in several 
samples. This finding is consistent with evidence from other parts 
of Nigeria showing that contamination of drinking -water sources 
remains an important environmental -health concern. Udoh et al. 
(2021), in a systematic review and meta -analysis of packaged 
drinking water in Nigeria, reported contamination with total 
coliforms and faecal indicator bacteria, including E. coli , and 
concluded t hat stronger monitoring of packaged drinking -water 
quality is required. Similarly, Agbasi et al. (2024) reviewed more 
than 50 studies of sachet water across Nigeria and identified 
substantial regional variation in physicochemical and 
microbiological qualit y, including the occurrence of E. coli , 
Salmonella, Enterobacter and other organisms. These findings 
support the observation in the present study that the 
microbiological quality of drinking water cannot be assumed from 
the type or appearance of the water source alone. 
 
The detection of E. coli in 4 of 11 observations (36.4%) is 
particularly important because E. coli is widely used as an 
indicator of faecal contamination and inadequate microbiological 
safety of drinking water. The finding is broadly compar able with 
previous Nigerian evidence demonstrating the occurrence of 
faecal indicator organisms in packaged, borehole and well water. 
Udoh et al . (2021) reported an overall E. coli contamination 
prevalence of 13.30% across the packaged-water studies included 
in their review, although the studies varied considerably by 
location and methodology. A broader systematic review of

beverages consumed in Nigeria also identified microbiological 
hazards in borehole/well water and sachet water, demonstrating 
that contamination occurs across multiple commonly consumed 
water sources (Oduori et al., 2022). More recently, Agbasi et al. 
(2024) similarly reported faecal contamination across different 
geographical zones of Nigeria. The comparatively high proportion 
observed in the present dataset therefore reinforces the importance 
of routine microbiological monitoring of drinking -water sources 
in Akure. 
 
The occurrence of total coliforms and faecal streptococci further 
supports evidence of microbiological variability among the water 
sources. In the present study, BORBDA observations recorded 
comparatively higher mean concentrations of E. coli , total 
coliforms and faecal streptococci than several of the other source 
categories. Such differences may reflect variations in source 
protection, sanitation conditions, treatment processes, storage and 
handling. Previous Nigerian investigations have similarly 
demonstrated differences in microbial quality between 
groundwater and packaged -water sources. Oduori et al . (2022) 
found evidence of important bacterial hazards in borehole and 
well water as well as packaged water, while Udoh et al. (2021) 
identified treatment failure and post -treatment contamination as 
important contributors to microbial contamination of sachet 
water. These observations suggest that monitoring should extend 
beyond the point of production or extraction to include storage, 
distribution and household handling where relevant. 
 
The physicochemical results also showed considerable variation. 
Electrical conductivity, total dissolved solids, nitrate and turbidity 
varied across the assessed water sources, indicating differences in 
mineral content and environmental conditions. The relatively 
higher electrical conductivity and TDS observed in some 
groundwater sources are consis tent with the understanding that 
groundwater chemistry is influenced by interactions between 
water and the geological materials through which groundwater 
moves. At the same time, elevated nitrate concentrations may 
reflect anthropogenic influences such as sanitation systems, waste 
disposal and agricultural activities, particularly where 
groundwater sources are vulnerable to surface -derived 
contamination. Nigerian groundwater studies have similarly 
reported substantial spatial variation in physicochemical 
characteristics and have highlighted the influence of geology and 
human activities on groundwater quality (Egbueri et al ., 2020; 
Ganiyu et al., 2021). The present findings therefore reinforce the 
importance of assessing both physicochemical and 
microbiological characteristics when evaluating drinking -water 
safety. 
 
The guideline assessment provides an important public -health 
interpretation of these findings. Although most of the 
observations for several physicochemical parameters were within 
the applicable reference values, four of the 11 E. coli observations 
exceeded the requirement for non -detectable E. coli in drinking 
water. One nitrate observation and one turbidity observation also 
exceeded their respective reference criteria. This pattern 
illustrates an important distinction between general 
physicochemical acceptability and microbiological safety. Water 
may have relatively acceptable conductivity, TDS or temperature 
while still presenting a microbiological concern. This observation 
agrees with previous re views of Nigerian drinking water, which 
have reported situations in which physicochemical characteristics 
may appear acceptable while microbial contamination remains 
present (Udoh et al ., 2021; Agbasi et al ., 2024). The finding 
therefore supports the continued use of microbiological indicators 
alongside physicochemical measurements in routine drinking -
water surveillance. 
 
The pH findings require a more cautious interpretation. Although 
11 of the 14 observations fell outside the 6.5 –8.5 operational 
range used in the present analysis, this should not be interpreted 
as evidence that these waters necessarily posed a direct health 
hazard because pH is primarily an operational water -quality 
parameter rather than a health -based toxicological limit at these 
concentrations. The variation in pH may nevertheless be relevant 
to water treatment, corrosion and the behaviour of other chemical 
constituents. Nigerian groundwater investigations have reported 
variation in pH associated with geological conditions and local 
environmental influences, supporting the interpretation that 
differences in pH should be considered within the 
hydrogeological context of each water source (Egbueri et al ., 
2020; Ganiyu et al., 2021). 
 
The inferential analysis of the matched water -quality datas et 
showed statistically significant differences in electrical 
conductivity, TDS, chloride, potassium and faecal streptococci. 
The pattern suggests that some water -quality characteristics 
varied systematically across the matched sampling observations. 
The s trong concordance observed for these parameters is also 
consistent with the expected relationship between conductivity 
and dissolved ionic constituents, since electrical conductivity 
reflects the concentration and mobility of dissolved ions in water. 
Similar relationships between physicochemical parameters have 
been reported in groundwater -quality investigations in Nigeria, 
where conductivity, TDS and major ions have been used to 
characterize hydrochemical variation (Egbueri et al., 2020). The 
microbiological finding involving faecal streptococci is 
particularly relevant because it complements the E. coli and total-
coliform findings and provides additional evidence of differences 
in microbial water quality. 
 
The climatic findings showed considerable variatio n in rainfall 
and temperature during the study period, with higher annual 
rainfall recorded in 2021 than in 2022 and higher mean 
temperature during the available 2023 observations. Rainfall is 
relevant to water quality because precipitation can influence 
runoff, groundwater recharge and the movement of contaminants 
from contaminated surfaces into water sources. However, the 
relationship between rainfall and waterborne disease is complex 
and may vary according to local sanitation, drainage, water 
infrastructure and seasonal conditions. Kraay et al. (2020), in a 
systematic review and meta -analysis, reported evidence that 
rainfall can influence diarrhoeal disease through mechanisms 
involving pathogen mobilisation and changes in environmental 
exposure. The clima tic variability observed in Akure therefore 
provides an important environmental context for interpreting 
water-quality and disease-surveillance patterns. 
 
The disease-surveillance findings showed reported diarrhoea and 
typhoid cases in Akure North and Akur e South during June and 
July 2023. Akure South recorded 59 diarrhoea cases and 202 
typhoid cases in June, while Akure North recorded six typhoid 
cases during the same month. In July, 26 diarrhoea and 152 
typhoid cases were reported in Akure North, while Ak ure South 
recorded 37 diarrhoea cases. These observations are consistent 
with the broader public-health literature linking inadequate water, 
sanitation and hygiene conditions with enteric disease 
transmission. Adamu et al. (2022), using nationally representative 
Nigerian data, found an association between drinking -water 
source and diarrhoeal illness, while Wolf et al . (2022)

demonstrated through a systematic review that improvements in 
drinking-water, sanitation and hygiene can reduce diarrhoeal 
disease. Th e surveillance observations in the present study 
therefore reinforce the relevance of maintaining safe drinking -
water systems as part of broader enteric-disease prevention. 
The observed disease pattern should, however, be interpreted 
within the wider envir onmental context rather than attributing 
reported cases directly to a particular water source. Diarrhoea and 
typhoid transmission may be influenced by multiple factors, 
including food hygiene, sanitation, household water storage, 
personal hygiene, healthca re-seeking behaviour and 
environmental exposure. Consequently, the coexistence of 
reported disease cases and water -quality concerns provides an 
important public -health signal but does not by itself establish a 
causal relationship. This broader interpretati on is consistent with 
evidence from Nigeria showing that water, sanitation and hygiene 
conditions interact in determining enteric -disease risk (Adamu et 
al., 2022; Oduori et al., 2022). 
 
Taken together, the findings indicate that drinking -water quality 
in Akure Metropolis is variable, with microbiological 
contamination representing the most important immediate public-
health concern identified in the available data. The findings are 
broadly consistent with the wider Nigerian literature, which 
continues to re port microbial contamination across groundwater 
and packaged -water sources despite improvements in water 
access (Udoh et al., 2021; Agbasi et al., 2024). The combination 
of physicochemical variability, microbiological contamination, 
climatic variability an d reported enteric diseases highlights the 
importance of integrated environmental -health surveillance. 
Strengthening routine testing of drinking -water sources, 
improving protection of groundwater sources, maintaining 
effective treatment and distribution pr actices, and linking 
environmental monitoring with disease surveillance could 
provide a stronger basis for preventing water -related health risks 
in Akure Metropolis. 
 
5. Conclusion 
This study demonstrates that drinking -water quality in Akure 
Metropolis var ies across water sources, with microbiological 
contamination representing an important public -health concern. 
The detection of Escherichia coli, total coliforms and faecal 
streptococci in some water-quality observations indicates the need 
for continued att ention to the safety of drinking -water sources. 
Variations were also observed in physicochemical parameters, 
particularly electrical conductivity, total dissolved solids, nitrate 
and turbidity, while the climatic data demonstrated variation in 
rainfall and temperature during the study period. The disease -
surveillance findings further showed the occurrence of reported 
diarrhoea and typhoid cases within Akure North and Akure South. 
Overall, the findings highlight the importance of regular 
monitoring of drinki ng-water quality, protection of groundwater 
sources, effective water treatment and strengthened 
environmental-health surveillance in Akure Metropolis. An 
integrated approach linking water -quality monitoring, 
environmental information and disease surveillan ce is 
recommended to support evidence -based public -health planning 
and reduce potential water-related health risks in the metropolis. 
 
6. Recommendations 
Based on the findings of this study, the following 
recommendations are made: 
1. Routine water -quality monitoring: Regular physicochemical 
and microbiological testing of drinking -water sources in Akure 
Metropolis should be strengthened, with particular attention to E. 
coli, total coliforms, turbidity, nitrate and other key indicators of 
water safety. 
2. Pro tection of water sources: Boreholes, hand -dug wells and 
other groundwater sources should be adequately protected from 
sewage, refuse dumps, drainage channels and other potential 
sources of contamination. 
3. Effective treatment of contaminated water: Water sources 
found to contain microbiological contamination should undergo 
appropriate treatment and disinfection before consumption, with 
treated water monitored regularly to ensure continued safety. 
4. Strengthening packaged -water quality control: Producers a nd 
regulatory authorities should maintain regular quality assessment 
of packaged drinking water to ensure that water meets applicable 
drinking-water standards throughout production, storage and 
distribution. 
5. Integrated environmental -health surveillance: Water-quality, 
climatic and disease-surveillance information should be routinely 
coordinated among relevant environmental -health and public -
health authorities to support early identification of potential 
water-related health risks. 
6. Public health education: Communities should be encouraged to 
adopt safe water -storage, handling and household treatment 
practices and to avoid activities that can contaminate wells and 
other drinking-water sources. 
7. Further research and monitoring: Periodic studies covering a 
wider range of locations, water sources and environmental-health 
indicators should be conducted to strengthen the evidence base for 
drinking-water management and public-health planning in Akure 
Metropolis. 
 
7. Limitation of the Study 
This study has some limitations that should be considered when 
interpreting the findings. The analysis was based on secondary 
data obtained from existing water -quality, climatic and disease -
surveillance sources; consequently, the study was dependent on 
the variables, sampling periods and measurements available in the 
original datasets. Differences in sampling locations, water sources 
and periods of data collection may also introduce variation in the 
underlying observations. In addition, the study did not directly 
control the original field-sampling and laboratory procedures used 
to generate the water -quality measurements. The disease -
surveillance component was based on reported cases and therefore 
reflects available surveillance records rather than independently 
confirmed case s. Finally, the observational nature of the 
integrated dataset means that the findings describe patterns and 
associations but should not be interpreted as establishing direct 
causal relationships between water quality, climatic conditions 
and reported dise ase occurrence. These limitations do not 
diminish the value of the findings but indicate the need for 
continued prospective monitoring and standardized data 
collection in future studies.
