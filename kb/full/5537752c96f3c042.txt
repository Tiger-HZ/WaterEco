Abstract
Promoting market-based trading in irrigation water rights and improving allocation mechanisms are critical for
strengthening water governance and advancing sustainable agricultural development. This study develops a four-
party evolutionary game model involving local government (LCG), irrigation district management unit (IMU), social
capital (SCP), and irrigation water users (IWU) to examine the evolutionary dynamics and key determinants of the
market-based allocation of irrigation water rights. The results show that: (1) The system admits 11 pure-strategy
equilibria that are locally asymptotically stable under speci ﬁc parameter conditions, and 1 ; 1; 1; 1ðÞ is the primary
locally stable outcome under the baseline scenario. (2) System evolution is jointly shaped by the four actors ’ initial
strategy probabilities, which mainly affect convergence speed and evolutionary trajectories rather than the long-run
direction or ﬁnal equilibrium. (3) Single-factor sensitivity analysis shows that higher service fee, expected return,
reward-penalty intensity, repurchase price multiplier, subsidy rates for IMU and SCP, and reputational bene ﬁts,
together with lower water resources taxes and a moderate reduction in reserved water volume, promote the market-
based development of irrigation water-rights trading. (4) Two-factor sensitivity analysis identi ﬁes synergies between
subsidy rates and reputational bene ﬁts, water resources taxes and reward-penalty intensity, expected return and
management-technical factors, service fee and irrigation water-use bene ﬁts, and repurchase price multiplier and
irrigation water-use bene ﬁts, with coordinated adjustments improving stability and accelerating convergence. This
study provides theoretical and policy insights for improving market-based allocation of water resources and
encouraging multi-actor participation in irrigation water-rights trading.
Introduction
Under the combined pressures of climate change and sustained economic growth, the global imbalance between water
supply and demand is widening, and water scarcity is increasingly constraining sustainable socioeconomic devel-
opment. Agriculture has long been the largest user of global freshwater, accounting for more than 70% of total
freshwater withdrawals. However, irrigation practices remain relatively extensive, and management and metering
systems remain underdeveloped, resulting in low output per unit of water, weak water-saving incentives, and
persistent risks to food security and ecosystem stability (Qin et al. 2024). SDG 6 of the United Nations calls for
improved water-use ef ﬁciency through integrated water resources management and for more equitable and

low agricultural water-use ef ﬁciency, impose binding constraints on allocative ef ﬁciency and the realisation of water-
saving potential. Accordingly, governance and allocation mechanisms that promote ef ﬁciency while safeguarding
equity, together with stronger coordination between market instruments and institutional constraints, can help ease
supply–demand tensions and reduce allocative distortions in water-scarce contexts. These mechanisms also provide
an institutional basis for improving agricultural water-use ef ﬁciency and maintaining ecosystem stability.
Water-rights trading is a key institutional arrangement for introducing market mechanisms into water allocation.
By enabling the transferability of water rights and transmitting price signals, it facilitates the orderly reallocation of
water resources among actors, thereby improving allocative ef ﬁciency and supporting the transition towards market-
based water governance. In China, water-rights trading has evolved from local experimentation into a more insti-
tutionalised and platform-based system. At the beginning of the twenty- ﬁrst century, the interregional transfer
between Dongyang and Yiwu in Zhejiang Province provided an early practical case of water-rights trading. Sub-
sequent transactions in other regions increasingly adopted a hybrid model that combined government guidance with
market participation, while trading arrangements gradually expanded to include interregional, cross-sectoral and user-
to-user exchanges (Lv et al. 2021). As foundational measures such as water-rights registration, total water-use caps
and trading-platform development have progressed, water-rights trading has moved from dispersed pilots into a more
standardised operational phase (Moore, 2015; Jiang et al. 2022). Nevertheless, water-rights trading in China remains
at a pivotal stage of transition from institution building to market deepening. Compared with mature water-rights
markets in several developed countries, China ’s water-rights trading system still has a lower level of marketisation
and operational ef ﬁciency. According to data from the China Water Rights Exchange, water-use rights trading in
2024 was recorded in 29 provincial-level regions, involving 12,997 transactions and a total traded volume of 1.499
billion m³. In terms of traded volume, interregional water-rights trades and water-withdrawal-rights trades dominated,
together accounting for 93.6% of the total traded volume. By contrast, trades involving irrigation water users
accounted for 88.4% of all transactions, yet their traded volume was only 0.182 billion m³, representing less than 7%
of the total traded volume. This “high-count, low-volume” pattern suggests that trading among irrigation water users
has not yet developed a scaled-up and consolidated operating mechanism, leaving substantial scope for further gains
in allocative ef ﬁciency (Sheng et al. 2024).
In terms of theory, the market-based allocation problem in irrigation water-rights trading falls within the domains
of common-resource governance and institutional economics (Schlager and Ostrom, 1992; Meinzen-Dick, 2007).
Coasean theory suggests that, when property rights are well de ﬁned and transaction costs are negligible, parties can
achieve efﬁcient allocation through voluntary bargaining (Friesen et al. 2023). However, water resources have strong
public-good attributes and externalities, and market coordination is often constrained in practice by unclear rights
delineation and high transaction costs (Ehrman, 2020). Property-rights theory further argues that clear rights deli-
neation is a prerequisite for shifting common resources from administrative allocation to market-based allocation and
provides the basis for designing incentive and constraint mechanisms (Sikor et al. 2017). Although water rights are
divisible and transferable, they also involve public responsibilities and ecological sustainability requirements.
Accordingly, a well-de ﬁned property-rights regime delineates rights boundaries and establishes a framework of
responsibility and accountability, thereby providing institutional support for the effective operation of incentive
mechanisms. From a behavioural perspective, incentive-design theory posits that aligning bene ﬁts with responsi-
bilities can strengthen intrinsic motivation for water saving and sustainable use under a regime that combines
incentives and constraints, thereby mitigating the tragedy of the commons (Ostrom, 1990; Farley and Costanza,

Common-resource governance theory extends the analytical lens to multi-actor interactions and emphasises the
integration of institutionalised cooperation with the strategic relations among actors. By embedding incentives,
constraints and coordination mechanisms into institutional design, such a governance framework can dynamically
balance stakeholder interests while improving allocative ef ﬁciency and overall governance performance (Ostrom,
2009; Pahl-Wostl, 2009).
Existing research on the market-based development of irrigation water-rights trading can be broadly grouped into
three main strands. In terms of research objects, property-rights institutions are widely regarded as the institutional
foundation of market-based water allocation. However, cross-country differences in institutional arrangements have
led to considerable heterogeneity in the legal attributes of water rights and the corresponding allocation logic. In
countries such as the United States, Australia and Chile, water is often treated as a scarce resource with property-like
attributes. Under privatised or divisible rights arrangements, these systems allow investors, irrigation associations and
private ﬁrms to participate in water allocation and typically operate through a combination of public regulation and
market coordination (Grafton et al. 2011; Del Moral and Saurí, 1999). By contrast, China ’s water-rights regime is
embedded in a framework of public ownership and administratively guided allocation. Water resources are state-
owned, and water rights are mainly expressed through abstraction permits or use quotas, leaving the legal basis of
trading and the boundaries of rights in need of further institutional clari ﬁcation. Under this arrangement, water
allocation remains largely government-led, while private participation and market coordination remain comparatively
constrained (Deng et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2025). Taken together, these studies provide an important institutional
basis for understanding the market-based development of irrigation water-rights trading across different governance
contexts.
From an analytical perspective, existing studies on irrigation water-rights trading have largely relied on
comparative-static approaches, focusing on outcome-oriented issues such as pricing mechanisms, behavioural
responses to water-saving incentives, water-use ef ﬁciency and economic performance. Studies on pricing mechan-
isms suggest that price formation not only re ﬂects water scarcity but also guides the reallocation of water among users
through price signals, thereby helping to reconcile ecological constraints with economic objectives (Wheeler et al.
2025). Behavioural studies commonly adopt a “property-rights incentives and price constraints ” perspective, arguing
that tradability enables water-saving surpluses to be monetised, while price signals reshape water-use behaviour by
strengthening opportunity-cost considerations (Chen et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2020; Wheeler et al. 2013; Hadjigeorgalis,
2008). Studies of ef ﬁciency and performance further indicate that trading-induced reallocation can redirect water
towards users with higher marginal returns, thereby improving overall allocative ef ﬁciency (Debaere and Li, 2020). In
addition, studies of institutional performance have examined the effects of irrigation water-rights trading on agri-
cultural costs, farm income and regional economic activity, while emphasising the need for multidimensional per-
formance assessment (Deng and Zhang, 2025; Huang et al. 2017). This body of literature provides substantial
evidence on the economic and policy effects of irrigation water-rights trading.
With respect to determinants, existing studies can be broadly grouped into two strands: single-factor analyses and
multi-factor interaction analyses. Single-factor studies typically examine one dimension at a time, such as technical
conditions, resource endowments, or institutional arrangements, and assess the marginal effects of water-saving
technologies, cropping patterns, and irrigation-related regulations on the development of water-rights trading (Pfeiffer
and Lin, 2014; Zeleke and Luckett, 2025; Wheeler et al. 2017). As research has progressed, increasing attention has
been paid to interactions among multiple determinants. Using interprovincial or basin-level panel data, recent studies
have employed multi-period difference-in-differences models, panel regressions and spatial econometric methods,
often combined with mediation analysis, to examine how transaction costs, market power, price elasticity, regulatory
intensity and intermediary services shape water-rights trading outcomes (Zhao et al. 2024; Du et al. 2022). These
studies suggest that the market-based development of irrigation water-rights trading is not driven by any single factor
but is shaped by the combined in ﬂuence of institutional, technical and economic conditions. This strand of literature
highlights the complexity of the determinants underlying irrigation water-rights trading and provides an important
foundation for further analysing their joint effects.

Despite these advances, the literature on irrigation water-rights trading and its market-based development still has
several limitations. In terms of research objects, existing studies have mainly examined interactions among local
government (LCG), irrigation district management unit (IMU) and irrigation water users (IWU), while paying
relatively limited attention to the role of social capital (SCP) in the market-based allocation of irrigation water rights,
particularly in the context of emerging public-private governance arrangements in China. From an analytical per-
spective, existing studies have largely focused on outcome-oriented issues, such as pricing mechanisms, water-saving
effects, water-use ef ﬁciency and economic performance, whereas dynamic strategic interactions and evolutionary
mechanisms among multiple stakeholders remain insuf ﬁciently explored. Regarding the determinants of market
development, although existing studies have identi ﬁed a range of institutional, technical and economic factors,
systematic evidence remains limited on how heterogeneous factors and their interactions jointly shape the evolu-
tionary trajectories, convergence processes and stable outcomes of irrigation water-rights trading.
To address these limitations, this study makes three main contributions. First, we construct a four-party evolu-
tionary game model that integrates LCG, IMU, SCP, and IWU within a uni ﬁed analytical framework. By explicitly
introducing SCP, this study extends the actor structure considered in previous research and better re ﬂects the
institutional complexity of irrigation water-rights trading in China. Second, we move beyond static outcome
assessment and examine the dynamic strategic interactions underlying irrigation water-rights trading, thereby
revealing the adjustment processes, equilibrium conditions and evolutionary mechanisms of market-based allocation
among multiple stakeholders. Third, we develop an integrated parameter framework covering foundational, policy-
related, and management-technical factors, and systematically examine both single-factor and two-factor effects on
the market-based development process. In doing so, this study identi ﬁes the directional effects of key determinants
and clari ﬁes how their interactions in ﬂuence convergence speed, evolutionary paths and stable equilibria.
The remainder of this study is structured as follows (Fig. 1). Section 2 develops a four-party evolutionary game
model involving LCG, IMU, SCP, and IWU and characterises the strategic interactions among these actors in water-
rights trading. Section 3 analyses the evolutionary dynamics and stability of the model and identi ﬁes patterns
associated with different strategy pro ﬁles. Section 4 uses numerical simulations to examine the evolutionary tra-
jectories of actors ’ strategies and their key determinants and to investigate the mechanisms underpinning stable
outcomes. Section 5 provides a comprehensive discussion and offers further interpretation and extension of the

Methods
Problem description
With a large population and substantial food demand, China relies on the rational allocation and ef ﬁcient use of water
resources to safeguard food security and advance agricultural modernisation. Of ﬁcial statistics indicate that China ’s
surface water resources total 2989.56 billion m³, while its groundwater resources total 867.92 billion m³. The spatial
distribution of these resources remains highly uneven, with relative abundance in the southeast and scarcity in the
northwest. In China ’s water-withdrawal structure, agriculture accounts for 61.6% of total withdrawals, with irrigation
representing the largest share of agricultural water use. However, conventional irrigation remains widespread, and
overall agricultural water-use ef ﬁciency remains low, resulting in substantial water losses (Levidow et al. 2014;
Sajjad et al. 2025). To improve agricultural water-use ef ﬁciency and support sustainable water use, water-use rights
reform has become a core institutional instrument (Xu and Yang, 2025; Xu et al. 2023). Against this backdrop,
relevant authorities jointly issued a policy document on advancing water-use rights reform, setting out a market-
oriented pathway. The document calls for the initial allocation system to be largely established by 2025 and for
trading arrangements for irrigation water users to be further improved.
The market-based allocation of irrigation water rights among irrigation water users operates as a complex system
characterised by multi-actor participation, strategic interactions, and co-evolutionary dynamics, involving LCG, IMU,
SCP, and IWU. LCG chooses between an incentive-oriented strategy and direct intervention. It uses policy instru-
ments such as water pricing, subsidies, and reward-penalty intensity to in ﬂuence the decisions of the other actors.
IMU chooses between high-ef ﬁciency management and inert management. This choice directly affects water-delivery
efﬁciency and the structure of operating costs. SCP chooses between active participation and opportunistic

Basic assumptions of the model
Litong District in Wuzhong City, Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, was selected as the representative case for this
study. Using this case as the empirical background, the model hypotheses and parameter settings were developed
based on the institutional context, case evidence and ﬁeld investigations. Policy documents related to water-rights
reform, agricultural water pricing, water resources tax and water-saving incentives provide the institutional basis for
the model. Project materials from the Modernised Ecological Irrigation District Project in Litong District, together
with ﬁeldwork and semi-structured interviews with relevant stakeholders, provide empirical support for scenario
construction and parameter calibration. In the subsequent sensitivity analysis, selected key parameters were extended
within reasonable ranges to capture their potential effects under different practical conditions. The main hypotheses
are presented below.
Hypothesis 1. The model includes four stakeholder groups, namely LCG, IMU, SCP, and IWU. Each group is
represented as a population of agents with similar institutional roles rather than as a single fully rational decision-
maker. Under bounded rationality, agents within each population gradually adjust their strategy choices through
learning, imitation and payoff feedback, so that the shares of alternative strategies evolve over time towards relatively
stable states.

Results of evolutionarily stable strategies
Evolutionary game theory provides a suitable framework for analysing strategic interactions under bounded
rationality (Taylor and Jonker, 1978; Na et al. 2024). This study employs replicator dynamics to characterise the
payoff-driven adaptive evolution of LCG, IMU, SCP, and IWU in the context of irrigation water-rights trading.
Compared with best-response dynamics, stochastic learning, and hierarchical adjustment mechanisms, replicator
dynamics more effectively capture the payoff-comparison logic of boundedly rational actors in repeated interactions
and provide a formal basis for analysing equilibrium selection and local stability. Accordingly, this section develops
the replicator dynamic system and examines the local stability of the corresponding equilibrium points.

Given the above parameter settings and stability conditions, each pure-strategy equilibrium point is substituted into
the Jacobian matrix of the replicator dynamic system to obtain the corresponding eigenvalues (Appendix A,

equilibrium point have negative real parts, that equilibrium point is locally asymptotically stable, and the corre-
sponding strategy pro ﬁle can be regarded as an evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS) (Liu and Li, 2023). The results
indicate that, among the 16 pure-strategy equilibrium points, 11 possible equilibrium points satisfy the stability
conditions (Table 2).
Based on the set of locally stable pure-strategy equilibria identi ﬁed above, the market-based allocation process may
be interpreted through four stylised evolutionary states, namely the initial, development, formation, and maturation
phases. This classi ﬁcation provides an institutional interpretation of the locally stable strategy pro ﬁles rather than
describing a unique global transition path applicable to all initial conditions and parameter regions.
Initial phase. At this stage, all participating actors exhibit cautious or passive attitudes towards water-rights trading,
and no effective coordination mechanism has yet emerged. LCG predominantly adopts the direct intervention strategy
to maintain basic operational order, and the system evolves towards the equilibrium point 0 ; 0; 0; 0ðÞ ,r e ﬂecting an
initial state characterised by a low overall level of participation.
Development phase. As cognitive awareness improves and institutional arrangements develop, SCP and IWU
begin to show stronger participation incentives, and the system evolves towards single-actor stable equilibrium
points, including 0 ; 0; 1; 0ðÞ and 0 ; 0; 0; 1ðÞ . As the system moves into the middle and later stages of development,
diversiﬁed bilateral partnerships begin to emerge. SCP and IMU coordinate to form cooperative con ﬁgurations such
as 0 ; 1; 1; 0ðÞ and 0 ; 0; 1; 1ðÞ . In parallel, LCG shifts towards the incentive-oriented strategy, giving rise to con ﬁg-
urations such as 1 ; 0; 1; 0ðÞ and 1 ; 0; 0; 1ðÞ .
Formation phase. As multi-actor coordination mechanisms are gradually established, LCG ’s policy orientation
steers the system towards distinct stable con ﬁgurations. Under the incentive-oriented strategy, the system evolves
towards 1 ; 1; 0; 1ðÞ and 1 ; 0; 1; 1ðÞ ,r eﬂecting positive interactions among high-ef ﬁciency management by IMU, active
participation by SCP, and active water-saving behaviour by IWU. By contrast, under the direct intervention strategy,
the system evolves towards 0 ; 1; 1; 1ðÞ , representing a stable con ﬁguration dominated by administrative governance.
Maturation phase. LCG consistently adopts the incentive-oriented strategy, under which the water-rights trading
mechanism becomes increasingly institutionalised, accompanied by improved IMU management ef ﬁciency, active
SCP participation, and more active water-saving behaviour among IWU. Under parameter settings favourable to
coordinated participation and within the simulated scenarios considered in this study, the system trajectory tends to
converge towards the strategy pro ﬁle 1 ; 1; 1; 1ðÞ , which can be interpreted as a desirable mature state of the market-
based allocation mechanism.
Simulation and results analysis
The simulation analysis is grounded in the practical context of the Modernised Ecological Irrigation District Project in
Litong District, Wuzhong City, Ningxia. As an important pilot initiative in modern ecological irrigation-district
construction and water-rights reform, the project re ﬂects key institutional features of agricultural water-saving reform,
quota-based water management, and market-based water-rights trading in the water-scarce regions of northern China.
The project involves the coordinated participation of LCG, IMU, SCP, and IWU, which corresponds closely to the
four-party structure developed in this study and provides a realistic basis for examining multi-actor strategic inter-
actions. The project also reveals several practical issues, including local ﬁscal pressure, the need to improve irrigation
management ef ﬁciency, insuf ﬁcient risk-sharing and return-guarantee mechanisms for SCP, and heterogeneous
incentives for IWU water-saving behaviour. These issues are not unique to this case but are widely encountered in
market-based irrigation water-rights reform across China. Taken together, these features make the project an
appropriate case context for analysing how market-based allocation mechanisms operate under resource constraints
and institutional transition.

Discussion
Theoretical framework and interpretation of main ﬁndings
Compared with existing studies that mainly focus on static outcomes, pricing effects, or simpli ﬁed actor relationships,
the four-party evolutionary game framework developed in this study provides a more comprehensive explanation of
the market-based allocation mechanism of irrigation water rights. By incorporating LCG, IMU, SCP, and IWU into a
uniﬁed analytical framework, the model captures strategic interdependence among key stakeholders more fully than
conventional three-actor frameworks and better re ﬂects the institutional complexity of irrigation water-rights trading
in China.
Methodologically, this study shifts the analytical focus from static comparisons of market performance to the
dynamic process through which market-based allocation emerges, stabilises, or loses stability under bounded
rationality. This approach explains which outcomes may occur and clari ﬁes how, and under what conditions, different
actors adjust their strategies and move towards speci ﬁc equilibrium con ﬁgurations. The results suggest that the
market-based development of irrigation water-rights trading emerges from continuous strategic adjustment among
multiple actors under changing incentive and constraint conditions, rather than from a single policy variable.
The main ﬁndings can be interpreted through the broader theoretical lens of incentive compatibility, institutional
constraints, and multi-actor coordination in common-resource governance. The positive effects of ﬁscal subsidies and
reputational beneﬁts on LCG ’s incentive-oriented strategy suggest that LCG behaviour is shaped jointly by economic
incentives and external accountability. IMU ’s sensitivity to service fee, reward-penalty intensity, and reserved water
volume reﬂects the combined role of operational compensation and institutional discipline in management decision-
making. SCP ’s strong dependence on expected return and management ef ﬁciency is consistent with the logic of
risk–return matching in public –private cooperation. IWU ’s responsiveness to repurchase multipliers, service fee, and
water resources taxes indicates that water-saving behaviour depends on whether conservation efforts can be translated
into stable and observable economic returns. Overall, these ﬁndings suggest that the market-based allocation of
irrigation water rights is governed by the coordinated interaction of incentives, constraints, and participation
expectations across actors, rather than by any single policy instrument.
Bridging theory and practice
The four-party evolutionary game framework developed in this study provides a dynamic perspective for explaining
how incentives, constraints, and participation expectations jointly shape the evolution of market-based irrigation
water-rights allocation. Rather than treating water-rights trading as a static institutional arrangement, the framework
highlights the behavioural mechanisms through which multiple actors, under bounded rationality, gradually move
towards or away from market-based con ﬁgurations.
Theoretically, the main ﬁndings are broadly consistent with common-resource governance and institutional
incentive theory. The positive effects of ﬁscal subsidies, reputational bene ﬁts, expected return, and repurchase
mechanisms indicate that market-based allocation is more likely to emerge when actors obtain clear and sustained
beneﬁts from participation, ef ﬁcient management, and water-saving behaviour. The effects of reward-penalty
intensity, taxation, and management conditions further suggest that market-based allocation requires institutional
constraints, performance-oriented governance, and credible implementation mechanisms in addition to price signals.
This implies that the effectiveness of irrigation water-rights trading depends on the coordinated alignment of eco-
nomic incentives, managerial capacity, and behavioural responses across actors.

These theoretical implications are partly supported by practical observations from China ’s water-rights trading
reforms. In existing pilot areas, clearer trading rules, compensation arrangements, and supporting incentives have
been associated with stronger water-saving incentives and more active participation in water reallocation. Evidence
from Ningxia and other northwestern irrigation districts further suggests that, under strict resource constraints,
appropriately designed pricing and compensation mechanisms can reduce users ’ burdens while balancing agricultural,
ecological, and other competing water demands (Yin et al. 2025; Qin et al. 2022). These observations are consistent
with the model result that market-based allocation is more likely to evolve when pricing incentives, ﬁscal support,
and compensation arrangements operate in a coordinated manner. International experience further supports the
institutional logic identiﬁed in this study. Research on water markets in Australia, Spain, and other regions shows that
clearly de ﬁned rights, transparent trading platforms, and effective information disclosure can improve water real-
location ef ﬁciency by strengthening property-rights incentives and reducing transaction uncertainty, while weak
institutional enforcement, insuf ﬁcient coordination, and high uncertainty tend to constrain market performance
(Grafton et al. 2010; Palomo-Hierro et al. 2015; Calatrava and Martínez-Granados, 2018). This is consistent with the
present model, in which stable market-based evolution depends on tradable rights and on the broader institutional
environment that shapes trust, enforcement, and participation expectations.
Therefore, the value of the present framework lies in clarifying the underlying mechanisms through which multi-
actor incentives and constraints jointly shape the evolution of market-based irrigation water-rights trading, rather than
in reproducing all institutional and behavioural complexities observed in practice. In this way, the framework serves
as an analytical bridge between institutional theory and practical reform design.
Distributional consequences, accountability risks, and ecological safeguards
Although coordinated incentives can promote market-based convergence, heterogeneity among IWU shapes their
effects. Differences in farm size, cropping structure, irrigation dependence, water-saving capacity, access to infor-
mation, and risk tolerance in ﬂuence farmers’ ability to respond to price signals and participate in water-rights trading
(Zhang et al. 2019; Rong and Cheng 2024). Larger or more commercially oriented farmers are better positioned to
convert water savings into tradable gains, while smallholders and farmers with inelastic irrigation demand may face
higher adjustment costs and weaker bargaining power (Hadjigeorgalis, 2008; Danso et al. 2021). Consequently,
repurchase prices, service fee, subsidies, and water resources taxes may generate uneven participation incentives and
distributional effects. Without adequate metering, transparent information disclosure, and transaction-support
mechanisms, water-rights trading may improve aggregate ef ﬁciency while marginalising relatively vulnerable users.
SCP participation may also create accountability risks. Although stable returns are necessary to sustain SCP
participation, SCP may selectively serve pro ﬁtable areas, transfer costs to water users, underinvest in long-term
maintenance, or engage in opportunistic behaviour under weak monitoring (Iossa and Martimort, 2015). Contractual
arrangements should clearly de ﬁne investment obligations, maintenance responsibilities, service standards, disclosure
requirements, risk-sharing rules, and exit mechanisms. Market-based allocation also requires robust ecological
safeguards (Tisdell, 2001). Tradable water volumes should be determined only after ecological water demand, basic
agricultural water security, groundwater protection, and regional water-resource carrying capacity have been secured.
Thus, irrigation water-rights trading should be understood as a market mechanism embedded in institutional and
ecological constraints, rather than as an unconstrained instrument for ef ﬁciency improvement.
Research limitations and future directions
Although this study provides a useful theoretical framework for understanding the market-based allocation of
irrigation water rights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the model is developed under the
assumption of bounded rationality and abstracts from information asymmetry, heterogeneous risk preferences, and
cognitive biases. It is therefore better suited to explaining the general direction and underlying mechanisms of
strategic evolution than to reproducing the full complexity of individual behavioural responses in practice. Second,

several policy-related and behavioural variables, such as subsidy rates, reputational bene ﬁts, expected return, and
irrigation water-use bene ﬁts, are parameterised as deterministic factors. This treatment facilitates comparative ana-
lysis but cannot fully capture time-varying institutional adjustments, ﬁscal changes, technological progress, or
external environmental disturbances. Third, the stability analysis focuses mainly on local asymptotic stability and
therefore cannot fully capture path switching, transitions across multiple equilibria, or pronounced dynamic ﬂuc-
tuations under major external shocks.
These limitations point to several directions for future research. Future studies may incorporate spatial hetero-
geneity and regional differentiation into the evolutionary framework to examine how differences in resource
endowments, management ef ﬁciency, and policy constraints shape market development across irrigation districts.
Future studies could also introduce time-varying parameters and multi-stage dynamic settings to better capture policy
feedback, institutional adjustment, and improvements in management over time. In addition, the integration of the
theoretical framework with empirical evidence could be strengthened through broader case comparisons, multi-source
datasets, more reﬁned parameter estimation, and additional validation procedures, thereby improving the framework ’s
explanatory power and external applicability.
Conclusions and policy implications
Conclusions
Improving the market-based allocation mechanism of water rights for irrigation water users in China is consistent
with sustainable agricultural water management, helps alleviate imbalances between water supply and demand,
mitigates ecological and environmental risks, and provides institutional support for high-quality agricultural devel-
opment. This study develops a four-party evolutionary game model involving LCG, IMU, SCP, and IWU to explain
the evolutionary dynamics and potential improvement pathways of market-based irrigation water-rights trading. The
main ﬁndings are as follows.
(1) The system admits up to 11 pure-strategy equilibrium points that are locally asymptotically stable under speci ﬁc
parameter conditions. Under the baseline parameter setting adopted in this study, the strategy combination
ð1; 1; 1; 1Þ emerges as the dominant locally stable outcome in the numerical simulations. Under this equilibrium,
LCG primarily adopts an incentive-oriented strategy that enhances policy effectiveness. IMU maintains high-
efﬁciency management and stable service provision. SCP remains actively participating in investment. IWU ’s
strategy converges towards active water-saving behaviour.
(2) The evolutionary trajectory and stability of the market-based irrigation water-rights trading system are jointly
inﬂuenced by the initial strategy probabilities of LCG, IMU, SCP, and IWU. Differences in these initial
probabilities mainly affect convergence speed and adjustment paths, thereby generating heterogeneous dynamic
trajectories. However, within the baseline setting and the simulated range considered in this study, these
differences do not alter the system ’s observed tendency to move towards the same locally stable outcome.
(3) Single-factor sensitivity analysis indicates that parameter variations exert heterogeneous effects on the system ’s
evolutionary dynamics. Increases in service fee, expected return, reward-penalty intensity, and repurchase price
multipliers, together with stronger ﬁscal subsidies for IMU and SCP, higher reputational bene ﬁts, lower water
resources taxes, and moderate reductions in reserved water volumes, promote the market-based development of
irrigation water-rights trading. By contrast, variations in irrigation water-use bene ﬁts, ﬁscal subsidies directed to
IWU, and water utilisation coef ﬁcient have relatively weak effects on strategic evolution and make limited
contributions to the market-based process.
(4) Two-factor sensitivity analysis identi ﬁes synergies between subsidy rates and reputational bene ﬁts, water
resources taxes and reward-penalty intensity, expected return and management-technical factors, service fee and
irrigation water-use bene ﬁts, and repurchase price multiplier and irrigation water-use bene ﬁts. Overall,
coordinated parameter adjustments improve system stability and accelerate strategic convergence.

Compared with existing studies (Yuan et al. 2024; Yuan et al. 2020; Yuan et al. 2023), which have mainly

Policy implications
Strengthening the mechanisms for ﬁscal incentives and reputational constraints
The results indicate that subsidy rates and reputational bene ﬁts jointly strengthen LCG ’s incentive-oriented gov-
ernance capacity and promote system convergence towards a market-based equilibrium. Accordingly, LCG should
adopt a more targeted policy design by linking subsidies to IMU ’s management performance, SCP ’s investment
outcomes, and IWU ’s water-saving behaviour, while providing differentiated support for actors with different per-
formance levels. A transparent accountability mechanism should also be established through the regular disclosure of
transaction performance, subsidy use, and water-saving outcomes, with these results incorporated into government
performance assessment and public oversight. In this way, ﬁscal incentives and reputational constraints can jointly
shift LCG from direct administrative intervention towards rule-based and incentive-oriented governance, thereby
improving policy implementation ef ﬁciency and enhancing the long-term stability of irrigation water-rights trading
reform.
Establishing a management mechanism balancing incentives and constraints
The results suggest that service fee, subsidy rates, reward-penalty intensity, and reserved water volume have mea-
surable effects on IMU ’s strategic choices. Accordingly, IMU governance should combine performance-based
incentives with binding constraints. Service fee and subsidies should be linked to indicators such as water-saving
performance, water-delivery stability, and operational ef ﬁciency, so that high-performing units receive greater sup-
port. A tiered tax-and-penalty mechanism should also be introduced, under which IMU with higher ef ﬁciency and
stronger management performance receives moderate tax reductions, while those characterised by extensive man-
agement or high energy intensity face stricter penalties and heavier tax burdens. Reserved water volume should also
be maintained within a reasonable range to avoid weakening management ﬂexibility and trading ef ﬁciency. Through
this combination of differentiated incentives, performance assessment, and tax constraints, IMU can be guided from
inert management towards ef ﬁcient management, thereby improving operational ef ﬁciency and the reliability of
agricultural water supply.
Stabilising expected returns and strengthening performance linkages
The results indicate that SCP ’s strategic choices are jointly shaped by expected return, ﬁscal subsidies, and man-
agement ef ﬁciency. Accordingly, LCG should promote SCP participation through mechanisms that combine stable
returns with performance-linked incentives. In practice, contracts should specify minimum return guarantees,
revenue-sharing rules, and risk-sharing arrangements to reduce investment uncertainty, while ﬁscal subsidies should
be provided in a timely and transparent manner. IMU should also improve management ef ﬁciency through infra-
structure upgrading and smart monitoring to reduce SCP ’s operating risks. Economic returns should also be linked to
Article in Press https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-026-09047-1
Humanities and Social Sciences Communications
measurable indicators, such as water-supply reliability and water-saving performance, so that subsidies, revenue
distribution, and risk compensation are tied to actual performance outcomes. In this way, SCP can be encouraged to
shift from short-term participation to sustained investment, thereby providing stable ﬁnancial and technical support
for the market-based development of irrigation water-rights trading.
Optimising income incentives and endogenous behavioural mechanisms
The results indicate that IWU ’s behavioural decisions are jointly in ﬂuenced by service fee levels, repurchase mul-
tipliers, water resources taxes, and irrigation water-use bene ﬁts. Accordingly, policy design should focus on
strengthening IWU ’s water-saving incentives through differentiated charging, stable return mechanisms, and rea-
sonable tax regulation. Speci ﬁcally, service fee should be linked to actual water-saving performance, with users
achieving better outcomes receiving fee reductions or subsidies, while the use of collected fees should be disclosed
regularly to enhance trust. Repurchase rules and trading-revenue arrangements should also be re ﬁned by establishing
dedicated funds and differentiated repurchase standards based on saved water volumes and trading participation
levels, so that water-saving efforts can be translated into tangible and stable economic returns. Water resources taxes
should also be kept within a reasonable range to avoid weakening users ’ willingness to participate. Through this
combination of cost regulation, return guarantees, and transparent implementation, IWU can be guided from passive
compliance towards sustained participation in water saving and water-rights trading.
