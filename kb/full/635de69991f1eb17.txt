Substituting Inorganic Fertilizer with Fermented Oil Palm Empty 
Fruit Bunch Liquid Organic Fertilizer: Nutrient Uptake Efficiency 
and Yield of Hybrid Maize in Bengkulu, Indonesia 
Suryadi *, Usman, Elni Mutmainah, Suliasih, Rapiski, Aulia Rahma Gunawan 
Department of Agrotechnology, Faculty of Agriculture, Universita s Muhammadiyah Bengkulu, Bengkulu , 
Indonesia. 
 suryadi@umb.ac.id 
Abstract. Hybrid maize on acidic Ultisols depends on mineral NPK fertilizer, and its cost limits 
smallholder returns in Bengkulu, Indonesia. Oil palm empty fruit bunch (EFB) is an abundant 
local waste that can be fermented into a liquid organic fertilizer (POC). We tested how much of 
the recommended NPK dose POC can replace without lowering grain yield or nutrient uptake. 
A randomized complete block field experiment with four replications compared an unfertilized 
control, 100% NPK (138 kg N, 54 kg P 2O5, and 60 kg K2O ha−1), 75%, 50%, and 25% of that dose 
combined with 3,333 L ha −1 of POC, and POC alone. Full NPK produced 7.92 t ha −1 of grain. 
Replacing 25% and 50% of the NPK dose gave 8.15 and 7.34 t ha −1, and neither differed from 
full NPK (Tukey HSD, p < 0.05), whereas replacing 75% reduced yield to 6.01 t ha −1. Nitrogen 
uptake mat ched full NPK at 25% replacement and was 13% lower at 50% replacement. 
Agronomic efficiency of N rose from 30.6 to 37.9 kg grain per kg N at 25% replacement. 
Apparent N recovery (49.8 to 62.0%) was not separated by Tukey's test. Soil pH after harvest 
was higher than under full NPK once 50% or more of the NPK dose was replaced. Replacing up 
to 25% of NPK with fermented EFB liquid fertilizer maintained yield and N uptake on this Ultisol, 
and 50% replacement kept yield but reduced N uptake. The result comes fr om one season at 
one site and lacks a cost analysis. 
Keywords: Acid Soil , Zea Mays , Liquid Organic Fertilizer , Oil Palm Waste , Nitrogen Use 
Efficiency. 
1. Introduction 
Ultisols cover 45.8 million ha, about a quarter of Indonesia's land area, and farmers 
often use them to expand agricultural production [1]. These soils are acidic, carry 
exchangeable aluminium, and bind applied phosphorus in aluminium and iron 
compounds [2] -[5]. Hybrid maize on an Ultisol therefore depends on mineral NPK 
fertilizer. Rising prices of non -subsidised fertilizer already burden oil palm farmers in 
Indonesia [1], and maize growers on the same soils buy from the same input market. A 
practice that cuts the mineral dose without cutting yield would lower input cost for them. 
Oil palm mills supply a local source of organic matter. Empty fruit bunch (EFB) 
accounts for about 23% of fresh fruit bunch mass, and Indonesia produced 47.7 million t 
of palm oil in 2024 [6]. EFB also harbors phosphate-solubilizing microorganisms [7]. Trials 
with EFB products on maize have used compost or biochar. In a greenhouse study, EFB 
compost combined with coal fly ash raised P uptake and grain yield of maize on an Ultisol 
from East Kalimantan [8]. An EFB biochar -compost car rier with phosphate -solubilizing 
fungi improved maize growth, P uptake, and P uptake efficiency on an Ultisol from Central 
Kalimantan [7], and EFB compost with rice husk charcoal raised maize growth and yield 
on a red -yellow podzolic soil [9]. Blankson et al. [10] reported that EFB biochar and 
compost raised N and P use efficiency of okra above that of mineral NPK, although only 
the biochar-compost combination held its yield advantage into a second cycle.

Liquid products have received less attention. A liqu id organic fertilizer enriched 
with phosphate-solubilizing bacteria allowed a 50% cut in P fertilizer for maize on a West 
Java Inceptisol [11]. Reviews of humic -based and other biostimulants list increased root 
growth and nutrient uptake among their most c ommon plant responses [12], which 
suggests that a fermented liquid could act on nutrient uptake as well as supply nutrients. 
Studies that replace mineral fertilizer with organic fertilizer in maize disagree on 
the best share. A meta -analysis of 133 maize s tudies placed the yield -optimal share of 
organic N at 40 to 60% [13]. In a rainfed semi -arid system in northern China, replacing 
30% of chemical N raised grain yield by 9.2% and improved N recovery, while replacing 
45% or 60% reduced yield [14]. A 1:1 mixture of chemical and organic N gave lower maize 
yield and lower N agronomic efficiency than chemical N alone in a soybean -maize 
intercrop [15], and 20% replacement gave the highest annual yield in a semi-arid wheat-
maize rotation [16]. Other field and pot s tudies likewise show that moderate organic 
substitution can improve maize productivity and resource -use efficiency, while the 
response depends strongly on the replacement proportion and soil conditions [17], [18]. 
The single -site trials cited here ran outs ide humid tropical Ultisols and none used a 
fermented EFB liquid. 
Prior work therefore shows that EFB products help maize on acidic tropical soils and 
that partial organic substitution can hold or raise yield. It leaves open whether a 
fermented liquid EFB product can take over part of the NPK dose on an Ultisol, and at 
what share nutrient uptake begins to fall. We addressed these questions in a field 
experiment in Bengkulu that graded the replacement of NPK by fermented EFB liquid 
fertilizer (POC, from the Indonesian pupuk organik cair) and evaluated yield together with 
N, P, and K uptake and N use efficiency indices [19], [20]. These indices complement 
fertilizer-N recovery evidence showing that management and soil organic carbon strongly 
influence maize nitrogen-use efficiency [21]. The reported optimum shares of 20 to 60% 
guided two hypotheses. H1 states that replacing up to 50% of the recommended NPK 
dose with POC leaves grain yield equal to that of full NPK. H2 states that replacement 
raises the apparent recovery efficiency of N. 
2. Method 
2.1 Site and soil 
The experiment ran for one maize growing season on a farmer -managed field in 
Bengkulu Province, Sumatra, Indonesia [INSERT site name, coordinates, elevation, and 
planting dates]. The soi l is an Ultisol. Before planting, ten cores from 0 to 20 cm were 
pooled and analyzed for pH (H2O, 1:2.5), organic C by the Walkley -Black method [22], 
total N (Kjeldahl), Bray -1 P [23], exchangeable K and Al, and cation exchange capacity. 
The soil was a cla y loam with pH 4.62, organic C 1.38%, total N 0.11%, Bray -1 P 5.8 mg 
kg−1, exchangeable K 0.21 cmolc kg−1, exchangeable Al 2.9 cmolc kg−1, and cation exchange 
capacity 12.4 cmolc kg−1. 
2.2 Preparation of POC 
We shredded 20 kg of EFB from a local palm oil m ill and fermented it under 
anaerobic conditions in a closed drum with 100 L of water, 2 L of molasses, and 1 L of a 
microbial starter [INSERT product] for 21 days at ambient temperature. The liquid was 
filtered through a 1 mm sieve before use. The final POC had pH 5.4, electrical conductivity 
6.2 dS m−1, organic C 1.8%, N 0.42%, P2O5 0.23%, and K2O 0.85% (w/v), which gives a C/N 
ratio of 4.3.

2.3 Experimental design and treatments 
The experiment used a randomized complete block design with six treatments and 
four blocks (24 plots of 4.5 × 3.5 m). Plots received no fertilizer (control), the full 
recommended NPK dose, 75, 50, or 25% of that dose plus POC, or POC alone (Table 1). 
The recommended dose was 300 kg urea, 150 kg SP-36, and 100 kg KCl ha−1, equal to 138 
kg N, 54 kg P 2O5, and 60 kg K 2O ha −1. All P and K, and one third of the N, went on at 
planting, and the remaining N went on at 30 days after planting (DAP). POC was diluted 
1:1 with water and applied as a soil drench in three equal splits at 10, 25, and 40 DAP, for 
a total of 3,333 L ha −1 of undiluted POC (50 mL per plant). This volume supplied 14.0 kg 
N, 7.7 kg P 2O5, and 28.3 kg K 2O ha −1. A commercial hybrid maize [INSERT variety] was 
sown at 75 × 20 cm with one plant per hill (66,667 plants ha−1), and all plots received the 
same weed and pest management.

2.4 Measurements and efficiency indices 
Plant height (10 plants per plot) was recorded at 60 DAP and ear -leaf chlorophyll 
(SPAD-502 meter, 10 plants per plot) at silking. A net area o f 6.75 m 2 per plot was 
harvested at 110 DAP, ears were shelled, and grain mass was adjusted to 15% moisture. 
Kernel weight was recorded from 100 kernels. At harvest, three plants per plot were 
separated into grain and stover, dried at 70 °C to constant mass, and ground. Total N was 
determined by the Kjeldahl method, and P and K by molybdenum -blue 
spectrophotometry and flame photometry after wet digestion. Uptake equals tissue 
concentration multiplied by dry mass, summed over grain and stover and scaled to a 
hectare. A composite soil sample (0 to 20 cm) from each plot after harvest was analyzed 
for pH and Bray-1 P. 
Efficiency indices follow Cassman et al. [19] and Ladha et al. [20]. Partial factor 
productivity (PFPN) is grain yield divided by total N applied, agronomic efficiency (AEN) is 
the yield gain over the control divided by total N applied, and apparent recovery 
efficiency (REN) is the gain in N uptake over the control divided by total N applied, 
expressed as a percentage. Total N applied includ es the N in POC, and each plot was 
compared with the control plot of its own block. We did not compute the indices for the 
control or for POC alone, because 14 kg N ha−1 makes the ratios unstable. 
2.5 Statistical analysis 
Data were analyzed by analysis of variance for a randomized complete block design, 
and means were separated with Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test at p < 
0.05. The analysis of the efficiency indices included only the four treatments with NPK. 
Residual normality (Shapiro–Wilk) and variance homogeneity (Levene) were checked for 
grain yield, and the Pearson correlation between yield and nutrient uptake used all 24 
plots. The response of grain yield to the share of the NPK dose replaced by POC (0% for 
full NPK to 100% for POC a lone) was described with a quadratic model fitted to the 20

3. Result and Discussion 
3.1 Grain yield and yield components 
Treatment affected every measured variable (p < 0.001) except RE N (p = 0.044). 
Residuals of grain yield met the assumptions of the analysis (Shapiro –Wilk W = 0.95, p = 
0.30; Levene p = 0.66). Grain yield ranged from 3.70 t ha −1 in the control to 8.15 t ha −1 
with 75% NPK plus POC (F 5,15 = 93.9, CV = 6.0%; Table 3, Figure 1a). Full NPK gave 7.92 t 
ha−1. The yields with 75% and 50% NPK plus POC equaled 102.9 and 92.7% of full NPK and 
shared its letter, because both fell within the HSD of 0.86 t ha−1. Yield dropped to 6.01 t 
ha−1 (75.8% of full NPK) with 25% NPK plus POC, and POC alone gave 4.69 t ha −1, about 
1.0 t ha−1 above the control. Kernel weight followed the same order, except that the 50% 
level (32.1 g) fell below full NPK ( 33.9 g) although its yield did not. Plant height did not 
separate the four treatments that received NPK, and SPAD readings dropped below full 
NPK only at 25% NPK plus POC (44.0 against 51.6; Table 2).

3.2 Nutrient uptake 
Total N uptake was 128.4 kg ha−1 under full NPK and 132.4 kg ha−1 at 75% NPK plus 
POC, with no difference between them (Table 2, Figure 1b). At 50% NPK plus POC, N

3.4 Soil after harvest 
Soil pH after harvest was 4.50 under full NPK (initial value 4. 62) and 4.66 at 75% 
NPK plus POC. It reached 4.82, 4.87, and 4.94 when 50% or more of the NPK dose was 
replaced, and these three values exceeded that of full NPK (HSD = 0.12). Bray-1 P ranged 
from 7.5 to 9.8 mg kg −1 across the four treatments with NPK, against 5.3 mg kg −1 in the 
control and 5.8 mg kg−1 with POC alone (HSD = 1.3). 
3.5 Response of yield to the replacement share 
A quadratic model described the response of grain yield to the replaced share x (%) 
of the NPK dose across treatments T1 to T5, with yield = 8.03 + 0.0069x − 0.000413x2 (R2 
= 0.94, F 2,17 = 124.5, p < 0.001). The quadratic term was significant (p < 0.001) and the 
linear term was not (p = 0.41), so yield stayed flat at low replacement shares and th en 
declined. Predicted yield fell to 95% of its value at 0% replacement when 40.6% of the

3.6 Discussion 
The data support H1 at 25% replacement and support it with a caveat at 50%. 
Replacing a quarter of the NPK dose with POC produced the same grain yield and the 
same N, P, and K uptake as full NPK, and the same held for yield alone at 50% 
replacement. This range sits inside the 20 to 60% optima reported for maize [13], [14], 
[16] and agrees with the pot result of Purwani and Nurjaya [24], who found the highest 
maize yield on a West Java Inceptisol at 50% of the recommended NPK plus a microb ial 
biofertilizer. Our 25% level did not raise yield by the 9.2% that Wang et al. [14] recorded 
at 30% replacement, but the design could detect only differences above 0.86 t ha−1, 
about 11% of full NPK. 
The 50% level needs a closer look. Its yield was 7% l ower than that of full NPK, a 
gap the HSD could not separate, its kernel weight was lower, and its N uptake was 13% 
lower. Maize can hold yield while drawing on soil N, and this Ultisol holds little (0.11% 
total N, 1.38% organic C). The N deficit then fall s on the soil pool, and repeated seasons 
could deplete it. Blankson et al. [10] saw such a time effect in okra, where the yield 
advantage of EFB amendments in the first cycle faded in the second as soil organic carbon 
and cation exchange capacity declined. A single-season trial cannot show whether 50% 
replacement is sustainable. The sole -organic treatment points the same way, since POC 
alone reached only 59% of full -NPK yield, in line with reports that organic inputs alone 
underperform in cereal systems [25]. 
The response at 75% NPK cannot be traced to POC nutrients alone. Total N supply 
at this level was 117.5 kg ha−1, 15% below full NPK, yet N uptake was equal. Three routes 
could account for this, and the study tested none. Biostimulant activity may have 
increased root growth and uptake, a common response to humic -based and related 
products [12]. Phosphate -solubilizing microbes carried by fermented EFB may have 
raised P availability, as reported for EFB materials on Ultisols [7] and for a liquid organic 
fertilizer on maize [11]. A higher soil pH may have eased Al toxicity and P fixation, which 
organic amendments achieve on acid soils [2], [3]. Organic -acid-mediated Al 
complexation and P mobilization provide a plausible mechanistic basis for this response 
[26]. Post-harvest pH under 50% or more replacement was 0.3 to 0.4 units above full NPK, 
and part of that gap is consistent with acidification by urea, since pH under full NPK fell 
0.12 below the initial value. The 75% treatment also received 73.3 kg K2O ha−1 , more 
than the 60 kg of full NPK, so extra K may explain part of its numerical yield advantage. 
The regression places the 95% yield threshold at about 41% replacement, between 
the 25% and 50% treatments and inside the 30 to 60% range reported for maize [1 3], 
[14]. The model rests on five replacement levels and a single POC rate, and the POC-only 
treatment anchors its tail. The threshold therefore interpolates between two tested 
levels, and we do not offer it as a recommended rate. 
POC also differs from the compost and biochar of earlier EFB trials in the organic 
matter it carries. Compost trials applied 5 to 20 t ha−1 of solid material [8], [9], whereas 
3,333 L ha−1 of POC at 1.8% organic C adds about 60 kg C ha−1. The liquid cannot build 
soil organic matte r, so any effect on the soil came through soluble constituents and 
microbes. That limits what a liquid product can do for an Ultisol with 1.38% organic C, 
and it is one more reason to expect the yield effect to depend on continued mineral 
inputs. AEN and PFPN rose as the NPK share fell, but part of that rise is arithmetic, since 
the denominator shrinks while yield holds. REN gives a cleaner test of uptake efficiency 
and gave only weak support to H2. Recovery was about 12 percentage points higher at 
75% and 50% NPK plus POC than under full NPK, yet the overall F test was marginal and 
Tukey's test separated no pair. We treat H2 as unresolved. This caution is consistent with

global tracer evidence showing that N recovery is highly sensitive to soil conditions a nd 
fertilizer management [21]. Four blocks give limited power for a ratio with a CV of 9.8%. 
Practical use faces a logistics constraint. The tested rate of 3,333 L ha −1 requires 
hauling and drenching a large liquid volume, and we did not compare its cost w ith the 
saving on NPK. That comparison decides adoption and needs mill-gate prices for EFB and 
local prices for urea, SP -36, and KCl. The study has further limits. It covers one season, 
one site, and one hybrid. Roots, microbial activity, exchangeable Al, and N losses were 
not measured, so the mechanisms above remain hypotheses. Soil pH stayed below 5 in 
every treatment, so POC did not replace lime on this Ultisol. Multi-season trials with root 
and soil measurements and a partial budget would test both the mechanisms and the 
economics. 
4. Conclusion 
On this Ultisol, replacing 25% of the recommended NPK dose with fermented EFB 
liquid fertilizer kept grain yield and N, P, and K uptake at the level of full NPK. Replacing 
50% kept yield but lowered N uptake by 13%, and replacing 75% cut yield by about 24%. 
The gain of about 12 percentage points in apparent N recovery at 25 to 50% replacement 
was not confirmed by mean separation. We propose 25% replacement as the working 
rate for on-farm testing in Bengkulu, and 50% only with multi-season soil N monitoring.
