Abstract 
 
Slow- and controlled -release fertilizers (SRFs and CRFs) are increasingly used to improve 
nutrient-use efficiency and reduce nutrient losses, yet slower release does not inherently translate 
into superior field performance. This critical review synthesizes 130 included publications 
published from 2020 to 2026, supplemented by three foundational sources used only for historical 
and technology -readiness context, using a performance -first framework that links material 
architecture, material release, root -zone nutrient availability, plant uptake, yield, nutrient loss, 
environmental impact, cost, and industrial readiness. The evidence shows that dissolution, 
diffusion, osmotic transport, swelling, coating degradation, ion exchange, mineral reactions, and 
biological processes can dominate under different conditions. Nitrogen systems have the strongest 
evidence base, phosphorus is moderately supported, potassium and mineral systems remain more 
heterogeneous, and evidence for micronutrients is comparatively limited. A crop –supply 
synchronization concept and a five-level field-specific technology-readiness framework for slow- 
and controlled -release fertilizers (TRL -SRF) are proposed as conceptual tools requiring 
independent validation; TRL -SRF is not equivalent to conv entional nine -level TRL systems.

1. Introduction 
1.1. Problem definition and performance gap 
Fertilizer efficiency is constrained by a structural mismatch between the rapid availability of 
many conventional soluble fertilizers and the time-dependent nutrient demand of crops. Nitrogen 
can be lost through ammonia volatilization, nitrate leaching, an d nitrous oxide emissions; 
phosphorus can become immobilized by sorption or precipitation; and potassium can be displaced 
below the effective rooting zone or supplied in forms whose dissolution is too slow for peak crop 
demand. Enhanced-efficiency fertilizers attempt to reduce this mismatch by controlling the rate, 
timing, or biochemical transformation of nutrient supply. Recent reviews and meta-analyses show 
substantial agronomic promise, but they also reveal strong dependence on crop, soil, climate, 
formulation, and management context (Jariwala et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; 
Pan et al., 2024; Priya, Sarkar, et al., 2024; Siudak et al., 2026; Vejan et al., 2021; Yang et al., 
2021; Zhang, Wang, et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2020). 
Slow-release fertilizers (SRFs) and controlled -release fertilizers (CRFs) are often grouped 
together, yet their governing principles are not identical. Mineral -derived SRFs may rely on 
inherently slow dissolution, ion exchange, or reaction -limited nutrient liberation, whereas CRFs 
more commonly use a coating or engineered matrix to regulate water ingress and nutrient diffusion. 
The distinction matters because the same apparent release duration can arise from different rate -
controlling mechanisms, with diffe rent sensitivities to temperature, moisture, ionic composition, 
abrasion, and microbial activity (Duan et al., 2023; Lakshani et al., 2023; Lawrencia et al., 2021; 
Liu et al., 2022; Priya, Sarkar, et al., 2024; Vejan et al., 2021).

1.2. Why slower release is not automatically better 
The central limitation of much of the literature is therefore conceptual rather than merely 
technological: a slower dissolution curve measured in water does not, by itself, demonstrate higher 
nutrient-use efficiency (NUE), higher yield, lower environmental burden, or economic superiority 
in the field. Studies that explicitly connect fertilizer formulation with crop response, nutrient 
losses, and long -term soil function are more informative than release tests alone. Long -duration 
field evidence also shows th at one environmental benefit can coexist with another burden; for 
example, reducing ammonia loss does not necessarily guarantee a reduction in nitrous oxide 
emissions (Gao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2024; Liu, Chen, et al., 2025; Lü et al. , 
2023; Ren et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). 
Accordingly, this review is organized around a performance -first question: under which 
combinations of fertilizer chemistry, release architecture, soil conditions, crop demand, 
environmental exposure, and manufacturing constraints does slower nutrient release translate into 
genuinely improved field performance? The review evaluates the evidence chain from material 
design and release mechanism to soil interaction, nutrient availability, plant uptake, yield, nutrient 
loss, environmental impact, cost, and indu strial readiness. This framing intentionally shifts the 
discussion from cataloging materials toward identifying the conditions under which release control 
is agronomically and commercially meaningful.

2.2. Evidence hierarchy 
Evidence was classified by experimental level: (i) aqueous or solution release tests, (ii) soil 
incubation, (iii) pot or greenhouse studies, (iv) single -season field trials, (v) multi -season field 
trials, (vi) pilot-scale manufacturing or application, and (vii) industrial or commercial validation. 
This hierarchy is not a quality ranking in the conventional sense; rather, it reflects increasing 
exposure to interacting variables that can alter release and performance. A formulation with 
excellent reproducibility in water may fail in soil, while a field-effective material may show release 
behavior that cannot be explained by a single idealized kinetic model (Bonetti et al., 2022; Duan 
et al., 2023; Lipin et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2021; Ransom et al., 2020; Swify et al., 2023).

2.3. Critical assessment framework 
 
The critical assessment framework used throughout the review is shown in Figure 1. It links 
material architecture to material release, soil and rhizosphere interactions, root -zone nutrient 
availability, plant uptake, yield and NUE, nutrient loss, environmental impact, and cost/readiness. 
The framework represents a hypothesized causal evidence chain in which each downstream 
relationship requires independent empirical validation; the review does not assume that causality 
has been demonstrated across every tra nsition. For example, an improved diffusion barrier can 
demonstrate material-release control, but it does not by itself establish root -zone availability or 
yield response; likewise, a yield response does not establish lower life -cycle impact. Throughout 
the manuscript, three rates are therefore kept conceptually distinct: (i) material release rate from 
the fertilizer, (ii) soil/root -zone plant -available nutrient supply rate after transport, sorption, 
precipitation, mineralization, immobilization, and other transformations, and (iii) plant uptake rate. 
Figure 1 integrates the evidence chain used throughout this review and makes explicit that each 
downstream claim requires its own validation step.

3. Terminology, Classification, and Functional Boundaries 
3.1. Functional definitions 
 
Terminology is a major source of inconsistency in this field. Conventional soluble fertilizers 
are designed to dissolve rapidly and provide immediate nutrient availability. SRFs reduce nutrient 
availability through intrinsic material properties or matrix e ffects, whereas CRFs use an 
intentionally engineered barrier, membrane, or encapsulating phase to regulate release. Stabilized 
fertilizers constitute a separate functional class because they primarily slow biochemical 
transformations, such as urea hydrolysis or nitrification, rather than physically controlling nutrient 
release. Hybrid products can combine these functions and should be described by the dominant 
mechanisms rather than by marketing terminology alone (Lawrencia et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022; 
Priya, Sarkar, et al., 2024; Siudak et al., 2026; Vejan et al., 2021). These functional distinctions 
are consistent with foundational controlled-release and enhanced-efficiency fertilizer frameworks, 
while the present review applies them to the current 2020 –2026 evidence base (Shaviv, 2001; 
Trenkel, 2010).

3.3. Coated, matrix-based, and hybrid systems 
A second boundary concerns coated versus matrix -based systems. In coated systems, a 
relatively soluble nutrient core is separated from the environment by a layer whose thickness, 
permeability, defects, and degradation determine transport. In matrix systems, nutrient species are 
dispersed or chemically incorporated within a solid phase, and release can depend on coupled 
dissolution, pore transport, ion exchange, or matrix degradation. Composite systems blur this 
boundary, particularly when biochar, clays, hydrogels, or biodegradable polymers simultaneously 
retain water, sorb nutrients, and provide diffusion resistance (Bauli et al., 2021; Bi et al., 2020;

4. Fundamentals of Nutrient Release 
4.1. Governing release mechanisms 
Nutrient release emerges from transport, reaction, and structural change. Dissolution -
controlled systems are governed by the solubility and reactive surface of the nutrient -bearing 
phase; diffusion-controlled systems depend on concentration gradients and effective diffusivity 
through coatings or matrices; osmotic systems depend on water ingress and internal pressure; 
swelling-controlled systems change permeability as hydrophilic networks expand; and degradable 
coatings evolve structurally with time. Ion exc hange, adsorption/desorption, mineral 
transformation, and biological activity can add further rate limitations (Bi et al., 2020; Bortoletto-
Santos et al., 2020; Duan et al., 2023; Lakshani et al., 2023; Lawrencia et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024; 
Lipin et al., 2023; Maghsoodi, Najafi, et al., 2020; Mendonca Cidreira et al., 2026; Mihok et al., 
2020; Priya, Sarkar, et al., 2024; Sakhno et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2020).

4.4. Boundary-condition dependence 
A critical consequence is that 'controlled release' should be treated as a boundary -condition-
dependent property. A coating that behaves as a diffusion barrier in water can become defect -
controlled after drying, mechanically damaged during handling, or bio logically altered in soil. 
Similarly, a low -solubility phosphate or potassium mineral can shift from reaction -limited to 
transport-limited behavior as weathering products accumulate. Mechanistic claims should 
therefore be tested under more than one chemica lly relevant environment whenever the intended 
application is field agriculture. 
5. Materials and Fertilizer Architectures 
5.1. Polymer and biopolymer coatings 
 
Material design determines both the fertilizer release mechanism and its environmental 
burden. Synthetic polymers provide tunable permeability and mechanical integrity, but persistence 
and microplastic generation remain concerns. Biobased polyurethanes, li gnin blends, starch, 
cellulose, chitosan, alginate, collagen-derived networks, and biomass waxes are being developed 
to retain release control while improving feedstock renewability and end -of-life behavior (Arafa 
et al., 2022; Channab et al., 2023; dos Sa ntos et al., 2021; Fertahi et al., 2021; Ji et al., 2024;

5.2. Hydrogels and water-retaining matrices 
Hydrogels and superabsorbent matrices can provide dual functionality by storing water and 
delaying nutrient transport. However, their performance depends on swelling stability, ionic 
sensitivity, mechanical strength, and how well water-retention benefits persist under repeated 
wetting-drying cycles. The literature increasingly combines biopolymers with nanocellulose, 
clays, or other reinforcements to improve structure while maintaining biodegradability (Bauli et 
al., 2021; Bora et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024 ; Li & Zhang, 2023; Perera et al., 2026; Priya, Jha, et 
al., 2024; Purba et al., 2026; Sarhan et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2021; Song et al., 2024; Xu et al., 
2026).

6. Nutrient-Specific Slow-Release Systems 
6.1. Nitrogen 
Nitrogen dominates the CRF literature because urea is highly soluble, widely used, and 
vulnerable to multiple loss pathways. Polymer -coated, sulfur-modified, hydrogel-based, biochar-
based, and blended controlled -release urea formulations have been evaluated across rice, maize, 
cotton, wheat, sunflower, oilseed rape, vegetables, and other systems (Chen et al., 2020; Etminani-

Phosphorus requires a different logic because the dominant constraint is often low mobility 
and chemical fixation rather than excessive solubility alone. Struvite, LDHs, hydroxyapatite,

loss risk and sustain crop uptake, but performance is especially sensitive to soil pH, sorption 
capacity, rhizosphere chemistry, and the ability of roots or microbes to mobilize the source (Buates

6.3. Potassium 
Potassium SRFs include controlled -release KCl and K₂SO₄, zeolites, biochar -derived K

dissolution; therefore, the same term 'slow release' covers fundamentally different design problems

7. Factors Governing Release under Real Soil Conditions 
7.1. Temperature, water, and transport 
Release tests performed in water are useful for screening, but the field environment introduces 
coupled thermal, hydraulic, chemical, and biological controls. Temperature alters diffusivity, 
polymer mobility, reaction rates, and microbial activity; water c ontent controls both solute 
transport and the continuity of aqueous pathways; and ionic strength changes swelling, ion 
exchange, and activity gradients. Placement and particle size also modify the local boundary layer 
and root access. Ransom et al. (2020) demonstrated the importance of placement and temperature,

7.2. pH, mineralogy, and reactive soils 
Soil pH and mineralogy are particularly important for P - and K -bearing SRFs. Acid -base 
conditions control phosphate speciation, apatite dissolution, struvite stability, and silicate-mineral 
weathering; clays and oxides can either retain released nutrients or reduce their effective diffusion 
toward roots. Comparisons between hydroponic and soil systems demonstrate why release 
behavior cannot be extrapolated directly from simplified media (El Messbahi et al., 2026; 
Gonçalves & Roque, 2026; Maghsoodi, Ghodszad, et al., 2020; Peixoto et al., 2022; Perera et al., 
2026; Roy et al., 2023; Sakhno et al., 2022; Teles et al., 2020). 
7.3. Biological and management controls 
Biological and management variables are equally important. Controlled -release fertilization 
can alter microbial co-occurrence patterns and N-cycling functions, while straw return, irrigation 
regime, mulching, and deep placement modify both nutrient transport and crop access (Chen et al.,

7.4. Validation envelopes 
The design implication is that laboratory protocols should reproduce the dominant field 
stressors expected for the target use. For irrigated systems, repeated wetting and temperature cycles 
may be more informative than a single constant -temperature water test. For arid soils, low water 
activity and salt accumulation may dominate. For tropical acid soils, mineral dissolution and 
sorption processes may be more important than coating permeability. A robust SRF/CRF therefore 
requires an application-specific validation envelope rather than a universal release specification.

8. Matching Nutrient Release with Crop Demand 
8.1. Crop-demand dynamics 
The most important performance concept in this review is synchronization. Crop demand is 
dynamic: early vegetative demand can be low, uptake may accelerate sharply during biomass 
accumulation, and demand later declines or shifts among nutrients. A fertiliz er that releases very 
slowly can therefore be as poorly synchronized as one that releases too rapidly. Recent work 
explicitly linking controlled-release N supply to plant-soil synchrony, nutrient remobilization, and 
oilseed-rape uptake supports moving beyo nd simple release -duration targets (Ma et al., 2026; 
Moon et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2026).

Crop-specific fertilizer design should therefore use phenology, rooting depth, expected soil

9. Agronomic Performance and Nutrient-Use Efficiency 
9.1. Meta-analytic evidence 
 
Agronomic performance is the decisive test of release -control technology. Meta -analyses 
across rice, cotton, maize, and broader crop datasets generally report positive average effects of 
controlled-release N on yield and NUE, but they also identify substan tial heterogeneity. Soil, 
climate, crop, coating type, application rate, and the proportion of CRF in blended programs act 
as moderators, which is why average treatment effects should not be interpreted as guaranteed 
field benefits (Jiang et al., 2022; Liu, Zhang, et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2024; Qiao et 
al., 2025; Tian et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022; Zhang, Wang, et al., 2026; Zhang 
et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2020). These meta-analytic conclusions are strongest for N-based systems; 
the smaller and more heterogeneous P and K evidence bases should not be assumed to have the 
same effect size, mechanism maturity, or transferability, and micronutrient systems remain 
insufficient for broad cross-nutrient inference.

9.2. Long-term field evidence 
Long-term and field-scale studies are particularly informative. Controlled-release K has been 
associated with changes in K availability, nutrient uptake, and maize ecosystem properties over 
multiple years; controlled-release N studies report effects on yield stability, soil quality, microbial 
functions, and greenhouse-gas behavior; and field studies with struvite show that lower-solubility 
P sources can maintain crop production while changing loss risk (Guo et al., 2026; Li et al., 2020; 
Liu, Chen, et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). These findings 
reinforce that fertilizer value accumulates through both current-season crop response and residual 
soil effects. 
9.3. Yield, uptake, and NUE are not equivalent 
The relationships among release rate, NUE, and yield are not equivalent. A slower release can 
increase NUE without increasing yield if the crop is already near its yield ceiling; it can increase 
yield without proportionally improving recovery efficiency if soil nutrient supply is high; and it 
can fail on both metrics when release is delayed beyond periods of active uptake. Field studies in 
rice, peanut, sunflower, soybean, wheat, and other crops demonstrate the need to report multiple 
efficiency metrics rather than yield alone (Chen et al., 2020; Geng et al., 2026; Qu et al., 2025; 
Ren et al., 2025; Ren et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2026).

10. Environmental Performance and Sustainability Trade-Offs 
10.1. Nutrient-loss mitigation 
Environmental performance is often cited as a major advantage of SRFs and CRFs because 
lower early nutrient concentrations can reduce leaching, volatilization, and runoff. Meta -analysis 
and field studies support reductions in reactive-N losses under many conditions, but the direction 
and magnitude of response vary with soil, climate, formulation, and management (Jiang et al., 
2022; Lü et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2021; Yang 
et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). The environmental case is therefore strongest 
when losses are measured directly rather than inferred from slower release.

12. Critical Gaps and Research Priorities 
12.1. Release and mechanism gaps 
The first research gap is a release -characterization gap. A large fraction of formulations are 
still evaluated primarily in water, despite evidence that placement, temperature, soil texture, ionic 
composition, and mineral reactions can materially alter release (El Messbahi et al., 2026; Li et al., 
2024; Ransom et al., 2020; Roy et al., 2023; Swify et al., 2023; Teles et al., 2020). Standardized 
protocols should therefore include both a reference aqueous test and at least one soil -relevant test 
matrix. 
The second gap is mechanistic. Empirical equations remain useful descriptors, but 
mechanistic interpretation should be supported by changes in coating morphology, matrix 
Received: 16/08/2026 - Accepted: 15/09/2026 
Vol: 01.05 
DOI: 10.66104/jmfjme46 
Pages: 1-64

structure, speciation, or mineral phase. Combining release experiments with modeling, 
synchrotron characterization, molecular dynamics, and structural analysis provides a stronger basis 
for mechanism attribution (El Messbahi et al., 2026; Lakshani et al., 2023; Lipin et al., 2023; 
Mendonca Cidreira et al., 2026; Mihok et al., 2020). 
12.2. Scale and duration gaps 
The third gap is scale and duration. Pilot manufacturing, multi-year field trials, and industrial 
validation remain much rarer than material synthesis studies. The most decision-relevant evidence 
includes long -term nutrient cycling, soil -quality response, multi-season crop response, product 
breakage, and manufacturing reproducibility (Bonetti et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2024; Li et al., 2020; 
Liu, Chen, et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang, Chen, et 
al., 2026). 
12.3. Economic and sustainability gaps 
 
The fourth gap is economic. Cost is commonly discussed qualitatively, but few studies report 
cost per kilogram of nutrient effectively absorbed, cost per unit of yield gain, or the value of 
avoided nutrient loss. Future techno-economic analysis should integrate coating yield, energy, raw 
materials, application frequency, product damage, field efficacy, and residual soil value. The same 
principle applies to circular materials: waste-derived feedstocks are not automatically low-cost if 
purification, activation, or process complexity is high (Ghumman et al., 2023; Hamidi et al., 2022; 
Hu et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2026; Su et al., 2024). Techno -economic analysis (TEA) should report 
transparent functional metrics such as cost per kilogram of nutrient delivered, cost per kilogram of 
nutrient effectively absorbed, cost per unit of yield gain, coating/material cost per tonne of product, 
energy demand, manufacturing yield, and sensitivity to throughput and raw-material price. When

possible, avoided application events, avoided nutrient losses, residual soil value, and end -of-life

The fifth gap is the lack of standardization in sustainability claims. Biobased coatings, 
biodegradable polymers, hydrogels, mineral matrices, and recovered -nutrient fertilizers require 
distinct end-of-life metrics. Persistence, fragmentation, ecotoxicity, greenhouse -gas effects, and 
nutrient-loss reduction should be reported in compatible functional units so that environmental

2024; Jo et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025; Pereira, 2026; Tian et al., 2026; Tian et al., 2024).

nutrient-delivery synchronization. That change would align formulation science with crop

12.5. Limitations of the Review 
This review has several boundary conditions that should temper interpretation. First, it is a 
curated critical integrative review rather than a prospectively registered systematic review, and 
raw initial -hit, screening, and exclusion counts were not logged in real time. Second, Google 
Scholar provides broad interdisciplinary coverage but has variable indexing transparency, and the 
final corpus is predominantly English -language. Third, publication bias may favor successful 
formulations, while negative pilot-scale or industrial results are often proprietary or unpublished. 
Fourth, publication growth during 2025 –2026 means that the evidence base is evolving rapidly. 
Fifth, terminology and test protocols remain inconsistent across studies, particularly for relea se 
duration, biodegradability, NUE, and environmental endpoints. Sixth, the evidence base is 
nutrient-imbalanced: N is strongly represented, P is moderate, K/mineral systems are more 
heterogeneous, and micronutrient evidence is limited. Seventh, geographic concentration and 
climate/soil specificity constrain direct transfer of some field results. Finally, the review was 
screened and synthesized by a single author; Supplementary File S1 and the explicit evidence 
hierarchy are provided to improve auditability , but independent replication of the selection and 
coding process would further strengthen confidence.

13. Conclusions and Outlook 
13.1. Conditions for real advantage 
Slow- and controlled-release fertilizers are most valuable when release control resolves a real 
temporal mismatch between nutrient supply and crop demand without introducing an offsetting 
environmental or economic burden. The evidence does not support a universal ranking that makes 
the slowest formulation best. Performance depends on nutrient chemistry, release mechanism, soil 
and climate, crop phenology, placement, management, product integrity, and the feasibility of 
manufacturing the formulation consistently. 
13.2. Nutrient-specific implications 
 
For nitrogen, the evidence base is strongest, and the clearest case arises where early losses are 
large and sustained supply can replace or simplify split applications. For phosphorus, the evidence 
is moderately developed and value depends on maintaining p lant availability while reducing 
fixation or loss. For potassium, controlled soluble salts and slowly reactive mineral sources require 
fundamentally different evaluation criteria, and evidence remains more heterogeneous. Broad 
conclusions for micronutrients are not yet justified. Across nutrients, multi -season field evidence 
is more informative than aqueous release tests because it captures crop uptake, residual fertility, 
soil interactions, weather variability, and management effects. 
13.3. Outlook 
The central conclusion of this review is therefore performance-first: the optimum fertilizer is 
not the formulation with the slowest nutrient release, but the formulation whose nutrient -delivery 
profile most closely matches crop demand under realistic soil and climatic conditions while 
minimizing environmental and economic burdens. Future progress will depend on integrating
