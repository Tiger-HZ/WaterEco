Abstract
Dry matter percentage (DM) is a critical physiological trait in sugar beet, functioning 
not only as a quantitative measure but also as an integrative marker of carbon 
assimilation, assimilate partitioning, and ionic regulation. We hypothesized that 
variation in DM across genotypes arises from differences in photosynthetic efficiency, 
sucrose allocation to storage roots, and mineral ion homeostasis. To test this, we 
evaluated 66 sugar beet cultivars across 47 field trials (2019–2024) and assessed the 
relationships between DM and key agronomic, quality, and physicochemical traits. 
DM ranged from 15.8 to 27.1%, reflecting substantial genotypic diversity together 
with interannual environmental variation. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis 
revealed strong positive associations between DM and sucrose content ( r = 0.88), 
white sugar content ( r = 0.87), and extraction coefficient of sugar ( r = 0.78), whereas 
moderate positive correlations were observed with sugar yield ( r = 0.34) and white 
sugar yield ( r = 0.54). Conversely, DM was negatively correlated with root yield ( r = 
− 0.22), sodium ( r = − 0.75), nitrogen ( r = − 0.31), and molasses sugar ( r = − 0.71). 
Multivariate regression confirmed that DM significantly improves quality-related traits 
while reducing impurity-related traits, with no significant influence on potassium or 
alkalinity. Year-specific correlation analyses further demonstrated that the positive 
associations of dry matter with sugar content, white sugar content, and extractable 
sugar coefficient, together with its negative association with sodium concentration, 
remained consistent across growing seasons, whereas relationships with yield-related 
traits were more variable. These findings support the role of DM as a mechanistic 
marker of source-sink dynamics and ion regulation in sugar beet physiology. By 
linking DM to underlying physiological processes, this study provides a framework 
for selecting genotypes with enhanced sucrose accumulation and extractable sugar 
quality. DM thus emerges as a reliable indicator for breeding programs aiming to 
improve sugar quality and processing efficiency while maintaining acceptable field 
performance.

 1 Introduction
Sugar beet ( Beta vulgaris L.) is a fundamental industrial crop whose economic value 
depends on both root biomass and extractable sucrose. Among root quality traits, dry 
matter percentage is of particular importance because it reflects the non-water fraction

of biomass, largely composed of sugars, and strongly influences sugar yield and process -
ing quality. Dry matter content integrates physiological processes including photosyn -
thetic carbon gain, assimilate partitioning into storage roots, and regulation of mineral 
ion uptake. While correlations between dry matter and sucrose concentration have long 
been reported, the physiological mechanisms underlying these associations remain 
poorly understood [1].
Carbon assimilation and source–sink dynamics play a central role in dry matter for -
mation. High- dry matter genotypes may exhibit greater activity of sucrose-phosphate 
synthase, invertases, or sucrose synthase, which enhance sucrose loading and storage in 
root vacuoles [ 2]. Similarly, efficient phloem transport and assimilate unloading to root 
parenchyma promote dry matter accumulation. Ion balance is also critical: excess Na + 
and NO3
− not only reduce sucrose purity but also alter osmotic status, thereby diluting 
dry matter content [3]. Thus, variation in dry matter across genotypes may reflect funda-
mental physiological differences in carbon metabolism and ionic regulation.
The accumulation of dry matter and sucrose is also influenced by environmental and 
agronomic factors such as growth duration, climatic conditions, irrigation, and nutri -
ent management. Drought and salinity stress, for instance, can increase dry matter by 
reducing water content, but simultaneously decrease juice volume and processing qual -
ity. Adaptations such as increased root-to-shoot ratios under drought [ 4–6] highlight 
the dual role of dry matter in stress tolerance and product quality. Improved irrigation 
and balanced nitrogen application significantly enhance dry matter, sucrose content, and 
yield [7]. Long growth duration and optimal planting density further promote dry matter 
and sucrose accumulation, while continuous monoculture negatively impacts photosyn -
thetic capacity and dry matter partitioning [8].
Genotypic variation also plays a major role. Although total dry matter production 
may be similar among cultivars, differences in allocation between root and shoot tissues 
account for much of the observed variation [ 9]. Breeding strategies aimed at improv -
ing root-to-shoot partitioning or enhancing sink strength are therefore promising 
approaches for increasing dry matter and sugar yield. Recent genomic studies have con -
firmed extensive genetic diversity underlying dry matter and sucrose-related traits, with 
complex marker-trait associations emphasizing the multifactorial control of these traits 
[10].
Despite extensive breeding for yield and sucrose concentration, relatively few stud -
ies have addressed dry matter as an integrative physiological trait. Most reports have 
remained descriptive, focusing on correlations rather than mechanisms. We hypothesize 
that variation in dry matter reflects differences in carbon partitioning efficiency and ion 
regulation, and that this integrative trait can predict sugar yield and purity. The objec -
tives of this study were therefore to: (i) quantify variation in dry matter across cultivars 
and years, (ii) assess associations between dry matter and agronomic/quality traits, and 
(iii) interpret these associations within a physiological framework to identify mechanis -
tic drivers of sucrose accumulation and root quality.

2 Materials and methods
This study utilized data obtained from 47 field trials conducted between 2019 and 2024 
at the Motahari Research Station in Karaj, Iran. The trials involved 66 registered mono -
germ diploid sugar beet cultivars (Supplementary Table S1). The physicochemical prop-
erties of the experimental soil during multi-year field trials are presented in Table 1.
Monthly meteorological data, including relative humidity, maximum temperature, 
minimum temperature, and temperature range are provided in Supplementary Table S2.
In autumn, agricultural operations including plowing, fertilizer application, and sup -
plementary tillage for seedbed preparation (shallow plowing, disking, and harrowing) 
were performed. Seeds were sown each year in a randomized complete block design 
with four replications in late April. Each experimental plot consisted of three rows, 
with intra-row spacing of 20 cm and inter-row spacing of 50 cm. Agronomic practices, 
including fertilizer application, irrigation, planting depth, and crop management, were 
kept as uniform as possible across years to minimize management-related variation. Pest 
control involved chemical pesticides, while weed management was conducted manu -
ally and mechanically. During the harvest stage, roots from each plot were harvested, 
weighed, and homogenized into pulp samples. These samples were then transferred to 
specialized trays and stored at −20 °C for one day to one week before being sent to the 
Sugar Technology Laboratory at the Sugar Beet Seed Institute for qualitative analysis. 
The qualitative evaluation of pulp samples was performed using an automated beet labo-
ratory system (Betalyser, Anton Paar, Germany), adhering to ICUMSA standard proto -
cols [11].

Models were fitted using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method in PROC MIXED of 
SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). The significance of the fixed effect of 
dry matter was evaluated using Type III tests of fixed effects, with statistical significance 
declared at P < 0.05. Model adequacy was assessed using Akaike’s Information Crite -
rion (AIC), corrected AIC (AICC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and graphical 
examination of residuals, including residual-versus-fitted plots, normal Q–Q plots, and 
histograms with fitted normal density curves.
Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated using the pooled multi-year 
dataset to quantify associations between dry matter and agronomic and technological 
traits. In addition, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated separately for each 
growing season to evaluate the consistency of trait relationships under different annual 
environmental conditions. Correlation heatmaps were generated in R (version 4.5.1). 
Monthly meteorological summaries used to characterize seasonal environmental varia -
tion are presented in Supplementary Table S2, and the year-specific Pearson correlation 
matrices are provided in Supplementary Table S3.
All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, 
Cary, NC, USA). Additional analyses and visualizations were performed in R to explore 
relationships among variables. Residual diagnostics including marginal and conditional 
residual plots, Q–Q plots, and histograms with overlaid normal density curves were 
used to verify assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity for each trait [13].
3 Results
The analysis of dry matter percentage across a diverse set of sugar beet genotypes 
revealed a considerable range, varying from 15.8 to 27.1%. This wide variation reflects 
substantial genetic diversity as well as the sensitivity of dry matter formation to environ -
mental and management factors. The mean dry matter percentage was approximately 
21.7%, consistent with earlier reports, and highlights the pivotal role of dry matter in 
determining sugar yield, processing quality, and tolerance to environmental stresses. 
Such variability presents an opportunity for targeted breeding programs aimed at select-
ing superior genotypes with enhanced dry matter traits.
Meteorological conditions varied among years (Supplementary Table S2). Annual pre-
cipitation ranged from 0.94 mm day −1 in 2022 to 1.75 mm day −1 in 2019, while maxi -
mum summer temperatures consistently exceeded 36  °C. Relative humidity showed 
considerable seasonal fluctuations, declining during summer months and increasing 
during autumn and winter.
Root yield also showed large variation, ranging from 49.7 to 140.8 t ha −1, with an aver-
age of 91.5 t ha −1, comparable to commercial production levels. The mean sugar con -
tent was 15.2%, while white sugar content averaged 13.4%. Mineral nutrients were highly 
variable: sodium (Na) ranged from 0.7 to 11.9 meq.100 g−1, potassium (K) from 2.2 to 7.2 
meq.100 g−1, and nitrogen (N) from 0.3 to 7.1 meq.100 g −1. This variability likely reflects 
differences in soil characteristics, environmental conditions, and fertilization practices. 
Importantly, such fluctuations directly affect plant physiology, as excess Na+ may induce

salinity stress, elevated N may alter assimilate partitioning toward amino compounds, 
and variable K levels influence osmotic adjustment and sugar translocation.
3.1 Correlation analysis
Spearman’s rank correlation revealed significant associations between dry matter and 
key agronomic and quality traits at the 1% probability level (Table  2). A weak negative 
correlation was observed between dry matter and root yield ( r = − 0.22), suggesting a 
tendency for increased dry matter to coincide with reduced fresh biomass; a trade-off 
between yield and quality frequently reported in sugar beet. Conversely, dry matter 
exhibited significant positive correlations with sugar yield ( r = 0.34), white sugar yield 
(r = 0.54), sucrose content ( r = 0.88), and white sugar content ( r = 0.87). These strong 
coefficients emphasize that genotypes with higher dry matter not only possess denser 
roots but also show superior sucrose accumulation and recoverable sugar.
The extraction coefficient of sugar also correlated strongly with dry matter ( r = 0.78), 
supporting the hypothesis that higher dry matter enhances the efficiency of sucrose 
recovery during processing. Mineral composition showed contrasting patterns: Na ( r 
= − 0.75) and N ( r = − 0.31) were negatively associated with dry matter, implying that 
higher- dry matter roots maintain tighter control over ion uptake or compartmentaliza -
tion, leading to fewer impurities and improved quality. Potassium ( r = − 0.05) and alka -
linity (r = − 0.09) showed weak, nonsignificant correlations, suggesting limited influence 
on dry matter variability. Molasses sugar content displayed a strong negative correla -
tion with dry matter ( r = − 0.71), indicating that higher dry matter reduces by-products 
that hinder sugar crystallization. To evaluate whether these relationships were stable 
under contrasting environmental conditions, Pearson correlation coefficients between 
dry matter and the measured traits were calculated separately for each experimental 
year (Supplementary Table S3). Despite considerable interannual variation in weather 
conditions (Supplementary Table S2), the positive associations of dry matter with sugar 
content, white sugar content, and extraction coefficient of sugar remained consistently 
strong across all six years. Likewise, dry matter maintained a stable negative relationship 
with sodium concentration and, in most years, with molasses sugar content. In contrast, 
correlations between dry matter and root yield, sugar yield, and white sugar yield var -
ied substantially among years, indicating a stronger environmental influence on these 
productivity-related traits than on the technological quality attributes.
The Pearson correlation heatmap revealed clear clusters of strongly associated traits in 
sugar beet roots. A pronounced positive correlation was detected among SC, WSC, and 
ECS ( r > 0.9), indicating that these quality-related traits are tightly interlinked (Fig.  1). 
Similarly, Sy and Wsy were highly correlated with each other (r = 0.95). These strong pos-
itive linkages suggest that improvement in one of these traits is likely to be accompanied

predictive models for DM. The mixed-effects regression model revealed that DM 
exerted a significant negative influence on root yield and sodium content, confirming

4 Discussion
Our study investigates the physiological basis of dry matter variation in sugar beet by 
integrating large-scale phenotypic data with agronomic and quality traits. We hypoth -
esize that dry matter is not merely a descriptive agronomic parameter but a mechanistic 
integrator of carbon assimilation, assimilate partitioning, and ion regulation, governed 
by complex physiological and molecular processes. Although all experiments were con -
ducted at the same location, interannual variation in rainfall, air temperature, and rela -
tive humidity was evident. Such seasonal differences may partly explain the observed 
variability among years and should be considered when interpreting the relationships 
between dry matter and quality traits. Nevertheless, because the objective of the pres -
ent study was to investigate overall associations across a large multi-year dataset rather 
than to quantify genotype × environment interaction, the analyses were performed using 
pooled data.
The observed strong positive correlations between dry matter and sucrose content, 
white sugar yield, and extraction coefficient of sugar suggest that high-dry matter gen -
otypes allocate a greater proportion of photosynthates toward carbohydrate storage in 
roots. This allocation likely involves enhanced phloem loading and unloading mecha -
nisms, mediated by sucrose transporter proteins, which regulate source-to-sink carbon 
flux [14–15]. Such genotypes may exhibit increased activity or expression of these trans-
porters, facilitating sucrose accumulation and contributing to improved sugar yield and 
quality.

Conversely, the significant negative correlations between dry matter and sodium and 
nitrogen contents point toward active ion homeostasis mechanisms in high-dry matter 
genotypes. Efficient exclusion or compartmentalization of Na + through ion transport -
ers family members may reduce ion toxicity and maintain osmotic balance [ 16–17]. 
Similarly, lower nitrogen content suggests a metabolic shift favoring carbon allocation to 
sucrose synthesis over amino acid biosynthesis, reflecting alterations in carbon-nitrogen 
metabolism that optimize sugar accumulation. These physiological adjustments likely 
confer enhanced tolerance to environmental stresses and improved extract purity.
While these correlations align with prior agronomic observations, our study advances 
the field by providing a comprehensive, multi-environment dataset supporting dry mat -
ter as a robust integrative trait linking carbon and ion dynamics in sugar beet roots. 
This insight opens new avenues for molecular and biochemical investigations, such as 
transcriptomic profiling of sucrose transporter and ion channel genes, enzyme activity 
assays related to carbon metabolism, and ionomic analyses to unravel genotype-specific 
regulatory networks.
The year-specific correlation analysis further supported the robustness of the over -
all relationships observed in the pooled dataset (Supplementary Table S3). Despite the 
interannual differences in temperature, and relative humidity (Supplementary Table 
S2), the positive associations of dry matter with sugar content, white sugar content, and 
extraction coefficient of sugar remained consistently strong across all growing seasons. 
Likewise, the negative relationship between dry matter and sodium concentration was 
maintained throughout the study period. By contrast, the relationships between dry 
matter and yield-related traits (root yield, sugar yield, and white sugar yield) showed 
greater year-to-year variation, indicating that these traits are more sensitive to seasonal 
environmental conditions than technological quality traits. These findings suggest that 
dry matter is more closely linked to the physiological processes governing sugar accu -
mulation and impurity regulation than to biomass production per se.
Furthermore, understanding the genetic architecture underlying dry matter varia -
tion will be critical for marker-assisted breeding. Recent genomic studies [ 10] indicate 
substantial genetic diversity associated with dry matter and sucrose traits, suggesting 
that targeted selection for transporter and ion regulation genes could enhance breeding 
efficiency.
In conclusion, dry matter content emerges as a physiologically meaningful marker 
reflecting the interplay between carbon assimilation and ion regulation, rather than a 
simple correlative trait. Incorporating mechanistic insights into dry matter variation will 
facilitate development of superior sugar beet cultivars with optimized yield, quality, and 
stress resilience.
