Multi‑method estimation of groundwater 
recharge in the Fetam catchment, Upper Blue 
Nile Basin, Ethiopia
Mekonen Gedewon Aynalem1*, Sileshi Mamo2, Melkamu Meseret2, Muralitharan Jothimani3* and 
Habtamu Wondem4 
Abstract 
Reliable groundwater resources are essential in catchments characterized by strong 
rainfall seasonality and intermittent surface-water availability, such as the Fetam 
catchment in the Upper Blue Nile Basin, Ethiopia. In this study, groundwater recharge 
was quantified using a multi-method approach to reduce method-dependent uncer-
tainty and provide a defensible recharge range for sustainable groundwater manage-
ment. Five independent methods were applied: soil moisture balance, baseflow sepa-
ration of streamflow, chloride mass balance, water-table fluctuation, and catchment 
water balance. Hydro-meteorological data, groundwater-level measurements, hydro-
chemical analyses, land use, soil, and hydrogeological information were integrated 
and spatially aggregated using Thiessen polygon weighting. Mean annual recharge 
estimates varied from 112.7 ± 2.2 to 244.9 ± 12.4 mm/year, corresponding to approxi-
mately 6.5–14% of mean annual rainfall. Higher recharge values were obtained 
from baseflow separation and water-table fluctuation methods, reflecting strong 
seasonal groundwater responses. In contrast, soil-moisture balance and water-balance 
methods yielded lower estimates because they explicitly accounted for evapotranspi-
ration and runoff losses. The chloride mass balance method produced intermediate 
values and represents longer-term effective recharge. Recharge was found to be highly 
seasonal and spatially heterogeneous, with highland areas acting as major recharge 
zones and lowland areas functioning mainly as discharge zones. Overall, convergence 
among multiple methods suggests a realistic recharge range of approximately 165–
220.1 mm/year for the Fetam catchment. The study demonstrates that multi-method 
recharge assessment is essential in data-limited, seasonally dry basins and provides 
a scientific basis for sustainable groundwater development and drought-resilient water 
management.

1 Introduction
Water is essential for human health, livelihoods, and economic development, yet reli -
able water supplies remain challenging in regions with seasonal rainfall and intermittent 
surface-water flow [28]. Groundwater is therefore a dependable source for domestic use 
and dry-season water availability [32]. Sustainable groundwater development depends 
on understanding recharge, which controls the amount of water that can be abstracted 
without long-term declines in groundwater levels, spring discharge, or river baseflow 
[61, 67]. Recharge is dynamic and influenced by climate variability, land use, hydrologic 
connectivity, and episodic rainfall, with climate change potentially altering recharge and 
groundwater storage [26, 36, 45, 57]. Recharge also controls groundwater–surface-water 
interactions and can be modified by human interventions such as artificial recharge [24, 
61, 67]. Recent evidence further shows that recharge can change rapidly following wet -
ting events and climatic anomalies, emphasizing the need to understand its spatial and 
temporal variability [39].
The Fetam catchment in the Upper Blue Nile system has strong hydroclimatic season -
ality, with most rainfall concentrated in a short wet season and many tributaries becom -
ing intermittent or ephemeral during the prolonged dry season [4, 34, 40]. Under these 
conditions, groundwater is strategically important for sustaining dry-season baseflow, 
springs, shallow wells, and water supply during droughts [49, 52, 64]. However, with -
out a quantified and uncertainty-bounded recharge baseline, groundwater development 
cannot be reliably assessed against replenishment, limiting yield assessment, well-field 
planning, drought management, and protection of groundwater-dependent ecosystems 
[52].
Recharge is a non-stationary flux controlled by rainfall, evapotranspiration, land use, 
soil properties, aquifer lithology, topography, and stream–aquifer connectivity [16, 36, 
46, 52]. These interacting controls create strong spatial and temporal variability, mak -
ing single-method estimates less reliable and supporting the use of multiple complemen-
tary approaches [8, 49, 52]. Ethiopian studies show substantial differences in recharge 
magnitude and seasonality across hydrogeological settings, including the Sana River 
Catchment using WetSpass [13], Wabe River Catchment using a GIS-based water bal -
ance model [56], and Maze–Zenti Catchment using WetSpass-M [43]. Method selection 
also affects recharge estimates, as demonstrated by soil-moisture balance, baseflow sep -
aration, WetSpass-M, and chloride mass balance approaches [6, 59, 62]. Multi-method 
studies further show that agreement among independent approaches provides greater 
confidence than reliance on a single estimator [48].
The overall objective of this research is to quantify groundwater recharge in the Fetam 
catchment and provide a defensible recharge range that can guide sustainable ground -
water utilization. Specifically, the study aims to (1) estimate long-term recharge using 
chloride mass balance, (2) quantify recharge from groundwater-level variations using the 
water-table fluctuation method, (3) compute recharge using soil-moisture balance analy-
sis, (4) estimate recharge using baseflow separation of streamflow records, and (5) apply 
a catchment water-budget approach and synthesize results across methods to character-
ize uncertainty and identify consistent recharge estimates for management applications.
The catchment is characterized by strong rainfall seasonality and intermittent tribu -
tary flows, which reduce the reliability of surface-water sources and elevate groundwater

as a primary dry-season supply. In such hydroclimatic settings, groundwater functions 
as a strategic buffer against drought; however, without quantified recharge, abstraction 
cannot be evaluated against replenishment, and any expansion of groundwater devel -
opment risks progressive water-level decline, reduced spring discharge, and diminished 
dry-season baseflow that supports riverine ecosystems and downstream users [34, 52].
Recharge estimation is necessary because recharge is spatially heterogeneous and 
temporally variable, with its magnitude governed by interacting climatic, land-surface, 
and subsurface controls. These characteristics limit the transferability of recharge values 
between basins and mean that extrapolating from other Ethiopian catchments—or rely -
ing on generalized national estimates—can be unreliable for local groundwater manage -
ment and planning [16, 18, 21, 52]. Recent studies in Ethiopia demonstrate that recharge 
rates and seasonal dynamics vary considerably across basins and hydrogeologic envi -
ronments, and that different methods—including GIS-based distributed water balance 
models and WetSpass/WetSpass-M applications—may yield different recharge estimates 
even within the same region [6, 13, 43, 56, 59, 62], underscoring the need for catchment-
specific assessment and uncertainty evaluation. Moreover, groundwater planning in a 
data-limited basin is particularly vulnerable to method-dependent bias,a single-method 
estimate can produce misleading results, whereas a multi-method framework improves 
robustness by constraining recharge using independent lines of evidence from the unsat-
urated zone, saturated zone, and streamflow response [25, 48, 52].
2 Materials and methods
2.1 Description of the study area
The Fetam River catchment, as shown in Fig.  1, is located within the Awi and West 
Gojam administrative zones of the Amhara National Regional State in the Upper Blue 
Nile River Basin, northwest Ethiopia. The catchment covers approximately 793.8 km2. 
The river originates from the Sekela Ridge, situated to the north of the Blue Nile source. 
The Fetam River flows southward for about 70  km before joining the Blue Nile River 
at the border between the Amhara and Oromia Regional States. Both the Abay (Blue 
Nile) River and the Fetam River originate near a locality called Gesh Abay, diverge in 
direction, and later converge at Boko Tabo in Ethiopia. The catchment includes numer -
ous perennial and ephemeral streams, as well as springs, which provide continuous 
contributions to the Fetam River. The total population within the catchment, based on 
data collected from each district, exceeds 250,000, with a population density of approxi -
mately 504.8 people per km2. The majority of the inhabitants, around 80% of the labor 
force, are engaged in agricultural activities. Geographically, the catchment is bordered 
on the east by the Birr River catchment, which originates from the Choke Shield and 
nearby mountains, including Satma, Mizan, and Jambera. The Blue Nile Gorge encircles 
the area from the north to the south. The primary water source appears to be the Choke 
Shield, where deep subsurface water circulation forms depression springs at Gish Abay 
and flows northward, as shown by the pink-bordered area in Fig.  1. The Fetam River 
is genetically related to the Birr, Blue Nile, and Zingini Rivers, with the Zingini River 
located on the western side of the Fetam catchment.

2.2 Elevation, soil, and lithology
Topographically, the catchment is characterized by relatively gentle relief, with slopes 
of 0–5% covering about 70% of the basin, 5–10% covering approximately 10%, and the 
remaining ~ 20% mainly within the 10–30% range, indicating that much of the area is 
flat to gently undulating. Land use and land cover are dominated by cultivated land, 
bushland, forest, grassland, and rural and urban settlements, consistent with patterns 
reported for Ethiopian agricultural catchments [55]. Cereal crops and grasses together 
account for more than 85% of the catchment area, underscoring the dominance of rain -
fed agriculture. Major crops grown in the area include teff, shimbra, maize, potato, and 
onion, which are widely cultivated across Ethiopian highlands and contribute signifi -
cantly to national food production [7, 30, 60].
Clay-rich units dominate the soils of the catchment, with Eutric Vertisols and Humic 
Nitisols covering more than 95% of the area, while Chromic Luvisols and Dystric Lep -
tosols occupy smaller proportions. Eutric Vertisols are widely distributed on plains 
and plateaus and are characterized by a clayey texture and imperfect drainage, which

2.3 Data used
This study integrated hydro-meteorological, hydrological, groundwater, hydrochemi -
cal, and catchment-parameter datasets to estimate groundwater recharge using multiple 
methods. Meteorological data from stations within and around the catchment included 
rainfall, temperature, wind speed, relative humidity, sunshine hours, evaporation, pres -
sure, and radiation, with reference evapotranspiration calculated using CROPWAT. 
Approximately 15 years of streamflow data were used for baseflow separation, while 
runoff from ungauged areas was estimated using a runoff coefficient of 0.5. Monthly 
groundwater levels from about 15 hand-dug wells were used for the WTF method, with 
specific yield values adopted from the literature. Chloride concentrations in rainfall, 
groundwater, and surface water were analyzed for the CMB method. Soil, vegetation, 
root depth, and plant-available water parameters were also compiled to support soil-
moisture-balance recharge estimation.
2.4 Methods (Groundwater recharge estimation)
2.4.1 Overview of the recharge‑estimation framework
Groundwater recharge was quantified using multiple complementary approaches: (i) 
soil moisture balance (daily and monthly time steps), (ii) base flow separation (includ -
ing recession-curve analysis), (iii) chloride mass balance (CMB), (iv) catchment water 
balance, and (v) water table fluctuation (WTF). Applying several methods in parallel 
reduces single-method bias and provides consistency checks across climate, streamflow, 
hydrochemical, and groundwater-level evidence. Hydro-meteorological inputs (pre -
cipitation, temperature, wind speed, relative humidity, and sunshine hours) were com -
piled as multi-year daily and monthly datasets from local meteorological stations, while 
multi-year daily river discharge was obtained for the main river gauge in the catchment. 
Point-based recharge estimates were spatially aggregated to the catchment scale using 
Thiessen polygon weighting.

3.2 Actual evapotranspiration
Actual evapotranspiration (AET) is a key controlling factor on groundwater recharge in 
water-balance and soil-moisture-balance approaches because it represents the most sig -
nificant “loss” term competing with infiltration and deep percolation; when AET is high, 
a greater fraction of rainfall is consumed by atmospheric demand and less remains avail-
able for effective recharge [46, 52]. The monthly AET pattern in Fig.  6 shows a strong 
seasonal cycle, with low values in January–February, a rapid rise through March–April, 
and a clear peak in April–May, indicating that evaporative demand and soil–plant water 
use intensify toward the end of the dry season as temperatures and atmospheric demand 
increase while soil moisture is still being depleted [12, 68].

approach the potential (energy-limited) rate; under these conditions, the partitioning 
of rainfall shifts toward sustaining evapotranspiration and restoring soil storage before 
substantial deep percolation can occur [15, 46, 68]. In contrast, during the dry season

3.3 Land use land cover
Land use change is a major factor for water resources distribution [66].
Five major land use/land cover (LULC) classes characterize the area: built-up land, 
grassland, cereal cropland, forest, and bare land, as mapped in Fig.  8 using a Sentinel 
image processed through unsupervised classification and then refined through field veri-
fication to ensure that the spectral classes correspond to real ground conditions [55, 60]. 
This workflow is appropriate for heterogeneous agricultural landscapes because unsu -
pervised clustering first captures dominant spectral groupings in the imagery, while field 
checks reduce class confusion that commonly occurs between spectrally similar covers 
such as cropped fields, dry grasslands, and sparsely vegetated bare surfaces [30, 60].

3.4 Groundwater flow system
The general flow direction of subsurface water in the catchment is north to south in the 
northern part, following the surface water flow direction, and north-west to south-east 
in the southern part, perpendicular to surface water due to concealed tectonic struc -
tures. Variation in hydraulic conductivity among different rocks can also cause local 
deviations in groundwater flow. The contour lines shown in the figure above are parallel 
and spaced at uniform intervals; this indicates that the topography is nearly uniform and

3.6 Recharge and discharge area
3.6.1 Recharge area
The primary source of groundwater in the area is precipitation in the Highlands. In addi-
tion, the area’s surrounding perennial rivers recharged local groundwater. Many of the 
hand-dug and shallow wells along these rivers are replenished by such conditions in 
the dry season. The corresponding Rivers recharge locally deposited alluvium along the 
Fetam River. The highly elevated areas, such as Kurb Mountain, Chitty, and locally devel-
oped ridges, are possible sources of recharge to the groundwater of the adjacent plateau 
and foothill aquifers. A deep water table and no springs characterize recharge areas in 
the highland of the catchment, which has the highest elevation. The groundwater con -
tour lines in the figure above are closely spaced in the highland and widely spaced in the 
lowland. On the other hand, the closely spaced contour lines indicate the occurrence of 
the water table at a deeper depth. In contrast, the widely spaced contour lines indicate 
the discharge area, indicating the presence of the water table near the surface.

3.6.2 Groundwater discharge area
The discharge zone of the catchment is saturated in the southern portion of the area, 
which is characterized by flat lands. In this area, the appearance of spring and the occur-
rence of water near the surface. Many springs emerge from the weathered and fractured 
parts of topographic breaks, intermountain depressions, deep gorges, and high cliffs. It 
is observed that the discharge of this groundwater is due to differences in hydraulic con -
ductivity resulting from lithological variation along the cliff, variation in the extent of 
weathering of the same rock, and variation in the degree and extent of fracturing. Gener-
ally, low-lying areas can be considered discharge areas. Most water chemistry data from 
drilling organizations indicate that higher TDS concentrations are found in the low-lying 
parts of the catchment, and the lowest TDS is found in the recharge area around Tillili.

3.9 Base flow separation
Baseflow separation indicates that streamflow in the catchment is composed of a large, 
quick-flow (direct runoff) component and a smaller, persistent baseflow component. The 
reported peak streamflow reaches 8.55 m3/s, of which 6.37 m3/s is attributed to direct 
runoff and 2.18 m3/s to baseflow. When these components are converted to catchment 
water depths, the annual runoff amounts to 712.4 mm/year, while the baseflow-derived 
recharge estimate is 244.9 ± 12.24  mm/year with errors derived from the measuring 
device. Interpreting recharge from baseflow is consistent with the widely used assump -
tion that, over annual or multi-seasonal time scales, sustained baseflow largely repre -
sents delayed drainage from groundwater storage to the river network, making it a 
practical proxy for diffuse groundwater recharge in gauged basins [22, 49, 52]. Using the 
rainfall reference from the Thiessen polygon method, the recharge estimate corresponds 
to 14.2% of mean monthly rainfall, while the baseflow component represents 35.9% of 
the river’s annual discharge,in contrast, total runoff accounts for 68.8% of annual dis -
charge, emphasizing that storm runoff dominates the flow regime but that groundwa -
ter contributions remain substantial and hydrologically important [9, 49, 50]. Baseflow 
separation parameters, particularly the filter coefficient (α), number of filter passes, and 
initial baseflow conditions, affect the estimated recharge rate by controlling the separa -
tion of groundwater-derived baseflow from total streamflow.
3.10 Recession method
In the Fetam catchment, the hydrograph recession analysis identifies 101 recession peri -
ods, with the recession sequence beginning around the 344th day of the record when 
discharge is 1.59 m3/s and ending when discharge declines to 0.296 m3/s. Using reces -
sion-period accounting, the corresponding water volumes are reported as 623,529.4 m3 
at the start and 1,392,564.7 m3 at the end of the recession, and the total baseflow contri -
bution to the river during the recession is estimated at 6,230,964.7m3. This type of analy-
sis is consistent with standard baseflow/recession approaches, where the falling limb of 
streamflow is used to isolate the delayed groundwater contribution (baseflow) and quan-
tify the groundwater-to-river flux over defined recession intervals [9, 49, 50, 52].

reflect the combined influence of geological, hydrogeological, and hydrometeorological

3.12 Chloride mass balance
Groundwater recharge was also estimated using the chloride mass balance (CMB) 
approach, computing recharge separately with monthly and daily precipitation datasets 
and averaging the two estimates. Using the monthly dataset, the CMB-derived annual 
recharge is 210.9 mm/year, equivalent to 12.2% of the mean annual precipitation. Using 
the daily dataset, the estimated annual recharge is 226.1  mm/year, which corresponds 
to 12.3% of the annual rainfall. The final CMB recharge adopted for the catchment is 
the average of the two computations, giving 218.5 ± 8.2  mm/year resulted from a 4% 
uncertainty derived from measuring dive of chloride and rainfall recording gauges. The 
small difference between the monthly- and daily-based estimates reflects differences 
in precipitation time-step aggregation, while keeping the same conceptual mass-bal -
ance basis [15, 46, 52]. In the study area, the chloride concentrations in precipitation 
and runoff are reported to be very similar, with precipitation slightly higher than runoff, 
which increases the net chloride input term and results in a relatively higher recharge 
estimate compared to situations where runoff chloride is higher [1, 10, 11, 64]. The esti-
mated recharge using the chloride mass balance (CMB) method is influenced by spatial 
and temporal variability in chloride concentrations in precipitation and groundwater, as 
these variations directly affect the calculated recharge rate.
3.13 Water balance
The water-balance method estimated groundwater recharge at 187 ± 3.7 mm/year using 
the Thornthwaite–Mather approach and 143 ± 2.8  mm/year using the Excel-based 
water-balance calculation, corresponding to 13.6% and 5.2% of the mean annual pre -
cipitation, respectively. The relatively low recharge estimates are partly related to the 
structure of the water-balance calculation, in which the available water is preferentially 
allocated to satisfy actual evapotranspiration (AET), thereby constraining the residual 
water available for recharge, which is the main component for groundwater recharge 
assessment. The 146 mm/year difference between the Thornthwaite–Mather (956 mm/
year) and Penman–Monteith (810 mm/year) AET estimates reflects methodological dif -
ferences and underlying assumptions, including the use of temperature-based empirical 
relationships in Thornthwaite–Mather versus a physically based approach incorporat -
ing radiation, air temperature, humidity, and wind speed in Penman–Monteith this my 
makes different recharge uncertainity in water balnce approach and take average of the 
two is more preferable.
The difference should be interpreted as an expression of methodological uncertainty 
in AET estimation and its subsequent influence on the water balance and recharge. An 
additional source of uncertainty is the hydrogeological boundary condition of the Fetam 
Catchment. The catchment contains an interconnected weathered and fractured aqui -
fer system that can transmit groundwater across the surface-water divide; therefore, the

system should be considered partly open from a groundwater-flow perspective. Conse -
quently, the water-balance approach may not explicitly account for lateral groundwater 
inflow or outflow across the catchment boundary. If groundwater leaves the catchment 
through the fractured aquifer, the calculated residual recharge may be underestimated, 
whereas groundwater inflow from adjacent areas may produce an apparent overes -
timation of local recharge. Thus, the estimated 143–187  mm/year should be regarded 
as a catchment-scale recharge estimate with uncertainty associated with groundwater 
boundary fluxes and seasonal water-storage fluctuations. Nevertheless, the magnitude of 
recharge is considered reasonable because it is broadly consistent with the independent 
recharge estimates obtained from the other methods applied in this study.
4 Discussion
Recharge in the Fetam catchment was estimated using five independent methods to 
reduce method-specific bias and to constrain uncertainty by combining evidence from 
the unsaturated zone, saturated zone, tracer mass balance, streamflow response (base -
flow separation), and basin-scale accounting (water balance). This approach is consist -
ent with watershed-scale studies showing that convergence across methods provides a 
more defensible basis for management than reliance on a single estimator [27, 35, 51]. 
Across the applied methods, annual recharge ranges from 112.7 to 244.9  mm/year, 
indicating a moderate spread among estimators within the same hydroclimatic setting. 
The largest estimates were obtained from baseflow separation (244.9 mm/year,10.7% of 
annual rainfall) and water table fluctuation (220.1  mm/year; 13.8%), followed by chlo -
ride mass balance (218.6 mm/year; 12.3%). The lower estimates were produced by daily 
SMB (170.5  mm/year; 9.3%), water balance (165  mm/year; 9.5%), and monthly SMB 
(112.7 mm/year; 6.5%).
Given the strong rainfall seasonality and the dry-season dependence on groundwater, 
defining a realistic recharge range is essential for evaluating whether abstraction can be 
sustained without progressive water-level decline and loss of dry-season baseflow sup -
port. Baseflow separation produced the highest recharge estimate [9, 19].
The relatively higher recharge estimates obtained from the WTF and baseflow-sepa -
ration methods should be interpreted as potential upper-bound estimates because both 
methods may capture water contributions beyond direct diffuse aquifer recharge. In the 
WTF method, observed groundwater-level fluctuations may reflect delayed interflow, 
seasonal release of shallow subsurface storage, and localized focused recharge, whereas 
hydrograph separation may attribute contributions from bank storage, wetlands, tem -
porary saturated zones, and shallow groundwater responses to baseflow. These pro -
cesses can increase the estimated recharge when the separated baseflow is assumed to 
represent groundwater discharge from the aquifer. Furthermore, recharge in the Fetam 
Catchment may occur through both diffuse recharge, resulting from relatively wide -
spread infiltration of precipitation through the soil and unsaturated zone, and focused 
recharge, where water is concentrated and rapidly infiltrates through stream channels, 
topographic depressions, fractures, and other preferential pathways. Therefore, the 
recharge estimates from WTF and baseflow separation represent integrated hydrologi -
cal responses and may include both diffuse and focused recharge components, as well as 
delayed subsurface storage contributions. This distinction is important when comparing 
Page 24 of 29Aynalem et al. Discover Environment (2026) 4:544 
recharge estimates obtained from different methods and interpreting their associated 
uncertainty [10, 50].
The WTF method yielded a recharge estimate close to the baseflow result. The study 
interpretation is that WTF is a strong option here because it is based on direct ground -
water-level observations and is less affected by evapotranspiration once recharge reaches 
the aquifer [23, 44]. Seasonal groundwater-level rise during the rainy season and decline 
during the dry season create a pronounced amplitude interpreted as recharge input to 
storage [29, 45]. The study also notes rapid aquifer response around Tillili, supporting 
the plausibility of strong seasonal recharge signals in shallow groundwater [37]. CMB 
produced an intermediate-high recharge estimate (~ 218.5 mm/year), which is reason -
able as it reflects longer-term effective recharge integrated through solute concentration 
rather than event-scale soil accounting [6, 14].
In this catchment, the chloride concentrations in precipitation and runoff are reported 
to be similar, but slightly higher in precipitation, which increases net chloride input and 
yields a relatively higher recharge estimate than would occur if runoff chloride were 
higher. The reliability of the CMB estimate depends strongly on assumptions of con -
servative chloride behavior and limited additional chloride sources, and the study notes 
that limited temporal sampling constrains confidence in year-to-year representativeness 
[1, 38]. The daily SMB estimate (170.5 mm/year) is lower than WTF/baseflow/CMB, as 
expected, because SMB explicitly accounts for large evaporative losses and runoff before 
deep percolation [11, 20]. The study highlights that the SMB implementation is highly 
sensitive to key inputs—especially runoff coefficient, initial soil moisture, and PAW—
and that the catchment’s combination of cultivated land, flat terrain, relatively high run -
off coefficient, and high AET tends to reduce modeled recharge [65].
The monthly SMB estimate is the lowest among all methods [2, 52]. This is attributed 
in the study to differences introduced by time-step aggregation and lower annual pre -
cipitation totals when using monthly data compared to daily accounting—effects that 
can dampen short wet-season pulses responsible for episodic drainage in clay-rich soils 
[15, 46, 52]. The water balance method yielded 165 mm/year, broadly consistent with the 
daily SMB magnitude [2, 12]. However, the study emphasizes that the catchment behaves 
as an open groundwater system, implying potential subsurface inflow/outflow across 
boundaries in deeper fractured aquifers [16, 21]. Because these fluxes are not quantified, 
the method assumes groundwater inflow equals groundwater outflow, which can bias 
recharge either upward or downward depending on the true net boundary exchange [11, 
16, 52].
The recharge pattern is consistent with the catchment’s groundwater flow system 
and physiography [16]. Groundwater generally flows from higher-head recharge areas 
toward lower-head discharge areas [16], and the study identifies Tillili and the sur -
rounding highlands as major recharge areas, while parts of Bure and Shendi function 
as discharge areas with relatively smaller annual recharge. Mapped groundwater flow 
directions and the association of discharge areas with shallow water tables and spring 
occurrence support this conceptualization, whereas recharge areas show deeper water 
tables and fewer springs [16, 21]. Seasonality also strongly controls recharge because the 
wet season produces the rainfall pulses needed to overcome soil moisture deficit and 
initiate deep percolation, while the dry season is dominated by soil moisture depletion 
Page 25 of 29
Aynalem et al. Discover Environment (2026) 4:544 
 
and groundwater recession [21, 37, 45, 46]. AET peaks during the late dry season (April–
May), and PET and AET tend to converge during the wet season when water availability 
is high—conditions that influence the timing of effective recharge and the partitioning of 
rainfall losses [12, 31, 68].
From a management perspective, the multi-method results define a realistic recharge 
envelope for planning abstraction in a catchment where groundwater is a primary dry-
season buffer. The higher estimates (baseflow/WTF) likely represent upper bounds 
influenced by streamflow separation behavior and strong seasonal water-level rise, while 
SMB and water balance provide lower-to-mid estimates that explicitly reflect evapora -
tive demand and runoff losses. Key uncertainties arise from: (i) limited temporal ground-
water-level monitoring for WTF, (ii) limited temporal chloride sampling for CMB, (iii) 
sensitivity of SMB to runoff coefficients and PAW in clay-rich soils, and (iv) unquantified 
groundwater boundary fluxes for water balance in an open fractured-aquifer system.
4.1 Groundwater management implications
The estimated groundwater recharge provides an important basis for evaluating the 
groundwater development potential of the Fetam Catchment. Based on the estimated 
annual groundwater recharge of 149.71 billion L/year, a preliminary groundwater allo -
cation framework was established to balance groundwater development with the main -
tenance of natural groundwater-system functions. In this framework, 60% of the total 
recharge, equivalent to 89.83 billion L/year, was allocated to natural discharge and 
environmental sustainability, including groundwater storage, spring discharge, stream 
baseflow, wetlands, and groundwater-dependent ecosystems. The remaining 40% of the 
total recharge, equivalent to 59.88 billion L/year, was considered potentially available for 
abstraction.
The potentially abstractable groundwater was further allocated among the major 
water-use sectors. Of the 59.88 billion L/year potentially available for abstraction, 70% 
(41.92 billion L/year) was allocated to irrigation, 23% (13.77 billion L/year) to domes -
tic water supply, and 7% (4.19 billion L/year) to industrial water use. This framework 
provides a preliminary water-allocation scenario rather than a fixed estimate of sustain -
able yield, because the actual amount of groundwater that can be abstracted depends on 
aquifer properties, spatial and temporal recharge variability, groundwater storage, natu -
ral discharge, and groundwater–surface-water interactions.
Under the current population scenario, 250,000 households with an average household 
size of five persons correspond to a total population of 1.25 million people. A domes -
tic water requirement of 25 L/capita/day, the current annual domestic water demand is 
estimated to be 11.41 billion L/year. This demand represents approximately 82.8% of the 
preliminary domestic allocation of 13.77 billion L/year, leaving approximately 2.36 bil -
lion L/year within the allocated domestic-water component.
Considering population growth, an annual growth rate of 2.6% was applied to project 
the population over a 10-year period. The projected population increases from approxi -
mately 1.25 million to 1.62 million people, resulting in an estimated domestic water 
demand of approximately 14.75 billion L/year, and the water requirement remains con -
stant at 25 L/capita/day. This projected demand would exceed the current domestic allo-
cation of 13.77 billion L/year by approximately 0.98 billion L/year. The result indicates 
Page 26 of 29Aynalem et al. Discover Environment (2026) 4:544 
that population growth alone could place increasing pressure on the domestic ground -
water allocation during the planning period.
The irrigation allocation of approximately 41.92 billion L/year and industrial alloca -
tion of approximately 4.19 billion L/year represent preliminary planning limits for these 
sectors. However, the actual abstraction should be controlled according to groundwater-
level trends, well yields, aquifer storage and transmissivity, spring discharge, streamflow, 
and other indicators of groundwater-system response. In addition, climate change and 
land-use/land-cover (LULC) change may alter groundwater recharge and water demand 
through changes in precipitation, evapotranspiration, infiltration, runoff, vegetation 
cover, agricultural expansion, and urbanization. Therefore, future groundwater-resource 
management should incorporate population-growth, climate-change, and LULC scenar-
ios to reassess recharge, natural discharge requirements, and sectoral abstraction lim -
its. The proposed 60% allocation for natural discharge and environmental sustainability 
and 40% for abstraction, divided into 70% irrigation, 23% domestic, and 7% industrial 
use, should consequently be regarded as a preliminary precautionary management sce -
nario that can be refined through long-term groundwater monitoring and integrated 
groundwater-flow and recharge modelling. Future hydrogeological work should include 
Numerical groundwater flow modelling, Isotope-based recharge assessment, Long-
term groundwater hydrograph analyses, and integrated groundwater–surface-water 
modelling.
5 Conclusions
Groundwater recharge in the Fetam Catchment is strongly seasonal and spatially het -
erogeneous. Five independent methods produced annual recharge estimates of 113–
245  mm/year, equivalent to approximately 6.5–14% of mean annual rainfall. Baseflow 
separation and water-table fluctuation yielded the highest values and are interpreted as 
possible upper bounds because they may include delayed interflow, bank storage, shal -
low groundwater responses, and focused recharge. Soil-moisture balance and catchment 
water-balance methods produced lower estimates because they explicitly account for 
evapotranspiration and runoff losses, while chloride mass balance provided an interme -
diate estimate representative of longer-term effective recharge. The combined evidence 
supports a defensible management range of approximately 165–220 mm/year. Recharge 
is concentrated in the Tillili uplands and other higher-elevation fractured and perme -
able zones, whereas lower areas around Bure and Shendi mainly function as discharge 
zones connected to springs and river baseflow. The multi-method comparison demon -
strates that recharge should be managed as a range rather than as a single deterministic 
value. Groundwater development should therefore be linked to continued monitoring of 
groundwater levels, spring discharge, streamflow, and hydrochemistry, with abstraction 
adjusted as new data refine recharge estimates and aquifer-response thresholds.
