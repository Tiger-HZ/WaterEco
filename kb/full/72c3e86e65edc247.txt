Performance -First Critical Review of Biochar and Hydrochar for 
Nutrient Recovery, Controlled Release, Agronomic Function, and 
Life-Cycle Climate Performance 
Antonio Clareti Pereira, PhD 
PhD in Chemical Engineering 
Federal University of Minas Gerais (UFMG)) 
Belo Horizonte, Minas Gerais, Brazil 
E-mail: claretipereira@gmail.com

Abstract 
Biochar and hydrochar are increasingly described as fertilizers, nutrient-recovery media, 
soil-carbon amendments, and climate -mitigation products, although these functions 
require different evidence. This structured critical narrative review reappraises a 122 -
source analytic corpus spanning 2019 –2026 and applies the Dual -Performance 
Boundary Framework for Carbonaceous Fertilizers (DPB -CF), which independently 
tests agronomic fertilizer function and net life -cycle climate benefit. The corpus is 
strongly recent, with 67 sources (54.9%) published in 2024 –2026 and 50 (41.0%) in 
2025–2026. Evidence is synthesized from feedstock chemistry through pyrolysis, co -
pyrolysis, and hydrothermal carbonization; nutrient partitioning and speciation; nutrient 
capture; controlled release; soil –plant response; product engineering; safety; techno -
economics; life-cycle assessment; regulation; and scale-up. A three -generation author-
developed framework is operationalized: amendment char, nutrient -enriched char, and 
engineered nutrient-recovery fertilizer. Phosphorus and potassium are often retained or 
concentrated in carbonaceous solids, whereas nitrogen is more vulnerable to 
volatilization during dry thermochemical conversion or partitioning into hydrothermal 
process water. Mg - and Ca -based engineering can increase phosphate capture and 
tailor release, but adsorption capacity or total nutrient content does not establish 
fertilizer value without evidence of release, plant uptake, nutrient -use efficiency, and 
crop response. Multi-year studies demonstrate both positive and neutral outcomes; soil 
dependence is therefore treated as part of product performance rather than 
experimental noise. For climate claims, carbon permanence must be integrated with 
feedstock counterfactuals, process energy, transport, co -products, avoided waste 
treatment, field greenhouse -gas emissions, and end -use assumptions. Evidence 
classification was performed as a single -reviewer interpretive synthesis and was not 
tested for inter -rater reliability . The central development priority is a mass -balanced 
pathway from nutrient recovery to crop uptake, coupled to safe product specifications, 
multi-season validation, and auditable techno-economic and life-cycle boundaries. 
Keywords: biochar; hydrochar; nutrient recovery; controlled-release fertilizer; nutrient 
use efficiency; life-cycle assessment.

1. Introduction: separating material identity from demonstrated function 
Biochar and hydrochar occupy an unusually broad conceptual space. They are 
described as soil amendments, sorbents, recovered -nutrient carriers, slow -release 
fertilizers, waste-management products, and carbon -removal materials. The problem is 
that these functions are not interchangeable. A material may increase soil carbon while 
contributing little plant nutrition, or it may deliver nutrients efficiently while having an 
uncertain or even unfavorable net greenhouse -gas balance. Meta -analyses and long -
term field studies likewise show that crop response is conditional rather than universal: 
benefits depend on nutrient status, soil pH, texture, climate, crop, and the chemistry 
inherited from the feedstock and conversion process (Bekchanova et al., 2024; Cheng 
et al., 2025; Han et al., 2023; Hosseini Bai et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2021). 
This review adopts a performance -first definition of fertilizer function within the Dual -
Performance Boundary Framework for Carbonaceous Fertilizers (DPB -CF). A 
carbonaceous product is treated as a fertilizer only when it supplies one or more 
nutrients in agronomically relevant quantities, at rates and chemical forms plants can 
access without unacceptable losses or toxicity. In operational terms, a nutrient -loaded 
carbonaceous sorbent becomes a fertilizer only when recovered nutrients are released 
into plant -accessible pools under relevant soil and crop conditions. This boundary 
distinguishes total nutrient concentration from plant availability and places nutrient -use 
efficiency (NUE), phosphorus -use efficiency (PUE), potassium -use efficiency (KUE), 
crop uptake, and yield closer to the decision endpoint. Recent biochar -based fertilizer 
studies support this shift by showing that nutrient enrichment and formulation can 
improve efficiency, but only when nutrient release is matched to crop demand (Al -Amin 
et al., 2026; Banik et al., 2023; Dissanayake et al., 2026; Preza Fontes et al., 2024; 
Zhao et al., 2022). 
A second, independent boundary is required for climate claims. Biochar carbon can 
persist for years to decades or longer, but permanence is only one term in a net life -
cycle greenhouse-gas balance. Feedstock counterfactuals, drying, heat and electricity,

reactor efficiency, transport, co-products, avoided waste treatment, changes in soil N₂O, 
CH₄ and CO ₂ emissions, and the durability of stored carbon all influence the result. 
Avoided emissions and durable carbon removal should not be treated as 
interchangeable accounting categories; both may improve climate performance, but 
they arise from different mechanisms and require transparent system boundaries 
(Chiaramonti et al., 2024; European Commission, 2026; Li et al., 2024; Sanei et al., 
2025; Sanei et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2024). 
The framework is grounded in durable agronomic and soil-chemical principles as well as 
recent biochar/hydrochar evidence. Controlled-release value depends on 
synchronization between nutrient release and crop demand (Shaviv, 2001); nitrogen-
use efficiency depends on retaining N in plant-accessible pools while limiting loss 
pathways (Fageria & Baligar, 2005); phosphate bioavailability reflects 
adsorption/desorption and precipitation/dissolution equilibria in the rhizosphere 
(Hinsinger, 2001); and carbon persistence depends on material chemistry and 
environmental context rather than on the label ‘biochar’ alone (Lehmann et al., 2006; 
Spokas, 2010). These five foundational sources serve as contextual anchors and are 
not included in the 122-source 2019–2026 analytic corpus. 
External validity is deliberately bounded. Claims should be transferred only among 
products with comparable feedstock chemistry, conversion conditions, nutrient form, soil 
context, contaminant profile, and product-engineering status. Soil dependence is 
therefore part of the technology-performance boundary, not a nuisance variable to be 
averaged away.

recovery, enrichment techniques, slow/controlled -release formulations, hydrothermal

single label such as “sustainable biochar fertilizer.” This review therefore links nutrient

2. Review methodology and critical evidence appraisal 
2.1. Review design, corpus, and eligibility 
This article is a structured critical narrative review , not a systematic review or meta -
analysis. The retained analytic corpus contains 122 unique references spanning 2019 –
2026. The scientific discovery window ran from 1 January 2020 to 4 September 2026; 
we retained the 2019 EU fertilizing -products regulation as an official regulatory anchor. 
We did not preserve the earliest Google Scholar discovery -stage retrieval totals,

duplicate-removal counts, or article -by-article exclusion log . No retrospective PRISMA -
style numbers are therefore invented. Instead, the revision makes the eligibility, coding, 
deduplication, update, and appraisal logic explicit and provides a source -level evidence 
matrix (Supplementary Table S1) and a transparent source -selection flow with 
unavailable historical counts identified as ‘not retained’ (Supplementary Table S2). 
Sources were eligible when they directly informed at least one decision boundary: 
feedstock/process chemistry, nutrient fate or recovery, fertilizer formulation and release, 
soil–plant performance, contaminant behavior, product engineering, techno -economics, 
life-cycle performance, regulation, scale -up, or data -driven prediction. Peer -reviewed 
experimental studies, field studies, meta -analyses/reviews, pilot or industrial 
demonstrations, TEA/LCA studies, and official regulatory/certification documents were 
retained. Studies unrelated to nutrient delivery or agricultural use were excluded, and 
adsorption studies were retained only when nutrient recovery, reuse, release, or 
fertilizer relevance could be connected to the review question. 
The corpus is strongly recent: 67 of 122 sources (54.9%) were published in 2024 –
2026 and 50 (41.0%) in 2025 –2026. Retrospective source -level coding in 
Supplementary Table S1 classifies the 122 records as 73 experimental/applied studies, 
11 reviews, nine meta -analyses, 10 machine -learning/predictive studies, seven field 
studies, five TEA/LCA studies, four regulatory/certification documents, and three 
pilot/industrial demonstrations. These mutually exclusive source -type labels are used 
only to map the primary evidence role; individual studies may contribute to more than 
one substantive section. The small pilot/industrial subset remains an important finding 
because it shows how much of the literature is still below full product-scale validation.

2.3. Evidence coding, source-level auditability, and reviewer reliability 
A single reviewer/author performed all evidence classification and interpretive coding. 
We did not perform independent duplicate coding, a calibration sample, or an external 
classification audit; therefore, we do not report Cohen’s kappa, Krippendorff’s alpha, or 
percentage agreement. The classification is therefore explicitly treated as single-
reviewer interpretive synthesis rather than reliability-tested coding. Supplementary 
Table S1 exposes the source-level coding fields used for auditability, including source 
type, year, feedstock, pathway, nutrient focus, fertilizer function, carbon assessment, 
scale, soil/crop context, safety, TEA/LCA, regulatory relevance, principal limitation, and 
synthesis contribution.

3. Feedstocks, thermochemical pathways, and process severity 
3.1. Feedstock chemistry as the upper performance boundary 
Feedstock chemistry establishes the upper boundary of what thermochemical 
conversion can achieve. Reviews of feedstock and process effects consistently identify 
ash content, inherent mineral phases, N concentration, P speciation, alkali and alkaline -
earth metals, and contaminant burden as first -order controls (Ippolito et al., 2020; Jalali 
et al., 2025; Qian et al., 2026). 
Crop residues typically provide high organic -carbon fractions and comparatively 
modest nutrient concentrations, although K can be abundant in soluble or weakly bound 
forms. Agricultural -residue pyrolysis can also generate PAHs depending on 
temperature, vapor residence, and condensation conditions, so clean feedstock does 
not eliminate process -derived organic contaminants (Bilias et al., 2023; Devi & Dalai, 
2023; Leng et al., 2022). 
Manures combine carbon with substantial N, P, K, Ca, and Mg. Chicken -manure 
systems can produce sustained P and K release through mineral co -addition, while 
comparative work shows that manure -derived biochar can recover and retain nutrients 
more effectively than hydrochar or compost under some conditions (Leite et al., 2023; 
Piash et al., 2022; Piash et al., 2025). 
Sewage sludge is attractive because it contains recoverable P and organic carbon, 
but it also faces the strongest safety and regulatory constraints. Field and extraction 
studies show that pyrolysis temperature strongly alters P extractability and that heavy -
metal behavior can remain acceptable only within feedstock - and process-specific limits 
(Chagas, de Figueiredo, & Paz -Ferreiro, 2021; Chagas, de Figueiredo, da Silva, & Paz -
Ferreiro, 2021; de Figueiredo et al., 2021; Hakeem et al., 2026; Mourão et al., 2026; 
Wei et al., 2024). 
Digestate is a wet, nutrient -rich residue that can be carbonized directly by HTC or 
dried for pyrolysis. Digestate -derived biochars have been used in slow -release fertilizer

Food waste is heterogeneous, moisture -rich, and often mineral -rich. The

3.2. Pyrolysis, co-pyrolysis, and hydrothermal carbonization 
Pyrolysis converts dry biomass in limited oxygen, progressively increasing 
aromaticity, ash concentration, pH, and often surface area as temperature rises. 
Process conditions must therefore be selected against a product specification rather 
than a generic “high -quality biochar” target (Ippolito et al., 2020; Jalali et al., 2025; 
Malghani et al., 2025). 
Co-pyrolysis deliberately combines feedstocks or additives so that one component 
compensates for another. Microwave co -pyrolysis and coating strategies illustrate how 
reaction and formulation steps can be integrated to create graded or sustained release 
rather than merely concentrating ash (An et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021; Piash et al., 
2022). 
HTC operates in hot compressed water and is particularly suited to wet biomass. 
Reviews and experimental studies consistently show that N and P fate is highly 
sensitive to HTC severity and feedstock chemistry (Alhnidi et al., 2020; Aragón -Briceño 
et al., 2021; Belete et al., 2021; Ge et al., 2025). 
Biochar and hydrochar should therefore not be compared as interchangeable solids. 
Figure 2 places these differences within a single nutrient -partition framework (Khosravi 
et al., 2024; Piash et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025).

important when comparing HTC with dry pyrolysis because HTC intentionally preserves 
an aqueous phase that can carry substantial nutrient value.

carbonaceous solids.

3.3. Process severity, atmosphere, mineralogy, and ash 
Thermochemical conditions translate feedstock composition into fertilizer -relevant 
properties. Machine -learning studies have begun to capture these interactions, but 
mechanistic interpretation remains essential because the same condition can improve 
one attribute while damaging another —for example, increasing carbon stability while 
lowering N retention or P solubility (Chen et al., 2023; Leng et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022; 
Liu et al., 2025). 
Temperature is the most consistently reported control variable. Direct 33P 
approaches and extractant comparisons demonstrate that higher total P after pyrolysis 
does not guarantee higher plant-available P (de Figueiredo et al., 2021; Lustosa Filho et 
al., 2025; Malghani et al., 2025). 
Residence time determines how completely secondary devolatilization, 
aromatization, mineral reactions, and gas –solid interactions proceed. Because 
residence time is frequently confounded with temperature and reactor type, fertilizer -
oriented optimization should report severity in relation to nutrient mass balances rather 
than relying only on final char composition (Ippolito et al., 2020; Jalali et al., 2025). 
Heating rate affects volatile release, pore development, secondary char formation, 
and the thermal history mineral phases experience . From a fertilizer perspective, treat 
heating rate as a controllable variable only when you measure the associated nutrient

Vol: 01.08 
DOI: 10.66104/hk5sra49 
Pages: 1-58 
 
 
retention, contaminant profile, and product handling properties (Devi & Dalai, 2023; 
Leng et al., 2022). 
The gas atmosphere changes redox conditions and heat transfer and can alter 
mineral phases, surface functionality, and gaseous N species. Scale -up therefore 
requires confirmation that the product formed under laboratory inert gas remains 
chemically and physically comparable under commercial reactor hydrodynamics 
(Hakeem et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2024). 
HTC severity combines temperature and time in an aqueous reaction environment. 
Data-driven models can predict hydrochar properties and phosphorus fate, but the 
fertilizer optimum must include both phases and any downstream concentration or 
recovery step (Chen et al., 2023; Ge et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025). 
Mineralogy governs nutrient immobilization and release. Mineral -engineered 
biochars show that these interactions can be used deliberately, but they can also 
unintentionally reduce nutrient availability if the resulting phases dissolve too slowly 
under field pH conditions (Leite et al., 2023; Sornhiran et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2025). 
Ash is not simply an inert residue. Product specifications should therefore report 
nutrient and contaminant mass per unit product together with plant -availability metrics 
(Albert et al., 2021; Bilias et al., 2023; Ippolito et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2024). 
Practically, process optimization cannot be reduced to char yield or surface area. A 
fertilizer-oriented objective function should maximize recoverable nutrient value and 
safe agronomic performance subject to carbon, energy, and manufacturability 
constraints.

4.2. Phosphorus: retention does not equal plant availability 
Phosphorus usually remains in the condensed phase during pyrolysis but undergoes 
substantial speciation change. Depending on soil pH and mineralogy, this can either 
provide gradual release or create recalcitrant P that contributes little to crop nutrition (de 
Figueiredo et al., 2021; Kooij et al., 2024; Lustosa Filho et al., 2025; Tesfaye et al., 
2021). 
The organic -to-inorganic P transition is especially important because conventional 
fertilizer analyses may interpret increased mineral P as improved value even when the 
resulting mineral is sparingly soluble. Meta -analysis confirms that pyrolysis can shift the 
relative agronomic performance of sewage -sludge biochar compared with conventional 
P fertilizers (Lustosa Filho et al., 2025; Malghani et al., 2025). 
4.3. Potassium, Ca, Mg, Si, and micronutrients 
Potassium is frequently enriched because it is retained in the ash while carbon and 
volatiles are lost. Field evidence confirms that biochar can increase soil K availability 
and maize K uptake, but the response is soil -specific (Bao et al., 2024; Bilias et al., 
2023; Fachini et al., 2022; Xiu et al., 2023). 
Ca and Mg have dual roles as nutrients and as mineral modifiers that control 
phosphate capture and dissolution. Mineralogy, not bulk Ca/Mg/Si content, therefore 
determines fertilizer function (Leite et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2025; Sornhiran et al., 
2022; Zhao et al., 2025). 
Micronutrients such as Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, and B may concentrate, but the same 
mechanism applies to potentially toxic elements. High pH and mineral stabilization can 
reduce metal mobility, yet total concentrations increase on a dry -mass basis, and long -
term field monitoring remains essential for sludge -derived products (Albert et al., 2021; 
Chagas et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2024).

5.1. Ammonium and phosphate capture 
Ammonium recovery relies on cation -exchange sites, surface oxygen groups, 
mineral phases, and electrostatic interactions. Engineering with clays or Mg -bearing 
phases can improve simultaneous N and P recovery, while urine -derived systems can 
recover NH ₄⁺-N directly onto sludge biochar for subsequent fertilizer use (Dai et al., 
2020; Shakoor et al., 2021; Xi et al., 2022; Yu, 2022). 
Phosphate adsorption is generally weak on negatively charged pristine carbon 
surfaces unless mineral sites are present. Bentonite-modified and Mg-modified biochars 
have therefore been investigated as P -recovery media with subsequent slow -release 
behavior (An et al., 2020; He et al., 2022; Shin et al., 2020; Shin et al., 2023). 
5.2. Mg-, Ca-, clay-, and mineral-assisted recovery 
Magnesium modification is one of the most developed strategies because Mg can 
both increase phosphate affinity and create agronomically useful Mg –P phases. Yet 
excessive Mg can convert P into phases whose dissolution depends strongly on soil 
acidity, so the optimum is not necessarily the maximum Mg loading (Leite et al., 2023; Li 
et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2021; Xi et al., 2022). 
Calcium modification follows a similar logic but creates different phosphate 
mineralogy. Fertilizer behavior is dominated by dissolution and soil pH rather than 
desorption alone (Liang et al., 2025; Pereira et al., 2025; Pereira & Marçal, 2025). 
Struvite formation links N and P recovery by precipitating MgNH4PO ₄·6H2O. 
Combined sewage-sludge ash, food wastewater, and Mg -biochar systems demonstrate

the feasibility of this route, but product purity, competing ions, and release behavior 
must be validated for agricultural use (Zin & Kim, 2021). 
5.3. Wastewater recovery, direct application, and regeneration 
Wastewater applications broaden the feedstock boundary beyond the char itself. 
Reviews of phosphate and ammonium recovery show the technical potential, while pilot-
scale phosphate recovery on biosolids biochar begins to address the evidence gap 
between batch adsorption and real wastewater operation (Marcińczyk et al., 2022; Pap 
et al., 2026; Pathy et al., 2021; Sagar et al., 2024; Shakoor et al., 2021). 
After nutrient loading, two options exist: regenerate the sorbent and recover a 
separate fertilizer, or apply the loaded carbon directly. The choice should be based on 
whole-process recovery, product specification, and crop response rather than 
adsorption capacity alone (He et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Sagar et al., 2024). 
A useful performance metric for these systems is therefore nutrient recovery to plant 
uptake: the fraction of nutrient removed from the waste stream that ultimately enters 
crop biomass without unacceptable leaching or accumulation. 
For these systems, the most decision -relevant metric is nutrient recovery to plant 
uptake: the fraction of nutrient removed from the source stream that is ultimately 
delivered to crop biomass without unacceptable leaching, toxicity, or residual 
accumulation.

Because thermal conversion can reduce N availability, post-loading is often the most

P-loaded biochars are produced by sorbing phosphate from wastewater or by adding 
P-rich minerals before or after carbonization. Modified biochars can improve P -use

NPK formulations treat the char as a matrix for multiple nutrient species rather than 
a single -nutrient carrier. Data -driven models predicting NPK composition and

Co-pyrolysis enables in-situ synthesis of nutrient-mineral phases. This approach can

Mineral–biochar composites broaden the design space by introducing bentonite,

and regulate release. Their benefit should therefore be expressed per unit nutrient 
delivered, not per unit char produced (An et al., 2020; Shin et al., 2023; Sornhiran et al., 
2022; Xi et al., 2022). 
Nano- and fine -mineral additives can increase reactive surface area and create 
highly selective nutrient -binding sites, but they also raise manufacturing and safety 
questions. Urban biosolids-to-nanobiochar research illustrates the opportunity while also 
highlighting the need for stronger field and risk evidence (Mourão et al., 2026). 
Hybrid smart fertilizers combine chemical loading with coatings, binders, microbial 
functions, or responsive matrices. The engineering challenge is to achieve a release 
profile that responds to soil moisture, pH, and biological activity without sacrificing

7. Soil–plant performance: the decisive fertilizer test 
The soil –plant system is the decisive test of fertilizer function. Evidence across 
meta-analyses and long -term trials shows both positive and neutral outcomes, 
underscoring that biochar-based fertilizer performance is conditional rather than intrinsic 
(Cheng et al., 2025; Han et al., 2023; Hosseini Bai et al., 2022; Jiao et al., 2025; Y. Zhai 
et al., 2026). 
7.1. Soil controls on response 
Biochar commonly increases soil pH when ash and alkaline minerals are abundant. 
Application rate must therefore be calibrated to soil buffering capacity rather than 
transferred directly between soils (Jin et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026). 
Surface oxidation and functional groups can increase effective CEC over time, 
particularly in weathered or sandy soils. The agronomic benefit is strongest when

nutrient retention is a limiting process rather than when soil fertility is already high 
(Knoblauch et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2021). 
Improved water retention can indirectly increase nutrient -use efficiency by 
maintaining root–soil contact and reducing drought stress. It should not be generalized 
to fine -textured soils, where hydrological changes may be small and crop response 
driven mainly by nutrient or pH effects (Han et al., 2023; Knoblauch et al., 2021). 
The microbiome mediates nutrient mineralization, immobilization, P solubilization, 
and N transformations. The resulting response is context -dependent because microbial 
communities, substrate availability, and soil moisture differ strongly among sites (Liu et 
al., 2023; Rong et al., 2023). 
Nutrient availability bridges material chemistry and crop response. Soil -specific 
testing is therefore essential before making broad fertilizer claims (Carneiro et al., 2021; 
Cheng et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025). 
7.2. Uptake, nutrient-use efficiency, and yield 
Crop uptake provides stronger evidence than extractable soil nutrients because it 
integrates root access over time. Localized placement may further improve uptake by 
concentrating nutrient and carbon near roots while reducing the total application rate 
(Bao et al., 2024; Frene et al., 2025; Loginova et al., 2026; Y. L. Zhai et al., 2026). 
Efficiency metrics are particularly useful because they separate crop production from 
nutrient input. PUE and KUE remain less consistently reported and should be 
standardized because they directly test whether the carbon matrix converts recovered 
nutrient into plant biomass (Al -Amin et al., 2026; Banik et al., 2023; Dissanayake et al., 
2026; Mustaffa et al., 2024; Preza Fontes et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2022). 
Yield remains the most policy- and farmer-relevant endpoint, but it can mask nutrient 
inefficiency at high fertilizer rates . Conversely, a seven -year field study found no crop -
growth or yield improvement from biochar in a semi -humid region, providing an

7.3. Context dependence and contradictory field evidence 
Soil dependence is not experimental noise; it is part of the technology. Multi -soil 
experiments and explainable machine learning increasingly identify pH, texture, 
application rate, and baseline fertility as key moderators (Jin et al., 2024; Wang et al., 
2026; Xu et al., 2025; Y. Zhai et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). 
The literature contains both positive and neutral responses, and this heterogeneity 
should be treated as mechanistic information rather than noise. The seven -year neutral-
yield result reported by Cheng et al. (2025), alongside positive nutrient -efficiency and 
crop-response studies, shows why baseline fertility, soil buffering, crop demand, rate, 
placement, and product chemistry must be reported before a fertilizer claim is 
generalized (Al-Amin et al., 2026; Dissanayake et al., 2026; Omidvar et al., 2026; Zhao 
et al., 2022).

8. Carbon permanence and net life-cycle climate performance 
Fertilizer function and climate performance can coexist, but they must be accounted 
for independently. Carbon permanence is evidence about storage durability; it is not, by 
itself, a complete greenhouse-gas balance or a certificate of carbon removal. 
8.1. Stable carbon, priming, and soil organic carbon 
Pyrolysis generally produces more aromatic and persistent carbon than HTC, 
although stability varies with feedstock and temperature. Field recovery of aged biochar 
provides valuable validation of persistence beyond short incubation studies 
(Chiaramonti et al., 2024; Sanei et al., 2025; Sanei et al., 2026). 
Biochar can alter the decomposition of native soil organic matter through positive or 
negative priming. Consequently, adding stable char does not guarantee an equivalent 
increase in net SOC; responses must account for interactions with pre -existing soil 
carbon (Bekchanova et al., 2024). 
SOC response reflects both the carbon added in biochar and changes in native 
SOC. Predictive models now attempt to estimate SOC response from site and product 
properties, though independent validation remains important (Xu et al., 2021; Xu et al., 
2025).

8.2. Field GHGs, energy, transport, and counterfactuals 
N₂O can materially change the climate balance because of its high global -warming 
potency. N ₂O should therefore be measured in field systems where N fertilizers are 
reduced or reformulated rather than assumed from generic biochar effects (L. L. Zhai et 
al., 2026). 
CO₂ emissions arise during feedstock decomposition, thermal conversion, energy 
generation, transport, and soil processes. Carbon -removal claims require the persistent 
fraction of char carbon to exceed upstream and downstream fossil and non -CO₂ 
greenhouse-gas burdens over the chosen accounting horizon (Gamaralalage et al., 
2025; Li et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024). 
Methane effects are most important when the feedstock would otherwise 
decompose anaerobically or when biochar changes emissions from flooded soils or 
waste management. A biochar system based on residue that would otherwise be 
efficiently utilized cannot automatically claim the same avoided -emission benefit 
(Gamaralalage et al., 2025; Qian et al., 2026). 
Transport matters because biochar can have low bulk density and long hauling 
distances. Carbon accounting should include feedstock collection, centralized versus 
distributed processing, product transport, and field application logistics (Furuhashi et al., 
2024; Senadheera et al., 2025). 
Process energy depends on feedstock moisture, reactor integration, heat recovery, 
and co -product use. LCA results are therefore technology -specific, and process 
integration can be as important as carbon yield (Gamaralalage et al., 2025; Li et al., 
2024; Xia et al., 2024). 
8.3. Permanence, avoided emissions, and carbon-removal claims 
Permanence is the fraction of stored carbon expected to remain outside the 
atmosphere over the accounting period. Regulations for certified permanent carbon 
removal are also evolving, increasing the need to connect analytical indicators with

recognized certification methodologies (European Commission, 2026; Sanei et al., 
2025; Sanei et al., 2026). 
A persistent carbon fraction can coexist with an unfavorable life -cycle GHG result if 
processing energy is fossil -intensive, transport is excessive, the char is insufficiently 
stable, process-water treatment is burdensome, or the feedstock counterfactual already 
avoids emissions. Conversely, avoided methane or displaced waste treatment can 
improve the project GHG balance without equaling durable atmospheric carbon 
removal. Report these categories separately before aggregation (Gamaralalage et al.,

9. Contaminants, safety, and environmental risk 
Agricultural use moves carbonaceous materials from a waste -management system 
into soils and food -production chains, making contaminant control a non -negotiable 
gate. Safety assessment must therefore combine product analysis with realistic soil 
exposure and crop uptake (Albert et al., 2021; Coelho et al., 2025; Dong et al., 2025). 
9.1. Potentially toxic elements and metal mobility 
Many mineral-associated metals tend to become concentrated on a dry -mass basis 
as organic matter is removed during pyrolysis, but volatility and phase partitioning are 
element-, species -, temperature -, atmosphere -, and matrix -dependent; the blanket 
assumption that all PTEs are nonvolatile is not defensible. Long -term field work 
indicates that bioavailability can remain limited for some sludge -derived chars, but this 
cannot be generalized across feedstocks or process conditions (Albert et al., 2021; 
Chagas, de Figueiredo, da Silva, & Paz-Ferreiro, 2021; Wei et al., 2024). 
Pyrolysis can stabilize metals through mineral encapsulation, precipitation, and 
changes in surface sorption, often reducing short -term leachability. The appropriate 
claim is therefore reduced mobility under tested conditions rather than permanent 
immobilization (Chagas, de Figueiredo, da Silva, & Paz -Ferreiro, 2021; Dong et al., 
2025).

9.2. PAHs, PFAS, dioxins, and PCBs 
PAHs may form during incomplete pyrolysis or condense from vapors onto char. 
Agricultural products should be produced under controlled conditions that minimize 
condensation of tars and should be tested using standardized PAH methods rather than 
assuming that thermal treatment eliminates organic contaminants (Devi & Dalai, 2023; 
European Biochar Certificate, 2025). 
PFAS have become a critical issue for biosolids -derived biochar. Pilot work and co -
pyrolysis studies are beginning to quantify destruction, transformation, and emission 
pathways, while techno-economic analysis indicates that PFAS treatment can materially 
affect system cost (Hakeem et al., 2026; Keller et al., 2024; Rathnayake et al., 2025; 
Sørmo et al., 2023). 
Dioxins and PCBs require targeted verification when chlorine, catalytic metals, or 
contaminated feedstocks are present. Product certification should therefore adopt 
feedstock risk screening and process -specific contaminant panels appropriate to the 
residue class, with particular attention to sewage sludge and mixed municipal 
feedstocks (Dong et al., 2025; European Biochar Certificate, 2025). 
9.3. Sewage-sludge products, leaching, and ecotoxicity 
Sewage-sludge biochar sits at the intersection of nutrient recovery and contaminant 
risk. Industrial -scale pyrolysis studies and pilot phosphate -recovery work show 
increasing technical maturity, yet agricultural acceptance depends on consistent feed 
quality and regulatory compliance (de Figueiredo et al., 2021; Hakeem et al., 2026; Pap 
et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2024). 
Leaching tests should address both nutrients and contaminants. Multiple extraction 
conditions, soil column tests, and crop uptake are therefore preferable to a single 
standardized leach test when assessing agricultural performance (Chagas, de 
Figueiredo, da Silva, & Paz-Ferreiro, 2021; Coelho et al., 2025; Costa et al., 2026).

Ecotoxicity integrates effects that chemical analysis can miss. Germination, soil -
organism, aquatic, and longer -term plant tests provide an additional safety layer, 
especially for engineered or waste -derived products (Coelho et al., 2025; Dong et al., 
2025). 
Safety should therefore be treated as a gate rather than a co -benefit. A product that 
performs agronomically but fails contaminant, ecotoxicity, or exposure requirements 
should not advance to agricultural commercialization. 
10. Product engineering, commercial quality, and regulatory fit 
A scientifically effective powder is not automatically a commercial fertilizer. Product 
engineering therefore deserves equal status with nutrient chemistry in scale -up 
decisions (Chen et al., 2025; Furuhashi et al., 2024; Grafmüller et al., 2024; 
Senadheera et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). 
10.1. Density, dust, granulation, and mechanical integrity 
Fine biochar powders have low bulk density, create dust, segregate during blending, 
and can be difficult to meter uniformly. Densification is therefore not only a convenience; 
it changes the economic and environmental performance of the product (Furuhashi et 
al., 2024; Senadheera et al., 2025). 
Dust loss reduces the applied nutrient and carbon dose while creating nuisance and 
inhalation concerns. Pelletization, granulation, binders, or slurry -based products can 
reduce dust, but each adds cost and can alter release kinetics (Chen et al., 2025; Rubel 
et al., 2026). 
Low bulk density increases storage and transport volume per unit nutrient. Economic 
comparisons should therefore use cost per kilogram of delivered nutrient and, where 
relevant, per tonne of durable carbon rather than cost per tonne of product (Rubel & 
Wei, 2025; Trollip & Merckel, 2025). 
Granulation can produce fertilizer -sized particles compatible with conventional 
spreaders and can integrate binders and mineral nutrients. Granule engineering should

optimize strength without sealing nutrients too effectively (Chen et al., 2025; Grafmüller 
et al., 2024). 
Pelletization is particularly relevant for manure compost and low -density biochar. In 
biofertilizers, pelletization conditions can also reduce microbial viability, creating a trade-
off between physical quality and biological function (Furuhashi et al., 2024; Rubel et al., 
2026). 
Crushing strength must be high enough for transport and handling but low enough to 
disintegrate when release depends on particle breakdown. Report mechanical testing 
alongside nutrient -release curves because a formulation that performs in intact 
laboratory granules may behave differently after real logistics and field spreading (Chen 
et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). 
10.2. Hygroscopicity, blending, and storage 
Hygroscopic fertilizer cakes deform and change release behavior during storage. 
Packaging and storage studies are rarely reported but become necessary once 
hygroscopic mineral nutrients are incorporated into carbon matrices (Sim et al., 2021; 
Sim et al., 2025). 
Blending biochar with conventional fertilizer is attractive because it uses existing 
nutrient products, but differences in particle size and density can cause segregation 
during transport and spreading. Blending studies should therefore assess physical 
homogeneity after realistic handling, not just initial laboratory mixing (Grafmüller et al., 
2024; Li et al., 2026). 
Storage stability includes moisture uptake, nutrient migration, coating integrity, 
microbial survival, and mechanical durability. Commercial product specifications should 
therefore include shelf -life testing under relevant temperature and humidity conditions 
(Rubel & Wei, 2022; Rubel et al., 2026; Sim et al., 2021). 
The product -engineering literature suggests that the future market will favor 
carbonaceous fertilizers that can be treated as standard fertilizer products—with defined

11.2. Techno-economic assessment and functional units 
TEA converts laboratory formulation into a cost per unit product and, more 
importantly, cost per unit nutrient delivered. Revenue from carbon credits or avoided 
waste treatment can improve economics but should be treated as scenario -dependent 
(Dissanayake et al., 2026; Keller et al., 2024; Rubel & Wei, 2025; Trollip & Merckel, 
2025). 
Drying is a dominant burden for wet residues, whereas HTC trades drying energy for 
pressurized aqueous processing and downstream water treatment. Energy integration 
with pyrolysis gas or local waste heat can materially change both OPEX and LCA, 
making site configuration a first-order design variable (Belete et al., 2021; Gamaralalage 
et al., 2025; Rubel & Wei, 2025). 
Cost per kilogram of nutrient is particularly useful for comparing engineered carbon 
fertilizers with conventional fertilizers. TEA should therefore use effective nutrient 
content rather than total nutrient content whenever possible (Dissanayake et al., 2026; 
Rubel & Wei, 2025; Trollip & Merckel, 2025).

12. Predictive design and priority research gaps 
The next phase of research is moving from descriptive characterization toward 
predictive product design. Machine learning can integrate feedstock composition, 
process severity, material properties, soil conditions, and crop response, but only if 
datasets contain consistent outputs that reflect fertilizer function. Models trained only on 
char yield, surface area, or total NPK risk optimizing variables that are weakly 
connected to crop performance (Chen et al., 2023; Leng et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022; Liu 
et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025). 
12.1. Machine learning as decision support, not a substitute for validation 
Machine-learning models have already predicted hydrochar characteristics, biochar 
yield and composition, P fate, crop yield, and soil organic carbon. Explainable 
approaches are particularly valuable because they can identify threshold effects and 
interactions among pH, application rate, feedstock, and process temperature. The 
strongest use case is decision support that narrows experimental space, not 
replacement of field validation (Chen et al., 2023; Ge et al., 2025; Jiao et al., 2025; Leng 
et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Y. Y. Zhai et al., 2026). 
Minimum reporting standards for machine-learning applications should include, at 
minimum, feedstock composition and provenance; process temperature, residence 
time, heating mode and/or HTC severity; nutrient speciation and analytical method; 
nutrient-loading and release-test method; initial soil pH, texture, CEC, organic carbon, 
salinity and baseline fertility; crop species and growth conditions; trial duration; 
contaminant and safety results where relevant; and field-validation status. Model studies 
should additionally report sample size, missing-data treatment, feature definition, 
train/validation/test splitting, performance metrics, uncertainty, applicability domain, and 
external validation when available. Without these fields, model accuracy can reflect 
data-set regularities while remaining weakly transferable to fertilizer performance.

12.2. Soil-specific and feedstock–process–property models 
Soil-specific recommendations are necessary because the same product can be 
beneficial, neutral, or potentially harmful across contrasting soils. Predictive tools should 
therefore use initial soil pH, texture, CEC, organic carbon, salinity, available N/P/K, 
climate, crop, and placement as input variables. Machine -learning approaches for P 
availability and crop growth demonstrate the feasibility of this direction, but independent 
datasets are still limited (Wang et al., 2026; Y. Zhai et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). 
Feedstock–process–property models should be extended from generic char 
properties to nutrient speciation, release, contaminant behavior, and physical product 
quality. For pyrolysis, key outputs include N retention, P mineralogy, K release, stable 
carbon, and PAHs; for HTC, they should include nutrient partition to process water and 
hydrochar stability. Data augmentation can help sparse datasets but cannot 
compensate for inconsistent analytical definitions (Ge et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Xie 
et al., 2025). 
12.3. Crop-response prediction and evidence balance 
Crop-response models should predict yield and nutrient uptake relative to a defined 
fertilizer control, not absolute yield alone. This requires comparable nutrient doses and 
baseline fertility data. Current models show that crop response can be predicted from 
biochar and soil features, but they must explicitly handle the class imbalance between 
positive and neutral/negative outcomes to avoid optimistic recommendations (Jiao et al., 
2025; Xu et al., 2025; Y. Y. Zhai et al., 2026). 
12.4. Priority research gaps 
Five research gaps are most consequential. First, whole -process nutrient mass 
balances are needed for pyrolysis and especially HTC, including liquid and gas streams. 
Second, nutrient -release tests should be calibrated against plant uptake and multi -
season field response. Third, safety studies must integrate PTEs, PAHs, PFAS, salinity, 
and ecotoxicity for waste -derived products. Fourth, product engineering should be 
reported alongside agronomy so granules, pellets, coatings, and microbial formulations

Vol: 01.08 
DOI: 10.66104/hk5sra49 
Pages: 1-58 
 
 
are evaluated after realistic storage and handling. Fifth, TEA and LCA should use 
functionally relevant units such as cost per kilogram plant -available nutrient and net 
greenhouse-gas balance per hectare or tonne CO ₂-equivalent removed (Costa et al., 
2026; Dong et al., 2025; Keller et al., 2024; Pap et al., 2026; Rubel & Wei, 2025; 
Senadheera et al., 2025; Sim et al., 2025; Trollip & Merckel, 2025). 
A sixth cross-cutting gap follows from the evidence-maturity appraisal: future studies 
should report negative and neutral outcomes with the same detail as positive outcomes. 
Models and reviews trained on selectively successful formulations risk inflating 
expected performance and obscuring the soil, crop, or process conditions under which a 
product should not be recommended. 
12.5. Limitations of the review method and evidence base 
This review has methodological limitations that constrain the strength of inference. First, 
it is a structured critical narrative review, not a systematic review: we did not preserve 
the earliest Google Scholar retrieval counts, intermediate duplicate-removal totals, or 
article-by-article full-text exclusion reasons, and we did not fabricate retrospective 
counts. Second, a single reviewer/author classified evidence without independent 
coding or inter-rater reliability testing. Supplementary Table S1 improves source-level 
auditability, but it is a retrospective evidence map of the retained corpus rather than an 
independent validation exercise. Third, the evidence base is heterogeneous across 
laboratory experiments, pot studies, field trials, reviews/meta-analyses, pilot 
demonstrations, TEA/LCA studies, regulatory documents, and machine-learning 
analyses, so the synthesis does not imply pooled effect estimates or common statistical 
weights. Fourth, full-text accessibility status was not prospectively logged during initial 
discovery, and bibliographic metadata for fast-moving 2026 literature may continue to 
evolve even after the 11 September 2026 verification audit. 
External validity is similarly bounded. A sewage-sludge biochar, manure-derived 
biochar, crop-residue biochar, digestate hydrochar, mineral-engineered phosphate 
sorbent, and coated NPK granule should not be treated as interchangeable products.

Claims should be transferred only among products with comparable feedstock 
chemistry, conversion conditions, nutrient form, soil context, contaminant profile, and 
product-engineering status. Regulatory conclusions are jurisdiction-specific, and soil 
dependence is a component of product performance. These boundaries are especially 
important for hydrochar because process-water management, dissolved-organic 
species, wet logistics, and generally lower carbon permanence relative to high-
temperature biochar can shift both agronomic and life-cycle outcomes. 
13. Conclusions 
Biochar and hydrochar are not inherently fertilizers, and persistent carbon does not 
inherently establish a net -negative life -cycle greenhouse -gas balance. The Dual -
Performance Boundary Framework for Carbonaceous Fertilizers (DPB -CF) separates 
these two claims and requires fit-for-purpose evidence for each . Fertilizer value 
emerges from a chain that begins with feedstock composition and thermochemical 
partitioning, continues through nutrient speciation, recovery, formulation , and release, 
and is ultimately tested by plant -available delivery, crop uptake, nutrient -use efficiency, 
yield, safety, and product quality under relevant soil conditions. Soil dependence is 
therefore part of the technology , not experimental noise. Hydrothermal carbonization 
requires explicit accounting of process water and post -treatment, whereas dry pyrolysis 
requires attention to volatile and gaseous nutrient losses as well as the condensed 
solid. The three-generation classification—amendment char, nutrient-enriched char, and 
engineered nutrient-recovery fertilizer—is an author-developed evidence framework, not 
a certification system. For climate claims, permanence must be embedded in a 
complete life -cycle boundary that includes counterfactual feedstock fate, energy, 
transport, co -products, field greenhouse -gas fluxes, and end use. The most credible 
development pathway is consequently not maximum char yield, total NPK, sorption 
capacity, or carbon concentration, but auditable recovery to crop uptake, multi -season 
validation, consistent product quality, contaminant control, appropriate functional units, 
and transparent scale-up and regulatory gates.
