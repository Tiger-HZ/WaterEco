Low-Cost IoT Water Quality Classification Using Three Deployable Sensors
and Edge-Deployed Machine Learning
Yash Aggarwal1, Moksh Sharma2, Bindu Thakral3
1,2,3School of Engineering & Technology, Sushant University, Gurugram, India

Abstract - Aquaculture pond health depends on continuous
water quality assessment. Dissolved oxygen probes and titra-
tion apparatus remain costly for smallholder farmers. Afford-
able monitoring therefore demands sensors a microcontroller
can read directly. Published IoT deployments converge on
temperature, pH and turbidity sensing. Most machine learn-
ing studies instead train on full laboratory panels. Such accu-
racy claims assume instrumentation that target farmers rarely
own. Our work restricts model inputs to three microcontroller-
measurable parameters. Five classifiers and one quantized neu-
ral network were benchmarked. A feature-set ablation quan-
tifies accuracy forfeited by excluding laboratory assays. Gra-
dient Boosting reached 87.21% accuracy and 0.8664 macro
F1. Restricting inputs to three sensors costs 0.1198 macro F1.
Quantized inference occupies 4.37 KB within ESP32 flash lim-
its. Findings give smallholder fish farmers an affordable water
monitoring path. Embedded systems researchers can reuse this
ablation methodology directly. Reported limitations also guide
future field validation on real ponds.
Key Words: Internet of Things, water quality monitoring,
aquaculture, edge computing, TinyML, ESP32.
1. INTRODUCTION
Aquaculture supplies a growing share of global protein, and
pond water quality directly governs stocking density, feed con-
version and mortality. Parameters that matter most to fish
health — dissolved oxygen (DO), biochemical oxygen demand
(BOD), ammonia, nitrite — are precisely those that are hard-
est to measure continuously [19]. A laboratory-grade optical
DO probe costs orders of magnitude more than a thermistor,
and alkalinity and hardness are not sensed at all in the con-
ventional sense: they are determined by acid and ethylenedi-
aminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) titration respectively, which re-
quires reagents, glassware and a trained operator.
Cost asymmetry shapes what is actually deployable. A re-
view of published Internet of Things (IoT) aquaculture deploy-
ments shows convergence on a small, consistent instrument set.
Islam and Kashem [1] monitored five ponds in Jamalpur Dis-
trict, Bangladesh using sensors and an Arduino microcontroller
to collect pH, temperature and turbidity, storing readings on
ThingSpeak and analysing them with ten machine learning al-
gorithms across 40,280 rows. Siswanto et al. [2] instrumented
an aquaponic system with a pH-4502C probe, a DFROBOT
analog total dissolved solids (TDS) sensor and a DS18B20
temperature sensor driven by a NodeMCU ESP8266. Udanor
et al. [3] report a comparable sensor complement for catfish
aquaponics in Nigeria. Across these independent deployments
the same three quantities recur: temperature, pH and turbidity.
Much of the published literature on machine learning for
water quality assessment, however, trains on the full laboratory
panel and reports accuracy figures that implicitly assume the
availability of instrumentation the target user does not own. An
accuracy of 98% obtained using ammonia, nitrite, phosphorus
and plankton counts describes a laboratory workflow, not a field
deployment. A practically useful question is different and is
rarely asked directly: how much accuracy is forfeited when the
model is restricted to what a microcontroller can read?
This paper answers that question for a three-class aquacul-
ture water quality task. Our contributions are four. First, a
quantified cost–accuracy trade-off, where a feature-set ablation
isolates the classification penalty attributable to abandoning all
wet-chemistry parameters, reported as both an absolute and a
relative F1 loss. Second, a deployable-by-construction feature
set, in which only temperature, pH and turbidity are used as
model inputs while alkalinity and hardness are explicitly clas-
sified as laboratory parameters and excluded, a distinction we
found is not consistently enforced in comparable work. Third,
an edge deployment characterisation, where a trained network
is quantized to 8-bit integer (INT8) precision and its memory
footprint evaluated against real ESP32 constraints. Fourth, a
reported negative result and its diagnosis, since soft-sensing of
DO from the deployable inputs fails and we present evidence
that the cause lies in the dataset’s synthetic construction rather
than in sensor inadequacy.
2. RELATED WORK
2.1 IoT Water Quality Monitoring Platforms
Public datasets from deployed IoT aquaculture systems consis-
tently reflect the economics described above. A real-time pond
dataset of Islam and Kashem [1] comprises four columns —
pH, temperature, turbidity and fish species as the target variable
— over 40,280 rows drawn from five ponds. Siswanto et al. [2]
published three months of continuous pH, TDS and tempera-
ture readings from an ESP8266-based aquaponic installation.
Baena-Navarro et al. [7], [8] recorded six months of hourly
dissolved oxygen, pH, temperature and turbidity measurements
from tilapia ponds in Montería, Colombia, explicitly framing
the work as targeting rural environments with limited techno-
logical infrastructure, and applied Random Forest and Support
Vector Machines for prediction. Recurrence of the same narrow
sensor set across four independent deployments on three conti-
nents is itself evidence that the constraint we model is real.
2.2 Machine Learning for Water Quality Classification
Tree ensembles dominate reported results on tabular water qual-
ity data. Benchmarking evidence supports this: Grinsztajn et
al. [6] found that tree-based models remain state-of-the-art
on medium-sized tabular datasets of roughly 10,000 samples,
even before accounting for their superior training speed, and
attributed the gap to differing inductive biases — in particu-
lar the robustness of tree ensembles to uninformative features
and their ability to learn irregular decision boundaries. Our
dataset, at 4,300 samples with 14 heterogeneous numeric fea-
tures, falls squarely within this regime, and our results repro-
duce the expected ordering. Classifiers evaluated here follow
standard formulations: gradient boosting [10], random forests
[11], the scalable boosting variant of [12], and support vector
machines with kernel expansion [13].
2.3 TinyML and Edge Inference
Running inference on the sensing node rather than the cloud
reduces bandwidth, removes dependence on continuous con-
nectivity and lowers energy per decision. TensorFlow Lite Mi-
cro [5] provides an interpreter-based inference framework de-
signed for embedded resource constraints, with an evaluation
demonstrating low resource requirements and minimal runtime
overhead. Post-training integer quantization [9] reduces model
weights from 32-bit floating point to 8-bit integers, which mat-
ters on microcontrollers lacking a floating-point unit. We adopt
both, though we are careful in Section 6 to state the limits
of what our footprint measurements establish. Standardised
benchmarking practice for this class of workload is surveyed
in [17], and practical deployment considerations for microcon-
troller inference are treated in [18].
3. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
Fig -1 shows the proposed four-tier architecture. A guiding de-
sign principle is that classification occurs at the edge, so that the
uplink carries a decision rather than a raw measurement stream.
Fig -1: Four-tier architecture. Inference runs on the ESP32; only the
resulting class label is transmitted, reducing uplink payload to
roughly 20 bytes per transmission and permitting continued operation
during connectivity loss.
3.1 Sensing Tier
Three probes are used. A DS18B20 digital sensor provides tem-
perature over a one-wire bus. A pH-4502C module provides an
analog pH signal, consistent with the instrumentation reported
by Siswanto et al. [2]. A turbidity sensor provides optical clar-
ity. Combined bill of materials is a small fraction of that for a
single optical DO probe, and critically requires no reagents or
consumables.
3.2 Edge Tier
An ESP32 samples the three channels, applies z-score nor-
malisation using constants exported at training time, and in-
vokes a quantized neural network through the TensorFlow Lite
Micro interpreter [5]. Normalisation constants used in our
implementation are µ = [ 25.727, 7.708, 38.817] and σ =
[9.932, 1.599, 20.786], ordered as temperature, pH, turbidity.
3.3 Communication and Cloud Tiers
A predicted class is published over Message Queuing Teleme-
try Transport (MQTT). Because the payload is a label rather
than a sample vector, transmission cost is largely independent
of sampling rate, and a node may buffer decisions locally dur-
ing network outages. Cloud tier stores the label time series and
renders a dashboard; it is not in the inference path.
4. DATASET AND METHODOLOGY
4.1 Dataset
We use the Aquaculture Water Quality Dataset published by
Veeramsetty, Arabelli and Bernatin [4]. It contains 1,500 poor,
1,400 excellent and 1,400 good quality samples — 4,300 in to-
tal with 14 input features and one output label — and was pre-
pared from threshold values defining acceptable, desirable and
stress ranges for fish growth. Classes are encoded 0 (Excellent),
1 (Good) and 2 (Poor). Data contained no missing values and
no duplicate rows.
We audited the data for physical plausibility because the
reported generation procedure — sampling within per-class
threshold ranges — does not guarantee that the joint distribu-
tion is physically realisable. Results of that audit, in Table -1,
confirm this concern.

2.33% of temperature values exceed 40 °C, reaching 84.25 °C, and 0.37% of
pH values exceed the pH scale maximum. These are not measurement outliers
but artifacts of range-based synthetic generation.
Fig -2: Correlation matrix of all 14 water quality parameters.
Near-absence of off-diagonal structure among physically coupled
quantities such as temperature and dissolved oxygen is a first
indication that the data were generated by independent sampling
within class ranges.
A second diagnostic is more consequential. In real water
bodies, dissolved oxygen and temperature are strongly neg-
atively correlated through gas solubility. In this dataset the
pooled correlation is r = + 0.027, and within-class correla-
tions are +0.025, −0.013 and +0.031 for the Excellent, Good
and Poor classes respectively. Near-zero within-class structure
indicates that features were sampled independently given the
label, so essentially all learnable signal resides in the class-
conditional marginal ranges. Section 6 discusses the conse-
quences.
4.2 Feature Partition
Table -2 states the partition. A governing criterion is not cost
in isolation but measurement modality: whether the quantity
can be transduced to an electrical signal by a probe on a mi-
crocontroller bus. Alkalinity requires acid titration to a de-
fined endpoint and hardness requires EDTA titration or an ion-
selective electrode; neither is a microcontroller sensor, and both
are therefore excluded from the model inputs irrespective of
their predictive value.

Processing begins with the raw dataset, which passes
through cleaning and an integrity check for duplicates, nulls and
type consistency. A physical plausibility audit follows, record-
ing range violations and within-class correlations that later jus-
tify the negative soft-sensing result. At the decision node each
parameter is tested against one question: can a probe on a mi-
crocontroller bus transduce it? Parameters answering yes form
the deployable set of three; the remaining eleven form the lab-
oratory set. Only the deployable set trains the proposed model,
while the laboratory set enters solely through the dashed ab-
lation path. Subsequent stages apply a stratified split, fit the
scaler on training data alone, train six model families, and eval-
uate with accuracy, macro F1 and a ten-seed stability check. A
final stage quantizes the trained network to INT8 and exports a
TensorFlow Lite Micro artifact sized for ESP32 flash.
4.4 Experimental Protocol
Data were split 80/20 with stratification on the class label and a
fixed seed, giving 3,440 training and 860 test samples. Features
were standardised using statistics fitted on the training parti-
tion only. For cross-validation, scaling was performed inside a
pipeline so that fold-specific test data never influenced the scal-
ing statistics — a precaution we note because fitting the scaler
on the full array before cross-validation is a common and silent
source of optimistic bias.
All models were implemented in scikit-learn [14] and Ten-
sorFlow [15], with the neural network optimised using Adam
[16] and figures produced with Matplotlib [22]. Five classi-
fiers were evaluated: Logistic Regression, k-Nearest Neigh-
bours (k = 5), Support Vector Machine with radial basis func-
tion (RBF) kernel, Random Forest (300 trees) and Gradient
Boosting. Macro-averaged precision, recall and F1 are reported
alongside accuracy, since macro averaging weights the minority
behaviour of each class equally. Because a single split yields a
point estimate with no error bar, we additionally repeat the clas-
sification over ten random splits and report mean and standard
deviation. Two regression models were also trained to predict
DO and BOD from the same three inputs, evaluated by root
mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE) and
R2 with five-fold cross-validation.
5. RESULTS
5.1 Classification Performance
Table -3 reports classification results using only the three de-
ployable sensors. Gradient Boosting is the strongest model at
87.21% accuracy and 0.8664 macro F1. Model ordering —
gradient boosting, then random forest, then kernel SVM, then
k-NN, with logistic regression far behind — matches the pat-
tern reported by Grinsztajn et al. [6] for medium-sized tabular
data. Collapse of logistic regression to 0.5399 F1 indicates that
the class boundaries in this three-dimensional feature space are
substantially non-linear.

5.2 Feature-Set Ablation
Table -6 is the central result of this paper. It isolates the
classification penalty attributable to restricting the model to
microcontroller-measurable inputs, holding the classifier and
protocol fixed.
Restricting the model to three deployable sensors costs
0.1198 macro F1 relative to the full 14-parameter panel, a rela-
tive reduction of 12.1%. We regard this as the honest statement
of the trade-off: a system requiring no reagents, no titration and
no trained operator retains roughly seven-eighths of the dis-
criminative performance of full laboratory assessment on this
dataset.
A second row deserves emphasis. Adding alkalinity and
hardness lifts F1 from 0.8664 to 0.9035 — a substantial 3.7-
point gain. It would be tempting to include them and report
the higher figure, and we note that doing so would be method-
ologically indefensible for a paper claiming low-cost deploy-
ment, since both require titration. We report the row explicitly
to make the temptation visible and to quantify what is being
declined.

5.4 Edge Deployment
A three-layer fully connected network (32–16–3 units, 707
parameters) was trained on the deployable inputs, reaching
84.07% test accuracy — below Gradient Boosting, as expected
from [6]. It is retained because tree ensembles are less conve-
nient to deploy through TensorFlow Lite Micro. Fig -9 shows
the training history.
A quantized model occupies 4.37 KB, or 0.107% of the
4 MB flash typical of an ESP32 module, and fits comfortably
within the roughly 320 KB of usable RAM. Deployment is
therefore not memory-constrained by a wide margin.
We deliberately do not claim quantization as a compression
success. An INT8 to float32 size ratio is only 1.10 ×, because
a 707-parameter model is dominated by FlatBuffer graph meta-
data rather than by weights. A larger apparent reduction from
33.22 KB to 4.81 KB is an artifact of Keras container overhead,
not of any optimisation. That 0.35 percentage point accuracy
difference in favour of INT8 is likewise within single-split noise
and is not interpreted as a benefit of quantization.

5.5 Comparison with Reported Deployments
Table -9 situates the proposed configuration against the sensor
complements of published IoT aquaculture systems. Our com-
parison is limited to instrumentation and scale rather than ac-
curacy, since the datasets differ in target variable, class def-
inition and collection methodology, and reported accuracies
across them are not commensurable. What the table establishes
is that the three-parameter configuration evaluated here is not an
artificial restriction imposed for this study but the modal con-
figuration of deployed systems in the literature.
One difference is worth noting. Field datasets in rows
one to four sample densely in time from a small number
of ponds, so consecutive records are strongly autocorrelated
and an independent-and-identically-distributed train/test split
would overstate generalisation. Our dataset has no temporal
structure at all, which removes that particular hazard but in-
troduces the different one discussed in Section 6.2. Neither
construction is unambiguously preferable; they fail in different
directions, and a study seeking to establish field performance
would need both.
6. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
6.1 Why Soft-Sensing Failed
A natural reading of Table -7 is that temperature, pH and turbid-
ity are inadequate predictors of dissolved oxygen. We believe
this reading is wrong, and that stating it would propagate an
incorrect conclusion into the literature.

Sample counts are not directly comparable: field studies log continuously from
few ponds, whereas the dataset used here is class-balanced by construction.
Dissolved oxygen saturation is governed by Henry’s law
and falls monotonically with temperature; in field data the
two are strongly negatively correlated. Our dataset exhibits
r = +0.027 pooled, and within-class correlations of +0.025,
−0.013 and +0.031. A physical relationship is simply absent.
Combined with the plausibility violations in Table -1 — tem-
peratures to 84.25 °C, pH values above the scale maximum —
evidence indicates that parameters were drawn independently
within class-conditional ranges. A regressor cannot recover a
relationship that was never encoded. That failure is a property
of this dataset, not a measurement of what low-cost sensors can
achieve, and we recommend that soft-sensing feasibility be as-
sessed only on field-collected data such as [1], [7] or [8].
6.2 Circularity of the Classification Task
A generative procedure has a subtler consequence for the clas-
sification result. Because labels were assigned from threshold
values on the very features used as inputs [4], the classification
task is partially circular: the model recovers a rule that was im-
posed rather than a relationship that was observed. That 87.21%
figure should therefore be read as an upper-bound estimate of
performance on threshold-consistent data, not as a prediction
of accuracy in a pond. Our ablation in Table -6 is more robust
to this concern than the absolute accuracy is, because circular-
ity affects all four rows comparably and largely cancels in the
comparison.
6.3 Latency Measurements
Table -8 reports sub-microsecond inference latencies measured
on an x86 host. These do not transfer to an ESP32. Float32
kernels on the host benefit from XNNPACK vectorisation while
INT8 kernels receive no comparable acceleration, which is why
quantization yields no host-side speedup. On a microcontroller
without a floating-point unit the ordering would likely reverse.
We therefore rest the deployment argument on memory foot-
print, which is a property of the artifact rather than of the bench-
marking host, and treat on-target latency as unmeasured.
6.4 Operational Implication of Poor-Class Recall
A recall of 0.65 on the Poor class means roughly one in three
water quality failures goes undetected. For an early-warning
system this is the error that matters, since a missed alarm can
precipitate a fish kill while a false alarm costs only an inspec-
tion. Future work should adjust the decision threshold or apply
class weighting to trade the currently very high Poor-class pre-
cision of 0.98 for improved recall.
6.5 Absence of Hardware Validation
Our most significant limitation is that no measurements from
physical sensors appear in this study. Architecture of Fig -1 is
specified but not instrumented, and the reported accuracy there-
fore characterises a model rather than a system. Sensor drift,
analog-to-digital converter noise, temperature cross-sensitivity
of pH probes and fouling of optical turbidity sensors are all ab-
sent from the evaluation and would each be expected to degrade
field performance.
7. CONCLUSIONS
We quantified the accuracy cost of restricting aquaculture water
quality classification to the three parameters a microcontroller
can measure. Gradient Boosting achieves 87.21% accuracy and
0.8664 macro F1 from temperature, pH and turbidity alone,
sacrificing 0.1198 F1 — 12.1% relative — against a full 14-
parameter laboratory panel, with stability confirmed across ten
random splits. A quantized inference artifact occupies 4.37 KB,
well within ESP32 constraints. We also report and diagnose a
negative soft-sensing result, attributing it to the synthetic con-
struction of the dataset rather than to sensor limitations.
Three directions follow. First, and most importantly, the
model should be validated against field measurements: even
fifty readings from a deployed node, labelled against a refer-
ence laboratory assay or a published potability standard [20],
[21], would convert this from a modelling study into a system
evaluation. Second, the same ablation should be repeated on
field-collected datasets such as [1], [7] and [8], where phys-
ical relationships between parameters are preserved and soft-
sensing may well succeed. Third, on-target latency and energy
per inference should be measured on ESP32 hardware, which
would allow the duty-cycling and battery-life analysis that a
solar-powered deployment requires.
