Irrigated rice paddies cover approximately 165 million hectares globally and feed more than half of humanity, yet their ecological dimension as functioning wetland ecosystems generating biodiversity, trophic networks, and measurable ecosystem services has received strikingly little integrated scientific attention. This review synthesises available evidence on the paddy field as an integrated aquatic agroecosystem, drawing on studies from South Asia, Southeast Asia, and cognate wetland systems worldwide. We examine the physicochemical environment that structures biotic communities, the primary producer base of phytoplankton and periphyton, the dominant invertebrate component (Chironomidae: Diptera), trophic linkages between Chironomid larvae and paddy-associated fish, bioturbation-mediated nutrient cycling, and the full portfolio of ecosystem services that emerge from these interactions. The evidence reviewed confirms that larval Chironomidae are the functional keystone of the paddy food web: simultaneously channelling energy from the decomposer and algal base to higher consumers, engineering sediment chemistry through bioturbation, and modulating methane flux from flooded soils. Integrated paddy-cum-fish cultivation, now practised across South and Southeast Asia, draws ecological credit directly from this invertebrate productivity fish yields are achievable without supplementary feeding because the Chironomid community acts as a self-renewing, high-protein food base. We argue that managing the paddy field as an integrated agroecosystem, rather than as a monoculture delivery system, offers a scientifically grounded and economically compelling pathway toward sustainable intensification of smallholder rice farming.
