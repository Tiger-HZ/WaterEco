INTRODUCTION
Community (or public) participation has long been
recognised as a core requirement for achieving equi-
table and universal health coverage through the primary
health care (PHC) approach and effective health systems
governance [1–4]. In low- and middle-income countries

enabling community participation in these settings in-
clude bottom-up initiatives such as community-led mon-
itoring [7] and peer support groups [8]. and top-down 
formal governance systems like health committees [6]. 
On one hand, community-led efforts centre community

and outside of routine governance and accountability 
mechanisms, thus rendering them vulnerable [7]. On 
the other hand, experiences with formal governance sys-
tems such as health committees have been mixed at best 
and highly context specific [5,6,14].
In South Africa, health committees are mandated by 
both national and provincial legislation. The National 
Health Act [15] mandates health committees as legal gov-
ernance structures for PHC facilities and outlines the 
membership requirements for each committee which 
must include local government councillors, members of 
the community served by the health care facility and 
the facility manager. In the Western Cape province, the 
establishment, functions and procedures of health com-
mittees are further outlined in the provincial Health 
Facility Boards and Committees Act [16], which aims to 
strengthen community participation in health. Evidence 
on health committees in the country, however, suggests 
that meaningful community participation within these 
structures is limited [17–20]. This highlights a gap be-
tween policy intentions and implementation experience.

The experience of health committees in South Africa
is set against wider efforts to improve the quality of care
provided by the country’s health system. In the public
sector, these efforts include quality improvement (QI)
initiatives such as the Ideal Clinic Realisation and Main-
tenance Framework (ICRMF) (see Box 1). The ICRMF
is a nationwide standards-based QI initiative aimed at
improving the quality of care at PHC facilities [21,22].
Despite its potential for facilitating learning and contin-
uous improvement, the ICRMF’ success and sustainabil-
ity is constrained by wider systemic challenges [23,24].
These challenges comprise complex interactions between
system ‘hardware’ issues such as shortage of critical
equipment and system ‘software’ factors like low staff
morale [24] (see Figure 1). Furthermore, the ICRMF
includes collecting information about community mem-
bers’ participation in support of health care delivery ,
but the initiative itself does not engage communities.
Yet experiences from other LMIC countries suggest that
integrating community participation in QI efforts can
help improve quality of care. In Uganda, for example,
the combination of information-sharing about quality of
care at health care facilities with community participa-
tion was reported to have helped reduce neonatal deaths
and improve facility process quality measures [25,26].

While health committees and the ICRMF are formally
mandated community participation and QI systems in
PHC, respectively , they have largely developed as ‘verti-
cal’ programmes and have not been connected to date.
However, there is some recognition of the potential of
and the need to synergise the two systems. In 2017, the
South African case study of the World Health Organi-
zation PHC Systems (PRIMASYS) series noted the need
“to use health facility committees to support the Ideal
Clinic [ICRMF] initiative and conversely use the Ideal
Clinic programme to institutionalize health facility com-
mittees.” [27]. This paper further explores this idea. It
reports on an action-learning experience in the Western
Cape province, that aimed to synergise heath commit-
tees and the ICRMF in two sub-districts (collectively

called sub- structure) to improve the quality of care in
PHC facilities. The embedded case is part of wider sys-
tems learning in this specific sub-structure [24,28]. In
this case study , ‘synergy’ refers to “a situation where
different entities combine advantageously – where the
whole becomes greater than the sum of the individual
parts.” [29]. This systems lens is important when inter-
vening in complex systems and for enabling collabora-
tion across programmatic silos or different parts of the
system [29,30]. Operationally , then, by synergising we
mean combining health committees and the ICRMF such
that each system benefits the other and they together
strengthen overall QI efforts. The main aim of this case
study was to explore whether health committees and
the ICRMF can be synergised. And if so, how , under
what conditions and with what potential consequences.
METHODS
Study design
This work is situated within health policy and sys-
tems research (HPSR) which is an “emerging field that
seeks to understand and improve how societies organize
themselves in achieving collective health goals, and how
different actors interact in the policy and implementa-
tion processes to contribute to policy outcomes.” [31].
HPSR is not characterized by any specific methodology
but rather the questions it seeks to answer [31] and it is,
in essence, applied research [32]. T o address our main
aim, we drew on action-learning which is relevant to
HPSR due to the applied nature of the field and the em-
beddedness of HPSR actors within the health system
[33,34] – as is the case in this study. While there are
many definitions of action-learning and the approach
has evolved over time, it can be understood as a “pro-
cess in specific contexts for inquiring into and taking
action on projects and practices within their complex,
multi-agent contexts” [35]. It has been proposed to offer
value for supporting quality and safety efforts in health
care settings by bringing together diverse actors to ask
questions, take action and reflect on progress around a
defined problem [36]. Our action-learning process con-
sisted of three core steps, adapted from Kemmis and Mc
Taggart’s [37] three-step action research spiral: Step 1 –
Situation appraisal, Step 2 – Action, Step 3 – Learning.
The three steps are intended to be iterative (spiral), how-
ever, in this paper we only report on the first iteration.
As noted, the overall aim was to explore whether
health committees and the ICRMF could be synergised.
And if so, how , under what conditions and with what
potential consequences. The three action-learning steps
were linked to the following objectives, and anchored
on our operational definition of ‘synergy’ as above:
Step 1 – Situation appraisal
• Objective 1: Understand the barriers to, and op-
portunities for, health committees to participate in
the ICRMF.
Step 2 – Action
• Objective 2: Develop and implement a practical
mechanism for health committees to participate in
the ICRMF (integration/combination).
Step 3- Learning
• Objective 3: Reflect on the action-learning pro-
cess to identify emerging synergies, early system
changes, implementation constraints and lessons
to guide the next iteration.
Study setting
The action-learning process took place in two sub-
districts (collectively called sub-structure), in the West-
ern Cape province of South Africa. This sub-structure
was selected because it had already begun facilitating
systems learning in the district health system [38], using
existing QI and other routine processes as platforms for
reflection, adaptation and improvement [24,28]. Further-
more, for the duration of this process and at the time
of writing, OM was employed as quality manager for
the sub-structure, responsible for leading the implemen-
tation of the ICRMF. The Western Cape Department of
Health and Wellness (WCDHW) operates 15 PHC fa-
cilities and one district hospital in this sub-structure.
Because the action-learning process sought to integrate
the health committees and the ICRMF which both relate
to PHC, our work therefore focused specifically on PHC
facilities.
Study population and sampling strategy
At the start of the process, there were ten clustered
PHC health committees across the sub-structure and
one of them was invited to participate in this process.
This was for both purposive and pragmatic reasons –
this committee was relatively more active and accessi-
ble, respectively. Given the small population size (10
officially appointed members in the committee), total
population sampling was used. This purposive sam-
pling method is commonly used in small populations
and involves the inclusion of the entire population that
meets specific criteria in the research study [39]. In this
case, the inclusion criterion was official appointment as
a health committee member. The committee consisted
of six active members supporting five facilities and all
of them were invited to participate. OM requested a
slot in one health committee meeting wherein he intro-
duced the project to the participants and invited them to
participate. However, this process was not conducting
research about [33] health committees and the ICRMF
but rather acting and learning with health committees
in the process of implementing the ICRMF. As such,
health committee members were seen as more than re-
search participants but rather as members of a diverse
action-learning team [36]. The action-learning process
was conducted as a collaborative knowledge generation
and collective action undertaking between the WCDHW
(OM & MP), the health committee (DM & DdP) and the
School of Public Health (LL & HH), with OM as the
learning facilitator [36].

Data collection
As the first step of the action-learning process, we
conducted a situation appraisal, involving a focus group
discussion (FGD) with the health committee (Decem-
ber 2023). The FGD was scheduled with health com-
mittee members who indicated willingness to partic-
ipate and was facilitated by OM using a topic guide.
Six health committee members participated in the dis-
cussion which was audio-recorded, with written con-
sent, and transcribed verbatim using Microsoft Word.
The FGD sought to understand the health committee’s
experience with the ICRMF and QI more broadly , fac-
tors influencing their participation (or lack thereof) in
the ICRMF and QI, and to explore ways in which their
participation could be improved – ultimately inform-
ing an intervention (Step 2 – Action). In addition to the
FGD (which specifically relates to Step 1 of the action-
learning process), other data sources included notes
from author debriefing meetings and three wider learn-
ing workshops/meetings and written inputs from man-
agers. While these data were not formally analysed (see
data analysis below), they supported the action-learning
process (see Steps 2 and 3 below) and broader reflections
in this paper.
Data analysis
The FGD data were thematically analysed (Febru-
ary 2024) using a conceptual framework (Figure 2). In
the context of community participation, hardware en-
compasses things such as stipends, transport, physical
spaces and technology , while tangible software speaks
to issues of knowledge, skills, systems and procedures
[40]. Intangible software on the other hand speaks to is-
sues of power, relationships, communication and norms,
which are critical in “shaping the behaviours of those
working towards community participation” [40]. OM
read and manually coded the transcript – looking for
phrases reflecting hardware and software issues. The
codes were categorised into initial themes which were
then discussed in debriefing meetings with LL and HH,
and then DM and DdP . The meetings served to facilitate
collective sense-making, consensus on emerging themes
and to generate insights. The initial themes were then
further synthesised by grouping around hardware, tan-
gible software and intangible software issues, resulting
in the final themes.
Three core strategies were used to ensure analytic
rigour: 1) Debriefing meetings between the authors to
consider emerging themes (including consensus) and in-
sights. 2) Member checking through learning workshops
with the FGD participants to check whether the analy-
sis results (and reflections on the wider action-learning
process) accurately represent their inputs and lived ex-
periences. 3) The use of a theory-based [41] analytical
framework to help interpret data, through a whole sys-
tem lens.

Action
As a second step we developed a guide to facilitate
collaboration with health committees and managers in
the ICRMF based on the insights from Step 1. We then
sought and received written input on the guide from
three managers. The managers were purposefully iden-
tified based on their experience of working in PHC, with
QI initiatives and communities, while ensuring perspec-
tives from both the sub-structure and facility levels. This
was followed by a workshop with the health commit-
tee, which included introductory training on the ICRMF
(May 2024).
Learning
The third step involved reflection on action and un-
folded in two stages. First, the overall experience was
presented and discussed in a meeting with 17 partic-
ipants from WCDHW , the health committee and the
School of Public Health (June 2024). This meeting
included participants outside the initial collaborative,
such as quality managers within and outside the sub-
structure and community representatives from other
sub-structures. The step enabled the sharing of experi-
ence with relevant stakeholders within and beyond the
immediate action ‘site’ (the sub- structure), while also
eliciting their feedback and insights. The second stage
consisted of a one- day reflective session (October 2024)
on this experience and community participation, more
broadly , with over 30 health care managers and frontline
staff.
Ethical considerations
Participation in the process was voluntary and with
written consent. All identifying information was re-
moved from both the audio file and transcript. Partic-
ipant names were replaced with alphanumeric codes,
also used in presenting the results, to protect anonymity.
Ethics approval was obtained from the University of
Cape T own´s Faculty of Health Sciences Human Re-
search Ethics Committee (HREC 393/2023).

action-learning steps, aligned to the study objectives.

Step 1 – Situation appraisal: Barriers to, and oppor-
tunities for, health committees to participate in the
ICRMF
The situation appraisal sought to understand what
constrained health committees’ participation in the
ICRMF, and also explored opportunities or levers that
could facilitate such participation. Figure 3 summarises
the key findings from the situation appraisal as synthe-
sised from the FGD data.
We found that although the FGD was intended to
be about the ICRMF, what emerged more prominently
were the contextual factors within which health com-
mittees undertake their role. These factors constrained
collaboration between health committee members and
health care managers. For example, one health commit-
tee member reflected on the challenge of collaboration
and, importantly , highlighted the underlying intangible
software issues:
“T o be honest with you, what I picked up… also is
that there is no trust, they [health care managers]
don’t trust us... and you can’t work as a family if
there is no trust. (FGD participant 4)”
Other constraints included hardware issues such as
lack of financial support and operational resources, tan-
gible software factors like lack of training on the ICRMF
and QI generally , and software issues such as poor com-
munication, power practices and imbalances and lack of
recognition as shown in Figure 3.
Poor collaboration with managers in turn limited
the role of health committee members, including par-
ticipation in QI initiatives and the ICRMF specifically.
One participant explained how their involvement was
limited to receiving complaints, although they felt they
could do more:
“When I got here… I realised what my actual role
is and not what I thought I let myself in because
I was thinking like what everybody else said: the
mediator , be the voice, …represent the people, be
all of that. That was my thinking but I came here
just to realise that I’m only here to open [com-
plaints] boxes on a weekly basis and that’s about
it. (FGD participant 1)”
However, there were also experiences of trusting and
collaborative relationships. Such relationships provide
an opportunity for enabling the participation of health
committees in the ICRMF, and were especially seen
where facility managers and health committee members
have worked together over longer periods of time:
“Y ou know now with these new people coming
in as facility managers, with our old people they
were fine, they trusted us [and] they worked with
us... (FGD participant 4)”
Despite these challenges and limited training, we
found that health committee members had experiential
knowledge of the on-the-ground situation valuable for
meaningful improvement. For example, one participant
queried how one of the facilities passed a recent ICRMF
assessment while having critical shortfalls in the recep-
tion area:
“…when you do your [ICRMF] audit maybe just
a check-up because as we know, I’ m sure that you
wouldn´t give them a good standing certificate if
you saw piles and piles of files line up, I´m sure
you wouldn´t. So, you need to find out where all
those things are being hidden, all the trimmings
is being put up, you need to investigate. (FGD
participant 3)”
The filing issue in this specific facility , which was
not picked up during the ICRMF assessment, was later
flagged in internal sub-structure reports.
Step 2 – Action: From a training manual to a collabora-
tion guide
The initial objective of this work was to develop a
manual for training health committees on the ICRMF as
an intervention towards enabling community participa-
tion in QI. However, the situation appraisal showed that
limited technical knowledge of the ICRMF was only one
constraint. Resource limitations, poor communication,
mistrust, unequal power relations and lack of recogni-
tion meant that technical training alone would not be
sufficient to enable meaningful participation. The action
therefore shifted from training health committees about
the ICRMF to developing a practical guide for managers
on how to collaborate with health committees through
different stages of the ICRMF cycle (supplementary file
1).
The guide was developed from the situation ap-
praisal findings and subsequent written input from man-
agers. Its purpose was not to solve all challenges facing
health committees, such as compensation or wider pol-
icy constraints, but to provide practical actions within
the influence of district and facility managers. It first
identifies three dimensions of an enabling environment
for collaboration – shared purpose, resources and rela-
tionships – before offering practical ideas for involving
health committees throughout the ICRMF cycle. These
include making health committee members part of the fa-
cility ICRMF team during assessment preparation; allo-
cating responsibilities in ways that pair committee mem-
bers with facility staff while protecting confidentiality;
inviting committee members into assessment processes
in areas suited to their strengths; presenting assessment
results in accessible formats; facilitating collective sense-

Step 3 – Learning: emerging synergies, early system
changes and limits
The learning step generated five main insights. First,
the ICRMF offers a structured entry point for strengthen-
ing health committee participation beyond complaints
handling, because it can create routine moments where
facility performance, improvement priorities and follow-
up actions could be discussed. Second, health commit-
tees offer experiential community knowledge that could
strengthen the usefulness of the ICRMF, including by
identifying quality issues not always visible in formal
assessment processes. Third, the action-learning process
helped raise the visibility of community participation
within the sub-structure, contributing to wider conver-
sations and early system changes such as health com-
mittee involvement in operational planning and annual
review processes, and formal encouragement for man-
agers to share facility performance information with
health committees. Fourth, while the situation appraisal
suggested that technical training on the ICRMF alone
was insufficient to enable health committee participa-
tion, feedback and reflections on the training (workshop)
we conducted indicated some value. Health committee
members felt that the training empowered them with
knowledge to carry out their role more effectively as they
now knew and had reference to a wide array of quality
standards against which they can hold facilities account-
able and work with them towards achieving. This was
particularly important as most of the health committee
members did not have a health care background. Fifth,
the participation of health committee members in the
action-learning process itself, and other processes as
noted above, was also experienced as a form of recogni-
tion of the importance of their role and the value they
bring to the system – something that was identified as
lacking during the situation appraisal. These insights
were generated through reflection meetings and work-
shops rather than through a formal outcome evaluation.
They are, therefore, presented as emerging – but impor-
tant – learning and early system changes rather than as
results of empirical testing of the guide itself.
DISCUSSION
In this section, we tease out some key ideas and
lessons for synergising health committees and the
ICRMF through collaboration between health care man-
agers and health committees, as proposed in the guide,
drawing on the insights from this action-learning pro-
cess and wider literature. First, we posit that there are
6

three critical dimensions for enabling effective collabora-
tion between health care managers and health commit-
tees in the ICRMF, before thinking about the technical
ICRMF cycle itself. It is important to first establish a
clear and common purpose – a defining feature for suc-
cessful collaboration with communities [11] and other
stakeholders [43,44]. This purpose should be well com-
municated and understood by all those who need to
collaborate, including health committee members and
facility staff.
The second dimension relates to resources. In the
South African public sector, health committees are not
compensated and generally do not receive financial sup-
port to carry out their role. This can be a barrier to
collaboration, especially in poorer communities. For ex-
ample, a health committee member may not be able to
attend a meeting due to lack of transport. While issues
of stipends are beyond the powers of district and facility
managers, basic day-to-day resources such as stationery ,
access to telephone and internet, could and should be
availed to support health committees in fulfilling their
role, and enabling them to participate in the ICRMF.
Furthermore, current legislation makes provision for fa-
cilities to support health committees with transport, but
this support has been inconsistent in practice.
Building trusting relationships is a third crucial di-
mension for enabling collaboration between different
stakeholders in health systems [45], including in QI
[24,28]. Trust-building is especially important between
community representatives and health care workers
where mistrust and suspicion are often a challenge
[46–49] – exacerbated by lack of transparency [50] and
unequal power dynamics [17,51]. Furthermore, while
trust is needed for community participation, it can also
be an outcome of the latter [1], thus helping restore trust
in the health system. An important consideration in this
dimension is that while health committees may enjoy
legitimacy within the community , health care managers
hold considerably more power as far as health care de-
livery is concerned. This power may stem from their
position in the bureaucracy and their professional knowl-
edge, for example. Some managers use this power to
work with communities more closely while others use
the same to sideline communities [20]. Sharing power is
important for nurturing trusting relationships needed
to enable collaboration with health committees in the
ICRMF and in QI more generally [19,24,28,40,51]. As
we found in this study , and as noted by Bhutta et al.,
[52] trusting relationships can be negatively impacted
by the turn-over of managers because trust building ef-
forts have to start again with each new manager. Stable
and strong facility-level leadership is, therefore, crucial
to support collaboration for improvement [9,53] – in-
cluding collaboration with communities. By design, the
ICRMF process follows a Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cy-
cle [22]. Important, here, is that health committee mem-
bers must be included early in the process and with
clearly defined roles [11,17].
The shift from a proposed training manual to a
collaboration guide is also important when consid-
ered against existing literature. It suggests that en-
abling health committee participation in QI cannot be
reduced to technical knowledge of the ICRMF. The situa-
tion appraisal showed that participation was shaped
by contextual factors that are well recognised in the
literature on community participation, including re-
sources, communication, role clarity , recognition, trust
and power [17,18,40,50,54,55]. When analysed through
the hardware/software lens more specifically , these fac-
tors constrained collaboration between health commit-
tee members and health care managers in ways consis-
tent with evidence showing that community participa-
tion is highly context-dependent and that formal struc-
tures such as health committees often struggle to achieve
meaningful participation without attention to everyday
practices, organisational routines and power relations
[2,5,6,19,46]. Renedo et al. [46], for example, argue that
patient and public involvement in QI requires new forms
of practice and relationships between professionals and
community actors. In this case, the guide sought to move
beyond information transfer by supporting managers to
create practical conditions for participation and collabo-
ration – nurturing the necessitated new forms of practice
and relationships.
The action-learning process suggests that the two
systems can benefit each other (mutual value proposi-
tion). The ICRMF can strengthen health committees by
offering a structured route into facility QI processes, in-
cluding access to standards, assessment findings and
improvement plans. This may help move health com-
mittee participation beyond complaints handling to-
wards more proactive involvement in QI, addressing
longstanding concerns about limited health committee
functionality in South Africa and other LMIC settings
[6,17,18,32,50,54,55]. Conversely , health committees can
strengthen the ICRMF by bringing community perspec-
tives and experiential knowledge into the assessment
and improvement cycle, consistent with wider literature
showing that community participation and patient/pub-
lic involvement can contribute to QI when communities
are engaged as partners rather than passive recipients of
information [5,6,25,40,46,49]. The reception-area filing
example illustrates how community knowledge can sur-
face quality problems that may be missed or temporar-
ily hidden during formal assessment processes, echo-
ing evidence that information- sharing and community
monitoring can strengthen accountability and service
improvement [25,26]. This example also speaks to wider
concerns about how standards-based QI systems are im-
plemented in practice – that formal ICRMF assessments
may not always capture the everyday realities experi-
enced by patients and community representatives, es-
pecially where facilities prepare for assessment in ways
that temporarily obscure routine service-delivery prob-
lems. This resonates with previous work on the ICRMF,
which has described the risk of artificial compliance

Motshweneng al.
and the need to leverage standards-based assessments
for learning and continuous improvement rather than
inspection-oriented performance display [23,24]. Health
committees may therefore help strengthen the learning
dimension of the ICRMF by bringing situated commu-
nity knowledge into assessment interpretation and im-
provement planning. In this sense, synergy does not
mean simply adding health committees to an existing
technical process. Instead, it means reshaping the rela-
tionship between community participation and QI so
that each strengthens the other.
Some early system changes were also observed
around the action-learning process (both during and af-
ter). These included wider conversations about commu-
nity participation, a world café session with health com-
mittees to inform the sub-structure annual operational
plan, inclusion of some health committee members in the
sub structure annual review meeting, and formal writ-
ten communication encouraging facility managers to
share a wider range of performance reports with health
committees. These shifts are important because they sug-
gest movement from informing health committees about
government priorities toward involving them earlier in
planning and review , which aligns with literature em-
phasising the value of early involvement, transparency
and access to information in enabling meaningful par-
ticipation [5,11,17,19]. Access to information can itself
be empowering, particularly where communities have
historically been excluded from technical or managerial
decision-making spaces [5,19]. The early system changes
around the action-learning process, which can be inter-
preted as positive unintended consequences, added to
the value of the action-learning process itself and to-
gether signalled an overall positive trajectory. These
observations should be interpreted as early indications
of system learning and possible ripple effects, rather
than conclusive evidence of sustained change.
This positive trajectory , however, was disrupted by
the realities of working in health systems which are com-
plex adaptive systems – non-linear, relational and diffi-
cult to control through fixed intervention plans [29,30].
Following the first iteration and subsequent workshops,
the guide was planned to be piloted from January 2025,
as a second iteration of the action-learning cycle. How-
ever, the health committee reached the end of its three-
year term and there were delays in (re)appointments
which meant that the pilot could not proceed as planned.
Delays in the annual updating of the ICRMF tool and
manual created an additional constraint. These disrup-
tions illustrate why system interventions require sus-
tained facilitation, institutional memory and adaptive
implementation over time. This experience is also rele-
vant to a wider and current policy challenge. As South
Africa moves towards a National Health Insurance under
a plural health system involving both public and private
providers, standards-based quality systems are becom-
ing increasingly central. A key question, therefore, is
how community participation can be meaningfully built
into these accreditations and QI arrangements, rather
than treated as a parallel or secondary process [56].
Limitations
This case study has several limitations. First, the sit-
uation appraisal drew on one FGD with one relatively
active clustered health committee, which limits transfer-
ability and means the findings should be read as situated
rather than representative. Second, the collaboration
guide was not empirically piloted, so we cannot make
claims about its effectiveness or sustainability in prac-
tice. Third, the embedded role of the lead facilitator was
both a strength and a constraint: it enabled access, prac-
tical relevance and responsiveness to system realities,
but may also have shaped how problems were framed,
how learning was interpreted and how openly partici-
pants engaged. Despite these limitations, the embedded
action-learning process generated context- specific learn-
ing, surfaced practical opportunities for linking com-
munity participation and QI, and contributed to early
system changes within the sub-structure. This kind of
situated learning is an important contribution of HPSR,
particularly where the aim is not only to describe health
system problems but to shifting the frame in how health
systems issues are viewed, discussed and solved, and
generating context-specific evidence from within to sup-
port practical change [57].
Conclusion
This paper shared experience of an action-learning
process seeking to synergise the health committees and
the ICRMF. This action-learning case study demon-
strated emerging synergies between health committees
and the ICRMF in one Western Cape sub-structure.
The process showed that health committees can con-
tribute experiential community knowledge to QI pro-
cesses, while the ICRMF can provide a structured oppor-
tunity to strengthen health committee participation in
facility improvement. However, full integration and em-
pirical testing of the guide remain future work. District
and facility managers seeking to strengthen community
participation in QI should invest in facilitation capac-
ity , clarify roles, share facility performance information
transparently , provide basic operational support, and
create routine spaces for health committees to partici-
pate in ICRMF planning, action and learning.
