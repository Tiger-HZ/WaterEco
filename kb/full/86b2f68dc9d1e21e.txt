 Abstract Flood routing in ephemeral streams of arid regions is characterized by high uncertainty due to limited data, variable transmission losses, and the transient nature of flow events. To address these challenges, this study introduces a newly developed convection–decay-based flood routing model that captures both the translation of the flood wave and the attenuation of peak discharge and volume caused by channel transmission losses. The model simulates hydrograph evolution in response to uncertain hydraulic conditions typical of arid environments. The model is applied to the Yiba Basin in western Saudi Arabia, a highly arid and data-scarce watershed where steep upstream slopes, permeable alluvial channels, and substantial infiltration losses strongly influence flood-wave propagation. The analysis uses observed inflow and outflow hydrographs from four hydrometric stations and 22 historical storm events. Uncertainty in flood-wave travel time and peak decay is incorporated using a Monte Carlo simulation framework. One thousand realizations are generated by randomly sampling the lag time between stations ( K ) and the decay factor ( λ ) from their fitted probability distributions to produce an ensemble of plausible outflow hydrographs while reflecting the natural variability in ephemeral channels. The resulting ensemble of simulated outflow hydrographs falls within a 90% confidence interval in most of the events. Flood peaks and volumes lie extremely close to the 1-to-1 line, indicating a highly successful and robust model for future predictions (r = 0.72 and RMSE = 30.9 m 3 /s for peak flows, and r = 0.73 and RMSE = 11.9 MCM for runoff volume). The probabilistic framework enables a more realistic and risk-informed understanding of flood behavior, supporting sustainable flood management in data-scarce arid regions. This approach offers a practical and adaptable tool for resilient infrastructure design and disaster preparedness under uncertain hydrologic conditions.

 Introduction Flood wave propagation in ephemeral streams is a critical consideration in the design of flood protection schemes, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions where hydrological processes exhibit high variability. Numerous studies have addressed this issue, including Goodrich ( 1990a , b ), Sorman and Abdulrazzak ( 1993 ), Abdulrazzak and Sorman ( 1994 ), Goodrich et al. ( 1997 ), Illangasekare and Morel-Seytoux ( 1984 ), Knighton and Nanson ( 2001 ), and Bahrawi et al. ( 2020 ). Two primary approaches are commonly employed to study flood wave propagation. The first is hydrological flood routing, which focuses on changes in hydrograph shape and travel time along the channel. Among the models used, Kundzewicz and Strupczewski ( 1982 ) noted that the Muskingum method has been widely applied in river engineering since the 1930s. Originally developed for perennial streams, its application in arid environments, where streams are ephemeral and catchments often ungauged, relies on synthetic hydrograph methods as observed hydrographs are typically unavailable (e.g., Chow et al. 1988 ; Keppel and Renard 1962 ). Adaptive revisions, such as O’Donnell’s three-parameter Muskingum method (O’Donnell et al. 1988 ), have been successfully applied in temperate catchments. Elfeki et al., ( 2014 ) have extended the approach to arid environments to account for transmission losses. The second approach is hydraulic routing, which integrates hydraulic equations to simulate flood wave movement. In this paper, we propose a two-parameter convection–decay hydraulic (CDH) model to describe flood propagation in ephemeral streams, capturing both transmission losses and attenuation effects. The model is an extension of the previous preliminary work by Elfeki et al. ( 2014 ), where they considered CDH with limited data and showed high variability of the model parameters. In addition, they did not account for parametric uncertainty in the model results which is essential for reliable design of the mitigation structures in the region. Despite advances in Muskingum and hydraulic routing models, significant challenges remain in uncertainty quantification and physical realism for arid-zone applications (Pappenberger et al. 2006 ; Basahi et al. 2016 ). The current research underscores this gap. Nonetheless, few studies have combined transmission-loss calibration with ensemble modelling in data-scarce arid regions with ephemeral streams. Studies have employed varied approaches and calibration methods for hydrological modeling. Al-Amri et al. ( 2023a , b ) used a Monte Carlo approach to study uncertainty in the rational method for arid basins and developed analytical model based on the First Order Second Moment Methods to validate the Mote Carlo approach, while Lahmers et al. ( 2019 ) applied WRF-Hydro NWM with a Bayesian ensemble across 56 basins. Di Ciacca et al. ( 2023 ) combined remote sensing and machine learning through a random forest ensemble, and Portoghese et al. ( 2022 ) utilized Reach Length Water Balance method (RLWB) and Flow Duration Curve (FDC) with statistical infiltration estimation. Pacheco-Guerrero et al. ( 2017 ) implemented 2D hydrodynamics using dynamic loss boundary conditions, whereas Schoener ( 2022 ) focused on urban scenario modeling with transmission-loss percentage calibration. El-Shinnawy ( 1993 ) integrated KINEROS and SCS for soil moisture–adjusted infiltration, and Miller et al. ( 2005 ) conducted Nevada field experiments using flume-based direct measurements. From the review of literature, it is clear that there are some gaps that not addressed in the hydraulic flood routing under uncertainty due to the high variability in the parameters involved. Building specifically on the preliminary convection–decay formulation of Elfeki et al. ( 2014 ), which was calibrated deterministically on a limited event set without quantifying parameter uncertainty, the present study advances the approach by (1) recalibrating the convection–decay parameters ( K and λ ) against an expanded 22-event record from four gauging stations, (2) formally characterizing the probability distributions of K and λ through K–S and AIC-based distribution fitting, and (3) propagating this parameter uncertainty through 1000 Monte Carlo realizations to generate probabilistic, rather than single-valued, flood-wave predictions. This study introduces a novel integrated framework that combines: 
 
 1. 
 
 Physically based calibration of convection–decay parameters (travel time K and decay rate λ ), informed by measured hydrographs and known loss constraints. 
 
 
 
 2. 
 
 A stochastic uncertainty analysis via probabilistic parameter sampling (e.g., Monte Carlo). 
 
 
 
 3. 
 
 Evaluation against historical flood events in the Yiba catchment, Saudi Arabia, to assess model robustness and support resilient infrastructure and disaster preparedness strategies. 
 
 
 Study area Location and physiography The Yiba Basin is located in the Makkah Al-Mukarramah region of western Saudi Arabia, within the Arabian Shield (Fig.  2 ). Geographically, it lies between 19°00′ and 19°30′ N latitude and 41°30′ and 42°00′ E longitude, encompassing a total area of approximately 2346 km 2 . The basin drains westward toward the Red Sea through Wadi Yiba, which debouches onto the Tihama coastal plain near the city of Habil. The topography is characterized by rugged mountains in the east that gradually flatten toward the coastal plain in the west, resulting in a steep hydraulic gradient. The alluvial fan deposits at the downstream section of the wadi form a promising aquifer system suitable for potential groundwater development (Al-Amri et al. 2023a , b ). Figure  1 shows observed hydrograph transmission in the Yiba catchment, Saudi Arabia. Figure  1 a displays flood wave transmission between stations 422 and 401, separated by approximately 10.5 km on 21 July 1985. Figure  1 b illustrates the event between stations 423 and 424 (33.2 km) on 11 April 1985. These events clearly demonstrate transmission losses: hydrograph shape remains similar, but both volume and peak discharge are reduced at the outlet (Sorman and Abdulrazzak 1997 ; Bajabaa 1996 ). Fig. 1 Full size image Observed hydrograph transmission between two stations: top row between stations 422 and 401 for 10.5 km channel length (events: a April 16th 1986 and b April 22nd 1986), and bottom row stations 423 and 424 for 33.2 km channel length (events: c August 19th 1984 and d September 20th 1984) Climate and rainfall The climate of the Yiba Basin is classified as arid, with high temperature variability and erratic rainfall. Mean annual rainfall ranges from 100 to 300 mm, increasing from the coastal plain toward the mountainous areas. Precipitation mainly occurs during short, intense convective storms, typically associated with flash floods. The combination of sparse vegetation cover, steep slopes, and shallow soils enhances surface runoff generation during these extreme events. According to Ewea et al. ( 2016 ), most hydrological responses in the Makkah region are characterized by rapid runoff peaks following short-duration rainfall bursts. Hydrology and drainage network The drainage system of the Yiba Basin is ephemeral, typical of arid-zone wadis. Flow occurs only during storm events, while the channels remain dry for most of the year. The main wadi collects runoff from several sub-basins, each contributing variable discharge depending on storm distribution and intensity. The basin’s geomorphological features, including steep upstream slopes and broad downstream alluvial plains, facilitate both rapid runoff and localized infiltration. The infiltration through wadi beds plays a critical role in groundwater recharge, as observed in other basins of the Arabian Shield (Sorman and Abdulrazzak 1997 ; Bajabaa 1996 ; Challan 2014 ; Alodah 2023 ). Figure  2 shows the delineated sub-basins and hydrometric stations used in this study, where rainfall stations are marked in red and runoff stations in white circles with black centers. The morphometric parameters of the Yiba sub-basins are summarized in Table  1 . Fig. 2 Full size image Location, stream network and runoff stations of the Wadi Yiba Basin within the Makkah Al-Mukarramah region, western Saudi Arabia Table 1 Location and hydrological attributes of runoff gauging stations within the Yiba catchment (modified from Elfeki et al. 2014 ) Full size table Geological and hydrogeological setting Geologically, the Yiba Basin forms part of the Arabian Shield, composed mainly of Precambrian crystalline rocks such as granites, schists, and gneisses. These rocks are highly fractured and weathered, providing secondary porosity that supports limited groundwater storage in upland areas. Downstream, the Quaternary alluvial deposits form an extensive unconfined aquifer within the wadi bed and fan zones. The structural setting of the region strongly influences both surface drainage patterns and groundwater flow paths, as faults and fractures enhance subsurface percolation (Zaigham et al. 2020 ). The combination of ephemeral surface flow and permeable alluvial sediments allows periodic recharge pulses during high-intensity rainfall events, making Wadi Yiba a potential site for managed aquifer recharge studies. Methodology The methodologies adopted in this study are organized into several key sections to ensure a systematic approach. These include: (1) formulation of the advection–decay hydraulic model, which mathematically represents flood wave translation and attenuation; (2) estimation of model parameters such as wave celerity and decay factor using observed hydrograph data; (3) statistical analysis of these parameters through probability distribution fitting and goodness-of-fit tests; and (4) uncertainty quantification using Monte Carlo simulation to generate multiple realizations of outflow hydrographs. Figure  3 provides a comprehensive summary of this workflow, illustrating the sequential steps from data acquisition and parameter estimation to probabilistic modeling and performance evaluation. Fig. 3 Full size image Methodological workflow of the proposed convection–decay flood routing model Model formulation The mathematical model that describes the convection decay processes is expressed as, $$\frac{\partial Q}{dt}=-c\frac{\partial Q}{dx}-\lambda Q where-\infty <x<\infty , \text{a}\text{n}\text{d} t>0$$ 
 (1)
 where c is the wave celerity (m/hr), Q is the discharge (m 3 /s), \(\lambda \) is the decay factor (1/hr), and x and t are the space and time variables, respectively. The numerical model of Eq.  1 using a forward-in-time and backward-in-space finite difference scheme is given as follows, $$Q\left(x,t+\Delta t\right)=\left(1-{C}_{r}\right)Q\left(x,t\right)+\left(1-\lambda\Delta t\right)Q\left(x-\Delta x,t\right)$$ 
 (2)
 where \({C}_{r}=\frac{c\Delta t}{\Delta x}\) is the Courant number. Figure  3 illustrates the study workflow, beginning with data collection, followed by formulation of the flood routing model and probabilistic analysis of parameters K and λ using candidate distributions (e.g., Gamma, Exponential). Subsequently, 1,000 Monte Carlo realizations are generated for all runoff stations, and the results are evaluated by computing 5% and 95% confidence intervals and comparing simulated hydrographs with observed data. Figures  4 and 5 present detailed graphical representations of the model computations, illustrating the underlying principles and overall framework of the convection–decay hydraulic routing approach. These visualizations highlight how the model captures flood wave translation and attenuation processes, providing an intuitive understanding of its structure and operational logic (Fig. 6 ).  (3)
 where L is the length of the reach between two stations, and K is the travel time of the flood wave, which is estimated by the time lag between the starting points of the inflow and outflow hydrographs or the time between the two peaks of the hydrographs. The estimation of the decay factor for the peak flow, \(\lambda ,\) is given by Elfeki et al. ( 2014 ) as, $$ \lambda = \left[ {\frac{{\ln Q_{{1,max}} - \ln Q_{{2,max}} }}{{\rm K}}} \right] $$ 
 (4)
 where \({Q}_{1,max}\) and \({Q}_{2,max}\) are the peak flows at station I and station O, respectively. Because λ is computed directly from K together with the observed peak discharges (Eq.  4 ), the two parameters are not statistically independent by construction. Using the K and λ values reported in Table  2 for all 22 events, we calculated a Pearson correlation coefficient between K and λ of r = −0.59 (p = 0.003), confirming a significant negative association: reaches with longer travel times tend to exhibit lower decay factors. In the Monte Carlo procedure (Sect. 3.6), K and λ are nonetheless sampled independently from their respective fitted marginal distributions (Beta and Exponential, respectively); this simplification does not preserve the observed K–λ correlation and is acknowledged as a limitation of the current framework. Future refinements could employ a joint (e.g., copula-based) sampling scheme to better represent this dependency. Goodness of fit was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K-S) test and the Akaike Information Criterion ( AIC ) was applied to identify the most suitable distribution. Table 3 Probability density function (PDF) used in the analysis Full size table Statistical analysis of model parameters The Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K-S) test is applied to test the best distribution of the model parameters. The test is performed by looking at the maximum difference between the cumulative distribution of the data and the fitted theoretical distribution as given by the equation, $$K-S={max}_{i=1,\dots ,n}\left|F\left({x}_{i},\theta \right)-{F}_{n}\left({x}_{i}\right)\right|$$ 
 (5)
 where, \(F\left({x}_{i},{\varvec{\theta}}\right)\) is the theoretical distribution function with parameters \({\varvec{\theta}}\) , and \({F}_{n}\left({x}_{i}\right)\) is the empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF). Once the maximum difference is greater than the critical value, then the distribution is rejected at a given level of significance \(\left(\upalpha \right)\) , taken to be 5% Akaike information criterion (AIC) When multiple probability distributions provide an acceptable fit to the data, identifying the most appropriate one requires a quantitative comparison. The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), proposed by Laio et al. ( 2009 ), is a widely used measure for model selection. It balances goodness-of-fit with model complexity and is expressed as, $$AIC=-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}ln f\left({z}_{i}\right)+2{\varvec{\theta}}$$ 
 (6)
 where, f ( z i ) is the theoretical PDF evaluated at z i and \({\varvec{\theta}}\) is the number of parameters of the distribution. Among the tested distributions, the one with the lowest AIC value is considered the most suitable representation of the data. A lower AIC indicates that the model fits the data well while using only the necessary number of parameters. Monte Carlo method The Monte Carlo method is a stochastic technique that utilizes random numbers and probability distributions to evaluate model prediction variability. The core principle involves generating multiple realizations from the developed model, using input data sampled according to its probability distribution. The resulting outputs are analyzed to quantify prediction uncertainty and to identify the influence of input variables contributing to this uncertainty. The procedure consists of the following steps: 
 
 1. 
 
 Random numbers uniformly distributed between 0 and 1 are generated and transformed into the random variable of interest according to its probability distribution function (PDF), in this case are the model parameters K and \(\lambda \) . 
 
 
 
 2. 
 
 The generated values are introduced into the numerical scheme in Eq.  2 to produce simulated output hydrographs. By repeating the process with different random numbers, multiple realizations are obtained. A VBA macro is constructed to perform such operations. 
 
 
 
 3. 
 
 All realizations are compiled for statistical analysis. The ensemble means and confidence intervals (5% and 95%) are calculated for each outflow prediction. A total of 1000 realizations are generated using a home-made EXCEL sheet with Macro VBA. Also, summary statistics of the outputs are provided. 
 
 
 Model performance assessment using flow peak and volume metrics The root-mean-square error (RMSE) is used to evaluate the precision of the flood wave routing model results for peak discharge and runoff volume. The following equation demonstrates the RMSE used for model evaluation. $${\varvec{R}}{\varvec{M}}{\varvec{S}}{\varvec{E}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{{\varvec{N}}}\sum_{{\varvec{i}}=1}^{{\varvec{N}}}{\left({{\varvec{S}}}_{{\varvec{i}}}-{{\varvec{O}}}_{{\varvec{i}}}\right)}^{2}}$$ 
 (7)
 where N is the number of data series, O and S denote observed and simulated datasets, respectively. The 1:1 model evaluation method visually and statistically compares simulated model outputs ( \({{\varvec{S}}}_{{\varvec{i}}}\) ) against actual observed data ( \({{\varvec{O}}}_{{\varvec{i}}}\) ) to identify performance accuracy and bias. In an ideal scenario, all data points fall on the 45-degree line where S  =  O; deviations above or below this line indicate overestimation or underestimation, respectively. To quantify this performance, RMSE is used, calculated as Eq.  7 , which measures the average magnitude of the error in the same units as the data. Also, the linear association of the two datasets was quantified using Pearson’s correlation coefficient ( r ). $${\varvec{r}}=\frac{\sum_{{\varvec{i}}=1}^{{\varvec{N}}}\left({{\varvec{O}}}_{{\varvec{i}}}- \overline{{\varvec{O}} }\right)\left({{\varvec{S}}}_{{\varvec{i}}}-\overline{{\varvec{S}} }\right)}{\sqrt{\sum_{{\varvec{i}}=1}^{{\varvec{N}}}\left({{({\varvec{O}}}_{{\varvec{i}}}- \overline{{\varvec{O}} })}^{2}\right)}\sqrt{\sum_{{\varvec{i}}=1}^{{\varvec{N}}}\left({{({\varvec{S}}}_{{\varvec{i}}}- \overline{{\varvec{S}} })}^{2}\right)}}$$ 
 (8)
 where \(\overline{{\varvec{O}} }\) and \(\overline{{\varvec{S}} }\) denote the means of observed and simulated datasets, respectively. Values of r range from −1 to +1; values closer to +1 indicate a stronger positive linear association. Clarification on parameter estimation and performance evaluation: the convection–decay parameters ( K and λ ) and their probability distributions were estimated using all 22 storm events listed in Table  2 , and model performance (Figs. 10 and 11 ) was subsequently assessed against this same set of events. Because parameter estimation and performance evaluation draw on the same event set, the results reported below constitute an in-sample performance assessment of the calibrated model rather than an independent validation; the terminology has been revised accordingly throughout this section. A split-sample or leave-one-out evaluation using an independent subset of events is recommended as a direction for future work. Results and discussions Distribution of the model parameters This section illustrates how the two key parameters: travel time ( \(K\) ) and decay factor ( \(\lambda \) ) behave statistically and which probability distributions best represent their variability. It provides the foundation for uncertainty analysis in flood routing. Figure  7 illustrates the cumulative distribution function (CDF) fitting for the two key model parameters: \(K\) on the left panel and \(\lambda \) on the right, based on all watershed events. Overall, most observations for both parameters show reasonable agreement with the fitted probability distributions. The K values exhibit slight deviations across distributions, whereas λ values generally align closely, with only a few outliers falling outside the expected range. Fig. 7 Full size image Fitting CDF of the distributions in Table  3 to the model parameters Figure  8 illustrates the frequency histogram of the two key model parameters \((K\) and \(\lambda \) ) overlaid with their respective fitted probability density functions. The histograms demonstrate that the observed data generally align well with the theoretical curves, indicating that the selected distributions provide a reasonable representation of parameter variability. While minor deviations are evident in the tails, the overall fit suggests that the probabilistic models capture the central tendency and spread of the data effectively. This agreement supports the robustness of the uncertainty analysis framework and validates the use of these distributions for Monte Carlo simulations in flood routing applications. Fig. 8 Full size image Fitting histograms of the distributions of the model parameters Table 4 summarizes the Kolmogorov–Smirnov ( K–S ) test to evaluate how well a theoretical probability distribution fits observed data by comparing their cumulative distribution functions (CDFs). Table 4 represents the 5% critical value of 0.283 as the threshold for statistical significance at the 95% confidence level, therefore, the null hypotheses will be as follows: if the K–S statistic exceeds this value, the null hypothesis that the data follow the specified distribution is rejected. In this study, all K–S values for both parameters ( \(K\) and \(\lambda \) ) are below 0.283, meaning none of the candidate distributions can be rejected at the 5% level, and each provides an acceptable fit for uncertainty modeling. Table 4 Results of the K-S test of the model parameters Full size table Table 5 summarizes the Akaike Information Criterion ( AIC ) values for the two key model parameters \(K\) and \(\lambda \) across six candidate probability distributions. For parameter \(K\) , the Beta distribution yields the lowest AIC value of 45.92, suggesting it provides the most suitable representation of travel time variability. For parameter \(\lambda \) , the Exponential distribution achieves the lowest AIC value of 5.91, indicating it best captures the decay factor’s behavior. Table 5 Results of the AIC to decide for the best of PDF Full size table Exponential distribution fits the decay factor ( \(\lambda \) ) best because attenuation of peak discharge in ephemeral streams is a memoryless process, where the reduction at any point depends only on current conditions. Exponential models this behavior effectively, being defined on \([0,\infty )\) and accommodating positive, continuous values typical of decay rates. On the contrary, the Beta distribution is most suitable for travel time ( \(K\) ) because \(K\) is bounded within a finite range determined by channel length and flow velocity. Beta’s flexibility in shape and its ability to represent skewed, bounded data make it ideal for capturing variability in flood-wave travel times, outperforming unbounded alternatives like Gaussian or Exponential. These characteristics explain why Beta and Exponential achieved the lowest AIC values for \(K\) and \(\lambda \) , respectively, ensuring both statistical rigor and physical plausibility in uncertainty modeling. Because the conventional two-parameter Beta distribution is defined on [0,1] while K is reported in hours, a four-parameter (generalized) Beta distribution was fitted directly on the original scale of K , with support bounds set to the sample minimum and maximum ( K min  = 0.2 h, K max  = 11.2 h) rather than [0,1]; shape parameters were obtained by the method of moments applied to the rescaled variable ( K − K min )/( K max − K min ). The resulting fitted Beta shape parameters were α = 0.986 and β = 2.800, consistent with the AIC (45.92) and K–S statistic (0.202) reported for K in Tables 4 and 5 . Fig. 11 Full size image the comparison between simulated volume and observed volume in million cubic meters (MCM) Summary and conclusions This study introduces a physically based convection–decay hydraulic (CDH) routing model for simulating flood wave transmission in ephemeral streams of arid regions, a domain where conventional routing methods often fail due to high variability and limited data. The model was formulated using finite difference techniques and implemented on a spreadsheet platform for practical usability. Application to the Yiba catchment in western Saudi Arabia demonstrated its capability to reproduce hydrograph shape and peak discharge under real flood events. The analysis highlights two critical parameters: travel time ( K ), which governs wave translation and peak timing, and decay factor ( λ ), which controls attenuation of peak discharge and volume. For the short reach (422–401), the flood wave speed varied between (6.6 to 70 km/h), the K varied between (0.7 to 3.3 h) with λ varied between (0.1 to 1.4 h⁻ 1 ), while for the longer reach (423–424), the speed varied between (10.7 to 2.9 km/h), the K varied between (3 to 11.2 h) and λ varied between (0.005 to 0.43) h⁻ 1 , reflecting the influence of channel length on flood speed and transmission losses. The Beta and Exponential distributions were identified as the best-fitting models for K and λ , respectively, based on AIC . These selections ensure both statistical rigor and physical plausibility in representing uncertainty. The CDH model successfully attenuated and delayed the flood wave. The Monte Carlo realizations with model performance is generally acceptable (r = 0.72 and RMSE = 30.9 m3/s for peak flow and r = 0.73 and RMSE = 11.9 MCM for runoff volume), moreover the measured outflow is contained within the 90% prediction uncertainty interval for most of the events, demonstrating that the model captures the overall routing behavior of the flood in ephemeral streams. The data points for both flood peaks and volumes lie extremely close to the 1-to-1 line, this outcome is indicative of a highly successful model calibration and validation for the total volume component of the hydrological process and therefore robust model for future predictions. A major strength of this work lies in its integration of uncertainty quantification through Monte Carlo simulation, which provides confidence bounds for predictions and moves beyond deterministic calibration. This probabilistic framework enables risk-informed flood management and resilient infrastructure design, offering a practical solution for data-scarce environments.
