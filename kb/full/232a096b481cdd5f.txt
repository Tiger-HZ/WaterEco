Synergistic effects of integrated nutrient management 
on growth and yield performance of Amaranthus sp. and 
Cleome gynandra under subtropical conditions
Jefline Jennifer Kodzwa · Esther Nyaradzo Masvaya · Godwil Mirirai Madamombe · 
Justice Nyamangara
Received: 24 March 2026 / Accepted: 9 September 2026
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract
Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynandra are nutrient rich African indigenous leafy vegetables with strong poten -
tial to improve food and nutrition security under climate change, but sustainable fertility guidelines for their 
production are lacking. This study evaluated the effects of integrating cattle manure (M) and mineral nitrogen 
(N) on the growth and yield of both species over three cropping seasons under subtropical conditions in Zim -
babwe. Six treatments, control (0 N), manure (M) at 10 t ha − 1, 30 kg N ha − 1 (30 N), 60 kg N ha − 1 (60 N), 
M + 30 N and M + 60 N, were tested in a randomised complete block design replicated three times. Combining 
manure with mineral N (M + 30 N and M + 60 N) consistently produced the greatest growth, fresh biomass 
and seed yield of both species, with benefits strengthening over successive seasons. M + 30 N achieved yields 
statistically comparable to M + 60 N while using half the mineral N, giving 87.7% higher agronomic efficiency. 
Integrating cattle manure with a moderate mineral N rate is an economically and environmentally sustainable 
fertility strategy. This is the first multi season evidence of integrated soil fertility management performance 
in Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynandra, offering smallholder farmers a practical, resource efficient nutrient 
management option that supports food security in the face of climate change.

Introduction
Most Zimbabwean soils are inherently infertile1 presenting significant challenges for nutrient management espe-
cially for the resource constrained smallholder farmers who form the majority of the farming community in 
Zimbabwe and sub-Saharan Africa in general. The decline in soil fertility is due to erosion, overuse of chemical 
fertilizers, sand abstraction and deforestation 2,3. Infertile soils translate to poor crop quality and decreased pro -
ductivity4,5 whilst excessive fertilization results in nutrient losses through runoff and leaching, causing environ -
mental pollution6,7. There is therefore need for fertilizer application strategies that maximize crop productivity

while reducing environmental degradation. By implementing agroecological methods and investing in organic 
inputs, farmers can improve soil quality, productivity and resilience in the face of climate change8.
Mineral fertilizers offer readily available nutrients that stimulate rapid crop growth 9, but their high cost and 
the need for continuous application affect long term soil health and structure, with excessive use resulting in 
eutrophication after leaching and runoff10. Organic fertilizers such as livestock manure are a more affordable and 
environmentally friendly alternative, supplying organic matter and mineral nutrients that support beneficial soil 
microbes and improve soil chemical, physical and biological properties, including structure, drainage and water 
holding capacity10–13. However, organic amendments are bulky, provide imbalanced nutrient ratios, and require a 
longer time to mineralize. Despite these limitations, organic amendments remain a key component of sustainable 
fertilizer management.
Integrated Soil Fertility Management (ISFM) combines mineral fertilizer with organic amendments, as a holis-
tic approach to improve soil fertility and crop yields. ISFM ensures combined immediate and gradual nutrient 
release from mineral and organic sources to cater for both the short term and long term nutrient needs of veg -
etables14,15. ISFM provides a more reliable and adequate nitrogen (N) supply for vegetable production than either 
input applied alone16,17, and can support productivity and ecological sustainability in leafy vegetable production 
on nutrient-depleted soils while contributing to Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 1 (no poverty), 2 (zero 
hunger), 12 (responsible consumption and production) and 13 (climate action).
Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynandra are among the most widely consumed African indigenous vegetables 
(AIVs)18, valued for their high nutrient content and medicinal benefits in improving health and nutrition across 
sub-Saharan African communities 19,20. Demand for AIVs has recently surged in recognition of their superior 
nutritional qualities and adaptation to local agroecological conditions17, positioning them as an option to reduce 
poverty and promote good health, and livelihoods. However, production of Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynan-
dra remains constrained by the lack of clear production guidelines, including sustainable fertility management 
options.
Despite the recognised benefits of ISFM and of AIVs, evidence on how the benefits of combining organic 
manure with mineral N fertilizer across successive cropping seasons remains scarce, particularly for Amaranthus 
sp. and Cleome gynandra grown on the nutrient depleted granitic sandy soils typical in Zimbabwe. Most ISFM 
work is on other crops such as cereals12,16, exotic vegetables21,22 and other indigenous vegetables23,24. Prior work 
on AIVs has focused on single-season trials and sole nutrient applications 25 which did not capture the residual 
and cumulative effects of integrated amendments that are central to sustainable fertility management. The aim of 
this study was therefore to determine, over three cropping seasons, the effects of combining cattle manure with 
varying rates of mineral N fertilizer on the growth and yield performance of Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gyn-
andra under Zimbabwe’s subtropical conditions. This multi-season design provides novel evidence on (i) how 
the manure and mineral N synergy develops over time under variable rainfall, and (ii) the minimum mineral N 
rate at which this synergy plateaus, information directly actionable for resource-constrained farmers. The study 
challenges the notion that AIVs are low-input or require no inputs, showing instead that their genetic potential for 
high yields is unlocked only through specific nutrient rates and combinations. We hypothesised (H0), that there is 
no significant difference in the growth and yield performance of Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynandra between 
the integrated application of cattle manure and mineral N fertilizer and their sole applications.

Agro Industrial Park (AIP) research site (18° 23’ S and 31° 47’ E) for three consecutive cropping seasons: season 
1 (2020/21), season 2 (2021/22) and season 3 (2022/23). The experimental site is located approximately 40 km

Experimental design and treatments
The experiment was laid out in a randomised complete block design (RCBD) with six treatments, replicated 
three times. The crops were grown in permanent beds where treatments were repeated on the same plots over 
three consecutive cropping seasons. Manure (M) was applied at a rate of 10 t ha− 1 which reflects locally realistic 
application levels for smallholder farmers. Nitrogen was applied at either 0, 30 and 60 kg ha− 1 and this gave six 
different treatment combinations: control where no fertility amendments were applied (0 N), M (only manure), 
30 N (N only at 30 kg ha− 1), M + 30 N (manure and 30 kg N ha− 1), 60 N (N only at 60 kg ha− 1) and M + 60 N 
(manure and 60 kg N ha− 1).
Experimental management
The land was prepared using a tractor-drawn mouldboard plough to a depth of 0.20 m and subsequently levelled 
with hand hoes to create experimental plots measuring 2.0 m × 1.5 m. Both Amaranthus sp. ‘Madira’ and Cleome 
gynandra seed materials described above were planted at a target population of 133 333 plants ha− 1 using a spac-
ing of 0.3 × 0.5 m, maintaining two plants per station. Planting was done on the 19th of January 2021 during the 
first season, for season 2 and 3 the planting dates were 27 December 2021 and 19 of December 2022 respectively. 
Ammonium nitrate was used to supply the N, and it was split applied, with one third N and all the cattle manure 
applied as basal fertilizer at planting. The remaining two thirds N balance was applied as top dressing after thin-
ning at 3 weeks after emergence. Routine weeding and scouting for pests and diseases were done to ensure best 
management practices for the trials. Removal of flowers (deflowering) was done daily until 5 weeks after emer -
gence to allow for branching and prevent early senescence.
Data collection
The number of branches per plant was obtained from three randomly selected plants in the net plot (1 m × 0.9 m) 
at 7 weeks after emergence. The number of branches were counted, and an average of three randomly selected 
plants was recorded.
Number of leaves per branch was obtained by counting the number of leaves on one randomly selected branch 
from three different plants in the net plot at 7 weeks after emergence. The average number of leaves was calcu -
lated and recorded.
Plant height was measured using a graduated meter rule in m. Plant height was measured from the base of the 
plant on the ground to the growing tip of the plant. Measurements were taken from three randomly selected plants 
in the net plot and their mean recorded at physiological maturity.
Fresh biomass is the economic yield of fresh leaves or shoots or branches that can be consumed or sold. Fresh 
biomass from three randomly selected plants in the net plot were harvested, weighed and their mean recorded at 
7 weeks after emergence.

Seed yield was harvested at physiological maturity from three randomly selected plants. For Amaranthus sp. 
the plant panicles/tassels/head were harvested and processed. The processed seed was weighed and the three

Results
Rainfall distribution and seasonality
Rainfall received at the experimental site was highly variable in both intensity and temporal distribution across 
the 2020/21, 2021/22 and 2022/23 cropping seasons (Fig. 1). The onset of season 1 was characterised by an 
extreme, high intensity rainfall event on 8 December 2020, which delivered 97 mm of rainfall within a single 
24 h period. This day constituted a substantial amount (34%) of the month’s total moisture. Following this event,

the remainder of season 1 was characterized by highly erratic, isolated rains that limited consistent soil moisture 
availability during critical early crop vegetative stages. Season 2 (2021/22) was also characterized by more fre -
quent and relatively intense rainfall events, especially in January which increased moisture availability and the 
risk of leaching (Fig. 1). Season 3 (2022/23) on the other hand was characterized by relatively good distribution 
of rainfall during the entire cropping season, resulting in favourable growing conditions for crop development 
(Fig. 1).
Growth parameters
There was a significant interaction between fertility treatments and growing season on all growth parameters 
(branches per plant, leaves per branch and plant height) of Cleome gynandra. The application of fertility amend-
ments increased Cleome gynandra branching per plant (F10, 34 = 7.85; p < 0.001), leaves per plant (F10, 34 = 3.50; 
p = 0.002) and plant height (F10,34 = 3.73; p = 0.002) over the seasons (Table 2). Treatment M + 60 N produced the 
most Cleome gynandra branches per plant (18.33), leaves per branch (13.67) and plant height of 0.7 m during 
season 3. There was no treatment × season interaction for Amaranthus sp. but these main effects (treatment and 
season) had significant (p < 0.05) effects on the number of branches per plant, leaves per branch and plant height. 
For Amaranthus sp. M + 30 N produced the most branches per plant (23.6) whilst M + 60 N produced the most 
leaves per plant (16.2) and the tallest plants (1.19 m).
Post-hoc comparisons using Fishers LSD at p < 0.05 showed that the control treatments (0 N) produced the low-
est branches per plant, leaves per branch and the shortest plants for both vegetables across all seasons (Table 2). 
There was a general decrease in growth parameters from season 1 to season 3 showing the need for nutrient 
application in Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynandra production.
Sole applications of manure only (M) and mineral N (30 N, 60 N) significantly increased branches per plant, 
leaves per branch and plant height of both vegetables more than 0 N though these were significantly outperformed 
by the integrated treatments (M + 30 N and M + 60 N). The application of mineral N alone (30 N, 60 N) increased 
Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynandra growth parameters, with 60 N producing 13.6% and 20.1% more leaves, 
−4% and 28% branches per plant and 6.7% and 10.2% taller plants than 30 N respectively.

Discussion
Erratic rains received in January and February of season 1 limited the consistent availability of soil moisture and 
reduced manure mineralization and subsequently plant nutrient availability and plant growth (Fig. 1). In Season 
3, the absence of extreme, leaching heavy rains coupled with consistent, gentle wetting events allowed the soil 
to maintain an optimal moisture to aeration balance. These results resonate with findings by Flynn et al. (2023) 
that water availability increases nutrient dynamics, uptake and crop growth. The sustained rainfall balance maxi-
mized the microbial decomposition of the applied manure and residual organic matter, successfully triggering the 
Birch effect31 without the destructive nutrient flushing or soil structural collapse that may have been caused by 
the floods on December 8 of Season 1 (Fig. 1). In typical granitic sandy soils characteristically low in soil organic 
matter, a sudden 97 mm downpour far exceeds the soil’s infiltration capacity resulting in leaching, crusting, ero-
sion and reduced mineralisation. More frequent and slightly intense rainfall days were experienced mostly in 
January of season 2 which increased water availability but also increased the risk of nutrient leaching.
The significant interaction effect (fertility treatment × cropping season) indicates that the response of Amaran-
thus sp. and Cleome gynandra to fertility amendments was highly dependent on seasonal variability and envi -
ronmental conditions, and demonstrates that the benefits of nutrient application were not constant over time but

were modified by rainfall distribution and the cumulative residual effects of manure application across successive 
cropping seasons. This season × fertility treatment interaction is consistent with recent meta-analytic evidence 
that manure and mineral N combinations progressively build soil organic carbon and N pools across seasons, 
with yield increases being a function of rainfall driven mineralization32. The benefits of nutrient management are 
thus dynamic, not static, fluctuating between seasons with changes in rainfall distribution, soil moisture avail -
ability, and other climate factors. In addition, the differing responses of Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynandra to 
the same nutrient inputs show that crops do not respond uniformly to identical nutrient regimes where nutrient 
requirements and utilisation efficiencies differ among AIV species18. These findings underscore the need for crop 
specific, season responsive nutrient management recommendations to optimize productivity and improve the 
sustainable production of AIVs under variable subtropical conditions.
The lowest yields recorded in the control (0 N) indicate that nutrient application is necessary for the produc -
tion of Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynandra. This result confirms how severely nutrient deficient most Zim -
babwean soils are4,33. Without adequate fertilization, sustainable production of these vital vegetables is severely 
constrained, as seen in the very low branches per plant, leaves per branch and plant height recorded in the 0 N 
treatments. Fresh biomass and seed yields within the unfertilized treatments decreased significantly over succes-
sive seasons, reaching a minimum in season 3 for both Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynandra (Figs. 2 and 3). 
This multi-season decline provides clear evidence of progressive nutrient mining and depletion of baseline soil 
nutrients with each consecutive harvest, and highlights the impossibility of sustaining indigenous vegetable pro-
duction without deliberate fertilizer inputs. Nevertheless, the distinct increase in growth parameters over the sea-
sons (Tables 2 and 3) in fertilised plots can also be attributed to the residual effect of nutrient amendments from 
the initial application and the cumulative positive effect of repeated fertilizer applications on soil fertility15,16.
Sole mineral N treatments (30 N and 60 N) exhibited reduced biomass and seed yields compared to integrated 
nutrient management plots (Figs. 2 and 3). This reduction is likely linked to the adverse impacts of continuous 
sole inorganic fertilizer applications on plant quality and crop productivity 10,17, which affects the overall plant 
productivity resulting in very low yields. Though, further studies are required as these variations in growth over 
seasons may be attributed to various factors such as temperature variability, changing growing conditions over 
time, better plant adaptation in later seasons.
Increasing the N application rate from 30 N to 60 N improved yield and growth parameters for both vegetables 
in all seasons, demonstrating a positive dose-response relationship for N in Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynan-
dra growth. This is because mineral fertilizers provide readily available nutrients that quickly boost growth 9 by 
promoting cell elongation and biomass accumulation, consistent with Orangi et al. (2020) who reported a similar 
trend of N application increasing growth of indigenous vegetables. However, the benefit of mineral fertiliser is 
short-lived owing to the limited soil organic matter and low cation-exchange capacity that characterise most gra-
nitic sandy soils, so sole N fertiliser application offers no long-term efficiency or soil fertility improvement 10,13. 
This was reflected in the significant drop in leaf and branch number and plant height during season 3 (Table 2) in 
the sole N treatments. The lack of organic matter under continuous mineral N application suppresses key micro-
bial processes such as ammonification and nitrification, reducing N retention and soil biological health10 Recent 
studies confirms that balanced mineral and organic fertilization, rather than mineral fertilizer alone, sustains 
greater soil microbial biomass and diversity and therefore more effective nutrient cycling and retention32,34,35. For 
this reason, applied N in sole mineral fertilizer treatments is possibly prone to leaching losses, reducing long term 
N availability to crops compared with M treatments, which improve soil quality over time. Sole mineral fertilizer 
application, cannot be held effectively by granitic sandy soils, which have limited capacity to retain ammonium 
and other cations2,3.
While the application of manure alone (M) may seem insufficient in the early seasons, as indicated by the lower 
fresh biomass and seed yields (Figs. 2 and 3; Table 2) in season 1 compared with season 3, this supports the idea 
that manure acts as a “slow release” nutrient source holding a wide range of nutrients over a longer time, with an 
associated build-up of organic matter. Manure application also enhances microbiological properties and promotes 
the beneficial microbes that cycle nutrients and decompose organic matter11 over time, hence the increase in fresh

The integration of manure with moderate N application (30–60 N) is a viable strategy for enhancing the growth 
performance of AIVs. ISFM combines the benefit of manure as a slow-release source of a wide range of nutrients 
(N, P, K, etc.) with the mineral fertilizer’s benefit of providing an immediate, readily available nutrient boost 
that may be lacking during critical growth stages or when manure mineralization is slow 10,16,36. These findings 
resonate with those of Mugwe et al. (2019) and Parwada and Chipomho (2023), who reported a complementary, 
synergistic effect from using organic and mineral fertilizers together to improve crop productivity. Similar syn -
ergistic responses to integrated nutrient management have recently been reported for other AIVs receiving com-
bined organic and alternative nutrient sources, reinforcing the applicability of these findings across AIV species 
and production systems21,23,38,39.
The lack of significant differences in growth parameters and yield between M + 60 N and M + 30 N is impor-
tant when considering resource availability among local farmers and the environmental sustainability of AIV 
production in Zimbabwe. Since M + 60 N and M + 30 N were statistically similar, it is more economically viable 
to apply M + 30 N, as it produces comparable yields at a lower inorganic fertilizer rate. This was confirmed by 
the AE results, where, despite M + 60 N producing the greatest fresh yield, the maximum AE was found in the 
M + 30 N treatment. This indicates that integrating manure with a moderate inorganic N boost optimizes nutrient 
uptake in Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynandra without the nutrient waste associated with higher application 
rates. Similar yields between M + 60 N and M + 30 N suggest a nutrient use efficiency plateau, at which the plant 
reaches its optimum nutrient uptake capacity and cannot productively use additional available nutrients 40. This 
implies that the extra N applied in M + 60 N did not translate into additional yield and may instead have been 
lost through leaching or immobilization. Reaching this plateau marks the point at which marginal costs outweigh 
marginal returns, making the lower rate (M + 30 N) the more sustainable and profitable choice for farmers relative 
to M + 60 N.
Implications for farmers
ISFM practices not only improve immediate crop yields but also contribute to long-term soil fertility, an impor -
tant consideration for smallholder farming systems in Zimbabwe and similar agro-ecological regions. The perfor-
mance of M + 30 N and M + 60 N, particularly in the later seasons (2 and 3), underscores the cumulative benefits 
of such integrated strategies (Tables 2 and 3; Figs. 2 and 3). This suggests that manure contributes not only 
nutrients but also improves soil properties and enhances the efficiency of mineral fertilizer over time. The ISFM 
practice has potential for long-term agricultural sustainability and resilience in the face of climate change, as it 
builds the soil health of Zimbabwe’s nutrient-deficient soils.
These results are pertinent across sub-Saharan Africa, where access to synthetic fertilizers is limited and 
enhancing soil fertility through organic amendments can provide both economic and ecological benefits. The 
study suggests that ISFM (M + 30 N and M + 60 N) is a sustainable agricultural practice that reduces dependency 
on costly inorganic fertilizers while improving soil fertility and the productivity of Amaranthus sp. and Cleome 
gynandra. M + 30 N in particular offers a sustainable, risk-averse option that balances productivity and afford -
ability for resource-constrained smallholder farmers. Long-term adoption of ISFM can strengthen food security, 
promote agroecological and climate resilience, and reduce reliance on synthetic inputs whilst contributing to 
achieving SDGs.

Conclusions
This study provides the first multi-season evidence that integrating cattle manure with a moderate mineral N 
rate sustainably improves the growth and yield of Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynandra, with the manure and 
mineral N synergy strengthening across successive cropping seasons and plateauing in nutrient-use efficiency 
beyond 30 kg N ha− 1.
Combining cattle manure (10 t ha − 1) with 30 kg N ha− 1 (M + 30 N) is agronomically effective, economically 
efficient and environmentally sustainable, achieving yields statistically comparable to the higher M + 60 N rate 
while requiring half the mineral N input. We recommend that farmers on similar granitic sandy soils adopt 
M + 30 N as a practical, resource-efficient fertility strategy for Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynandra production.
Limitations
While this study provides evidence for the benefits of ISFM in Amaranthus sp. and Cleome gynandra production, 
several limitations should be acknowledged. Moisture content of manure was not determined prior to applica -
tion although all manure used in the experiment was obtained from the same source and handled uniformly. The 
high seasonal rainfall variability and the inherent genetic variability of these underutilised species mean that 
results should be interpreted within the context of the specific soil and climatic conditions of the Marondera 
region. Future research should prioritise long-term soil health monitoring beyond three seasons, characterisation 
of microbial community responses to combined organic-mineral amendments, and formal economic and cost-
benefit analysis beyond agronomic efficiency, to fully establish the long-term sustainability and profitability of 
integrated soil fertility management for African indigenous vegetables.
