Temporal assessment of N‑cycling genes 
and nutrient‑acquiring enzymes under rotational grazing
Eloa M. Araujo · Isabella C. F. Maciel · 
Maira L. Sparks · Guilhermo F. S. Congio · 
Erin E. Oliver · Myoung‑Hwan Chi
Received: 6 January 2026 / Accepted: 7 September 2026 
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract Despite the importance of microbial 
activity for the sustainability of grazing systems, 
longitudinal assessments of microbial responses to 
grazing remain scarce. We monitored functional 
N-cycling genes and C-, N-, and P-acquiring extra-
cellular enzyme activities across two contrasting 
systems: Native Rangeland (mixed prairie veg-
etation) and Introduced Pasture [Cynodon dactylon 
(L.) Pers.]. Two grazing strategies were compared: 
Adaptive (AD; higher stocking density with vari-
able rest periods) and Prescriptive (PR; lower stock -
ing density with fixed rest periods) across three 
years. Strong temporal variation was evident across 
genes and enzyme activities, with greater values 
in 2022 compared to 2023 and 2024. Overall, graz-
ing affected the nitrate reduction and denitrification 
pathways. In Native Rangeland, the abundance of 
napA was up to 57% greater under AD, narG up to 
twofold greater under PR, and nirS showing incon-
sistent grazing treatment effects. In Introduced Pas-
tures, nirS was up to 57% greater under PR, whereas 
nirK showed inconsistent responses. Grazing greatly 
affected enzyme activity in Native Rangeland under 
AD, with C-enzymes up to 175%, N-enzymes up to 
271%, and P-enzymes up to 216% compared to PR. 
In Introduced Pastures the greatest differentiation 
was for N-enzymes, with PR exhibiting two-fold the 
activity of AD. Overall, our results show that graz-
ing mediates microbial nutrient acquisition in a con-
text-dependent manner. These findings reinforce the 
importance of accounting for site-specific conditions 
when applying grazing strategies and interpreting soil 
microbial responses.

Introduction
Grazing management is a key driver of soil micro-
bial functioning in grassland ecosystems because it 
regulates plant biomass removal (Souther et al. 2019), 
animal-derived nutrient returns (Bai et al. 2012; Aba-
gandura et  al. 2024), and soil physical disturbance 
(Pereira Valani et  al. 2025). Grazing management 
is characterized by factors such as stocking method, 
stocking density, grazing frequency, and rest period, 
which influence microbial activity by controlling tim-
ing and intensity of defoliation, trampling, and nutri-
ent inputs to the soil (Van Syoc et al. 2022; Feng et al. 
2025). Unlike continuous or prescriptive grazing 
methods that follows a pre-stablished calendar rota-
tion, adaptive multi-paddock (AMP) grazing involves 
moving the herd between paddocks according to for -
age growth and animal nutritional needs (Teague 
et  al. 2013; Mehre et  al. 2024). Additionally, AMP 
may involve greater stocking density during a shorter 
period (Van Syoc et al. 2022).
Overall, grazing effects on microbial activity may 
occur through direct pathways such as trampling, 
defoliation, and excreta deposition (Yao et al. 2023), 
as well as indirect pathways mediated by changes in 
plant community composition (Sparks et  al. 2026) 
and soil structure (Byrnes et  al. 2018). These direct 
and indirect pathways can alter substrate availability 
and soil microenvironments, ultimately shaping the 
microbial community composition and activity (Van 
Syoc et al. 2022; Taylor et al. 2025; Meléndez-Aldana 
et al. 2025). Previous studies showed that AMP graz-
ing increased soil microbial populations and nutrient 
cycling (White et al. 2023) and soil organic C stocks 
(Mehre et  al. 2024) in comparison to conventional 
(low to moderate stocking densities with infrequent 
cattle movement) or continuous grazing.
Grazing-induced changes in soil microbial com-
munities are associated with shifts in the abundance 
of functional genes, resulting in increased N min-
eralization and nitrification while decreasing deni-
trification compared to non-grazed systems (Yang 
et  al. 2013). Similarly, Liu et  al. (2023) compared 
non-grazed and grazed soils and reported that graz-
ing-induced changes in microbial functional genes 
abundance can alter soil P solubilization and miner -
alization. Although gene abundance may not directly 
reflect potential enzyme activity, as only a portion of 
the genetic pool is actively expressed at a given time 
(Enwall et al. 2010), soil microbial functional genes 
are highly sensitive to management (Jia et  al. 2025; 
Li et al. 2025). These studies showed that soil micro-
bial functional genes can provide a more detailed 
and mechanistic understanding of soil processes and 
microbial functioning than traditional biochemi-
cal indicators alone. While functional genes indicate 
the potential for microbial transformations, extracel-
lular enzymes provide a complementary measure of 
microbial nutrient acquisition (Ndabankulu et  al. 
2022; Daunoras et al. 2024). Because these enzymes 
respond directly to microbial metabolic requirements, 
their activity and stoichiometry provide valuable 
insights into soil fertility, nutrient limitations, and 
overall microbial functioning (Daunoras et  al. 2024; 
Morales-Salmerón et al. 2025).
Grazing represents a major disturbance in grass-
land ecosystems and can alter enzyme activity, 
although the magnitude and direction of these effects 
often depend on vegetation characteristics, including 
growth patterns and photosynthetic pathways (Wang 
et al. 2023a; Feng et al. 2025). Overall, heavy grazing 
reduces plant biomass and litter input, thereby limit-
ing nutrient recycling and microbial nutrient supply, 
which can deplete soil C, N, and P pools and restrict 
the substrates available for enzyme production (Ji 
et  al. 2014; Tang et  al. 2019; Bai et  al. 2021; Feng 
et  al. 2025). Consequently, enzyme activities often 
decline with increasing grazing intensity; however, 
long-term grazing may lead to microbial and enzy -
matic adaptations that partially offset these negative 
effects over time (Feng et  al. 2025). Although the 
suitability of extracellular enzyme activity meas-
urements as proxies for overall microbial activity 
remains debated, these assays serve as useful early 
indicators of shifts in microbial nutrient cycling in 
response to grazing management (Luo et  al. 2017; 
Zhang et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2025).
Although grazing can have long-term effects on 
microbial functioning, climatic factors are the major 
drivers of seasonal variation in soil microbial activ -
ity and nutrient cycling (Bai et  al. 2021). Seasonal 
shifts in soil temperature and moisture alter microbial 
metabolism, growth rates, and substrate availability, 
thereby influencing the rates of key biogeochemical 
processes such as decomposition, nutrient miner -
alization, and extracellular enzyme production (Szu-
kics et al. 2010; Waghmode et al. 2018; Bytnerowicz 
et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2023; Daunoras et al. 2024).

Grazing can further interact with these climate con-
ditions to differentially affect extracellular enzyme 
activity and, consequently, SOM decomposition 
(Rajper et al. 2024). Given these interacting temporal 
and management effects, longitudinal studies, which 
involve observations over multiple time points, are 
particularly valuable for understanding how grazing 
alters microbial functioning within dynamic environ-
mental contexts (Van Syoc et al. 2022; Li et al. 2022; 
Bastani et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2025). This 3-yr study 
evaluated the effect of different grazing strategies on 
N-cycling genes abundance and enzyme activities in 
both native and introduced systems in the southern 
Great Plains. We hypothesized that greater stocking 
densities, followed by longer rest periods under adap-
tive grazing would alter microbial functioning rela-
tive to prescriptive grazing management, resulting in 
distinct patterns of N-cycling genes abundance and 
extracellular enzyme activities across grazing treat-
ments. Understanding how grazing affects microbial 
functionality, while accounting for seasonal variabil-
ity, can provide a better understanding of its impact 
on soil health, nutrient cycling, and overall ecosystem 
functioning. Because the studied systems differ in 
soil, vegetation, and land-use history, grazing effects 
were evaluated within each system independently.
Material and methods
Study area
This study was conducted on two ranches at the Noble 
Research Institute, in the southern Oklahoma—
USA: Coffey Ranch (33.9384° N, − 97.219147° 
W) and Red River Grazing Facility (33.896825° N, 
− 97.272948° W). The climate is classified as Cfa 
according to the Köppen climate classification system 
(Kottek et al. 2006), representing a humid subtropical 
condition, characterized by hot and humid summers, 
and cool to mild winters. Historical mean annual tem-
perature is 17 °C, with a bimodal rainfall distribution 
peaking in May–June, and September–October, with 
an annual accumulated precipitation of 967 mm (Fie-
brich and Illston, 2025). According to NOAA (2021), 
precipitation occurs on approximately 68  days per 
year, with the greatest frequency of precipitation 
during spring (21  days) and Summer (19  days), fol-
lowed by Autumn (15  days) and Winter (13  days).
Coffey Ranch is a native prairie rangeland with a 
mixed plant community. Common species include 
little bluestem [Schizachyrium scoparium (Michx.) 
Nash], Indiangrass [Sorghastrum nutans (L.) Nash], 
broomsedge bluestem (Andropogon virginicus L.), 
western ragweed (Ambrosia psilostachya DC.), pur -
pletop [Tridens flavus (L.) Hitchc.], and white heath 
aster [Symphyotrichum ericoides (L.) G.L. Nel-
som]. In contrast, Red River Grazing Facility was 
formerly cropland and has since been converted to 
an introduced pasture system. From 1980 to 2017, 
this site was intensively cropped with peanuts (Ara -
chis hypogaea L.) and cereal rye (Secale cereale L.), 
including conventional use of tillage, fertilization, 
pesticide application, and irrigation. In 2017, the site 
was planted with bermudagrass [Cynodon dactylon 
(L.) Pers.] for hay production and continuously ferti-
lized and pesticide treated until 2020. From late 2020 
to until June 2022, this site was transitioned from 
hay production to being directly grazed. During this 
time, cover crops were introduced, pastures were rota-
tionally grazed, and fertilizer and pesticide use was 
reduced but not eliminated. After June 2022, fertilizer 
and pesticide applications were discontinued, distin-
guishing current management from typical bermud-
agrass production systems. Predominant plant spe-
cies are bermudagrass [Cynodon dactylon (L.) Pers.], 
along with spring annuals such as vetch (Vicia spp.) 
and cereal rye (Secale cereale L.). Common native 
species include sixweeks fescue [Vulpia octoflora 
(Walter) Rydb.], marestail [Conyza canadensis (L.) 
Cronquist] and tropic croton (Croton glandulosus L.). 
The soils are classified as Mollisols; at Coffey Ranch, 
the predominant soil series include Durant, Scullin, 
Slaughterville, and Teller, whereas at the Red River 
Grazing Facility, the Slaughterville series is the pre-
dominant according to USDA Soil Taxonomy (Soil 
Survey Staff, 2025). To better represent the contrast 
between the ranches, henceforth, Coffey Ranch will 
be referenced as “Native Rangeland”, and Red River 
Grazing Facility will be “Introduced Pasture”. More 
details of the vegetation and management history of 
both ranches can be found in Sparks et al. (2026).
Experimental design
Six pastures (averaging 9.7–12.4  ha each) were 
selected to be monitored over time on each ranch. 
The experiment was conducted using a randomized

complete block design with three replicate blocks 
based on ecological site descriptions to account for 
potential variability across experimental units. Each 
block was a split-plot design where the grazing treat-
ments were randomly assigned. Two grazing treat-
ments were implemented: an adaptive (AD) and a 
prescriptive (PR) approach, designed to evaluate 
flexible versus fixed rotational management. AD is 
characterized by high density short-duration stocking 
[similar to what was described by Allen et al. (2011) 
as mob stocking, or AMP as described by Teague and 
Kreuter (2020)] associated with variable rest periods. 
PR grazing is characterized by low density medium-
duration stocking and fixed rest period during the 
growing season, usually between April and Novem-
ber (75-days rest period for Native Rangeland and 
60-days for Introduced Pasture). Grazing days varied 
based on forage availability and season. When forage 
availability was less than the target (1,680 kg  ha−1 of 
dry matter), then the plots would be deferred beyond 
the usual rest period (usually during winter season), 
until they could be grazed again in spring of the fol-
lowing year. The stocking density was calculated for 
each grazing event, following Allen et al. (2011).
In each ranch, both AD and PR were grazed by two 
different herds of Angus-cross cow-calf pairs com-
posed of the same number of animals and similar live-
weights. The AD plots were subdivided into smaller 
paddocks to allow for the use of greater stocking 
densities, and cattle were moved daily, with the tar -
get of achieving a minimum stocking density ten-fold 
greater than PR. Both grazing treatments had a target 
of 50% forage utilization and at least 1,100 kg  ha−1 of 
dry matter target residual biomass. The herds grazed 
additional areas of the ranches while not in the study 
areas.
This study was performed from June 2022 through 
December 2024. To standardize soil assessments over 
time, three permanent sampling areas (i.e. triangle; 
140 m2) were established in each pasture prior to the 
grazing initiation and maintained for the duration of 
the study.
Soil sampling and analysis
Soil samples (0–15  cm depth) were collected with 
a 1.9-cm diameter soil probe (JMC Soil Samplers, 
Iowa, USA) following the grazing schedule: in 
Native Rangeland, with slower forage recovery 
rates, samples were collected 30 days after the graz-
ing event (4 replicates per triangle, totaling 12 sam-
ples per pasture); in Introduced Pasture, samples 
were collected 15  days after the grazing event (2 
replicates per triangle, totaling 6 samples per pas-
ture). Each soil sample was composed of nine soil 
cores sub-sampled randomly within the triangles 
and stored at 4  °C until processing. Samples were 
then homogenized, sieved, and segregated into two 
portions for further analyses: soil characterization 
and enzyme activity (kept at 4 °C) and DNA extrac-
tion (kept at − 80  °C) following sample handling 
and pretreatment guidelines (Dick 2011). Samplings 
started in June 2022 (summer) and occurred three 
times per year during the growing season [spring 
(March–May), summer (June–August), and fall 
(September–November)] through 2024, totaling 
eight sampling events.
For soil characterization, soil samples were sent 
to Regen Ag Lab, LLC (Pleasanton, Nebraska, US) 
where the sample was air-dried and sieved (2 mm). 
Soil pH was determined using the potentiometric 
method (soil–water ratio 1:1) (Watson and Brown 
1988) and soil organic matter (SOM) by loss of 
ignition (Combs and Nathan 1998). For soil respira-
tion, samples were incubated for 24 h at 24 °C, and 
the carbon dioxide produced was quantified in SR-1 
Soil Respiration System created by Soil Health 
Innovations, LLC [Pleasanton, NE, USA (Haney 
et al. 2008)]. Water extractable organic carbon, and 
organic nitrogen were determined in elemental ana-
lyzer Vario TOC-TN (Haney et al. 1999), then used 
to calculate soil dissolved organic C to dissolved 
organic N ratio (C:N). Nitrate and ammonium were 
extracted with potassium chloride and determined 
colorimetrically (Haney et  al. 2006). Soil samples 
were also treated with organic acids (malic, cit-
ric, and oxalic). From the slurry, total phosphorus 
was determined using Inductively Coupled Plasma 
spectroscopy, inorganic phosphorus (IP) was deter -
mined colorimetrically, and organic phosphorus 
(OP) was calculated by subtracting the inorganic 
fraction from total phosphorus (Haney et al. 2006). 
Soil gravimetric water content (GWC) was deter -
mined using the drying method (105 °C, 24 h). The 
descriptive analysis of the soil parameters at the 
beginning of the study can be found in Table S1.

DNA extraction and N-cycling genes determination
Soil DNA was extracted from 500  mg of soil using 
the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, 
Maryland, USA). The extracted DNA was quantified 
using the Qubit dsDNA HS and BR Assay Kits (Inv -
itrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Quantitative 
PCR (qPCR) was performed to quantify levels of N 
cycling genes, using 16S and ITS primers (Table S2). 
Reactions were performed in a 10 µl volume contain-
ing 2 µM of each primer, approximately 2 µg of total 
soil DNA (3 µl) and 5 µl of 2× Forget-Me-Not™ Eva-
Green® qPCR Master Mix (Biotum, Fremont, CA). 
qPCR was run on the QuantStudio 7 flex System 
(Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). Amplifica-
tion specificity was checked with a dissociation curve 
acquired by heating the samples from 60 to 95  °C. 
Normalization and comparison of mean threshold 
cycle (Ct) values were performed as described by 
(Livak and Schmittgen 2001). To compare relative 
abundance of target genes, the mean Ct of triplicate 
reactions was normalized by that of 16S gene. The 
selected N-cycling genes are involved in the process 
of N fixation (nifH), ammonia oxidation (amoA), and 
denitrification (napA, narG, nirK, nirS, and nosZ).
Extracellular enzyme activities and stoichiometry
C, N, and P cycling extracellular enzymes were quan-
tified fluorometrically following Bell et al. (2013) and 
German et al. (2011). Soil samples were collected and 
kept at 4 °C for 15 days prior to analysis. Briefly, 2 g 
of field-moist soil was blended with 50 mL of 50 mM 
sodium acetate at pH 6 (average pH of all soil sam-
ples). The suspensions (soil slurry + substrate) were 
incubated at 250  rpm at 20  °C for four hours. Fol-
lowing incubation, 20 µL of 0.5 M NaOH was added 
to the plates. Fluorescent assays of α-glucosidase 
(AG), β-glucosidase (BG), β-D-cellubiosidase (CB), 
β-xylosidase (XYL), leucine aminopeptidase (LAP), 
N-acetyl-β-glucosaminidase (NAG), and phosphatase 
(PHOS) were read at an excitation wavelength of 
360 nm and an emission wavelength of 450 nm using 
a BioTek Synergy HTX reader (BioTek, Inc., Win-
ooski, VT, USA).
The above-listed enzymes are involved in reac-
tions that produce assimilable C and nutrients from 
C-, N-, and P-rich organic matter (Sinsabaugh et  al. 
2008), which are considered as appropriate indicators 
of overall C, N, and P acquisition (Wang et al. 2023a, 
b). Therefore, C-enzymes comprise the sum of AG, 
BG, CB, and XYL activities; N-enzymes the sum of 
LAP and NAG activities; and PHOS represents the 
P-enzymes (Cui et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2020; Massac-
cesi et  al. 2023; Sinsabaugh and Shah 2011; Zhang 
et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2025). The enzyme stoichiom-
etry was calculated using enzymatic ratios (Eqs. 1–3), 
as an indication of enzymatic resources directed 
towards acquisition of organic N and organic P rela-
tive to C, and organic P relative to N (Sinsabaugh 
et al. 2008):
where ln(AG + BG + CB + XYL) is the natural log of 
the sum of C-acquiring enzymes, ln(LAP + NAG ) is 
the natural log of the sum of N-acquiring enzymes, 
and lnPHOS is the natural log of the P-acquiring 
enzyme. According to Sinsabaugh et  al. (2008), 
C:N:P ratio of enzyme activities remains near 1:1:1, 
therefore, a value of 1 can be used as the threshold for 
identifying potential nutrient limitations.
Data analysis
All statistical analyses were conducted using R soft-
ware version 4.2.1 (R Core Team 2022). Potential 
outliers were first assessed using boxplots grouped by 
grazing treatment and sampling time. Outliers were 
identified within each grazing treatment–time com-
bination using the Tukey interquartile range (IQR) 
method and removed prior to analysis. Data distri-
bution was inspected visually through histograms 
and Q–Q plots and statistically evaluated with the 
Shapiro–Wilk test. Generalized linear mixed models 
(GLMMs) were fitted for each variable using Gamma 
family [glmer function from the “lme4” package 
(Bates et  al. 2015)]. Fixed effects included graz-
ing, season, year, and their interactions. Gravimet-
ric water content was used as a covariate. Random 
effects accounted for nested experimental structure

and repeated measures, with sampling locations 
nested within blocks. Model residuals were evalu-
ated for normality (Q–Q plots of Pearson residuals), 
homoscedasticity (Pearson residuals vs. fitted values), 
and overdispersion. When applicable (p < 0.05), post-
hoc comparisons of estimated marginal means were 
conducted using the emmeans package, with pairwise 
contrasts adjusted by Bonferroni correction [emmeans 
function from “emmeans” package (Lenth 2022)]. 
Among the tested variables, nirS (from the Introduced 
Pasture samples only) required additional transforma-
tion prior to the modeling. Based on the models, the 
p-values from the individual factors (grazing, sea-
son, and year) as well as their interactions are pre-
sented in Table S3. To evaluate the contributions of 
random and fixed effects to the overall variability of 
the tested variable, marginal R2, conditional R2, ΔR2 
(marginal—conditional), and intraclass correlation 
coefficient (ICC) were estimated [r2_nakagawa and 
icc functions from “performance” package (Lüdecke 
et  al. 2019)] and presented in Table  S4. To repre-
sent the variables according to the significance of 
the interactions, we applied two formats: non signifi-
cant variance and three-way interactions, the variable 
was plotted over the sampling times, and the grazing 
effect was highlighted; and variables that presented 
significant two-way interaction (and non-significant 
three-way interaction) were presented in tables. Some 
gene abundances were presented on different y-axis 
scales across sampling periods due to differences 
in magnitude; as a result, lines are not connected 
between periods. The individual main effects were 
not discussed when three- or two-way interactions 
were significant. Although the Season × Year interac-
tion was tested, those results are not presented since 
the primary focus is on grazing treatment effects.
Distance-based redundance analysis (dbRDA) was 
performed to test the relationship between gene abun-
dances, soil biochemical parameters, and stocking 
density using vegan package (Oksanen et  al. 2025). 
Bray–Curtis dissimilarities were calculated from the 
gene abundance matrix; a stepwise selection was 
applied to select a subset of parameters. The final 
dbRDA model retained the subset of predictors that 
significantly explained variation in gene composition, 
while controlling temporal and soil moisture effects. 
Model significance was assessed using permutation 
ANOVA (overall and by term, number of permuta-
tions = 999), and variance inflation factors (vif.cca) 
were examined to diagnose multicollinearity among 
retained predictors. The association between soil bio-
chemical parameters, stocking density, and enzyme 
activities (C-, N-, and P-enzymes) were analyzed 
using Bayesian multilevel generalized linear mixed 
models, family Gaussian [brm function from “brms” 
package (Bürkner 2015)], to account for temporal 
and spatial dependencies. Each enzyme category was 
modeled separately as a gamma-distributed response 
with a log link to accommodate strictly positive and 
right-skewed values. Fixed effects included stand-
ardized soil predictors selected based on correlation 
and collinearity analyses. Random intercepts were 
included for sampling time and pasture to account for 
temporal and spatial variation. Weakly informative 
priors were applied, including a horseshoe prior for 
fixed effects to encourage sparsity, normal priors for 
the intercepts, and exponential priors for the standard 
deviation of random effects. Models were fit using 
four Markov chains, and convergence was assessed 
through the potential scale reduction factor and effec-
tive sample size. Model adequacy was further evalu-
ated using posterior predictive checks, including 
comparisons of observed versus simulated distribu-
tions, posterior predictive residuals, and the coverage 
of credible intervals.

Discussion
This study evaluated the impact of grazing manage-
ment on N-cycling genes and C-, N-, P- acquiring 
enzymes over three years in two contrasting pasture 
systems: Native Rangeland and Introduced Pasture. 
We hypothesized that AD grazing, characterized by 
greater stocking densities and longer rest periods, 
would enhance microbial functioning compared to 
PR (lower stocking densities with fixed rest periods 
during the growing season). Our results revealed 
complex and system-specific responses. In Native 
Rangeland, AD generally promoted greater micro-
bial gene abundance and enzyme activity, particu-
larly during spring and summer seasons, with notable 
increases in napA and N-enzymes, compared to PR. 
However, some genes (e.g., nirS) showed shifts over 
time, with greater abundance in AD than PR in early 
stages of the study, but the opposite in subsequent 
years. In Introduced Pasture, PR supported greater 
nirS abundance and N-enzyme activity. Enzyme stoi-
chiometric ratios further highlighted shifts in micro-
bial nutrient acquisition strategies, with AD favoring 
N and P acquisition in Native Rangeland and show -
ing late-stage improvements in C and P acquisition 
in Introduced Pasture. These findings underscore the 
importance of land-use, seasonal dynamics, and long-
term monitoring in evaluating grazing impacts on soil 
microbial processes.
From summer 2022 to fall 2024, we observed an 
overall decline in the abundance of N-cycling genes 
and enzyme activity. During this period, the studied 
area was under severe drought for several consecu-
tive months, mostly during 2022 and 2023 (Sparks 
et al. 2026). For example, from summer 2022 through 
fall 2024, summers presented rainfall averages 123% 
lower than historical average, while spring 2023 was 
15% lower, and falls were close to historical aver -
ages. While microbial functionality was high in 2022, 
it dropped significantly in 2023 and remained low 
through 2024. This pattern aligns with recent findings 
suggesting that drought-induced N₂O emissions fol-
low an inverted U-shaped response curve post rewet-
ting (Barrat et  al. 2022), where moderate droughts 
can stimulate microbial activity and N₂O emissions, 
but prolonged droughts suppress microbial function 
due to stress-induced cell death and lysis (Clark and 
Hirsch 2008; Meisner et  al. 2015; Schimel 2018). 
Microbial community may utilize a two-stage dor -
mancy strategy, storing osmolytes during dry periods 
and catabolizing them upon rewetting. However, at 
prolonged negative water potentials this strategy is 
no longer effective, and microbes enter a deeper state 
of dormancy, resulting in a community that can no 
longer respond rapidly to changes in water availabil-
ity (Barrat et al. 2022). The lack of recovery in gene

Changes in N-genes abundance under grazing
Significant differences between AD and PR were 
more commonly observed in the dissimilatory nitrate 
reduction and denitrification pathway (napA, narG, 
nirK, and nirS). NapA encodes the periplasmic nitrate 
reductase, an enzyme involved in the dissimilatory 
nitrate reduction pathway that ultimately generates 
 NH4
+ (Asamoto et al. 2021; He et al. 2025). In this 
study, napA abundance was consistently greater under 
AD than PR in Native Rangeland. This pattern may 
reflect the more dynamic redox environment under 
AD grazing, where greater root and litter inputs 
stimulate microbial respiration and transient oxy -
gen depletion (Chen et  al. 2016; Zhou et  al. 2017).

can operate as nitrate scavenger in a redox-balanc-
ing mechanism in both aerobic and micro-anaerobic 
environments (Asamoto et al. 2021). Ellington et al. 
(2002) demonstrated that napA abundance increases 
when reduced C sourced creates a redox imbalance, 
allowing cells to use nap pathway as an alternative 
electron sink under limited oxygen conditions. Wu 
et al. (2021) reported that organic amendments such

grazing events influence N accumulation in the soil. 
According to Gourley et  al. (2025), when urine is

reducing N uptake, and increasing nitrate accumula-

grazing occurred consecutively once per season dur -

similar frequency between the two grazing treatments

frequency in PR may have led to more frequent nutri-
ent inputs, particularly during limited plant uptake 
periods, potentially contributing to elevated nitrate 
levels and stimulating narG abundance. Corrobora-
tively, stocking density was a significant predictor of 
variance in gene abundance in Native Rangeland.
The nirK and nirS genes are commonly used as 
markers to quantify denitrifying microorganisms 
(Ouyang et  al. 2018; Song et  al. 2020) and are sen-
sitive to agricultural management. Although both 
encode functionally similar nitrate reductases, differ -
ences in their abundance reflect distinct habitat pref-
erences and functional traits, suggesting that nirK- 
and nirS-type denitrifiers occupy different ecological 
niches (Enwall et al. 2010). In this study, nirS showed 
significant grazing treatment effect in both ranches, 
whereas nirK only showed significance in Introduced 
Pasture, indicating that the response of these genes 
to grazing was site-specific. Within Introduced Pas-
ture, nirS was consistently greater under PR than AD, 
while responses to grazingin the Native Rangeland 
were inconsistent. In contrast, nirK showed sparse 
and inconsistent grazing treatment effects. These 
results align with previous findings showing that 
edaphic factors can shape the distribution and dynam-
ics of denitrifying communities (Enwall et  al. 2010; 
Yang et al. 2025).
White et  al. (2023) reported that nirK was 113% 
more abundant under high stocking density grazing 
(> 60 AU ha−1; rest/grazed ratio > 40 days) compared 
to conventional grazing, characterized by low to mod-
erate stocking densities with infrequent movement of 
cattle among paddocks. In contrast, nirK responses in 
our study were less consistent. In Native Rangeland, 
nirK abundance in AD was 8% greater than PR only 
in summer. In Introduced Pasture, nirK was greater in 
AD than PR only in summer 2022, with a reversed 
effect in spring 2024. For nirS, grazing effects were 
minimal in Native Rangeland, whereas in Introduced 
Pasture, nirS was consistently greater under PR than 
AD, particularly in 2023 and 2024. This pattern may 
be linked to greater grazing events frequency asso-
ciated with lower stocking density [5 AU ha−1 vs 
35 AU ha−1 (Sparks et al. 2026)] observed in PR in 
Introduced Pasture, potentially allowing for greater 
plant biomass accumulation (Kotzé et  al. 2017) and 
organic matter inputs. Such conditions can enhance 
soil C and N availability, thereby stimulating denitri-
fication activity. González-Coria et  al. (2025) simi-
larly observed that denitrifying microbes rely on C 
and N substrates, and that microbial respiration stim-
ulated by phenolic compounds can release recalcitrant 
C, which further supports denitrification processes. 
Additionally, the observed contrasting responses 
between nirK and nirS suggest that the two denitrifier 
groups may occupy distinct ecological niches or differ 
in their sensitivity to substrate availability, as previ-
ously proposed by Braker et al. (1998), Enwall et al. 
(2010), and Yang et al. (2021).
Enwall et al. (2010) found that soil nitrate, DOC, 
and clay content significantly influenced the spatial 
distribution of nirS communities. Similarly, Yang 
et  al. (2025) reported that the soil texture affected 
the temporal response of both nirK and nirS genes 
to management practices. In our study, differences in 
soil texture [finer in Native Rangeland and coarser in

Among the seven tested genes, nifH was the only 
one that showed no grazing treatment effect, either as 
two- or three-way interactions, in either location. This 
lack of response suggests that, under the conditions 
of this study, N-fixation potential was less responsive 
to grazing-induced changes than nitrate reduction and 
denitrification pathways. Previous work indicates that 
nifH responses to herbivory can depend strongly on 
the biological compartment evaluated. In a Serengeti 
herbivore exclusion experiment, exclusion increased 
nifH copy number in roots of the legume Indigofera 
volkensii, but did not affect nifH copy number in 
roots of the grass Themeda triandra or in soil (Ritchie 
and Raina 2016). This is consistent with our results, 
because our measurements were based on bulk soil 
rather than host-specific root associations, where 
grazing effects on diazotrophs may be more detect-
able. In addition, Mitsuta et  al. (2026) showed that 
nifH abundance in an acidic grassland meadow was 
shaped primarily by soil pH changes induced by long-
term fertilizer application, rather than by short-term 
nutrient inputs. In the present study, soils were close 
to neutral pH, which may have reduced pH-related 
constraints on diazotroph abundance and contributed 
to the absence of a grazing response. Together, these 
findings suggest that nifH may be more strongly regu-
lated by host identity, soil pH, and broader edaphic 
conditions than by the grazing treatments evaluated in 
this study.
Changes in enzyme activity and stoichiometry under 
grazing
Herbivore grazing alters plant tissue stoichiometry, 
with subsequent effects on soil nutrient inputs and 
microbial processes. Previous studies have shown 
that grazing may enhance N concentrations in plant 
tissues, although responses are often species-spe-
cific (Bai et  al. 2012; Yang et  al. 2017; Ma et  al. 
2019). Since leaf, stem, and root stoichiometry are 
closely linked to soil C, N, and P pools (Ordoñez 
et al. 2009; Zheng et al. 2012), shifts in plant allo-
cation can cascade to the soil microbial community. 
Zhang et al. (2025) showed that the dung deposition 
accelerates soil nutrient cycling, mainly by shifting 
soil pH and moisture, altering the composition of 
bacterial communities and ultimately enhancing the 
enzymatic activities. In our study, enzyme activity 
and stoichiometric ratios exhibited significant inter -
actions with grazing, season and year, but the direc-
tion of these responses varied among systems.
Both LAP and NAG are crucial enzymes respon-
sible for N acquisition trough the degradation of 
proteins and chitin (Daunoras et  al. 2024), and 
their activities were combined to represent the 
N-enzymes in this study. The activity of N-enzymes 
is typically enhanced under N-limited conditions, 
as microbes increase enzyme production to access 
N sources (Uwituze et  al. 2022). In Introduced 
Pasture, PR presented greater N-enzyme activ -
ity than AD, suggesting lower N availability in PR 
soils. Greater nirS abundance under PR may reflect 
enhanced denitrification potential driven by higher 
soil C availability, which can further deplete inor -
ganic N pools and reinforce microbial N limita-
tion. Also, soil respiration had a positive relation-
ship with the changes in N-enzymes over time. 
These results suggest that PR grazing fosters condi-
tions of elevated C availability but lower N acces-
sibility, leading to increased microbial demand for 
N-acquiring enzymes (Wang et al. 2020).
The use of enzyme stoichiometry has been pro-
posed as an effective approach to infer microbial 
nutrient limitations and resource allocation strategies 
(Liu et al. 2020). Based on a global-scale meta-anal-
ysis of the seven-most widely measured soil enzyme 
activities across 40 ecosystems, Sinsabaugh et  al. 
(2008) concluded that microbial C:N:P acquisition 
ratios converged towards a 1:1:1 balance, reflecting 
coordinated nutrient cycling. In Native Rangeland, 
where stoichiometric ratios were close to 1.0, AD 
consistently exhibited greater C:P and N:P ratios 
compared to PR. This pattern suggests that the greater 
stocking density (76 AU ha⁻ 1) and longer rest period 
(121  days) under AD enhanced microbial energy 
allocation toward C and N acquisition, likely due to 
increased substrate inputs and turnover. Temporal 
trends further indicate a potential cumulative grazing 
effect—for C:N ratio, PR initially exceeded AD, with 
no differences at the end of the study; for C:P ratio, 
PR peaked in spring 2023, after which AD consist-
ently exceeded PR from summer 2023 onward; and

for N:P ratio, PR showed greater seasonal variability, 
whereas AD remain consistently greater then PR.
In Introduced Pasture, enzyme stoichiometric 
ratios revealed no consistent differences between 
grazing treatments but indicated persistent nutrient 
limitation across the system. It is important to note 
that this system had not received fertilizer inputs for 
the previous four years. C:N ratio remained near or 
above 1.0 under both grazing treatments, suggesting 
balanced microbial investment in C and N acquisi-
tion, with a slight preference toward C. In contrast, 
C:P and N:P ratios consistently remained below 0.8, 
reflecting a sustained predominance of P-mineralizing 
enzymes and a strong microbial demand for P. This 
pattern aligns with our dbRDA results, that revealed 
a significant association between soil environmental 
characteristics and gene community composition after 
accounting for sampling time and soil water content 
(totaling 87% of gene variance explained), in which 
P fractions were consistently selected as predictors of 
N-cycling gene abundance, indicating a relationship 
between P availability and microbial functionality.
Nitrogen availability can stimulate microbial func-
tion and increase demand for C and P, prompting 
microbes to produce additional enzymes to hydrolyze 
labile SOM (Yang et al. 2022), and in our study tem-
poral shifts in P-enzymes were associated with SOM 
and soil C:N dynamics. Zhao et  al. (2025) reported 
that grazing can shift microbial metabolic limitations 
depending on grazing intensity and ecosystem type, 
with P limitation often prevailing under low graz-
ing pressure and transitions toward N or C limitation 
emerging as grazing intensity increases. The domi-
nance of P-acquisition in our Introduced Pasture sug-
gests that, despite grazing-mediated nutrient returns, 
microbial processes remain constrained by P avail-
ability. Overall, these findings reinforce that grazing 
effects on microbial metabolism are context-depend-
ent and shaped by site-specific properties and the bal-
ance among nutrient inputs, removals, and recycling.
The minimal differences in enzymatic stoichiom-
etry observed between grazing treatments in Intro-
duced Pasture suggest that grazing effects were weak 
at the time of sampling. This limited response is likely 
linked to the temporal sensitivity of enzyme activi-
ties, as samples were collected 15 days after grazing. 
Because enzyme dynamics can fluctuate over short 
time scales, our measurements may not have cap-
tured transient shifts immediately following livestock 
movement. Prior work has shown strong temporal 
fluxes in enzyme activity within days to weeks after 
grazing, regardless of grazing intensity or duration 
(Van Syoc et al. 2022), and enzymatic processes are 
known to exhibit rapid variability in response to envi-
ronmental and abiotic conditions (Baldrian 2014; 
Boeddinghaus et al. 2015).
The absence of significant estimates for enzyme 
activity and stocking density can also be explained by 
minimal contrast between grazing strategies, poten-
tially reflecting the moderate difference in grazing 
intensity (35 AU ha⁻1 in AD versus 5 AU ha⁻1 in PR). 
An additional explanation is that microbial activ -
ity was driven primarily by seasonal and interannual 
variability, with grazing effects secondary to broader 
environmental drivers. A review of nearly 300 studies 
by Daunoras et al. (2024) reported that soil tempera-
ture, moisture, substrate availability, climatic factors 
such as average annual temperature and precipitation 
strongly influence microbial processes. Likewise, a 
meta-analysis by Brzostek et  al. (2012) showed that 
potential proteolytic activity (an important step in 
N cycling) declines under soil moisture deficits (the 
difference between precipitation and evapotranspira-
tion) in dry grasslands. Thus, the severe drought con-
ditions observed during our study likely constrained 
enzyme activity, functioning as a limiting factor. Col-
lectively, these findings emphasize the need for finer 
temporal resolution in longitudinal grazing research. 
Future sampling strategies should incorporate shorter 
and repeated intervals immediately following grazing 
events to resolve short-term microbial responses and 
better distinguish management effects from overarch-
ing environmental variability.
Our results highlight how the interaction of stock -
ing density and recovery time mediates microbial 
nutrient acquisition strategies in a context-dependent 
manner, reinforcing the need to account for site-
specific conditions when applying sustainable graz-
ing strategies (Liu et al. 2024). Overall, our findings 
demonstrate that the effects of AD and PR on soil 
microbial functioning diverge between Native and 
Introduced systems, reflecting differences in plant 
communities, litter dynamics, and nutrient cycling 
pathways. However, seasonal and interannual vari-
ability strongly modulated microbial functioning in 
both systems, with drought years (2022–2023) coin-
ciding with subsequent reduced gene abundance 
and enzyme activity (2023–2024). This pattern is

consistent with our recent work, where treatment-
associated shifts in dominant bacterial phyla could 
not be attributed to grazing management alone but 
instead appeared to reflect the combined influence 
of recurring drought–rewetting cycles and grazing-
associated environmental filtering (Chi et  al. 2026). 
Together, these findings reinforce that microbial func-
tioning is jointly controlled by grazing management 
and climatic conditions. Similarly, Jia et  al. (2025) 
reported that microbial functional genes were highly 
sensitive to fertilization in a long-term wheat–maize 
system, proposing their use as indicators of soil 
health. While our results support the potential use of 
N-cycling genes and nutrient-acquiring enzymes as 
indicators of management impact on soil health, the 
high sensitivity of these parameters to environmental 
variability suggests that replication and climatic con-
text are critical for robust interpretation.
Conclusions
We monitored N-cycling genes and C-, N-, and 
P-acquiring enzymes under two grazing strategies: 
AD (higher stocking density with longer rest peri-
ods) and PR (lower stocking density with fixed rest 
periods). By examining contrasting systems (Native 
Rangeland and Introduced Pasture), microbial 
responses to grazing and the influence of site-specific 
conditions were evaluated. Our hypothesis that graz-
ing management would alter N-cycling gene abun-
dance was partially supported. Effects were most 
evident in genes involved in dissimilatory nitrate 
reduction and denitrification (napA, narG, nirK, 
nirS), indicating shifts in N availability, but responses 
were both gene- and site-dependent. These contrast-
ing responses among genes suggest that the denitri-
fier groups occupy distinct ecological niches or differ 
in sensitivity to substrate availability. Across models, 
SOM, N and P fractions were significant predictors 
of gene abundance, linking substrate availability to 
microbial functional responses. Enzyme activity pat-
terns reinforced these findings. N-enzymes showed 
the most consistent grazing response across both 
systems, with greater activity under AD in Native 
Rangeland and under PR in Introduced Pasture. Stoi-
chiometric ratios close to 1.0 in Native Rangeland 
indicated balanced microbial investment in nutrient 
acquisition. In contrast, C:P and N:P ratios remained 
below 0.8 in Introduced Pasture, reflecting predomi-
nant P-mineralizing activity and sustained microbial 
demand for phosphorus. Overall, our results show that 
N-cycling genes and nutrient-acquiring enzymes have 
strong potential as indicators of management effects 
on soil functioning. However, their high sensitivity to 
environmental variability presents both a challenge 
and an opportunity: while appropriate replication and 
climatic context are critical for interpretation, these 
microbial parameters provide valuable resolution for 
tracking functional responses to management.
