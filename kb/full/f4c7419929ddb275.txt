Abstract
Access to safe drinking water is a fundamental environmental health requirement, 
yet integrated assessments of treatment performance and health risks under 
different land-use pressures remain limited in South Africa. This study evaluated the 
performance of two drinking water treatment plants in Mahikeng and Klerksdorp, 
North-West province, representing agricultural and industrial catchments, respectively. 
A cross-sectional study design was employed. Physicochemical parameters were 
analysed using the American Public Health Association (APHA) Standard Methods 
for the Examination of Water and Wastewater, while microbiological quality was 
assessed by membrane filtration to enumerate Escherichia coli and total coliforms. 
Data were analysed using descriptive statistics and one-way Analysis of Variance 
(ANOVA), and results were evaluated against South African National Standards 
(SANS 241) and World Health Organisation (WHO) drinking water guidelines. Both 
treatment plants achieved complete microbial removal, without detectable E. coli or 
total coliforms, indicating effective disinfection. The water quality index (WQI) values 
classified treated water as moderate in Mahikeng (70–73) and good in Klerksdorp 
(30–35). However, physicochemical deviations were identified. Mahikeng recorded 
elevated turbidity (6.64 ± 9.50 NTU), electrical conductivity (183.50 ± 2.12 µS/cm), 
total hardness (261.94 ± 15.27 mg/L), and residual chlorine (4.14 ± 0.33 mg/L), while 
Klerksdorp showed an increase in total dissolved solids (491.50 ± 54.45 mg/L) with 
lower residual chlorine (1.50 ± 0.37 mg/L). These deviations may reduce treatment 
efficiency, promote microbial regrowth, and affect water acceptability. Although 
most of the parameters met the SANS 241 and WHO guidelines, the findings reveal 
system-level vulnerabilities linked to the quality of source water and the optimisation 
of the treatment. The study highlights the need for integrated, risk-based monitoring 
frameworks to ensure sustainable drinking water safety under heterogeneous 
environmental pressures.
Highlights
 • The Klerksdorp water treatment plant achieved a higher treatment efficiency 
despite poorer source water quality.
 • The treated water from both plants was largely in accordance with WHO 
guidelines and SANS 241 drinking water standards.

1 Introduction
Water is a fundamental resource for the sustainability of life, and the provision of safe 
drinking water is critical to protecting public health [ 1]. Potable water should not pose 
significant acute or chronic health risks to consumers [ 9]. The quality of drinking water 
is evaluated by measuring key physicochemical and microbiological parameters and 
comparing them with established guideline values, including those set by the World 
Health Organisation [48] and national regulatory frameworks. The South African Bureau 
of Standards (SABS) [ 37] establishes the permissible limits and compliance criteria for 
domestic water use under the South African National Standards (SANS-241), thereby 
ensuring that water quality meets prescribed requirements for safety and acceptability 
[18].
Despite comprehensive regulatory frameworks and international commitments 
under Sustainable Development Goal 6, the provision of safe drinking water remains a 
significant global challenge, with approximately one-quarter of the world’s population 
still lacking access to safe managed drinking water services [ 49]. The Joint Monitoring

Programme (JMP) of the World Health Organisation (WHO) and the United Nations 
Children’s Fund (UNICEF) (2025) reported that approximately 2.2 billion people lacked 
access to safely managed drinking water services in 2022, highlighting the continu -
ing gap between policy commitments and practical implementation. This challenge is 
further exacerbated by increased population growth, rapid urbanisation, industrialisa -
tion, contaminated agricultural runoff by pesticides, and climate variability, all of which 
place additional pressure on already limited freshwater resources [ 30]. To address the 
increasing pressure on freshwater resources, alternative water management strategies, 
including wastewater reclamation and greywater reuse, have been widely advocated and 
increasingly adopted [ 33]. However, the successful implementation of these approaches 
depends on effective treatment processes, as inadequately treated reclaimed water can 
contain biological, chemical, and radiological contaminants that pose potential risks to 
public health [51].
While microbiological safety is often prioritised, elevated turbidity reduces disin -
fection efficiency by shielding microorganisms and facilitating pathogen persistence, 
thereby increasing the risk of waterborne disease transmission [ 23, 47]. In contrast, 
elevated turbidity levels are commonly associated with raised concentrations of natural 
organic matter in water, which may promote the formation of disinfection by-products 
(DBPs) during chlorination. Additionally, DBPs have also been associated with poten -
tial carcinogenic and mutagenic effects in long-term exposure scenarios [ 25, 48]. More-
over, elevated electrical conductivity indicates increased concentrations of dissolved 
ions, including sodium, chloride, nitrates, and potentially toxic metals in drinking water. 
According to the World Health Organisation (2022), prolonged exposure to high con -
centrations of these dissolved constituents may contribute to adverse health outcomes, 
including hypertension, kidney dysfunction, and metabolic disturbances, particularly 
among vulnerable populations.
Total hardness, which is determined primarily by calcium and magnesium, is not 
inherently toxic; its health implications remain complex and context-dependent [21]. An 
excessive level of hardness may reduce the acceptability of water and promote scaling 
within distribution pipelines [ 24], whereas low hardness (soft water) increases corrosiv -
ity, thereby causing a substantial enhancement of the leaching of toxic metals into drink-
ing-water supplies [52]. In general, deviations in physicochemical parameters, including 
pH and total dissolved solids, can alter the bioavailability of contaminants, indirectly 
increasing human exposure to harmful substances [36].
In South Africa, municipalities and water boards are mandated under the Water Ser -
vices Act (Act No. 108 of 1997) to abstract, treat, and distribute potable water to urban, 
peri-urban, and selected rural communities. To support this mandate and ensure com -
pliance with national drinking water standards, the Department of Water and Sanita -
tion [ 13] has established comprehensive regulatory monitoring systems, including the 
Blue Drop Certification Programme and the Integrated Regulatory Information System 
(IRIS), which provide annual assessments of drinking water quality, treatment plant per-
formance, and regulatory compliance. Nevertheless, the 2023 Blue Drop Report high -
lights considerable variation in the operational performance, process control, technical 
capacity, and regulatory compliance of water treatment systems across municipalities, 
indicating that maintaining consistently safe drinking water remains an ongoing chal -
lenge. Consequently, site-specific evaluations of treatment efficiency and drinking water

quality are essential to identify performance gaps and support evidence-based interven -
tions for improving water treatment operations and protecting public health.
The North-West province presents a unique context for assessing the performance of 
drinking water treatment due to its diverse land-use patterns, encompassing agricultur -
ally dominated and industrialised catchments. For example, the Hartbeespoort Dam, 
one of the water resources of the province, has experienced persistent eutrophication 
associated with excessive nutrient enrichment from wastewater effluent, agricultural 
runoff, and urban activities, resulting in increased algal biomass and declining ecologi -
cal water quality [ 15]. Similarly, the Mooi River system, which supports communities 
and industrial activities in the Klerksdorp–Potchefstroom region, has been affected by 
elevated salinity and metal contamination associated with mining activities, agricultural 
practices, and wastewater management challenges, increasing demand for treatment 
for downstream water purification systems [ 20]. Mahikeng is characterised by agricul -
tural activities that can influence nutrient and mineral loading in source water, whereas 
Klerksdorp is influenced by industrial activities that can contribute to elevated dissolved 
solids and other contaminants. This diversity contributes to considerable variability in 
raw water quality, as agricultural runoff, industrial discharges, mining activities, and nat-
ural geochemical processes can introduce nutrients, dissolved salts, metals, and other 
contaminants that may increase the complexity of treatment and influence final water 
quality. These contrasting settings provide an opportunity to comparatively evaluate the 
performance of the treatment plant in differing environmental settings.
Therefore, this study aims to comparatively assess the performance of domestic water 
treatment plants in Mahikeng and Klerksdorp by evaluating seasonal variations in key 
physicochemical and microbiological parameters at both raw and treated water points 
while examining compliance with regulatory standards. In addition, overall treatment 
performance was evaluated using the Water Quality Index, a valuable tool to summarise 
water quality for drinking purposes. Unlike previous studies that focus primarily on 
compliance, this work explicitly links drinking water treatment efficiency to potential 
human health risks associated with deviations in key parameters. By examining the per -
formance of drinking water treatment in contrasting agricultural and industrial catch -
ments, the study advances understanding of how the characteristics of the source water 
influence the outcomes of treatment and residual risk. The findings contribute new 
empirical evidence to the limited body of knowledge on the operational performance 
of municipal treatment systems in South Africa and similar developing countries. The 
results provide a framework for risk-informed water quality management, regulatory 
decision-making, and targeted optimisation of treatment processes in resource-con -
strained settings.
2 Materials and methods
2.1 Description of the study area
The present research study was conducted in Mahikeng and Klerksdorp in the North-
West province of South Africa (Fig.  1). Mahikeng, the capital of the North-West prov -
ince, is located within Latitude − 25°51′ S and Longitude 25°38 ′ E, covering a total land 
area of 3 698 km 2. Most of the land in the municipality is farmland (55%) and then tra -
ditional area (44%). Less than 2% of the Mahikeng area is urban area [ 29]. In Mahikeng, 
the Mahikeng Water Treatment Works (Mahikeng WTW) treats raw water abstracted

from Setumo dam to produce potable water (Fig.  1C), which is subsequently distributed 
to consumers within the Mahikeng Local Municipality [31].
The Mahikeng WTW is a conventional surface water treatment facility that supplies 
potable water to the town of Mahikeng and surrounding communities. In 2016, the plant 
was upgraded from an initial capacity of 20 ML/day to a design capacity of 30 ML/day 
to improve the reliability of the bulk water supply in the area [ 13]. The upgrade incor -
porated additional treatment infrastructure, including flocculation mixing channels, 
settling tanks, dissolved air flotation units, and rapid gravity sand filters, enhancing the 
plant’s capacity and treatment performance [ 14]. Based on the plant’s design capacity 
and typical municipal domestic water consumption rates, the facility has the potential to 
supply approximately 150,000–200,000 people and Mahikeng has an estimated popula -
tion of 354 504 [41].
Klerksdorp is located at approximately 26°52 ′ S latitude and 26°40 ′ E longitude and 
encompasses an area of approximately 105.98 km2. The town is characterised by hetero -
geneous and intensive land-use patterns, shaped predominantly by mining, agricultural 
activities, commercial and industrial zones, and urban settlements [ 44]. The MidVaal 
Water Company (MWC) Water Treatment Plant (hereafter referred to as Klerksdorp 
Water Treatment Plant WTP), located near Klerksdorp in the North-West province, is a 
major bulk potable water supplier serving the City of Matlosana, including Klerksdorp, 
Orkney, Stilfontein and Hartbeesfontein, as well as mining and industrial consumers 
[44]. The plant abstracts water from the Vaal River (Fig.  1D) and has a design treatment 
capacity of approximately 320 ML/day, although operational assessments have indicated 
that it currently operates below its design capacity, with an assessed operating capac -
ity of approximately 130 ML/day and a potential optimisation capacity of approximately 
250 ML/day. The treatment process consists of a conventional multi-barrier surface 
water treatment system incorporating coagulation, flocculation, dissolved air flotation, 
clari-flocculation, filtration, ozonation, chlorination, and clear water storage. Based on

2.2 Materials
2.2.1 Workflow
The methodological workflow used in this study is presented in Fig.  2, highlighting the 
sequential steps used for sample collection, laboratory analyses, determination of the 
Water Quality Index (WQI), statistical evaluation, and assessment of the drinking water 
treatment performance.
2.2.2 Water sampling, physicochemical testing and data processing
Water sampling
Grab water samples were collected at the influent (source water) and treated water 
(effluent) after the completion of the treatment process, allowing the evaluation of the 
treatment performance and compliance with the drinking water quality standards. The 
collection and analysis of water samples was conducted bi-weekly during two sampling 
campaigns, in June 2018 (lower temperatures) and October 2018 (higher temperatures), 
to capture seasonal variation in physicochemical and microbiological parameters [ 32]. 
Unless otherwise specified by the guidelines of the former Department of Water Affairs 
and Forestry (DWAF), now the Department of Water and Sanitation (DWS), ultrapure 
deionised water was used for the preparation and dilution of all standards and samples 
[10, 12]. All field equipment and laboratory instruments were calibrated according to the 
manufacturer’s instructions, and analyses were performed following established stan -
dard operating procedures for water quality assessment.
Six batches were collected per study site in triplicate using high-density polyethylene 
(HDPE) bottles (1000 mL) for physicochemical analyses. The bottles were washed with 
laboratory detergent, thoroughly rinsed with tap water followed by deionised water, 
soaked in 10% nitric acid for 24 h, rinsed with ultrapure deionised water, and air-dried 
before use. Before sampling, bottles were rinsed three times with sample water, except 
where preservation requirements prohibited rinsing [10]. Four containers were allocated 
for physicochemical analyses and required no pre-treatment. Sterile polypropylene sam -
pling bottles used for microbiological analyses were autoclaved at 121  °C for 15  min 
before use and pre-dosed with sterile 10% Na2S2O3 solution (0.1 mL per 100 mL sample) 
to neutralise residual chlorine and prevent disinfection during transport [3].
The sampling points at Mahikeng WTW are illustrated in Fig. 3, while Fig. 4 shows the 
sampling points at Klerksdorp WTP . Water samples were taken at the inlet (1) to deter-
mine the physicochemical and biological status of water before the treatment process, 
and the outlet (2) to determine the physicochemical and biological status of water after 
the treatment process.

2.3.2 Microbiological analysis
The enumeration of Escherichia coli (E. coli) and total coliforms was carried out using 
the membrane filtration technique according to APHA Standard Method 9222 [ 3]. The 
samples (50 mL) were filtered through sterile 0.45 μm membrane filters (manufactured 
by Derbgum, Johannesburg, South Africa) using a Gelman Little Giant pressure/vacuum 
pump machine (model 13156, Gelman Sciences, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Membrane fil -
ters were aseptically transferred onto m-Endo agar and m-FC agar (Merck, Darmstadt, 
Germany) to selectively isolate total coliform and faecal coliform bacteria, respectively. 
The membranes were placed onto m-Endo agar LES for total coliforms and mFC agar for 
E. coli, followed by incubation at 37 ± 0.5 °C and 44.5 ± 0.2 °C, respectively, for 24 h. Pre -
sumptive colonies were confirmed using Gram staining, oxidase, citrate utilisation, triple 
sugar iron tests, and the EnteroPluri-Test (Ref: 78618–78619) [34].
2.3.3 Water quality control and assurance
Water quality control and assurance procedures were implemented to ensure the accu -
racy, reliability, and reproducibility of the analytical results. The sampling was conducted 
using standard protocols to minimise contamination and variability during collection, 
preservation, transport, and analysis. Field parameters, including pH, electrical conduc -
tivity, temperature, and turbidity, were measured using calibrated portable instruments, 
while laboratory analyses were performed according to the APHA Standard Methods 
for the Examination of Water and Wastewater [3]. Quality assurance measures included 
instrument calibration, analysis of reagent blanks, duplicate samples, and quality control 
standards to verify analytical precision and accuracy. Data validation involved review -
ing analytical results for consistency, assessing replicate measurements, and confirming 
compliance with quality control criteria prior to statistical analysis and Water Quality 
Index (WQI) calculations. The reliability of the dataset was further ensured by compar -
ing the measured water quality parameters with the limits specified in the South African 
National Standard (SANS 241: Drinking Water Specification) [ 37] and the Domes -
tic Drinking Water guidelines of the World Health Organisation [ 48]. SANS 241 was 
selected as the primary benchmark because it represents the legally recognised national 
standard for the quality of potable water in South Africa and defines acceptable limits 
for microbiological, chemical, physical and aesthetic parameters. The WHO guidelines 
were used as an international reference framework to provide a broader context and 
facilitate comparison with internationally accepted drinking water quality criteria. The 
use of both standards allowed for an assessment of treatment performance, regulatory 
compliance, and the general suitability of treated water for human consumption.

3 Results and discussion
3.1 Physical water quality
The comparative physicochemical characteristics of the water samples from the study 
area and their corresponding World Health Organisation and South African National 
Standards allowable limits are presented in Figs.  5 and 6. Table  3 shows the seasonal 
mean values for the physical parameters from the two study sites.
The pH of treated water from the Klerksdorp WTP remained within the recom -
mended WHO guidelines and the standard limits of SANS 241 throughout the sampling 
period, ranging between 7.68 and 8.24 in summer and winter, respectively (Fig.  5). In 
comparison, the pH of Mahikeng WTW treated water ranged from 8.29 to 9.33 during 
winter and summer, respectively (Fig.  6). These values were within the recommended 
limits for potable water as stipulated by the World Health Organisation and SANS 241. 
Although both water treatment plants effectively maintained pH within acceptable lim -
its, the elevated summer pH values observed in Mahikeng may be of concern, as pH

Long-term consumption of water with elevated electrical conductivity (EC), such as 
that observed in Mahikeng WTW, is not necessarily harmful, but it reflects a high con -

their composition. Previous studies have shown that prolonged consumption of highly 
saline drinking water, as indicated by elevated electrical conductivity, can contribute to

Turbidity is a key indicator of water clarity that is widely used to assess drinking 
water quality because it reflects the presence of suspended particles, including silt, clay, 
organic matter, and microorganisms. Elevated turbidity has both health and aesthetic 
implications, as it can interfere with disinfection processes, facilitate microbial persis -
tence, and reduce the acceptability of drinking water by consumers [ 50]. In Klerksdorp 
WTP , elevated turbidity levels were observed at the inlet, ranging from 21.50 ± 1.65 NTU 
in summer to 20.67 ± 0.94 NTU in winter. Following treatment, turbidity levels were 
substantially reduced to 0.53 ± 0.03 NTU and 0.39 ± 0.12 NTU for summer and winter, 
respectively (Fig.  5), falling within the recommended WHO guidelines and the SANS 
241 drinking water standards, indicating an effective treatment process. This reduction 
was statistically significant ( p < 0.001). In contrast, Mahikeng WTW exhibited markedly 
high levels of turbidity at the inlet, with mean values of 23.34 ± 100.37 NTU in sum -
mer and 23.43 ± 34.97 NTU in winter, exceeding the allowed limits of 1–5 NTU. These 
high levels of turbidity can be attributed to seasonal rainfall-induced runoff, catchment 
erosion, agricultural and urban runoff, and the influx of organic matter into the source 
water [ 39]. Although treatment reduced turbidity to 5.37 ± 16.92 NTU and 6.64 ± 9.50 
NTU for summer and winter, respectively (Fig.  6), these values remained above accept -
able standards, suggesting suboptimal treatment efficiency and the potential inadequacy 
of the disinfection process to remove particulate matter and associated microbial con -
taminants. A statistically significant difference was also observed between the levels of 
turbidity at the inlet and outlet of the Mahikeng WTW (P = 0.008). Similar findings were 
reported by [ 27], who found that turbidity at two rural water treatment plants and sev -
eral points within the distribution system in the Vhembe District frequently exceeded 
both the aesthetic guideline of 5 NTU and the operational target of 1 NTU. The authors 
attributed the elevated turbidity to operational and maintenance challenges, noting that 
high turbidity can compromise disinfection efficiency by shielding microorganisms 
from chlorine exposure. In contrast, Bwapwa [ 5] reported that the Hazelmere Water

The elevated levels of turbidity observed in Mahikeng WTW are of considerable pub -

ter that can harbour and protect pathogenic microorganisms from disinfection [ 47, 50]. 
Consequently, the associated health implication is due to the potential persistence of 
microbial contaminants rather than to turbidity itself [45]. Elevated turbidity can reduce 
the effectiveness of disinfection processes by shielding microbes, thus increasing the 
likelihood of waterborne diseases caused by bacteria, viruses, and protozoa [ 23]. This 
can result in acute gastrointestinal illnesses, particularly among vulnerable populations. 
Additionally, the presence of organic matter in turbid water can promote the formation 
of harmful disinfection by-products during chlorination, which are linked to long-term 
health effects including carcinogenic and reproductive risks [ 25, 48]. Therefore, the ele-
vated post-treatment turbidity observed in the present study suggests that optimisation 
of coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, and filtration processes may be required to 
improve treatment performance and ensure more effective reduction of microbial risk.
3.2 Chemical indicators of water quality
Table 4 shows the seasonal mean chemical parameters from the two study sites.
Total hardness (TH) represents the combined concentration of calcium and mag -
nesium ions in water [ 22]. Drinking water is classified as soft (< 61  mg/L as CaCO 3), 
moderately hard (61–120  mg/L as CaCO 3), hard (121–180  mg/L as CaCO 3), and very 
hard (> 180 mg/L as CaCO 3) [11]. After treatment, only the TH values for Klerksdorp 
WTP were reduced to within acceptable limits, decreasing from 240.83 ± 46.22  mg/L 
to 67.81 ± 20.98  mg/L in summer, and from 216.58 ± 76.09  mg/L to 42.56 ± 11.76  mg/L 
in winter (Fig.  5), indicating effective treatment performance. In contrast, TH val -

261.94 ± 15.27 mg/L in winter (Fig.  6), exceeding the limits of the WHO guidelines and 
SANS 241 standards and suggesting suboptimal treatment efficiency. The difference in 
TH concentrations between study areas could be attributed to the natural geology of the 
areas [42]. The northern region of the North-West province, where Mahikeng is located, 
is rich in limestone deposits. Ca 2+, the main cation that contributes to the hardness of 
the water, may leach into groundwater and surface water as it percolates through these 
limestone formations, particularly during the rainy summer season [46].

health hazard; however, excessive TH may have indirect implications for human health. 
Mirzabeygi [29] elaborated that high concentrations of calcium and magnesium can con-
tribute to scaling in domestic systems. Kozisek (2020) noted that elevated total hardness 
(TH) can reduce the palatability and general consumer acceptability of drinking water. 
Although hardness is generally not considered a direct health concern, the consump -
tion of elevated hardness of water has been associated with gastrointestinal discomfort 
in susceptible individuals. In addition, elevated water hardness together with a high level

3.3 Microbiological parameters
The E. coli and total coliform values for both Klerksdorp WTP and the Mahikeng WTW 
post-treatment were within the WHO and SANS 241 recommended limits, indicating 
compliance with microbiological drinking water standards (Fig. 7A and B; Table 5).
The results further show that the raw water sources that supply both sites are highly 
contaminated with bacteria, particularly during the summer season. Overall, the treat -
ment processes were effective in reducing microbial contamination to acceptable lev -
els before distribution. This reduction can be attributed to the integrated performance 
of conventional treatment barriers, including coagulation–flocculation, sedimentation, 
filtration, and disinfection. Coagulation and sedimentation promote the removal of par -
ticulate matter and particle-associated microorganisms, while filtration and disinfection 
are the primary barriers to the presence of harmful pathogens in drinking water [ 43]. 
The final disinfection step provides microbial inactivation, ensuring that treated water 
complies with microbiological quality requirements before distribution.

3.4 Water quality index
The WHO guidelines for drinking water quality were used for the calculation of WQI. 
The rating of the water was computed using the physicochemical parameters obtained. 
Table 6 presents the weights assigned to each parameter for the calculation of the water 
quality index (WQI) based on their relative importance to the quality of drinking water. 
Total dissolved solids, turbidity, and sulphate received the highest relative weights 
(0.152), indicating their greater influence on WQI, while conductivity and sodium were 
assigned moderate weights (0.121). In contrast, pH, free chlorine, total hardness, and 
nitrate were assigned lower relative weights, reflecting their comparatively smaller con -
tribution to the overall index. The use of WHO guideline values as reference standards 
ensures a reliable and internationally recognised assessment of drinking water quality.
The WQI was calculated to evaluate the suitability of treated water for human con -
sumption, with results presented in Table 7. Both plants received raw water classified as 
‘poor, ’ with Mahikeng WTW exhibiting the poorest raw water quality (WQI ˃120) dur-
ing summer.
The overall performance of the treatment varied between the plants: Klerksdorp WTP 
showed a substantial improvement, with WQI reductions of 70.9% in summer and 61.1% 
in winter. In contrast, Mahikeng WTW demonstrated moderate improvement, poten -
tially due to the poorer quality of its raw water and the ageing infrastructure limiting 
treatment efficiency. Similar trends have been reported in South Africa, where treatment 
plants receiving poor-quality source water often experience reduced treatment effi -
ciency due to increased contaminant loads and operational challenges [27]. In particular, 
municipal water systems affected by ageing infrastructure and inadequate maintenance 
have shown greater difficulty in consistently achieving optimal treated water quality [14].

3.5 Treatment efficiency
Comparison of treatment efficiencies revealed distinct performance patterns between 
the two water treatment plants (Table 8). Mahikeng WTW consistently achieved moder-
ate to high removal of turbidity (71.6–77.1%), total dissolved solids (27.7–34.3%), total 
hardness (25.7–25.8%), and nitrate (28.1–73.0%), with relatively stable treatment effi -
ciencies across seasons. In contrast, the lower removal of TDS and nitrate at Mahikeng 
is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that conventional treatment processes 
have limited capacity to remove dissolved inorganic constituents without advanced 
treatment technologies [2, 6].
Alternatively, Klerksdorp exhibited exceptional turbidity (97.5–98.1%) and total hard -
ness (71.8–80.3%) removal but showed marked seasonal variability, with negative treat -
ment efficiencies for total dissolved solids, conductivity, nitrate, sulphate, sodium, and 
temperature during winter. Similar seasonal variations in drinking water treatment per -
formance have been attributed to changes in raw water quality, temperature, coagulant 
demand and operational conditions [ 8]. The turbidity removal efficiencies observed at 
Klerksdorp (97.5–98.1%) are comparable to the 95–99% removal efficiencies reported 
for optimised conventional drinking water treatment plants [8]. Overall, Klerksdorp was 
more effective at removing particulate contaminants, whereas Mahikeng displayed more 
consistent performance and greater stability in reducing dissolved constituents across 
seasons, suggesting differences in source water characteristics and treatment operations.

5 Conclusion and recommendations
5.1 Conclusions
Effective drinking water treatment is essential for protecting public health, particu -
larly in regions where source water quality is influenced by anthropogenic activities 
and natural variability. This study demonstrated that, despite receiving comparatively 
more impacted source water, the Klerksdorp treatment system outperformed Mahikeng 
WTW, consistently producing higher-quality treated water. The Water Quality Index 
(WQI) classified Klerksdorp water as good (30–35) and Mahikeng water as moder -
ate (70–73). Although the treated water of both plants generally complied with WHO 
guidelines and the SANS 241 drinking water standard, persistent exceedances in turbid -
ity and total hardness indicate the need for targeted process optimisation and routine 
monitoring to improve treatment efficiency and distribution system performance. These 
findings provide valuable evidence for water resource managers and policymakers to pri-
oritise treatment upgrades, strengthen operational controls, and implement site-specific 
interventions to ensure sustainable delivery of safe and acceptable drinking water.
5.2 Recommendations
The study recommends the following:
 • Implement continuous and systematic monitoring of treated water quality to ensure 
a consistent supply of potable water that complies with national and international 
regulatory standards.
 • Upgrade and modernise existing water treatment infrastructure, particularly ageing 
treatment systems, to improve operational efficiency, enhance treatment reliability, 
and reduce the risk of water quality failures.
 • Optimise treatment processes to address persistent turbidity and total hardness 
exceedances, especially in Mahikeng, through improved coagulation–flocculation, 
filtration and hardness control strategies to improve consumer acceptability.
 • Strengthen catchment management practices to reduce the entry of contaminants 
from anthropogenic activities, including agricultural practices and mining. Improved 
catchment protection would reduce raw water contamination and lower the 
treatment burden on water purification systems.
