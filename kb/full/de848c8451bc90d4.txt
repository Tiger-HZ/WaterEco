Under-water ignition and combustion of PDMS 
processed Al/CuO nanoparticle composite
Z. B. Wang · A. Q. Wang · J. Ehling  · C. J. MacRobbie · S. Kim · L. Saini · S. Deng  ·

Abstract
This study investigates the fabrication and performance of hydrophobic nanothermite powders prepared by 
combining liquid polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) prepolymers with thermite powders in a dispersion medium. 
The resulting composite remained dry when submerged underwater and could be ignited by laser irradiation. 
High-speed imaging measurements showed an ignition delay of 2.3 ms under underwater conditions. Finite-
element simulations based on a two-phase flow model revealed rapid cooling and condensation of water vapor 
during ignition. Preliminary analysis of the bubble composition suggests the evolution of gaseous products 
from the thermite reactions. These results demonstrate the feasibility of underwater laser ignition of ther -
mite–PDMS composites and provide insights into the complex physical and chemical processes involved in 
underwater combustion.

Introduction
Thermite formulations represent a category of highly energetic materials with characteristic exothermic reac -
tions, generating substantial heat upon ignition. Nanothermite refers to a solid mixture of nano-sized reactive 
particles. These compositions are commonly referred to as metastable intermolecular composites, denoting their 
distinctive reactive properties1. The resulting high temperatures, combined with their ease of use and portabil -
ity, have earned thermite great interest across various applications, including propulsion systems, controlled 
demolitions, welding and metal cutting, and firework displays. An easy way to initiate and sustain underwater 
combustion could offer significant potential for various marine and aquatic applications, including welding 2,3, 
cutting, and propulsion. However, the hydrophilic nature of aluminum 4 and copper oxide5 causes wetting when 
submerged in water, leading to heat loss that hinders ignition and flame propagation. Consequently, research in 
underwater combustion is limited, with a lack of understanding of reaction propagation and energy release from 
nanothermite reactions underwater.

They are most commonly composed of metal powder as a reducing agent and an oxidizer such as metal oxides, 
while fluoropolymers6 or iodine oxides7 can also be used. In this study, aluminum and copper oxide are used, and 
the reaction can be described as:
 2Al + 3CuO → Al2O3 + 3Cu (1)
The initiation of thermite combustion typically requires a high-temperature heat source, such as spark or flame. 
Once activated, the reaction between the metal powder and metal oxide can reach temperature up to 2843 K for 
Al/CuO composites8,9. This characteristic imparts thermite with remarkable effectiveness as a potent heat source.
The aluminum thermite system has been well studied, revealing two main modes of aluminum oxidation: one 
occurring in the condensed phase, where oxygen ions traverse the Al/CuO interface, and the other in the gas 
phase, involving the dissociation of CuO before O2 gas engages with aluminum, resembling the oxidation process 
in an oxygen-rich environment10. Upon ignition of the Al/CuO mixture, the flame propagates through mass and 
heat transfer processes such as thermal conduction and mass convection. Experimental observations using electri-
cally heated nanothermite coatings have indicated that CuO decomposition precedes ignition when the heating 
rate exceeds 2,000 K/s11.
Additionally, lightly packed Al/CuO powders were observed to have three modes of combustion with increas-
ing surrounding inert gas pressure, with combustion velocity decreasing from 1,000 to 2 m/s 12. Convection 
dominates at low pressures, and thermal conduction dominants at high pressures, suggesting a gas-solid reaction 
mechanism13.
In addition to these mechanisms, when the particle sizes drop to the nanoscale, nanothermites behave slightly 
differently from traditional ones, as the thickness of the oxidation layer on the aluminum powder can no longer 
be neglected, forming a shell of alumina, which not only reduces the effective aluminum weight fraction, but 
also inhibits the reaction. At this scale, Al/CuO nanothermite can also react through reactive sintering 14, where 
reaction initiation occurs at the Al/CuO interface, and capillary forces sustain the process by delivering unreacted 
material to the reaction site. Reactive sintering occurs much faster than the burning time, typically within 10 µs.
Nevertheless, efforts on underwater combustion have been made. Meir et al. initiated combustion in a naturally 
hydrophobic iron thermite using microwaves. The sample was confined underwater within a bubble by a magnet 
using the bubble-marble effect15. Yu et al. employed a different approach by igniting superhydrophobic Co 3O4/
Al core-shell nano-wires with a nanosecond Nd/YAG laser. The fabrication involved vacuum-coating aluminum 
onto Co3O4 nanowires, before applying a hydrophobic fluoroalkylsilane layer. While these pioneering methods 
show promise, they exhibit limitations, the iron thermite is not easily generalized to other components, and the 
nanowires involve a complicated fabrication that is slow and expensive and are limited to two-dimensional 
structures.
This paper presents a simple method to achieve underwater combustion of thermites by introducing a novel 
approach for crafting hydrophobic nanothermite powders. The sample is made by mixing liquid PDMS prepoly-
mers with thermite powders within a dispersion liquid. The resultant composite remained dry when submerged 
underwater and could be readily ignited by laser irradiation. Furthermore, the concept of creating a hydrophobic 
coating using pre-curing polymers could be applied to materials beyond CuO/Al thermite or PDMS.
Beyond demonstrating underwater ignition and combustion, this research investigates the mechanisms in Al/
CuO nanothermite when immersed underwater with PDMS as an additional reagent, to deepen the understanding 
of heat dissipation channels, water phase change with rapid heating, and potential gas generation.

Sample preparation and characterization
To prepare a thermite sample with an equivalence ratio of 1(ER = 1), 0.8 g CuO and 0.27 g Al nano-powder 
(~ 50 nm diameter, SkySpring Nanomaterials, Inc.) were used. The excess aluminum compensates for the oxi -
dation layer on the Al nano-powders. The prepolymer and catalyst (Dow Corning Sylgard 184 kit) were mixed 
thoroughly at 10:1 mass ratio. The powders and about 60 mg of the prepolymer were added to a beaker followed 
by the addition of approximately 5 ml acetone to fully immerse the powders. The mixture was ultrasonicated 
and thoroughly mixed then dried and cured for 2 days in a wide container under well-ventilated conditions. The 
sample dried and naturally cracked into ~ 3 mm pieces.
The SEM images were taken by a JEOL JSM 7401 F unit and the results are shown in Fig. 1. The raw thermite 
sample is presented in Fig. 1a, while the 5% PDMS composite is shown in Fig. 1b. Compared to the raw ther -
mite sample, the morphology does not change significantly, although the size of the particles may have increased 
slightly. If the PDMS is assumed to be evenly coated on each particle, the thickness is estimated to be less than 
2 nm for a 5% PDMS mass ratio.
By submerging the sample with 5% PDMS ratio in 1% hydrochloric (HCl) acid for several days, the acid slowly 
dissolves the CuO and Al particles, leaving behind the PDMS framework that binds these nanoparticles, and its 
SEM image is shown in Fig. 1f. The spherical cavities visible in the green box in Fig. 1f suggest that PDMS acts 
as a binding agent, clustering the nanoparticles into distinct clumps rather than uniformly coating each individual 
particle. This indicates that the CuO/Al nanoparticles are still in contact with each other, and existing combustion 
mechanism, such as reactive sintering may still be valid with the introduction of the PDMS coating. The smaller 
bright spots within the green box in Fig. 1f are sputter-coated gold particles for better imaging.
The coated sample exhibits visual similarity to the original sample, and the evaluation of its hydrophobic -
ity was conducted through contact angle measurements 16. Upon gently pressing the powder into a 5-mm flake, 
the contact angle is measured to be approximately 150° for an 11.7% PDMS mass ratio, as illustrated in Fig. 2. 
This value is greater than the typical contact angle of PDMS, which is around 110° 17. Sabbah et al. have previ -
ously reported an increase in the contact angle for PDMS with wrinkles, attributed to surface roughness 18. The 
observed superhydrophobic effect in our study can be explained by the surface roughness induced by the thermite 
nanoparticles. Li et al. have also documented the fabrication of a superhydrophobic surface by coating PDMS on 
aluminum, aligning with the observed hydrophobic behavior in our coated thermite sample19.
The reported values correspond to static contact angles measured under ambient conditions. Dynamic contact 
angles (advancing/receding) and hysteresis were not measured, but are expected to influence wetting transitions 
and long-term stability of the hydrophobic state, as discussed by Butt et al. 20. These effects will be investigated 
in future work.
The optical reflection spectra (250–2500 nm) of Al nanoparticles, CuO nanoparticles, and their composite 
mixtures were measured using a UV–Vis–NIR spectrophotometer equipped with an integrating sphere attach -
ment, designed specifically for rough pellet surfaces. Kramers–Kronig (KK) analysis typically assumes specular 
reflectance at a well-defined interface, such as the surface of a bulk solid. Nevertheless, it has been successfully 
applied to porous and nanostructured films, where the surfaces are rough, but the reflectance remains predomi -
nantly specular. Using KK analysis, we obtained the corresponding extinction spectra and found that the extinc-
tion coefficient of the Al–CuO nanocomposite is lower than the value predicted by a simple linear combination 
of the individual components. Furthermore, the characteristic absorption bands in the spectra shift to lower ener-
gies relative to those of the individual components [support data]. These spectral changes suggest that the close 
contact between Al and CuO nanoparticles influences the optical response of the composite. However, the optical 
measurements alone do not provide direct evidence of electronic coupling, charge transfer, or carrier-mediated 
interactions.

Experiments and results
Experimental setup and ignition conditions
As shown in Fig. 3, a piece of the flake (about 3 mg) was placed into a container before water was added. The 
sample was then placed in a combustion chamber and a 5 W continuous laser with 532 nm wavelength was used 
to ignite the sample. The laser spot diameter was approximately 200 μm, corresponding to a power density of 
approximately 1.6 × 10 4 W/cm
2
. The 532 nm wavelength was chosen due to the strong absorption by CuO and the 
Al/CuO composite, enabling efficient photothermal heating and rapid ignition. A function generator triggered the 
laser and the camera simultaneously.

Ignition behavior and delay time
Selected frames from the high-speed video are shown in Fig. 4. The complete video is provided in the Support -
ing Information. Figure 4b, c show that ignition occurred about 2.2 ms after the laser onset, delivering 11 mJ 
of energy to the sample. This is comparable to another experiment using a 1040 nm, 100-fs pulsed laser with a 
10-MHz repetition rate and 4 W average power, which also ignited the sample in 2 ms. At a lower equivalence 
ratio (ER = 0.6), insufficient fuel significantly delayed the ignition to around 30 ms for both the green laser and 
the infrared laser. This trend is consistent with previous studies on Al/CuO nanothermites, where fuel-lean mix-
tures exhibit longer ignition delays compared to fuel-rich compositions due to reduced heat release and slower 
reaction kinetics. Additionally, ignition delay is known to strongly depend on equivalence ratio, with lean mix -
tures generally increasing ignition delay because of reduced reactive species concentration and slower chain-
branching processes1.

Effect of PDMS content on combustion behavior
Samples with different PDMS ratios were also tested, and their respective contact angles were measured, as 
shown in Fig. 6. Each data point represents the average of about 6 measurements, with error bars indicating the 
standard deviation. The contact angle demonstrates a notable upward trend with increasing PDMS coating thick-
ness. While most samples remain ignitable by laser, a transition occurs around a mass ratio of ~ 12%, beyond 
which the sample become fire- retardant. The SEM images in Fig. 1d suggest increased clumping, attributed to 
excess PDMS, potentially explaining the observed flame-retardant properties. At a ~ 1% PDMS ratio, rapid water 
permeation occurs, preventing ignition by lasers irradiation (blue triangle). As the PDMS content increases, the 
reaction tends to be less vigorous, with a higher likelihood of incomplete combustion at elevated PDMS ratios 
(green circles). The SEM images in Fig. 1a-d suggest that PDMS content does not significantly alter the morphol-
ogy of the sample. These combustion characteristics suggest that PDMS retains flame-retardant properties, and 
an excess amount inhibits the reaction. Since the nanostructures of the samples remain similar, the thickness of 
the PDMS coating might be the key factor causing these differences, which can be investigated in future work.
Spathi et al. dry-milled paper sludge ash with stearic acid and reported similar contact angles and a comparable 
increasing trend with mass ratio 22, which matches our observations. This suggests that the increase in contact 
angle when powders are coated with hydrophobic substances is a general phenomenon rather than a distinctive

Analysis and discussion
Role of PDMS in underwater ignition
The role of PDMS in the present system is multifaceted. Experimentally, its primary function is to provide a 
hydrophobic coating that facilitates underwater ignition by reducing direct contact between water and the ther -
mite particles. However, additional effects may also contribute, including thermal insulation, interfacial modifi -
cation, and possible gas generation during decomposition. The relative importance of these mechanisms cannot 
be resolved based on the current data, and their contributions are coupled under the transient high-temperature 
conditions of ignition. A more detailed understanding of the role of the polymer coating would require systematic 
studies, such as comparisons with non-reactive hydrophobic coatings, systematic variations in coating thickness 
and composition, and direct characterization of thermal and gas-generation behavior. These investigations are 
beyond the scope of the present work and will be addressed in future studies.
Bubble dynamics modeling
During the nanothermite reaction, the initial nanoparticles are rapidly transformed into Al₂O₃ and Cu products, 
which are generated in a highly transient and expanding environment. A significant portion of these particles is 
entrained within the bubble or concentrated near its interface rather than uniformly dispersed in the surrounding 
liquid. While studies have shown that clustered nanoparticles can significantly modify local rheological behavior 
even at low concentrations23,24, in the present system such effects are expected to be localized. Consequently, the 
bulk liquid may still be approximated as Newtonian to first order, while localized particle–fluid interactions near 
the interface may enhance viscous dissipation, damping, and deviations from idealized bubble dynamics. These 
effects are not explicitly included in the current model and represent a source of uncertainty.
The effect of ambient pressure was not explicitly investigated in this work. At increased water depth, higher 
pressure is expected to enhance gas solubility and suppress bubble formation and persistence. This may reduce 
the effectiveness of the hydrophobic air layer and increase heat loss to the surrounding liquid, potentially inhib -
iting ignition and sustained combustion. Therefore, the present approach may have limited applicability under 
high-pressure underwater conditions.
The Rayleigh–Plesset equation describes the radial dynamics of a gas bubble in a liquid by balancing inertial, 
viscous, surface tension, and pressure forces acting on a spherical interface. It is derived under the assumptions 
of a perfectly spherical bubble in an infinite, incompressible liquid, with symmetric radial motion. In the present 
study, the observed bubble is not perfectly spherical but rather approximately hemispherical and attached to a 
solid surface, which introduces geometric asymmetry and boundary effects. These conditions violate the strict

interpreted qualitatively. Accordingly, any gas temperatures, pressures, or gas quantities inferred from the model 
should be interpreted as approximate estimates subject to the assumptions of the analysis rather than directly 
measured quantities.

To assess the plausibility of the assumed gas content, an order-of-magnitude energy-balance analysis was 
performed. For a typical sample mass of approximately 3 mg and an experimentally measured heat release of 
1030 J/g obtained from the DSC measurements, the total released energy is approximately 3.1 J. If this energy 
were used entirely for water vaporization, the maximum amount of water vapor produced would be approxi -
mately 7.6 × 10 −5mol. This value is of the same order of magnitude as the gas quantity used in the bubble model 
( ∼ 10−5 mol), suggesting that the assumed gas content is physically reasonable. Since heat losses to the surround-
ing liquid, sensible heating of the products, and bubble-expansion work are neglected, this estimate represents an 
upper bound on the amount of vapor generated.
Based on this analysis and the experimentally observed bubble volume after 20 ms, a representative total gas 
quantity of 3.8 × 10 −5mol was assumed at 10 ms, comprising approximately 2.6 × 10 −5mol of water vapor and 
1.2 × 10 −5mol of other gases. It should be emphasized that these gas quantities are not directly measured. Rather, 
they are representative values selected within physically reasonable bounds to reproduce the observed bubble 
dynamics through the Rayleigh–Plesset analysis and thermodynamic considerations. Under these assumptions, 
the calculated effective gas temperature can reach approximately 750 K. This temperature should be regarded as 
a model-derived effective value rather than an experimentally measured gas temperature.
The choice of temperature and gas quantity is not unique, as their product determines the bubble pressure 
through the ideal gas law. The maximum temperature is constrained by the nanothermite reaction temperature and

Oscillation behavior and frequency analysis
To further characterize the oscillatory behavior of the bubble, the natural frequency can be estimated using Min-
naert’s formulation. It should be noted, however, that this model is derived under the assumptions of a spherical 
bubble undergoing small-amplitude oscillations in an infinite, incompressible, and Newtonian liquid. In contrast, 
the bubbles observed in the present study are millimeter-scale, approximately hemispherical, and attached to a 
solid boundary, and they undergo large-amplitude oscillations with continuous gas generation and heat transfer. 
These conditions deviate from the assumptions of the Minnaert model and can affect the effective inertia, damp-
ing, and restoring forces governing the oscillation.
Therefore, the Minnaert equation is employed here only as a first-order, order-of-magnitude estimate of the 
oscillation frequency. Deviations between predicted and observed frequencies are expected due to geometric 
asymmetry, boundary effects, and nonlinear oscillation dynamics. The natural bubble frequency could be esti -
mated by a formula proposed by Minnaert in adiabatic conditions27:

Equation (4) yields an estimated frequency of approximately 360 Hz, which is consistent with the simulated

First, the bubble does not exist in an infinite liquid environment as assumed in Minnaert’s derivation, and the 
presence of a nearby solid surface reduces the effective inertia of the surrounding liquid. In addition, the bubble is 
approximately hemispherical rather than spherical, resulting in asymmetric boundary conditions that are known 
to increase the oscillation frequency.
Furthermore, the bubble shape evolves over time and deviates from the spherical assumption, particularly 
after 14 ms during the collapse stage. The Minnaert model also assumes a uniform bubble with small-amplitude 
oscillations, whereas in the present system the bubble undergoes large-amplitude dynamics with continuous gas 
generation and heat exchange. These non-ideal and transient effects introduce additional complexity and contrib-
ute to the observed deviation between the predicted and measured frequencies.

Gas generation mechanisms
Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) can provide further insight into 
the behavior of solid combustion under water. TGA/DSC results were measured by a Simultaneous Thermal 
Analyzer (Netzsch STA 449 F1 Jupiter®). For each experiment, about 5 mg sample was placed in an alumina pan 
with lid, heated to 1500 ℃ at a rate of 20 ℃/min. The samples were also protected by argon at a flow rate of 200 
mL/min.
As shown in Fig. 8, the DSC of Al/CuO/PDMS shows the main exothermic reaction of nanothermite around 
600 ℃. The energy release is 1030 J/g, which is lower than the theoretical 4080 J/g9. This discrepancy is normal 
as the energy release measured by DSC is usually much lower than the theoretical and combustion values due 
to incomplete reaction11. The relatively slow heating rate in DSC measurements differ significantly from rapid 
ignition conditions, which can alter reaction pathways and reduce the extent of reaction, thereby contributing to 
lower measured energy release10. Additionally, the incorporation of PDMS may further contribute to incomplete 
reaction, which requires more detailed investigation in future work.
A small endothermal dent was found at around 224–294 ℃, which may be credited to the small amount of 
PDMS in the system that decomposed. The reaction temperature loosely matches the range of the PDMS decom-
position. Therefore, under flash pyrolysis conditions, the nanothermite reaction temperature is high enough to 
decompose the PDMS. Besides, the small endothermal reaction at 1085 ℃ is the melting of Cu produced from 
the nanothermite reaction.

With this information, a preliminary analysis of the bubble’s composition can be attempted. The generated 
gases in the final state illustrated in Fig. 4p is estimated to be approximately 10 small bubbles with a total volume 
of about 0.1 ml. On the other hand, the 0.25 mg PDMS in the sample, under standard conditions, is anticipated 
to generate approximately 0.01 ml of gas, assuming the gas products are oligomers with an average degree of 
polymerization of 4 in a fast-reacting scenario 30, resulting in a molar mass of approximately 300. However, the 
boiling points of these oligomers are around 140 to 290 ℃31, which are significantly higher than the boiling point 
of water. According to the previous simulation, the bubble undergoes rapid cooling, suggesting that these oligo-
mers would not remain in a gaseous state.
A different simulation by Bellama et al. suggests that smaller molecular species may be generated under rapid 
heating conditions32, and that PDMS decomposition under irradiation can produce light hydrocarbons such as 
methane or ethane through methyl radicals generated by Si–C bond cleavage 32,33. However, the extent to which 
such species are produced under the present conditions cannot be determined from the current experiments. 
Based on an idealized estimation, the maximum gas volume generated solely from PDMS decomposition would 
be on the order of ~ 0.1 mL, which is comparable to the observed bubble volume. This comparison suggests that 
additional gas sources may contribute; however, no direct measurement of gas composition was performed, and 
therefore this interpretation remains uncertain.
Other possible gas sources may include decomposition products associated with the nanothermite reaction. 
For example, partial decomposition of CuO could, in principle, release oxygen under certain conditions, although 
gas-phase oxygen generation is not required for nanothermite reactions 34. In addition, PDMS may undergo fur -
ther reactions in the high-temperature environment, potentially forming gaseous products such as CO or CO₂35.
In addition to gas generation from PDMS decomposition and possible contributions from the nanothermite 
reaction, the formation of water vapor due to rapid heating of the surrounding liquid is expected to play a sig -
nificant role in the bubble dynamics. Given the substantial temperature difference between the reacting thermite 
(which can reach thousand Kelvin) and the surrounding water, localized boiling at the solid–liquid interface is 
likely to occur on very short timescales. An order-of-magnitude comparison suggests that the volume of vapor 
generated by water phase change can be comparable to, or even exceed, that produced solely from PDMS decom-
position under the present conditions. Therefore, water vapor is likely a major component of the bubble, particu-
larly during the early stages of expansion.
However, due to rapid cooling and condensation processes indicated by the simulations, the relative contribu-
tion of water vapor is expected to decrease during later stages of bubble evolution. In the absence of direct gas 
composition measurements, the bubble is interpreted as a mixture of water vapor and reaction-generated gases, 
including PDMS decomposition products and possible thermite-related species. While the presence of water 
vapor is strongly supported by thermal considerations, the precise composition and relative contributions of these 
gas species remain uncertain.
It should be emphasized that the proposed gas-generation pathways are qualitative, as the chemical composi -
tion of the bubble cannot be determined from the present data. A definitive understanding would require direct 
experimental characterization, such as gas chromatography, mass spectrometry, or in-situ spectroscopic diagnos-
tics, which are beyond the scope of this study.

Bubble evolution mechanism
Based on the energy properties of the reagents and the simulations, a more detailed analysis on the evolution of 
the bubble could be made as follows:
Before the bubble appeared at 2.3 ms, the sample remained nearly motionless, undergoing continuous heating 
with only negligible movements observed in the air pocket.
While the bubble that emerged at 2.3 ms could be water vapor, observations by Akhatov et al. revealed that a 
millimeter-sized bubble induced in water by a laser pulse collapses within a microsecond, and experiments by 
Kim et al. suggests that it takes seconds for focused continuous laser to induce a bubble in water 36. Considering 
the rapid heat transfer at the liquid/vapor interface summarized by Kim 37, it is suggested that the bubble com -
prised other gases. Considering that PDMS can decompose at approximately 170 ℃35, CuO starts to decompose 
at above 700–800 ℃38, and our DSG results demonstrated weight loss in the Al/CuO-PDMS composite starting 
at about 300 ℃, the initial slow-expansion stage may be attributed to PDMS decomposition at this temperature. 
This endothermic decomposition was sustained by the external laser, and with the gas generated in this process, 
heat loss to the water was greatly reduced.
When the sample reached approximately 550 ℃ in Fig. 4d, the nanothermite reaction started. This exothermic 
reaction became self-sustaining, resulting in the rapid expansion of the bubble. Numerous events, including ther-
mal expansion, water evaporation, release of dissolved gasses, PDMS decomposition into smaller molecules, and 
CuO decomposition into O2, together pushing the bubble to its maximum size.
Despite the nanothermite reaction lasted until 28 ms as shown in Fig. 4n, o and completely ended in Fig. 4p, 
the average bubble size started to decrease 10 ms before the reaction ended. The bubble then underwent approxi-
mately 6 pulsations following this general trend, as discussed earlier. Furthermore, the bubble experiences desta-
bilization, the spherical surface fractured into smaller pieces over time.
This pulsation is quite different from the typical dynamics of other underwater bubbles, which contract to a 
much smaller size before rebounding, and maintaining a spherical form in the first cycle 39–42. This may suggest 
that gas generation occurs throughout the entire reaction, distinguishing it from the free expansion of the bubble.
Analyzing the bubble evolution and the sources of gas generation clarifies the chemical reactions occurring in 
the millisecond-scale regime. These observations also point toward the need to investigate the potential electronic 
interactions that happen on much shorter timescales that may help initiate the chemical reactions.
Interfacial considerations and conceptual energy-band alignment
The optical measurements presented in the Supplementary Material indicate that the optical response of the Al/
CuO nanothermite differs from that of its individual constituents. Specifically, changes in the measured optical 
spectra were observed after Al and CuO nanoparticles were combined to form the nanothermite system. These 
observations demonstrate that the nanothermite exhibits distinct optical behavior compared with the separate 
materials. However, the optical measurements alone do not provide direct evidence of electronic coupling, charge 
transfer, carrier transport, or other interfacial electronic processes.
CuO semiconductors can exhibit either p-type or n-type conductivity, as discussed by Aleksandar Živković 
and Nora H. de Leeuw43. Previous investigations have predominantly reported p-type conduction in CuO, which 
is attributed to the presence of copper vacancies and interstitial oxygen in the solid 44,45. The work function of

Figure 9 is presented solely as a conceptual illustration based on reported material properties and literature 
work-function values. It is intended to provide a qualitative visualization of the Al/CuO interface and should 
not be interpreted as representing experimentally observed electronic processes, carrier dynamics, or interfacial 
energy-transfer mechanisms. Under laser irradiation, the nanothermite absorbs optical energy, as evidenced by 
the measured optical response. However, the present study does not determine how the absorbed energy is distrib-

regarding electronic excitation, charge transfer, carrier transport, localized energy deposition, or reaction-initia -
tion mechanisms can be drawn from the present measurements.
Compared with micron-scale thermites, nanothermites possess substantially larger specific interfacial areas 
and a higher density of Al/CuO contacts. The optical measurements demonstrate that the nanothermite exhibits 
a different optical response from its individual constituents. While these observations indicate that the presence 
of Al/CuO interfaces is associated with altered optical behavior, the present measurements do not establish the 
microscopic origin of these differences or identify the underlying physical mechanisms.

established material properties reported in the literature and the experimentally observed optical behavior of the

transfer processes. Elucidation of the underlying microscopic mechanisms would require direct experimental 
characterization of electronic structure and carrier dynamics, which is beyond the scope of the present work.

Proposed mechanism of laser-induced bubble evolution
Figure 10 summarizes the proposed bubble evolution mechanism derived from the experimental observations and 
the preceding discussion, highlighting the coupled effects of nanothermite heat release, PDMS decomposition, 
and interfacial water vaporization that govern bubble nucleation, growth, oscillation, fragmentation, and col -
lapse. Upon laser irradiation, the incident energy is primarily absorbed by the embedded Al/CuO nanothermite 
particle, triggering a highly exothermic reaction that rapidly heats the surrounding PDMS matrix and initiates 
gas generation. The combined effects of PDMS pyrolysis and interfacial water vaporization lead to the formation 
and expansion of a hemispherical bubble, whose subsequent evolution is governed by the interplay among heat 
release, gas production, vapor condensation, and the inertia of the surrounding liquid.
The proposed mechanism consists of eight stages. First, the incident laser energy passes through the water 
and PDMS with negligible absorption and is predominantly absorbed by the Al/CuO nanothermite core. Second, 
localized heating initiates the thermite reaction, releasing a substantial amount of heat. Third, the surrounding 
PDMS undergoes rapid pyrolysis, generating gaseous decomposition products and forming a hemispherical gas 
dome at the water–PDMS interface. Fourth, heat transfer from the reaction zone to the adjacent water causes 
intense vaporization, and the generated water vapor mixes with the pyrolysis gases, resulting in further bubble 
inflation. Fifth, continued heat release and gas generation rapidly increase the internal pressure, driving dramatic 
hemispherical bubble expansion. Sixth, the bubble undergoes oscillatory motion due to the competition between 
internal pressure and the inertia of the surrounding liquid. Seventh, as the reaction subsides and heat dissipates,

Limitations and future work
Introducing PDMS and the surrounding water environment further increases the complexity of the system by add-
ing additional thermal, chemical, and transport processes, including potential gas generation. Although unlikely 
to dominate under the present conditions, it is theoretically possible for aluminum to react with water vapor to 
form hydrogen50, and such secondary pathways cannot be completely excluded.
Overall, a more definitive understanding of the underlying mechanisms would require additional experimental 
investigation, such as in-situ spectroscopic diagnostics, emission measurements, or direct analysis of reaction 
intermediates and products. Future work incorporating such techniques would help clarify the respective roles of 
interfacial processes, thermal effects, and gas generation in the ignition and combustion behavior.
The present study focuses on demonstrating underwater ignition feasibility and characterizing the associ -
ated bubble dynamics, which are markedly different from combustion behavior in air or vacuum. While these 
observations provide important insight into the ignition process, a comprehensive assessment of combustion 
performance—including ignition-energy thresholds, reaction duration statistics, propagation behavior, pressure 
generation, and energy efficiency—remains beyond the scope of the current work. Such quantitative characteriza-
tion is an important direction for future research and will be pursued to provide a more complete understanding 
of underwater nanothermite combustion.
Summary
By dispersing PDMS prepolymers and Al/CuO nanothermite powders in a solvent such as acetone, a hydrophobic 
PDMS coating was formed around the nanothermite particles, enabling laser ignition underwater. The hydropho-
bic coating plays a critical role by reducing heat loss to the surrounding water and facilitating energy accumula-
tion for ignition. The water contact angle increased from 115° to 150° as the PDMS content increased from 1% to 
12%; however, only samples containing 2–9% PDMS could be successfully ignited underwater.
This study further investigated the heat-transfer and ignition mechanisms of the Al/CuO nanothermite system. 
Analysis based on the Rayleigh–Plesset equation, together with experimental observations and thermodynamic 
considerations, suggests rapid cooling and condensation of water vapor during bubble evolution, indicating that 
the bubble likely contains both water vapor and reaction-generated gases.
These findings improve the understanding of heat transport and ignition processes in nanothermite systems and 
demonstrate an effective strategy for achieving underwater ignition through hydrophobic surface modification. 
The coating approach may be extended to other energetic materials and hydrophobic polymers. In addition, the 
observed optical behavior and large interfacial area of the Al/CuO nanothermite suggest that interfacial effects 
may influence energy absorption and localization during laser ignition, although the detailed underlying mecha-
nisms require further experimental investigation.
