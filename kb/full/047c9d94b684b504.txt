Pneumococcal population structure 
influences the effects of air pollution on 
invasive disease risk in South Africa
 
Sophie Belman   1,2,12 , Cebile Lekhuleni   3,4, Jackie Kleynhans3,5, 
Giovenale Moirano1,6, Daniela Lührsen   1, Cristina Carnerero   1, 
Susan Meiring   7, Stephanie W. Lo   2,8, Mignon du Plessis   3,4, 
Anne von Gottberg   3,4,9,13 & Rachel Lowe   1,10,11,13 
Streptococcus pneumoniae is highly diverse, comprising over 100 serotypes 
and hundreds of genomic lineages amid widespread vaccination. While it 
can cause invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) which exhibits pronounced 
seasonal spikes, the interplay between pneumococcal diversity and 
environmental drivers remains unexplored. Here we analysed 59,017 IPD cases 
over 19 years from South Africa, incorporating 4,350 genome-sequenced 
isolates, using Bayesian spatiotemporal models to link environmental 
exposure and pneumococcal diversity. Cumulatively, across an 8-week period, 
moderate relative humidity (33–49%) and cold minimum temperatures 
(4–10 °C) increased IPD risk by 5% and 4%, respectively. Conversely, warm 
maximum temperatures (27–38 °C) were associated with up to a 10% increased 
risk within a week of exposure. There was a positive association between air 
pollution (PM2.5) and IPD, although it varied by age, disease presentation, 
and most notably serotype and lineage. Specifically, the lag time between 
PM2.5 exposure and disease onset varied by serotype, with only serotypes 
4, 8 and 23F conferring an immediate IPD risk. High prevalence of GPSC21 
lineage (serotype 19F) also modified the pollution response, shifting the lag 
structure to produce immediate risk of disease following high PM2.5 exposure. 
Our results demonstrate that pneumococcal population structure shapes 
air quality risk which in turn can shape the fitness landscape of microbial 
populations. Integration of these data may inform public health policy.
Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus) is asymptomatically car-
ried in the human nasopharynx but can progress to non-invasive disease 
(for example, otitis media) or invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) (for 
example, bacteraemia or meningitis) when it breaches the epithelium1. 
Pneumococci are highly diverse, comprising hundreds of genomic line-
ages (Global Pneumococcal Sequence Clusters; GPSCs)2 and over 100 
serotypes3. Progression through asymptomatic carriage to invasive dis-
ease is mediated by a combination of environmental drivers, microbial 
virulence and host immunity. There is known variability in virulence, 
carriage durations and sociodemographic populations affected by 
different lineages and serotypes2,4. Despite high vaccination coverage, 
IPD shows consistent seasonal spikes each year5. Some serotypes are 
targeted by vaccines such as the pneumococcal conjugate vaccines 
(PCV) and despite overall IPD reductions, the years following vaccine 
implementation have seen replacement by non-vaccine-targeted sero-
types (NVTs) globally4,6–8. Efforts to reduce IPD burden focuses on

Meteorological models showed a nonlinear cumulative risk 
for temperature (Fig. 2a), with underlying divergent lag structures: 
warm temperatures triggered acute risks, whereas cold-related risks 
were delayed. Only cold temperatures (4–10 °C) showed a significant 
cumulative increase (6 °C relative risk (RR): 1.04; 95% credible inter -
vals (CI) 1.01–1.06), although in warmer weeks (31–38 °C) we found a 
5–11% risk increase relative to the median (Extended Data Fig. 5c and 
Supplementary Fig. 2).
Relative humidity provided a better model fit (Δ Watanabe–Akaike 
information criterion (WAIC) 77.7) than absolute humidity (the abso-
lute amount of vapour in air, g m−3). Absolute humidity had a stronger 
association at low values (4 g m−3, cumulative RR 1.23: 95% CI 1.16–1.30) 
with an acute increase in disease risk until a 2-week lag time. For relative 
humidity, the cumulative risk was nonlinear. The highest risk occurred 
at moderate humidity levels (34–47%) with a maximum cumulative 
RR of 1.05 (95% CI 1.03–1.07) at 39.8% relative to the median (Fig. 2b). 
However, at 74.8% relative humidity there was a strong protective effect 
(RR 0.77: 95% CI 0.73–0.88) (Fig. 2b). Weaker effects observed for low 
relative humidity probably reflect dependence on temperature (indi-
cating that temperature mediates humidity-related effects) (Fig. 2b, 
Extended Data Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 4).
Increasing precipitation and a higher standardized precipitation 
and evapotranspiration index (SPEI) (reduced drought conditions) 
decreased IPD risk (negative associations) (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). 
Increasing average wind speed increased cumulative IPD risk (positive 
association), while increasing ozone concentrations (ozone increases 
with increasing temperatures) decreased cumulative IPD risk, concord-
ant with decreasing risk in the summer months (Extended Data Fig. 6c,d 
and Supplementary Fig. 2).
We estimated the risk of disease above the 2016–2029 National 
Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) thresholds in South 
Africa22 for PM 2.5 (40 μg m−3), PM10 (75 μg m−3) and SO 2 (125 μg m −3) 
(Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). T o account for meteorological con-
founding in air pollution models, we evaluated multiple formula -
tions and lag structures of temperature and humidity. The final air 
pollution models were adjusted for mean temperature in the same 
week as the pollutant and absolute humidity at lag 3 (3 weeks earlier) 
(Supplementary Fig. 3 and Tables 4 and 7). PM2.5, PM10 and SO2 all had 
positive associations with IPD (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 5).
With a weekly average of 50 μg m −3 of PM 2.5, there was a 3.4% 
8-week cumulative increased risk of IPD (cumulative RR 1.03: 95% CI 
1.02–1.05). Strikingly, there was an 18% increased IPD risk (cumulative 
RR 1.18: 95% CI 1.12–1.23) with a weekly PM 2.5 of 100 μg m−3 (Fig. 2c). 
The risks from high (100 μg m−3) concentrations of PM2.5 were imme-
diate (lag week 0 RR 1.02: 95% CI 1.01–1.04) and persisted for 6 weeks 
(Extended Data Fig. 5). These patterns were robust to models fitted to 
Gauteng province alone, and nationally excluding Gauteng (Fig. 2a, and 
Supplementary Figs. 4–7 and Table 4). We also explored the potential 
for fine-scale spatiotemporal confounding of the PM2.5 effect by allow-
ing spatial effects to vary by year. This showed high correlation with 
the interannual province-level effects from the sociodemographic 
base model, with a few urban districts as outliers. However, the PM2.5 
exposure–response relationship was robust to this analysis, indicat-
ing that fine-scale spatiotemporal confounding was not influential 
(Supplementary Fig. 8). The city of Johannesburg regularly exceeded 
weekly averages of 100 μg m−3 of PM2.5 (32 weeks in 2023) and across 
South Africa, the weekly PM2.5 concentration was at least 50 μg m−3 in

and Supplementary Table 4). In 2019, Gauteng province had a mean

However, the role of air quality and meteorological factors, 
such as air pollution, temperature and humidity, in pneumococcal 
colonization and disease dynamics are typically overlooked. Many 
geographic regions with the highest pneumococcal disease bur -
den also have populations with extensive exposure to polluted air 11. 
Existing evidence around the impact of environmental factors on 
pneumococcal disease is heterogenous12. Impacts of temperature and 
humidity on pneumococcal disease vary by climate, with inconsist -
ent global patterns 13–16. Similarly, air pollution is widely considered 
to increase IPD risk but the mechanisms are poorly understood 12,17. 
Most studies have relied on correlations, few assessed delayed effects 
(lag time from exposure to outcome), or controlled for confounding 
meteorological effects 16,18,19.
Important gaps remain in understanding nonlinear and lagged 
effects, and how risks vary by age, disease type, serotype and lineage. 
Accounting for pneumococcal diversity together with environmental 
factors can uncover the biological mechanisms behind environment–
disease interactions ultimately towards improved vaccination strategy 
modelling and hospital capacity preparations with changing climates.
Here we investigate the relationship between a range of air qual-
ity and meteorological factors on IPD using case data from over 500 
hospitals across 19 years in South Africa. This is a setting with pneumo-
coccal colonization estimates ranging from ~40–60%20,21, and marked 
geographic and climatic diversity. Major public health interventions 
occurred during the study period (PCV7 introduction in 2009, PCV13 
in 2011, and widespread antiretroviral therapy (ART) for treatment of 
people living with HIV (PLHIV), since 2005) (Supplementary Table 1). 
We focus on a major component of air pollution, particulate matter 
<2.5 μm in size (PM2.5) and the associated IPD risk, while accounting for 
pneumococcal phenotypic and genomic data, to understand interac-
tions with underlying microbial ecology across different serotypes and 
lineages. We apply Bayesian hierarchical models to capture spatial and 
temporal variability, incorporate delayed and nonlinear relationships 
between environmental exposure and disease onset, and allow for 
stratification by age group and disease type. These models enable a 
better understanding of the interaction between environmental factors 
and pneumococcal populations, and how it plays a role in IPD dynamics.
Results
A total of 59,017 identified IPD cases were included from 531 hospitals 
in South Africa between 2005 and 2023. Most IPD cases were found 
in urban centres where the majority of the South African population 
resides, including 41% of total IPD from Gauteng province, 18% from 
Western Cape province, as well as 11 and 9% from KwaZulu-Natal and 
Eastern Cape provinces (Fig. 1a,b). Disease cases included bacteraemia, 
meningitis and other atypical IPD types such as pericarditis and empy-
ema. The majority of cases occurred in people aged 15–64 years (62%), 
followed by children under 6 years (23%). Of cases, 4.8% were from 
>64-year-olds. There was a well-characterized decrease in serotypes 
targeted by PCVs (Supplementary Table 1). We observed NVT persis-
tence (Fig. 1c) and consistent seasonality in IPD cases. Cases peaked in 
colder months across the time period coinciding with the seasonality 
of PM2.5 concentration and inversely correlating with relative humidity 
(Fig. 1d, Extended Data Fig. 1, and Supplementary Fig. 1 and Table 2).

Risk from PM2.5 varies by age, disease and pneumococcal 
serotype
T o assess the varying impact of air pollution across outcome types, we 
stratified IPD outcomes by particular demographic groups, disease 
outcomes and pneumococcal serotypes. We employed the same air pol-
lution model formulation described above, which included the sociode-
mographic base model with meteorological confounders (temperature 
and humidity), and a DLNM for PM2.5, PM10 and SO2, respectively. We 
focus here on PM2.5 due to its majority anthropogenic sourcing in South 
Africa23–25 and clear positive association with pneumococcal disease.

a cumulative RR for 50 μg m −3 weekly average PM 2.5 of 1.16 (95% CI

the higher prevalence of HIV among 15–64-year-olds 26. The risk for

NVT serotype 8 (94% GPSC3 in this dataset), and PCV13 serotypes 23F 
(68% GPSC14) and 4 (58% GPSC70) (Extended Data Fig. 8). In addition, 
we found a greater 8-week cumulative risk for some serotype-specific 
IPD than from the main model. With 50 μg m −3 average weekly PM2.5 
concentration, there was a higher risk of IPD with PCV7 serotype 14 
(RR ratio 1.06: 95% CI 1.00–1.11; 49% GPSC10), PCV13 serotype 19A (RR 
ratio 1.04: 95% CI 0.99–1.09; 92% GPSC17) and NVT serotype 8 (RR ratio 
1.05: 95% CI 0.99–1.11; 94% GPSC3) (Fig. 3a, Extended Data Fig. 8a–c, and 
Supplementary Tables 8 and 9). These results underscore the sensitivity 
of some invasive serotypes to air pollution5,27.
Timing of air quality effects is modified by lineage diversity
We estimated the impact of PM2.5 on IPD at the province level accord-
ing to particular GPSC-lineage prevalence, accounting for an 8-week 
lag time for the years 2005–2019. In these models, we included IPD 
as the outcome and incorporated genomic lineages into the model 
(n = 4,350) by including the proportion sequenced at each space–time 
increment as an effect modifier (Supplementary Fig. 11). We interacted 
the cross-basis for each environmental factor with the proportion of 
each dominant genomic lineage (n ≥ 100 and GPSC8) among sequenced 
isolates (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Table 2). Lineage diversity interac-
tion effects improved goodness-of-fit metrics (Supplementary Fig. 13 
and Table 4).
We explored the risk of IPD given the GPSC prevalence across 
concentrations of PM2.5 (where prevalence is across quantiles of GPSC 
proportions over the entire time period; low=0.1 and high=0.9) (Fig. 4a 
and Supplementary Table 10). While the 8-week cumulative effect did 
not vary with lineage diversity, there was variation in risk across lags 
and GPSCs (Supplementary Fig. 13a,b and Extended Data Fig. 9). There 
was an acute effect for GPSC21 whereby the risk of IPD was elevated 
given the high prevalence of these lineages. The cumulative effect with 
a GPSC21 interaction also had different exposure–response curves 
between high and low prevalence (Fig. 4c ). We found an immediate 
effect whereby locations with a high prevalence of GPSC21 had an acute 
risk of IPD in the same week as high concentrations of PM2.5, whereas 
times and locations with low prevalence of GPSC21 had an increased risk 
starting at 5 weeks after the exposure (Fig. 4c). In this dataset, GPSC21 
comprised serotype 19F (n  = 90) with one instance of 19A in 2005. 
Serotype 19F is targeted by PCV7 and is characterized in South Africa 
by an initial GPSC1 lineage (n  = 114) which decreased post PCV, when 
GPSC21-19F became predominant (Fig. 4b)5. GPSC21 interaction mod-
els consistently modified PM2.5 effect timing (Supplementary Fig. 14). 
Although the mechanism remains unclear, circulating lineage diversity 
evidently plays a role in environment–disease relationships.
Discussion
In this study, we applied spatiotemporal Bayesian hierarchical models 
to investigate the relationship between meteorological factors, air 
pollution and IPD risk. We identified complex, delayed and nonlinear 
effects from meteorological factors and heterogeneous effects from 
PM2.5 across demographic groups, disease outcomes and serotypes.

19F had high prevalence. Our study corroborates previous evidence 
for increased risk at lower humidity, temperature and precipitation, 
contributing to a growing pool of evidence across sociodemographic 
and climatic regions12. T emperature effects were nuanced: higher tem-
peratures conferred immediate risk while cold effects were delayed, 
possibly reflecting differing mechanistic relationships with IPD. Effect 
sizes for temperature and air pollution were modest (below 10%) while 
cumulative humidity effects were larger (25% risk reduction). We con-
firmed a positive relationship of PM2.5, PM10 and SO2 with IPD.
We observed the most immediate effect from PM2.5 in individuals 
aged 15–64 years, while the cumulative effect was greatest for those 
≥65 years old. These patterns are consistent with previous studies 
demonstrating heightened susceptibility among older adults 16,28,29, 
possibly reflecting cumulative exposure and reduced lung function16,30 
triggering increased risk for IPD from PM2.5. We found no differences 
with sex stratification, although the lack of household air pollution 
data may mask gendered effects31,32.
We identified heterogeneous effects of PM 2.5 across serotypes, 
with immediate increases in risk for some (including NVT serotype 
8 and PCV serotypes 4 and 23F) and lagged effects for others. This 
may reflect differences in colonization and invasion dynamics across 
serotypes33. These patterns could be partly explained by vaccine-driven 
shifts in carriage. Serotypes with higher population prevalence may 
respond immediately to air pollution exposure, whereas those with 
reduced post-vaccine carriage (PCV13 types) may exhibit delayed 
risk5,34,35. Mechanistically, the effect of PM2.5 on IPD is probably related to 
both the host (for example, increased susceptibility to colonization36, 
decreased mucociliary clearance37) and the bacterium (for example, 
increased transformation capacity38 and biofilm formation) 17,39. Pre-
vious studies have identified increased epithelial adhesion, biofilm 
formation and bacterial burden with air pollution 30,33,36,37, while oth-
ers found a decrease in carriage acquisition with increasing PM 2.5 
concentrations 13,39. The acute effects observed for serotype 4, 19A 
and 23F suggest divergent serotype-specific invasion pathways. For 
example, the persistence of serotype 4 in adult IPD 5,40,41 may reflect 
unique environmental sensitivity driven by its prevalent psrP virulence 
gene, which could interact with PM 2.5-induced microabrasions 42 to 
accelerate invasion with minimal lag time41,42.

advantages. This relationship is further shaped by the inextricable, 
bidirectional link between PM and climate change, whereby particulate 
matter influences atmospheric chemistry, while climatic shifts affect 
the formation and distribution of PM44. In high-exposure regions, such 
as the Gauteng province in South Africa where the concentrations of 
PM2.5 and PM10 are extremely high, these environmental pressures are 
particularly acute. The current daily threshold aim for PM2.5 in South 
Africa is 40 μg m−3 whereas the WHO threshold is 15 μg m−3. We found in 
the year 2019 that 15.7 cases might have been averted if the South Afri-

This study provides microbial diversity-aware estimates of envi-
ronmentally driven IPD risk, demonstrating that the risk is not uniform 
across host or microbial populations. These relationships should be 
studied across different regions with varied climatic conditions and 
demographic structures. T o improve our understanding of how envi-
ronmental factors affect different serotypes or lineages, stratified 
epidemiological analysis and mechanistic experiments are needed to 
define the biological interactions between microbial diversity, host 
susceptibility and the environment. The time period, high spatial 
resolution and extensive metadata of our study is an asset for robust 
space–time ecological modelling studies. Our approach could be lever-
aged for other infectious diseases to incorporate microbial genomic 
data into environmental epidemiological models.
The reanalysis and observational datasets for PM 2.5 are imper -
fect. Our study highlights the critical need for regional bias-corrected 
models to accurately assess the impact of air pollution on infectious 
diseases globally (Extended Data Fig. 10). Our use of South African 
NAAQS baselines and weekly means may underestimate air pollution 
risks relative to more stringent WHO 24-hour standards. While the lack 
of granular indoor air pollution data is a limitation, the strong correla-
tion between indoor and ambient pollutants suggests that our results 
still capture the primary exposure–outcome association31,45. Further-
more, we used proxy variables (for example, population density and 
spatiotemporal random effects) to account for unobserved risks from 
indoor household factors or extreme weather events such as flooding 
and associated population changes46. Subdistrict-level models could 
better elucidate these fine-scale effects.

In summary, environmental factors, specifically temperature, 
humidity and air pollution, influence IPD risk in South Africa. We iden-
tified delayed and nonlinear associations which vary by serotype or 
lineage. While PM2.5 was associated with IPD overall, the highest risk 
is among those ≥65 years old, and there is increased risk for particular 
serotypes with variable exposure–response patterns. The variable lag 
effects suggest that the biological mechanisms driving PM2.5-related 
risk differ given the underlying microbial population and sociodemo-
graphic factors. Bacterial populations respond non-uniformly to the 
environment. Our findings suggest that climate change and urbaniza-
tion may shift IPD dynamics. Meeting established air pollution policy 
thresholds remains a critical priority to protect human health.
Methods
Data processing
Pneumococcal disease datasets. Epidemiological cases . The case 
counts of invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) were provided by 
the National Institute for Communicable Diseases (NICD) in South 
Africa as part of national surveillance encompassing all nine prov -
inces (GERMS-SA). We included the entire dataset which comprised 
culture-positive for pneumococcus with viable isolates available 
for further analysis, culture-positive with no viable isolates avail -
able and culture-negative with metadata results from PCR. Isolates 
were phenotypically characterized by the Quellung method using 
serotype-specific antisera (Statens Serum Institute, Copenhagen, 
Denmark) as previously described 5,47, with >97% concordance with 
in silico typing for all serotypes of focus (PCV7 serotypes 14, 19F , 
23F , 3; PCV13 serotypes 1, 19A, 3, 6A; NVT serotypes 12F and 8) 48 
(Supplementary Table 9). We utilized the phenotypic serotypes 
for our analysis. Antimicrobial resistance was predicted from the 
genomes utilizing the CDC pneumococcal typing pipeline for the 
detection of resistance determinants and in silico minimum inhibi -
tory concentration (MIC) predictions 5,47. Penicillin resistance was 
determined using the meningitis MIC cut-off value 49. Finally, these 
included pneumococcal disease lines list cases (N  = 59,017) between 
2005 and 2023 collected from 531 hospitals across 52 districts in South 
Africa (Fig. 1a,b). These excluded n = 661 cases from the original data-
set without hospital geolocation. Most cases were found in Gaut -
eng province with 24,303 cases. Northern Cape reported the least 
number of cases, with 1,067 (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Disease types 
included 32,639 bacteraemia (55.3%), 21,167 cases of meningitis (35.9%) 
and 5,211 other IPD cases (8.8%), such as empyema, septic arthritis, 
endophthalmitis, peritonitis or pericarditis. These were classified 
given specimen type whereby meningitis cases were isolated from 
cerebrospinal fluid specimens, bacteraemia from blood specimens, 
and other invasive disease classes were from other typically sterile 
sites. Bacteraemic cases include bacteraemic pneumonia but not 
non-bacteraemic pneumonia. The median age was 32 years with 13% 
of cases reported in children <1 year old. There was missing age data 
for 4.3% of cases and serotype information was missing for 37.8% of 
cases (Fig. 1c). We included data from 2005 to 2023 in concordance 
with the environmental datasets for the initial model testing. As the 
genomic dataset ran until 2020, the year marking the beginning of 
the COVID-19 pandemic (with a substantial decrease in IPD counts), 
we restricted the models to the period 2005–2019 (n = 52,437) so as to 
overlap with epidemiological cases and exclude this case reduction. In 
the overall environmental epidemiology models, we included all data; 
for the model stratifications by outcome, we included all reported data 
for each outcome. The outcomes we assessed included different age 
groups, disease types and serotypes.
Genomic sequences. The genomes included in this study were 
sequenced as part of the Global Pneumococcal Sequencing project, 
GPS50, which is a global genomic survey of S. pneumoniae. Isolates were 
collected by the NICD in South Africa from 2005 to 2020 (n  = 4,912) 
from 48 of the 52 districts. We only included genomes through the year 
2019 (n = 4,350). Isolates were randomly sampled using a previously 
described protocol whereby ~300 invasive-disease isolates from each 
year (2005–2014) were selected with a specific target age breakdown 
(50% from <3-year-olds, 25% from 3–5-year-olds, 25% from >5-year-olds) 
in the first phase. In the second phase of sequencing, 100 disease iso-
lates from 2005 to 2010 from children <5 years old were additionally 
randomly sequenced. The sampling for 2015–2020 was similar to 
phase 1, but the sampling strategy was not adjusted for the reduc -
tion in IPD cases following PCV (thus included more >5-year-olds)5,8,51. 
Most sequences are from the districts with the highest population 
sizes (Supplementary Fig. 11). By incorporating the genomic lineages 
as a modifying proportional interaction effect, and normalizing this 
by the total number of sequences in each space–time increment, we 
attempted to limit any potential biases associated with their selec -
tion. Assembly quality control parameters included a minimum aver-
age sequencing depth of 20× and an assembly length of 1.9–2.3 Mb. 
Sequences with more than 220 heterozygous single nucleotide poly-
morphic (SNP) sites were excluded. The genomes were assembled 
using Spades assembly software52 and the gene annotations were made 
using Prokka53. PopPunk2 was used to assign each genome to a global 
pneumococcal sequence cluster (GPSC). We included each GPSC with 
an N > 90 and included GPSC8 due to its public health relevance5. We 
included the proportion as the number of each GPSC over the total 
number of sequences per week per province (Supplementary Fig. 12).
Sociodemographic data. The sociodemographic data were extracted 
from multiple sources. Annual district-level population estimates 
for the period 2005–2023 were obtained from Statistics South Africa 
mid-year population estimates (Statistical Release P0302). Specifically, 
we utilized the district municipality population datasets generated 
from the 2023 projection series, which applies demographic assump-
tions (births, deaths and migration) to project forward from the 2022 
South African National Census baseline while retrospectively model-
ling historical annual trends54,55. From the same report we included per 
province ART coverage, HIV prevalence, life expectancy, growth rate, 
% children (<15) and the % elderly over time (60+). We also included 
the aging index and dependency ratio. These are defined by Statistics 
South Africa as the number over age 65 per 100 youths and the num-
ber <15 years old combined with the number 60+ over the number of 
working-age population (15–64), respectively55. PCV vaccination has a 
2 + 1 schedule in South Africa and was first introduced in 2009 (PCV7: 
serotypes 4, 6B, 9V, 14, 18C, 19F , 23F), replaced by PCV13 (additional 
serotypes 1, 3, 5, 6A, 7F , 19A) in 2011 (Supplementary Table 1). PCV cover-
age for 1, 2 and 3 doses was included from the WHO Immunization Data 
report. ‘Three doses’ is defined as the percentage of surviving infants 
who receive the 3rd dose of PCVs. We included the official coverage 
estimates as well as the WHO/UNICEF Estimates of National Coverage 
(WUENIC)56 (Supplementary Fig. 1d). The official estimates are initially 
based on surveys and other administrative messages, which, due to 
biases and inaccuracies, are adjusted by the national authorities to 
provide estimates of the most likely coverage.
Environmental datasets. South Africa comprises temperate and arid 
climates according to Köppen classification57. Gauteng and Western 
Cape provinces (where the majority of our case data comes from) are 
subtropical highland and Mediterranean climates, respectively.

Meteorological data. The environmental datasets spanned 2005–2023 
(inclusive). We included temperature, precipitation, relative and abso-
lute humidity metrics from the global reanalysis dataset ERA5-Land58 
aggregated for weekly time points at the district level. This is gridded data 
with 0.1° resolution (accounting for the mean latitude of South Africa at 
−29°, so each grid square is ~108 km2). This resulted in a mean of 218 grid 
cells per district, ranging from 15 in Johannesburg, Gauteng, to 1,180 in 
Namakwa, Northern Cape. Daily temperature metrics were retrieved and 
aggregated to weekly, such that we included the weekly mean of the daily 
mean, maximum or minimum temperature. Precipitation was initially 
retrieved as hourly average metres (m) per second precipitation loss rate 
(prlr). We converted it to daily millimetres (mm) and then tested inclu-
sion of the weekly total precipitation and the mean daily precipitation.
We calculated the standardized precipitation and evapotranspi-
ration index (SPEI) at 3-month and 6-month time periods using the 
above-mentioned precipitation and temperature data from ERA5-Land. 
The packages used for this calculation were SPEI 59, ClimProjDiags60, 
CSindicator61 and CST ools62.
Absolute humidity (absh) is defined as the mass of water vapour in 
a volume of air (in g m−3). Relative humidity (hurs) is the water vapour 
present in the air relative to saturated air and defined as the partial 
pressure of water vapour as a percentage of the saturation pressure 
(%). Differences in apparent effect magnitude between absolute and 
relative humidity should be interpreted cautiously, as they depend on 
the distribution of each variable (Fig. 1d).
Air pollution data . The air pollution datasets spanned 2005–2023 
(inclusive). T o determine which estimates of air pollution to include, 
we compared air pollution estimates obtained from the Copernicus 
Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) global reanalysis dataset 
and NASA’s MERRA-2 (refs. 63,64). We performed an assessment of 
the correlation between the gridded air quality reanalysis models 
against observational data made available by the South African Air 
Quality Information Services (SAAQIS)65. PM2.5 and PM10 are not directly 
included in MERRA-2, so they were computed using methods detailed 
in the MERRA-2 documentation66 (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 5).
We included the MERRA-2 product from NASA for the entire time 
period and also included the bias-corrected MERRA-2 product from 
2020 to 202367. The CAMS global datasets are at ~80 km 2 resolution 
while the MERRA-2 datasets are at 50 km2 spatial resolution. We also 
included a gapless 1 km gridded machine learning model from ref. 68.
We extracted observational data from air quality monitoring sta-
tions provided by SAAQIS in South Africa. We calculated the mean abso-
lute error, root mean square error and Spearman’s correlation between 
the reanalysis products and the mean across observations in the district 
of Gert Sibande, Mpumalanga, Gauteng province and KwaZulu-Natal 
province (Extended Data Fig. 10a). These were the locations with the 
most-consistent observations across the time period available in SAA-
QIS. We performed a formal comparison of the datasets against obser-
vational data and found that CAMS was the best-performing reanalysis 
model (Extended Data Fig. 10b). CAMS performed best despite its poor 
performance in the Gert Sibande region where none of the products per-
formed well, either due to the observations being flawed, or a bias across 
all reanalysis products impacting this region. We included the air qual-
ity reanalysis data from CAMS in our study (Supplementary Table 6).
As we demonstrate in our comparison, air quality reanalysis mod-
els do not correlate well with the observational data across all sites. The 
available observational data from SAAQIS across this time period are 
sparse, which limits our ability to select an ideal reanalysis product. 
The correlation between the observations and CAMS was better than 
that with MERRA-2 and other bias-corrected datasets. The absence of 
reanalysis models which are bias corrected to this region and the lack of 
observational data points for many less-urban regions highlight the lim-
itations of addressing the environmental impact of high-burden infec-
tious diseases in low-income settings with uncorrected environmental 
data69,70. T o improve understanding of the environment’s impact on 
high-mortality infectious diseases, efforts must be made to improve 
air pollution global reanalysis models 71. In addition, we used weekly 
means rather than the 24-hour means for air quality metrics, so these 
thresholds are lenient (for example, if 1 day within a 7-day period has a 
24-hour concentration exceeding the threshold but the other 6 days do 
not, the weekly mean concentration will likely be below the threshold).
The R package startR was used to retrieve and reformat all multi-
dimensional environmental datasets72.
Epidemiological model specification
Bayesian hierarchical mixed models using integrated nested 
Laplace approximation. In brief, here we summarize our modelling 
strategy for identifying meteorological and air pollution factors that 
drive patterns of pneumococcal disease, with an in-depth analysis of 
the effects of PM2.5 on different outcomes (for example, serotype, age 
group, disease type).
T o assess the risk from environmental factors on IPD, we adopted 
the following modelling strategy:
 1. We first developed a sociodemographic base model which ac-
counted for population density and vaccination period as well 
as residual seasonal, spatial and interannual effects.
 2. We tested different metrics for vaccination and antiretroviral 
therapy to assess their impact on disease patterns. We chose a 
sociodemographic base model given a balance between model 
fit and tractable complexity, ultimately including confounding 
effects of vaccination as a categorical variable (across 3 vaccina-
tion periods), and population density as a continuous variable, 
alongside the residual random effects.
 3. We then developed a meteorological model that utilized DL-
NMs to assess the risk from weekly, district-level, meteorologi-
cal factors (xs,t) across lag periods. These included absolute and 
relative humidity, mean, minimum and maximum temperature, 
precipitation, wind speed, and the SPEI (a drought indicator). 
We evaluated model fit and effect size across 8 weeks of lag time 
from exposure.
 4. T o elucidate the risk from air pollution, we developed an air 
pollution model that included a DLNM cross-basis for PM2.5, 
PM10 and SO2 (aqs,t), including the same sociodemographic base 
model and meteorological confounding factors. We tested 
different formulations for inclusion of the meteorological con-
founding, including linear and nonlinear covariates of humidity 
and temperature with different lag times. We also evaluated 
the inclusion of a temperature cross-basis alongside the PM2.5 
cross-basis, and temperature as a modifying interaction with 
the PM2.5 cross-basis. We found the WAIC to be similar across 
the temperature covariate model, the temperature cross-basis 
model and the interaction model. The PM2.5 effect sizes were 
also unchanged across these model types. The final air pollution 
model included a nonlinear lag 3 humidity covariate and linear 
lag 0 covariate for temperature.
 5. Using our final air pollution model, we then focused on PM2.5 
to determine how the risk from PM2.5 varies given different 
outcomes (for example, serotype, age group, disease type).
 6. Finally, we incorporated a modification by different underly-
ing genomic lineages with PM2.5 in the same model to elucidate 
how the underlying microbial population modifies these PM2.5 
effects. The PM2.5 air pollution models all accounted for the 
potentially confounding effects of temperature, humidity, 
population density and vaccination.
Statistical analysis. All statistical analysis and visualizations were 
conducted using R (v.4.4.2)73 with key packages including R-INLA 74, 
dlnm75, dplyr76 and sf77. A glossary of symbols and variables used is 
included in Supplementary Table 11.

Sociodemographic base model development. We began by building a 
model to account for residual spatiotemporal effects and important 
sociodemographic covariates such as population density, PCV or ART 
(treatment for HIV). We modelled weekly or monthly pneumococcal 
disease counts in South Africa between 2005 and 2023 or for 2005–2019 
(excluding the post-COVID-19 period), by specifying a Bayesian hier-
archical mixed model using pneumococcal disease case counts at the 
district (n = 52) or province (n = 9) level as the response variable. T o 
account for overdispersion, we assumed that case counts, ys,t, where 
s represents a spatial unit and t represents time step (1–987 weeks, or 
228 months), follow a negative binomial distribution such that:
ys,t|μs,t ∼ NegBin(μs,t, κ) (1)
log(μs,t)= log(pops,a(t))+log(ρ s,t) (2)
where μs,t is the expected number of cases and κ is the overdispersion 
parameter. The distribution mean, μ s,t, is defined as the product of 
the population, pops,a(t), (per 100,000 people) in a given spatial unit 
(district or province), s, and year, a(t), with the disease incidence rate 
ρs,t estimated via a combination of covariates and random effects.
For all sociodemographic model formulations, we evaluated dif-
ferent spatial (district or province) and temporal (weekly or monthly) 
resolutions, ultimately only including the weekly district-level models 
in our main analysis (Supplementary Table 12).
Spatiotemporal random-effect assessment. We evaluated model per -
formance by defining the response variable both at district and prov-
ince level for weekly and monthly case counts and across various 
random and fixed-effect combinations, comparing them to the 
intercept-only model (Supplementary Table 12). These models included 
an intercept α as well as temporal random effects accounting for 
unknown and unmeasured monthly variation (calendar months January 
(1) to December (12)), δm(t), and interannual effect γa(t) at the national 
level. We tested both the monthly variation, δm(pr(s),t), and interannual 
effect, γpr(s),a(t), replicated by province pr(s) (n = 9 provinces). We also 
included spatial effects with a Besag–York–Mollié (BYM-2) model78, 
with both structured (us) and unstructured effects (vs) at the district- 
and province-level models. In the province-level model, s = pr(s). We 
additionally evaluated including the spatial random effects with a 
yearly replication to adjust for fine-scale spatiotemporal confounding 
(Supplementary Fig. 8).
Impact of pneumococcal vaccine and ART on interannual variation. 
We assessed different formulations of pneumococcal vaccination, 
antiretroviral therapy and population density. We tested inclusion of a 
single categorical covariate with three phases to account for decreasing 
case counts after the implementation of the PCV (PCVperiods): pre-PCV7 
(years before 2009), PCV7 (2009–2010 inclusive) and PCV13 (2011 
onwards). We alternatively assessed models including only two peri-
ods for PCV (PCV 2009Vaccines): pre-PCV (years before 2009, as above) 
and then a single post-PCV period (2010 onwards). We also evaluated 
models with national-level PCV coverage (%) per year (PCVcoverage,a(t)), as 
well as a model accounting for two time periods for COVID-19 (covid): 
pre-covid (2005–2019) and post-covid (2020 onwards). T o assess the 
impact of the uptake of ART across South Africa, we included a variable 
for ART coverage. These included a national-level ART coverage (%) 
(ARTcoverage,a(t)) and province-level ART coverage (%) (ARTcoverage,pr(s),a(t)). 
We tested inclusion of the intervention variables (PCV, ART, covid) 
independently and together, where f indicates a nonlinear function. 
We also included district-level human population density (pd) as a 
continuous variable due to its particular relevance as a confounding 
factor with air pollution.
In summary, the main models tested included: (1) PCV as a categori-
cal variable with 2 categories, (2) PCV2009Vaccines (M10), (3) PCVperiods (M11), 
(4) PCVcoverage,a(t) (M13), (5) ARTcoverage,a(t) (M14), (6) ARTcoverage,pr(s),a(t) (M15) 
and (7) ARTcoverage,pr(s),a(t) and PCVperiods (M16) (Supplementary Table 13). 
M12 is the same as M11 with additional inclusion of population density. 
These were all run with and without replication of the interannual effect 
across provinces (Supplementary Table 3). We also ran different models 
for different types of IPD to assess the impact of these perturbations 
across different groups. These included children ≤5 years old, adults 
between 15 and 64 years old, adults 65 years old and above, IPD from 
only NVTs and from only VTs. We also ran these models including 
disease from 2005 to 2019 (excluding the post-COVID-19 period) and 
from 2005–2023 (Supplementary Table 3).

Sociodemographic and spatiotemporal effect assessment. Our sociode-
mographic base model, which included spatial autocorrelation across 
districts, seasonal effects and interannual random effects, was repli-
cated across provinces, as well as pneumococcal vaccine implemen-
tation periods and population density (Supplementary Table 13). We 
found similar effects for models spanning 2005–2023 and 2005–2019 
but due to the dramatic decrease in IPD cases during the COVID-19 pan-
demic period (2020 onwards), we proceeded with models restricted to 
the 2005–2019 period throughout (Supplementary Tables 12 and 14). 
These models were fitted to weekly invasive disease incidence across 
South Africa district levels (n  = 52) (Supplementary Fig. 1a,b). The 
Gauteng and Western Cape provinces had a higher risk of IPD than the 
national average. The RR derived from the spatial effect for Gauteng 
province was 13.35 (95% CI 4.19–44.34) and 1.54 (95% CI 0.84–2.70) for 
Western Cape, representing the area-specific risk relative to the average 
across all provinces. The risk in Western Cape was largely driven by the 
increased risk in the Cape T own district alone (relative to all-district 
average) (RR 10.16: 95% CI 7.89–13.09). All districts in Gauteng province 
exhibited elevated disease risk relative to the national average (RR > 1). 
Across the other provinces, districts that host major cities and trans-
port hubs had twice the risk of IPD compared to the average. Some of 
the exacerbated risk in the main cities, such as those around Johannes-
burg in Gauteng, may reflect travel to hospitals from more rural areas 
(Extended Data Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Fig. 1c and Table 14). There 
was a seasonal effect with an increased disease risk between the months 
of May and October, with peak risk in August (RR 1.37: 95% CI 1.33–1.41). 
In the interannual effect, we found a reduction in risk over time even 
while accounting for vaccination for Gauteng, Free State, Mpumalanga, 
KwaZulu-Natal and North West provinces (Extended Data Fig. 1c and 
Supplementary Table 14). This may be attributed to ART uptake also 
being influential there (Supplementary Fig. 1d).
In developing the sociodemographic base model, we examined 
the impact of ART and vaccination coverage. They were correlated 
(Pearson R2 = 0.84) and model formulations that included them had 
reduced interannual effect variance (Supplementary Fig. 1d and 
Extended Data Fig. 2a). We found no effect of ART or PCV on NVT sero-
types alone in the 2005–2019 period. A lack of PCV effect on NVTs is 
expected, as the vaccine does not protect against these serotypes. In 
contrast, ART is expected to confer protection against all pneumococ-
cal serotypes by improving immune function in PLHIV, particularly 
among adults, who represent the group most likely to be receiving ART 
in this high HIV-burden setting79,80. In the 2005–2019 period, NVT IPD 
cases were skewed towards children and older adults, but extending the 
analysis to 2020–2023 allowed an ART effect on NVTs to emerge. In this 
time period, there was a higher number of IPD cases among adults aged 
18–64 years who, in this setting, are the primary recipients of ART. This 
increase in adult cases might have resulted from the resurgence of IPD

after the COVID-19 pandemic and a gradual decline in indirect vaccine 
protection over time (Extended Data Fig. 2c–e)81–83. Overall, the impact 
of ART on opportunistic infections cannot be understated26,79,80,84. Due 
to the nuanced impact of ART, and the established direct relationship 
between pneumococcal vaccination and IPD, we included the three 
PCV periods (pre-PCV7, post-PCV7 and post-PCV13) as the intervention 
covariate in the final sociodemographic base model (equation ( 3)) 
(Extended Data Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 3).
DLNMs to estimate disease risk with environmental factors. We 
estimated IPD risk using DLNMs built on the sociodemographic base 
model described in equation (3). Correlations between environmental 
variables were assessed with Pearson correlation, while the relationship 
between exposure lag times and disease was explored using Spearman 
correlation (Extended Data Fig. 3). We tested all models including 
both the province of Gauteng alone and excluding Gauteng province, 
due to it being the most populous and having the highest incidence 
of IPD and highest maximum concentrations of PM 2.5, PM10 and SO2 
(Supplementary Figs. 4–6). We ran weekly models at both province 
(adm1) and district (adm2) levels. Sensitivity analyses across 6-, 8- and 
12-week windows for all variables and administrative regions revealed 
little variation across the cumulative-effects estimates; the marginal 
lag-response plots (Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16) demonstrated 
that associations typically stabilized towards null with an 8-week lag 
time from exposure. Previous literature identified environmental IPD 
influences up to 4 weeks after environmental exposure16,17,19, and our 
cross-correlation analyses indicated maximum association extending 
to a 5-week lag (Extended Data Fig. 3). Given this statistical evidence 
together with the estimated 30-day generation time for pneumo -
cocci, we selected an 8-week lag window as a robust time frame to 
capture both the statistically and biologically relevant effects8. While 
we evaluated many models across different spatial and temporal 
scales, our final meteorological and air pollution models presented 
in the main text were at the weekly district level from 2005 to 2019 
(Supplementary Table 4).
Meteorological and air pollution model DLNM specifications. We esti -
mated the exposure–lag-response association using the crosspred() 
function of the dlnm package for all DLNMs to generate estimates 
and Gaussian-approximate credible intervals derived from the INLA 
posterior confidence intervals. Although crosspred() typically reports 
confidence intervals, in this case the uncertainty reflects the Bayesian 
posterior distribution because the supplied coefficients and covariance 
matrix were obtained from INLA. T o model the nonlinear and delayed 
effects of environmental factors, we employed natural cubic splines 
for both the exposure–response (argvar) and lag-response (arglag) 
functions. We conducted sensitivity analyses to determine the optimal 
degrees of freedom (d.f.) for these splines (Supplementary Fig. 17 and 
Table 15). Some of these specification adjustments induced numerical 
instability in the exposure–response curves. Specifically, for absolute 
humidity, and maximum and minimum temperature, the hyper-flexible 
argvar specification produced spurious oscillations. These oscillations 
did not have any epidemiologically plausible explanation and probably 
reflected overfitting rather than meaningful structure. We ultimately 
included the more-constrained nonlinear specification which pre -
served overall shape while avoiding artificial fluctuations. The final 
DLNM cross-basis used a natural cubic spline for the lag response 
with 2 d.f. (covering an 8-week lag period), except for relative humid-
ity for which 3 d.f. was used to better capture potential nonlinear lag 
structures85,86. For each exposure–response function, we utilized a 
natural cubic spline with 3 d.f., placing internal knots at the 33rd and 
67th percentiles. For precipitation, two knots were placed at equally 
spaced intervals on the log-transformed distribution to account for its 
right skew. These specifications were consistent with established DLNM 
approaches and maintained model parsimony with sufficient flexibility 
to capture delayed and nonlinear exposure–response associations 
without overfitting87,88 (Supplementary Table 4).

to assess the effect of meteorological factors on pneumococcal disease 
dynamics. We added DLNMs for all meteorological factors to the soci-
odemographic base model (equation (4)). These included the weekly 
average of the daily temperature mean (tas), minimum (tasmin) and 
maximum (tasmax) temperature, absolute (absh) and relative humidity 
(hurs), the weekly precipitation mean (prlrmean) and sum (prlrsum), 
the SPEI (drought indicator) for 3 months (SPEI3) and 6 months (SPEI6), 
as well as weekly mean of the daily average windspeed (sfcWind) 75,89. 
We centred and scaled all meteorological factors. We centred the 
effect estimations at the median of each variable’s distribution, which 
provided a neutral benchmark to account for a meteorological base-
line. Including a centre at the median is recognized when a biological 
optimal exposure point is not defined for a health outcome, as is the 
case for IPD75.

Air pollution models. We developed an air pollution model to assess the 
risk from air pollution on IPD. We included the same sociodemographic 
base model as above, including vaccination period and population 
density covariates as well as residual spatiotemporal effects, and addi-
tionally tested different formulations of confounding meteorological 
effects. Meteorological factors are known confounders of air pollution 
and their inclusion also ensures that our model is applicable across 
varied climates. We developed our meteorological covariates given 
the known interactions between temperature and air pollution, and 
humidity and temperature13,17,86. Our final air pollution model included 
mean temperature as a linear term (lag 0) and absolute humidity as 
a nonlinear term (lag 3), implemented at a random walk of order 2 
(equation (5 )). We chose this formulation given the effect sizes of 
mean temperature and absolute humidity at these lag times and after 
comparison against other model formulations for the different con -
founders (described below) (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Table 4). For 
all air pollution models, we included 3 d.f. for the lag-response spline 
and equally distributed knots at the 33rd and 67th percentile for the 
exposure–response spline (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17).
We assessed air pollution models with the confounding effects of 
mean or maximum temperature alone, as well as with the additional 
effects of absolute or relative humidity. We formulated the humidity 
and temperature models including both variables at no-lag time, a 
3-week lag time, or a mixture of one at 3 weeks and another at 0. We 
included the humidity variables as nonlinear effects using random walk 
2 models with 5 cuts and included the temperature variables as linear 
effects given their exposure–response curves from the meteorologi-
cal models (Fig. 3 and Extended Data Fig. 5). We also assessed directly 
interacting a PM2.5 cross-basis with a mean temperature covariate, as 
well as including an additional mean temperature cross-basis together 
with an absolute humidity covariate, finding similar goodness of fit and 
risk estimates across all different formulations including both humid-
ity and temperature (ΔWAIC < 4) (Supplementary Tables 4 and 7). For 
parsimony, and given the goodness-of-fit metrics, we proceeded with 
the covariate model including lag-week 3 absolute humidity and mean 
temperature covariates with no lag-time (equation (5)).

exceedance rather than an arbitrary minimum value. We utilized the

PM2.5 effect across age, disease and vaccine status. T o identify differences 
in risk from PM2.5 across different stratification groups, we used our 
air pollution model formulation but changed the outcome from IPD 
overall to disease incidence per 100,000 people among each group 
for age group, disease outcome, vaccine status (whether cases were 
serotypes that were included in the PCV7 vaccine, PCV13 vaccine, or 
were non-vaccine type) and by specific serotype. The specific models 
we tested included (1) age ≤5 years old, (2) age 6–14 years old, (3) age 
15–64 years old, (4) age ≥65, (5) bacteraemia, (6) meningitis, (7) other 
invasive disease types and (8) penicillin-resistant cases (measured at 
the meningitis threshold). We also included serotype stratifications by 
(9) NVT, (10) PCV7 and (11) PCV13. Models 12–29 included stratification 
by IPD from particular serotypes (Supplementary Tables 2 and 4). T o 
estimate the relative risk ratio for each subgroup (serotype, disease 
type, age group) compared to the overall population, we crosscut the 
relative risk from the exposure–response curve at 50 μg m−3 concentra-
tion for each. We then included the relative risk for each subgroup in 
turn as the numerator compared to the relative risk from the overall 
model to estimate the increased (>1) or decreased (<1) risk of IPD in 
each subgroup, from PM 2.5 compared to the risk for pneumococcal 
disease overall (Fig. 3a).
DLNMs interacting with lineage prevalence . T o assess the impact 
of underlying lineage diversity on the environmental effects, we uti -
lized the same air pollution model framework as above (compris -
ing the sociodemographic base model (M12): seasonal, spatial and 
province-replicated interannual random effects, together with vaccina-
tion periods and population density, and adjustment for temperature 
and humidity) (Supplementary Table 13). We included all GPSCs with 
N ≥ 100 as well as GPSC8 due to its epidemiological importance in 
South Africa8,51. We then included an interaction between the expo -
sure cross-basis and a modifying variable representing GPSC-specific 
proportions per total number sequenced each week of dominant 
GPSCs. We ran these models at the province level weekly and thus 
included a province-level spatial effect for these interaction models. 
T o ensure model interpretability, we scaled and centred this variable 
(Supplementary Table 10).
We evaluated the exposure–response relationship at three repre-
sentative values of the effect modifier, corresponding to the 0.1, 0.5 
and 0.8 quantiles of the centred distribution of the modifying variable 
(excluding zeros). For each quantile value z, we derived level-specific 
coefficient vectors and corresponding variance–covariance matrices 
that reflect the combined main and interaction effects.
T o account for uncertainty in the interaction effects across dif-
ferent levels of the modifying variable, we computed level-specific 
coefficient vectors and their corresponding variance–covariance 
matrices. For a given value z of the centred interaction variable, the 
coefficient vector was calculated as:
βz = β0 + zβint (6)
and the associated variance–covariance matrix as:
Vz = V0 + z2Vint + 2zVcross (7)
where β0 and V0 are the estimated coefficients and variance–covariance 
matrix for the main (non-interaction) effect, βint and Vint are those for 
the interaction term, and Vcross represents the covariance between main 
and interaction coefficients. These were then passed to the crosspred() 
function of the DLNM package to generate estimates and Gaussian-
approximate credible intervals derived from the INLA posterior at each 
value of z (Supplementary Table 10).
We performed a sensitivity analysis whereby for GPSC pairs, which 
share the same dominant serotypes, we split the dataset into the pre- 
and post-vaccine period to ensure that the effect was not just driven 
by serotype replacement in the time series but by some underlying 
evolutionary process (Supplementary Fig. 14).
To estimate goodness of fit for each model, we calculated the 
WAIC90 and the mean absolute error. We also calculated the sum of 
the log10 conditional predictive ordinate for each observation to evalu-
ate the model’s out-of-sample predictive performance. We additionally 
computed a pseudo-R 2, defined as the proportionate reduction in 
deviance compared to an intercept-only model91,92. This measured the 
ability of each model to account for variation in disease incidence (0–1) 
whereby a model with R2 = 1 would indicate a model accounting for all 
of the variation in the data. This helped evaluate the improvement in 
model fit achieved by including additional covariates.
