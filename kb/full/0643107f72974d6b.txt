Abstract
4-Methylbenzylidene camphor (4-MBC, also known as enzacamene), a chemical UV 
filter prohibited or never permitted across the EU, UK, China, ASEAN, US, and Japan, 
remains legal in Australian therapeutic sunscreens at concentrations up to 4% w/w. 
The European Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety concluded in 2022 that no 
safe concentration could be established, and the UK Scientific Advisory Group on 
Chemical Safety confirmed this in 2025. In January 2026 the Australian Therapeutic 
Goods Administration also concluded that a safe dose cannot be established, yet 
declined to remove the compound from the Permissible Ingredients Determination, 
citing supply-shortage grounds. This article examines a narrower question: whether 
4-MBC is entering Great Barrier Reef catchment coastal waters through a direct, 
unmonitored wastewater pathway, and whether the concentrations reaching reef-
adjacent waters are sufficient to compound bleaching in already-stressed coral 
systems. Two Queensland focus zones and one downstream transport corridor 
are examined where documented bleaching overlaps with confirmed wastewater 
treatment plant discharge points: the inshore reefs north of Cairns, Magnetic Island 
and Cleveland Bay, and the transport corridor from Townsville to the Palm Islands. 
Peer-reviewed evidence establishes 4-MBC as a coral toxicant in single-compound 
laboratory exposure, though at concentrations well above those measured in coastal 
waters. A twelve-compound UV filter mixture containing 4-MBC at 200 ng/L per 
compound has been shown to amplify thermal bleaching in reef corals. Whether 
4-MBC occurs in Queensland reef-adjacent waters at concentrations relevant to either 
finding is unknown, because the discharge pathway has never been monitored for 
this class of contaminant. The case for action does not rest on causation of the 2024 
bleaching event. It rests on the continuous discharge of a coral toxicant, banned by 
Australia’s regulatory peers, through a pathway that has never been measured. This 
Perspective specifies the monitoring that would resolve the scientific question and 
identifies the existing Australian regulatory instrument through which alignment with 
the international scientific consensus could be achieved.

1 Introduction
Chemical ultraviolet (UV) filters are an under-characterised class of marine contami -
nants. The compounds enter coastal waters continuously through bathing, wastewater 
discharge, and direct recreational wash-off. Their environmental fate has only recently 
begun to be measured at scale. Their ecotoxicology, particularly to reef-building cor -
als, is documented but incomplete: regulators and systematic reviewers have repeatedly 
noted that the dose-response data needed for marine risk assessment is missing for sev -
eral compounds, including 4-methylbenzylidene camphor (4-MBC) [1, 2].
Against this background, 4-MBC is now the focus of accelerating international regula -
tory action. The European Union, the United Kingdom, China, and several ASEAN states 
have prohibited or are prohibiting the compound on the basis that no safe concentration 
can be established [3–5]. Vietnam has moved 4-MBC to its prohibited substances annex 
following the 42nd ASEAN Cosmetic Committee meeting [ 6]. The United States and 
Japan have never permitted it as a sunscreen active ingredient. Australia is now the only 
developed sunscreen market in its region in which 4-MBC remains permitted at concen-
trations of up to 4% w/w, and the only such market in which the national regulator has 
been formally asked to remove it and has declined to do so ([7]; IN-2025-AP-000920).
4-MBC, also known as enzacamene, is a camphor-based UV-B absorber. It is highly 
lipophilic, resistant to standard biological degradation, and stable under ultraviolet radi -
ation [4]. These properties make it persistent in aquatic environments. It is not removed 
by conventional wastewater treatment [ 8], and it has been detected in coral tissue at 
tropical reef field sites under ambient conditions [2].
1.1 The international regulatory picture
International regulatory action on 4-MBC has accelerated sharply since 2020. Two reef-
state jurisdictions moved first on environmental grounds: the Republic of Palau listed 
4-MBC among 32 banned sunscreen ingredients under its International Coral Reef Pro -
tection Act [9], and Thailand prohibited 4-MBC and three other UV filters in all marine 
national parks from 4 August 2021 [ 10]. The compound is not listed as an approved 
active ingredient in the U.S. OTC sunscreen monograph (21 CFR Part 352), and FDA 
has issued a tentative determination that enzacamene is not generally recognized as safe 
and effective (GRASE) as a sunscreen active [ 11]. The compound is also absent from 
the exhaustive positive list of permitted UV absorbers under the Japanese Standards for 
Cosmetics [12], meaning it has never been permitted for cosmetic use in Japan.
The decisive scientific assessment came from the European Union in 2022. The Sci -
entific Committee on Consumer Safety (SCCS), in opinion SCCS/1640/21, ‘cannot con-
clude on the safety of 4-MBC ’ because of insufficient data on potential genotoxicity, and 
found that the use of 4-MBC at 4% in cosmetic products ‘ would not be safe ’ [4]. On the 
basis of that opinion, Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/996 moved 4-MBC from the 
permitted UV filter list (Annex VI) to the prohibited substances list (Annex II) of the EU 
Cosmetics Regulation, with prohibition effective across all 27 member states from 1 May 
2025.
The United Kingdom reached the same conclusion independently. The Scientific Advi-
sory Group on Chemical Safety (SAG-CS) found in 2025 that 4-MBC could not be con -
sidered safe at any concentration [ 3], and Statutory Instrument 2026/23 was laid before 
Parliament on 15 January 2026, prohibiting 4-MBC from 15 July 2026. China followed

within days: the National Medical Products Administration prohibited 4-MBC in cos -
metic products under NMPA Announcement No. 6 of 12 January 2026 [13].
The ASEAN Cosmetic Committee voted at its 42nd meeting in November 2025 to 
move 4-MBC to Annex II (prohibited substances) of the ASEAN Cosmetic Directive. 
Vietnam has formally adopted the change through Official Letter 647/QLD-MP , with a 
national application date of 17 November 2028 [ 6]. Adoption by other ASEAN member 
states is rolling.
1.2 Australia’s position is now an outlier
Against this international pattern, Australia stands alone. 4-MBC remains permitted at 
up to 4% in therapeutic sunscreen products as a grandfathered active ingredient that has 
never been subjected to a modern formal safety and efficacy assessment under current 
Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) standards. The compound remains available 
on Australian retail shelves and continues to enter Australian wastewater systems daily.
The TGA refused an application to remove 4-MBC from the Permissible Ingredients 
Determination (IN-2025-AP-000920). Applying the Australian Sunscreen Exposure 
Model adopted by the TGA in January 2025, the TGA evaluator established a systemic 
exposure dose for 4-MBC of approximately 1.4 mg/kg bw/day but was unable to derive 
a systemic No Observed Adverse Effect Level from the available data. The Delegate con -
cluded that ‘a MoS of 4-MBC cannot be reached, and consequently, a safe dose of the 
ingredient cannot be established’ . The variation was nevertheless refused on the basis of 
‘the risk of sunscreen supply shortage, potentially leading to increasing rates of skin can-
cer’ , and the TGA’s public-facing review of 4-MBC remains ongoing [7].
The central feature of the Australian situation is structural: the safety finding under the 
TGA’s own exposure model has not been contested, but the regulatory action that would 
follow elsewhere has been declined on supply considerations rather than scientific ones.
The regulatory outlier status is the starting point for this Perspective, not its central 
question. The European, UK, Chinese, and ASEAN actions establish that 4-MBC cannot 
be considered safe under modern regulatory standards. Australia’s continued permission 
of the compound at up to 4% in therapeutic sunscreens means that 4-MBC continues to 
enter the Australian environment at a rate determined by Australian sunscreen use, not 
by international regulatory consensus. The specific question this Perspective addresses is 
what happensto that 4-MBC load once it leaves the consumer: whether it reaches Great 
Barrier Reef (GBR) coastal waters, through what pathway, at what concentration, and 
with what biological consequence for already-stressed coral systems. Section  2 traces 
the wastewater pathway from population to ocean outfall. Section  3 reviews the experi -
mental evidence on coral toxicity. Section  4 examines the specific Queensland reef 
zones where the discharge pathway and bleaching damage intersect.

2 The pathway: population, sunscreen load and direct ocean discharge
2.1 Population and sunscreen load in the two zones and transport corridor
Australian sunscreen use is materially higher than the European baselines that interna -
tional 4-MBC exposure estimates have historically used. The Therapeutic Goods Admin-
istration’s Australian Sunscreen Exposure Model (ASEM), published in August 2025, 
was established specifically to reflect Australian conditions, which differ from European 
inputs in both UV climate and behavioural application patterns. The Australian Cancer 
Council recommends applying 140 mL of sunscreen for full-day full-body sun protec -
tion, a figure cited in the TGA’s own ASEM Impact Analysis [ 14]. European regulatory 
calculations use 16 to 18 g per day. The approximately eightfold difference reflects the 
higher UV index, longer outdoor season, and sun-exposure lifestyle of tropical coastal 
Australians, and it directly scales the volume of 4-MBC entering Queensland sewage 
systems relative to European-derived estimates.
Sunscreen applied to skin enters domestic sewage through showering, bathing, and 
pool use. Peer-reviewed literature identifies this indirect wash-off route as the primary 
pathway by which chemical UV filters reach wastewater treatment systems, because it 
concentrates the chemical load into a manageable treatment stream rather than dispers-
ing it at source [8]. The critical variable is therefore how much sunscreen enters the sys -
tem in the first place, which is a function of resident population, tourist population, and 
per-capita application rate.
The two Queensland reef zones and the transport corridor examined in this article 
are all served by high-use coastal centres in the tropical north, where year-round UV 
radiation requires near-daily sunscreen application. Townsville (serving both Zone 2 and

then shower at tour-boat facilities and hotels, all of which feed into the Cairns wastewa -

to be materially above the national average used in the O’Malley et al. [ 8] study, both 
because tropical UV conditions drive higher resident usage and because concentrated 
high-application tourism activity is associated specifically with reef proximity.
2.2 Wastewater treatment does not fully remove 4-MBC
O’Malley et al. [ 8], in the only national-scale Australian study of UV filter release 
through wastewater treatment, sampled 33 wastewater treatment plants, with collection 
occurring predominantly during the August 2016 Australian Census period and a sec -
ond campaign in December 2018. At least one UV filter was detected in 96% of effluent 
samples. The summed concentration of UV filters in effluent ranged from 130 to 8,400 
ng/L, averaging 2,800 (± 1,900) ng/L. Across the five UV filters quantified, the authors 
estimated that approximately 40% of the influent load passes through to effluent, with 
an estimated national release of approximately 20 kg per day of UV filters from treated 
wastewater into Australian aquatic environments [8].
For 4-MBC specifically, the average removal efficiency was 59 (± 24)%, meaning 
approximately 41% of the influent 4-MBC load passes through to effluent unchanged. 
4-MBC was detected in fewer than 20% of effluent samples in the national survey, with a 
maximum measured effluent concentration of 691 ng/L. The compound is not the most 
prevalent UV filter in Australian effluent (PBSA and BP4 dominate by detection fre -
quency, at 95 and 58% detection respectively), but where 4-MBC is present in influent, 
the proportion reaching the environment is substantial [8].
The reason 4-MBC is not fully removed is its physicochemical profile. The compound 
is highly hydrophobic (log Kow approximately 5.1) and resistant to standard biologi -
cal degradation processes used in conventional municipal treatment. Where 4-MBC 
is removed, removal occurs primarily through sorption to sludge during treatment 
rather than through biological breakdown [ 8, 18–20]. This creates a secondary path -
way: where biosolids are subsequently applied to agricultural land, a practice common

across Queensland, 4-MBC and other hydrophobic UV filters can re-enter the broader 
environment via soil and runoff. The primary discharge route to coastal waters, however, 
remains direct liquid effluent through ocean outfalls and river discharge points.
Reliable removal of 4-MBC from wastewater requires advanced oxidation processes 
(photo-Fenton or UV/persulfate treatment), which have demonstrated 4-MBC degrada -

wastewater treatment plant currently employs advanced oxidation as part of its standard 
treatment train.
2.3 Effluent reaches coastal reef waters directly
The volume of treated sewage entering Great Barrier Reef catchment coastal waters is 
substantial. The Queensland State of the Environment Report 2020 documents com -
bined annual discharge of approximately 290 gigalitres (GL) of treated sewage to coastal 
waterways across Queensland, of which approximately 230 GL originates from South 
East Queensland and the balance (approximately 60 GL per year) from Great Barrier 
Reef catchment coastal sewage treatment plants [ 23]. The two reef zones examined in 
this article are served by the most direct discharge pathways on the Queensland coast.
The Cleveland Bay Purification Plant, Townsville, services approximately 70 per cent 
of the city’s population and operates at a post-2019 capacity of 87 (Mega Litres) ML per 
day following a major upgrade commissioned by Townsville City Council and completed 
in 2019 [24]. The plant discharges via an 800-metre ocean outfall within Cleveland Bay, 
Coral Sea. This is not a river or estuarine discharge: the pipe ends in open marine water 
within a partly enclosed coastal bay that adjoins the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park. 
Magnetic Island’s reef systems lie approximately 20 km to the north-east, within the 
Marine Park boundary. Middle Reef, an inshore turbid-zone reef monitored under the 
AIMS Long-Term Monitoring Programme since 1993, lies approximately 4 km offshore 
from Townsville and 6 km from the city centre within Cleveland Bay itself [25, 26]. Con-
don WWTP , the second Townsville-region plant, discharges to the Bohle River, which 
enters Cleveland Bay from the north-west, contributing to the same semi-enclosed 
receiving environment as the Cleveland Bay outfall.
The Cairns wastewater network consists of six municipal treatment plants operated by 
Cairns Regional Council, with combined nominal capacity of approximately 54 ML per 
day [27]. The five plants with published capacity data, in order of throughput, are: Aero -
glen (19.4 ML/day, servicing the Cairns central business district and northern beaches, 
discharging to the Barron River), Smithfield (8.3 ML/day, servicing the northern coastal 
suburbs from Yorkeys Knob to Palm Cove, discharging via a feeder drain to Half Moon 
Creek), Edmonton (6.7 ML/day, servicing southern Cairns suburbs, discharging to Trin -
ity Inlet), Woree (servicing central and southern suburbs), and Gordonvale (1.9 ML/day, 
discharging to the Mulgrave River). All four receiving water bodies (Barron River, Half 
Moon Creek, Trinity Inlet, Mulgrave River) discharge to the Coral Sea within the Great 
Barrier Reef World Heritage Area. The inshore reef zone that recorded the steepest coral 
losses in the central Great Barrier Reef during the 2024 mass bleaching event lies imme -
diately adjacent to these discharge points; Green Island reef lies approximately 27 km 
offshore of Trinity Inlet’s mouth.

carries this effluent toward reef systems rather than away from them. South-east trade

3 Biological effects: what 4-MBC does to coral
Direct laboratory exposure establishes 4-MBC as a coral toxicant. Danovaro et al. (2008) 
tested individual UV filters on Acropora species in field tanks at four reef sites (Carib -
bean, Celebes, Red, and Andaman Seas). 4-MBC, synthesised as a pure compound and 
dosed at the concentration equivalent to its 3% permitted use level in sunscreen, pro -
duced 63% chromatic-shift bleaching of Acropora sp. within 48 h at 33 µL/L; at 50 µL/L 
it produced 95% bleaching of Acropora pulchra within 62 h, with 95% of zooxanthellae 
released. The mechanism is symbiont damage: loss of photosynthetic pigments, swollen 
and vacuolated zooxanthellae, and loss of cell-membrane integrity, with viral induction 
of latent infections in zooxanthellae proposed by the authors as the broader mecha -
nism. The Danovaro 4-MBC exposure concentrations are higher than those measured 
in real coastal water; what the study establishes is the toxicant relationship and the cel -
lular mechanism, not the field-relevant dose. The field-relevant dose evidence comes 
from two sources [29]. Lyu et al. [2] sampled coral tissue across nine coral species in the 
Luhuitou fringing reef, Hainan, and detected 4-MBC in 83.3% of coral tissue samples at 
concentrations of 0.0 to 2.2 ng/g dry weight. Total chemical UV filter burden in coral 
tissue across the eight compounds detected ranged from 4.3 to 52.0 ng/g dry weight, 
with bioaccumulation factors for 4-MBC higher in macroalgae than in coral. Lyu et al. 
specifically noted that coral toxicity data were sufficient to compute predicted-no-effect 
concentrations (PNEC )only for BP-1, BP-3, BP-8 and octocrylene; the available 4-MBC 
coral toxicity data were too limited to derive a PNEC, and 4-MBC was therefore not 
included in the study’s quantitative ecological risk assessment [ 2]. The compound is 
consistently present in wild reef-building coral at a tropical tourist reef under ambient 
conditions; the dose-response data required to formally risk-assess it has not yet been 
generated. Tsui, Leung, [ 30], in a probabilistic risk assessment of surface waters from 
seven cities and the Arctic, found that 4-MBC posed greater risk to algae than to fish or 
coral [30]. The third category of evidence is the co-stressor experiment: what happens 
to coral exposed simultaneously to UV filters and thermal stress at concentrations that 
would be considered too low to matter in isolation. He et al. (2023) exposed three coral 
species (Seriatopora caliendrum, Pocillopora acuta, Montipora aequituberculata) to an 
equimolar mixture of 12 chemical UV filters including 4-MBC at 200 ng/L per com -
pound (total mixture concentration approximately 2,400 ng/L), with and without ther -
mal stress at 30 °C, over 60 days. Under thermal stress alone, the corals did not bleach 
severely. Under the UV filter mixture alone, 37.5% of S. caliendrum bleached and 12.5% 
died. Under combined exposure, 100% of S. caliendrum bleached with 100% mortality, 
100% of P. acuta bleached with 50% mortality, and 25% of M. aequituberculata bleached 
with no observed mortality [ 31]. This finding sits in direct tension with the position 
taken in Wheate [ 32], which argued that UV filter effects on aquatic life occur only at 
concentrations 10 to 10,000-fold higher than those found in the environment and that 
observed reef damage is attributable to other causes [ 32]. He et al. (2023) demonstrate 
measurable, severe effect at 200 ng/L under realistic co-exposure with thermal stress; 
the Wheate position holds for single-compound acute laboratory endpoints but does 
not address the mixture-with-thermal-stress mechanism. The broader systematic review 
by Watkins and Sallach [ 1] catalogues the underlying problem on which both positions 
ultimately depend: current data are insufficient to fully determine the ecotoxicological 
risks of UV filter exposure in coral reef ecosystems, with co-stressor effects, long-term

exposure, and mixture toxicity identified as priority research gaps [ 1]. The Lyu et al. 
(2025) and He et al. (2023) findings begin closing that gap; the field-monitoring data 
required to apply either finding to a specific Australian reef does not exist.
The evidence on 4-MBC toxicity to coral spans three categories: controlled laboratory 
exposure studies establishing direct bleaching effects, field surveys documenting tis -
sue uptake in wild coral, and co-stressor experiments testing UV filter mixtures against 
thermal stress. Each is relevant to the Great Barrier Reef context for a different reason. 
The first establishes that 4-MBC is a coral toxicant; the second establishes that the com -
pound reaches and accumulates in reef-building coral under ambient conditions; the 
third establishes that the bleaching threshold of an already-stressed coral can be lowered 
by UV filter exposure at concentrations measured in real coastal waters.
4 Focus zones and transport corridor: bleaching damage and WWTP proximity
The following two reef zones and one downstream transport corridor have been selected 
on the basis of documented bleaching damage overlapping with confirmed WWTP dis -
charge points. In each case, the primary cause of observed damage is identified before 
the UV filter co-stressor argument is assessed. The argument is not presented as an alter-
native explanation to climate change and known water quality threats; it is presented 
as an additional and unmonitored layer operating through the same coastal discharge 
infrastructure already identified as a problem.
4.1 Zone 1 — inshore reefs north of Cairns
Documented damage: In 2024, the Cairns–Cooktown sector recorded the largest single-
year decline in hard coral cover in 39 years of Australian Institute of Marine Science 
Long-Term Monitoring Program (AIMS LTMP) data, with the Cooktown–Lizard Island 
sub-sector falling from a recorded high of 31.4% cover to 19.3% [ 33]. Cyclone Jasper 
made landfall north of Cairns in December 2023, delivering record rainfall and freshwa -
ter inundation across catchments draining onto inshore reefs [ 34]. The primary cause of 
the 2024 coral loss is unambiguous: a marine heatwave coinciding with Coral Sea sum -
mer sea surface temperatures that reached the highest level in at least 400 years, exceed -
ing the previous record set in 2017 [35].
WWTP proximity and population context : Four treatment plants serving Cairns dis -
charge approximately 54 ML/day in combined capacity into Trinity Inlet, Half Moon 
Creek and the Barron River, all of which open onto the Coral Sea within kilometres 
of the inshore reefs that recorded the worst losses [ 27]. The plants serve a resident 
population of approximately 180,000. In the year ending March 2023, Tropical North 
Queensland recorded approximately 13.8 million domestic visitor nights and $4.3 billion 
in domestic overnight visitor expenditure [ 16]. A large proportion of these visitors par -
ticipate in reef-based activities. Cairns reef tourists represent a high-input user category: 
they apply sunscreen repeatedly for full-day reef immersion in high-UV conditions, and 
the resulting wash-off enters the city’s treatment system either directly through vessel-
based showering and toilet facilities or indirectly through accommodation-based bath -
ing. The O’Malley et al. [8] national average per-capita UV filter load is therefore likely to 
understate the load entering Cairns’ plants relative to its population characteristics [8].
UV filter plausibility assessment : The corals that suffered the heaviest losses in the 
2024 event were Acropora, fast-growing branching species with documented sensitivity

to thermal stress [ 33]. Acropora is also the genus in which direct 4-MBC bleaching was 
demonstrated by Danovaro et al. (2008), though at concentrations far above ambient 
[29]. The co-stressor evidence comes from a different set of species. He et al. (2023) 
exposed Seriatopora caliendrum , Pocillopora acuta and Montipora aequituberculata 
to an equimolar twelve-compound UV filter mixture at 200 ng/L per compound under 
30 °C thermal stress. Bleaching occurred only under co-exposure; elevated temperature 
alone produced none. After 60 days, 100% of S. caliendrum bleached with 100% mortal -
ity and 100% of P. acuta bleached with 50% mortality, while M. aequituberculata , the 
only acroporid tested, showed 25% bleaching and no mortality [31]. He et al. did not test 
any Acropora species, and whether Acropora would respond like the pocilloporids or 
like the acroporid tested here was not addressed by that study.
The precautionary argument is testable : if inshore Acropora communities adjacent to 
these discharge points were receiving a continuous sub-lethal UV filter load from Cairns’ 
treatment plants, their thermal bleaching threshold would be expected to be lower than 
in uncontaminated water. Whether sub-lethal UV filter exposure lowered the bleaching 
threshold of these communities cannot be confirmed or excluded without water quality 
monitoring, which has never been conducted.
4.2 Zone 2 — magnetic Island and cleveland bay
Documented damage . The Healthy Waters Partnership for the Dry Tropics 2023–2024 
Technical Report, drawing on monitoring data collected from July 2023 to June 2024, 
grades Cleveland Bay inshore coral as ‘poor’ (D, score 37) against a Cleveland Bay 
inshore water quality grade of ‘good’ (B, score 76) in the same reporting period [ 36]. 
The report notes that Cleveland Bay coral scores and grades have fluctuated within this 
range for the past four years, which it attributes to several pressures including cyclones 
and increased water temperatures leading to bleaching, and identifies the Juvenile and 
macroalgae indicators as the primary drivers of the low scores, indicating limited coral 
recruitment and high macroalgal density competing for available substrate [ 36]. Of 
the seven coral reefs surveyed across Cleveland and Halifax Bays, four received a ‘very 
poor’ grade for macroalgae and all but one reef measured ‘very poor’ or ‘poor’ for juve -
nile coral counts [ 36]. Cyclones and thermal bleaching are documented pressures on 
Cleveland Bay coral. Macroalgal dominance and juvenile coral failure are documented 
in the inshore monitoring data. UV filter exposure has never been measured in the bay. 
The water-column monitoring framework grades water quality as good but does not 
include any contaminant in this chemical class. Whether UV filters are contributing to 
the cumulative pressure on Cleveland Bay coral cannot be established or ruled out from 
existing data. Macroalgal dominance and juvenile coral failure are the two indicators 
most directly associated with reef-community collapse, because they signal that the next 
generation of coral is failing to establish on substrate already taken by competing algae.
Middle Reef, a nearshore inshore-turbid-zone reef located approximately 4 km off -
shore from Townsville in Cleveland Bay, has been monitored by the Australian Insti -
tute of Marine Science Long-Term Monitoring Program since 1993 [ 25, 33]. The reef is 
a known sentinel site for the response of inshore corals to compounded stressors and 
shows a sustained trajectory of decline.
WWTP proximity and population context . The Cleveland Bay Purification Plant dis -

Cleveland Bay’s physical geometry compounds this discharge geometry. The bay is 
a shallow, northward-facing embayment of approximately 325 km², landlocked on its

northern margin, with limited tidal flushing [25]. These are the same physical conditions 
identified in the international UV filter literature as producing the highest ambient con -

The Cleveland Bay Purification Plant is already explicitly named within the Black Ross 
(Townsville) Water Quality Improvement Plan as the largest point-source nutrient emit-

as fertiliser [ 41], establishing a re-entry pathway for solid-phase residuals back into the 
Reef catchment in addition to the direct ocean-outfall pathway for the liquid effluent.
UV filter plausibility assessment. Zone 2 is the most scientifically defensible site on the 
Queensland coast for the co-stressor hypothesis. Three factors converge.
1. Discharge pathway directness. The pathway from population to reef is the most 
direct on the Queensland coast: a single pipe, a single ocean-floor outfall, terminating 
in the bay where the reef decline is documented. There is no intermediate estuary, 
no river, and no offshore transport corridor between the outfall and Middle Reef. 
The plant has been identified as the largest WWTP point-source contributor in 
the catchment by the Queensland Government’s own water quality improvement 
programme [40].
2. Physical conditions favouring elevated ambient UV filter concentrations. 
Cleveland Bay’s semi-enclosed geometry, shallow water and limited tidal 
flushing are the bay characteristics associated with the highest ambient UV filter 
concentrations measured in Australian field studies. Carve et al. (2023), sampling 
19 sites in and around Port Phillip Bay, reported total maximum chemical UV filter 
concentrations of 119 to 8,597 ng/L at high-recreation coastal sites against 74 to 108 
ng/L at low-recreation sites. 4-MBC was the most widely detected filter, present at 
9 sites and reaching a maximum of 1,643 ng/L, and the aquatic predicted-no-effect 
concentration for 4-MBC of 40 ng/L [42] was exceeded at those 9 sites [38]. A coral-
specific PNEC for 4-MBC cannot currently be derived from available toxicity data 
[2], and the 40 ng/L value is used here as a general aquatic screening benchmark, 
not as a coral-relevant threshold. Allinson et al. (2018), in an earlier reconnaissance 
of four Port Phillip Bay estuaries, detected 16 of the screened UV filters and light 
stabilisers in water or sediment, including 4-MBC at 642 ng/L, but found that 
only two compounds, octocrylene and ethylhexyl methoxycinnamate, returned a 
risk quotient above 1, and concluded that few of the screened chemicals posed 
an individual short-term risk at the time of sampling [ 39]. Two features of these 
studies qualify their application here. Carve et al. attributed the concentrations they 
measured to direct recreational wash-off rather than to wastewater, and detected 
no UV filters at the river sites sampled on an ebbing tide, whereas Allinson et al. 
detected 4-MBC in the same bay. Both author groups call for further sampling 
in Port Phillip Bay. The two available Australian datasets therefore disagree on

whether the wastewater-influenced pathway delivers detectable UV filters to 
receiving waters, and both conclude that more measurement is required.
3. Biological outcome matches predicted UV filter mechanism. The third factor is that 
the observed biological outcomes match the predicted UV filter mechanism. Two 
biological outcomes have been documented in Cleveland Bay. Juvenile coral counts 
have been graded ‘very poor’ or ‘poor’ on six of seven monitored reefs. Macroalgal 
cover has been graded ‘very poor’ on four of seven monitored reefs across Cleveland 
and Halifax Bays. Both outcomes are consistent with the co-stressor mechanism 
demonstrated by He et al. (2023) for a UV filter mixture under thermal stress. That 
study was conducted in aquaria using a defined twelve-compound mixture, and its 
findings have not been tested against field conditions in Cleveland Bay or at any 
other Great Barrier Reef location. Nutrient-driven macroalgal competition provides 
an equally valid explanation for the same outcomes. The two mechanisms are not 
mutually exclusive. Neither can be confirmed nor excluded as a contributing factor 
to Cleveland Bay coral condition without water column and sediment monitoring 
for UV filters. No such monitoring has ever been conducted in the bay. 
The same outfall delivers confirmed nutrient pollution into Cleveland Bay. This is 
already acknowledged by the Townsville City Council’s own water quality improvement 
programme [40]. The same outfall is the unmonitored delivery point for any 4-MBC that 
enters the plant and survives treatment. Both the influent presence of 4-MBC in Aus -
tralian wastewater [ 8] and its incomplete removal by conventional treatment processes 
[43] are established in the published literature. What has not been established at this 
plant is the concentration leaving the outfall, because it has never been measured. This is 
not a new pathway. It is an additional, unmonitored chemical through an acknowledged 
one. The case for monitoring is most difficult to dismiss here because it does not require 
establishing a new argument. It requires only extending an existing one.
4.3 Transport corridor: townsville to the palm Islands
The Palm Islands reef group sits approximately 65 km northwest of Townsville. Towns -
ville is the largest local government area in regional Queensland, with an Estimated Res -
ident Population of 206,260 at 30 June 2025 [ 15]. Its principal coastal treatment plants 
discharge into Cleveland Bay (Cleveland Bay Purification Plant via an 800 m ocean out -
fall) and into the Bohle River, which enters Cleveland Bay (Condon WWTP) [44]. Wind-
driven surface circulation on the central Great Barrier Reef inner shelf is dominated by 
the south-easterly trade winds, which blow consistently through the winter dry season 
and drive northward-flowing coastal currents on the inner shelf [ 17]. Modelling studies 
of the central Great Barrier Reef confirm a vertical and cross-shelf disconnect between 
the wind-driven northward inner-shelf flow and the East Australian Current-driven 
southward mid-lagoon flow [ 45]. Devlin and Brodie [ 46], examining the dispersion of 
terrestrial discharge in the Great Barrier Reef lagoon, established that river plumes in 
the region are primarily dispersed northwards along the coast under wind and Coriolis 
effect [46]. The same physical regime carries treated effluent from the Townsville out -
falls northward along the same coastal corridor, where it is joined by discharge from the 
Ingham and Innisfail treatment plants before reaching the latitude of the Palm Islands. 
No water quality monitoring programme has measured 4-MBC concentrations in this 
corridor at any point between the Townsville outfalls and the Palm Islands reefs, and the

present Perspective does not attribute observed Palm Islands reef condition to UV filter 
exposure. The corridor is identified here only to establish that the discharge geometry 
places Townsville WWTP effluent in continuous downstream proximity to a major reef 
group of the inshore Great Barrier Reef.
5 What this evidence base supports and what it does not
The authors do not claim that 4-MBC caused the 2024 Great Barrier Reef bleaching 
event. Climate-change-driven marine heatwave is the primary, sufficient cause. Coral 
Sea temperatures in 2024 were the warmest in 400 years [ 35]. The AIMS Long-Term 
Monitoring Program 2024–25 Annual Report attributes the largest spatial bleaching 
footprint ever recorded to climate-change-induced heat stress.
This Perspective claims the following.
1. Treated wastewater is discharged continuously into Great Barrier Reef catchment 
coastal waters at scale, providing a pathway through which 4-MBC enters reef-adjacent 
waters where present in influent. Approximately 60 gigalitres of treated sewage is 
released annually into Great Barrier Reef catchment coastal waters, representing 
approximately 20 per cent of the total volume released from Queensland sewage 
treatment plants [47]. The Cleveland Bay Purification Plant, servicing approximately 
70 per cent of Townsville’s population, operates at a post-2019 capacity of 87 ML per 
day and discharges through an 800-metre ocean outfall terminating in Cleveland Bay, 
where Middle Reef sits [24, 37, 48].
2. 4-MBC is a demonstrated coral toxicant under experimental exposure, and thermal 
bleaching has been amplified by a UV filter mixture containing it. Direct bleaching 
has been demonstrated in Acropora species under laboratory exposure [ 29]. 
Bioaccumulation in wild reef-building coral has been documented at 0.0 to 2.2 ng/g 
dry weight in 83.3% of sampled tissue across nine species [2]. Amplification of thermal 
bleaching under co-stressor conditions has been demonstrated at 200 ng/L [ 31]. The 
position taken in Wheate (2022), that UV filter concentrations are too low to cause 
environmental harm, holds for single-compound acute exposure but does not address 
the mixture-with-thermal-stress mechanism that He et al. (2023) demonstrate.
3. The discharge pathway has never been monitored. No Queensland authority has 
measured 4-MBC concentrations in Cleveland Bay, Trinity Inlet, Half Moon Creek, 
or any other GBR-adjacent coastal water body.
4. 4-MBC is prohibited, never permitted, or under enacted prohibition in the European 
Union, the United Kingdom, China, ASEAN states, the United States, and Japan. The 
compound remains legal in Australian therapeutic sunscreens at up to 4% w/w.
5. A contributing factor of this kind cannot be confirmed or excluded without 
monitoring. The absence of monitoring is not evidence of safety.
The monitoring rationale advanced here is not specific to chemical UV filters. No UV 
filter permitted in Australian sunscreens, whether chemical or mineral, has been subject 
to environmental monitoring in Great Barrier Reef coastal waters, and the sampling pro-
gramme proposed below would screen for the principal permitted filters of both classes. 
A comparison of the environmental profiles of sunscreen product classes is outside the 
scope of this Perspective. The argument for action does not require establishing that 
4-MBC caused observed bleaching. It requires only acknowledging that a coral toxicant,

banned by Australia’s regulatory peers, reaches GBR catchment waters through a path -
way already acknowledged by the Queensland Government as ecologically significant. 
That pathway has been left unmonitored under a regulatory architecture in which no 
single Australian authority is responsible for closing the loop.
6 What monitoring would resolve, and the regulatory route to closure
Resolving the question requires two stages, and they should not be conflated. The first 
is a reconnaissance survey capable of establishing whether 4-MBC occurs in reef-adja -
cent water. Targeted sampling of the Cleveland Bay outfall plume, the Townsville-region 
receiving waters, the four Cairns receiving waterways (Barron River, Half Moon Creek, 
Trinity Inlet, Mulgrave River), and inshore reef waters at Magnetic Island and the Palm 
Islands would establish within weeks whether 4-MBC is present and at what order of 
magnitude. Because the co-stressor effect demonstrated by He et al. (2023) arose from 
a mixture rather than from any single compound, 4-MBC concentrations cannot be 
interpreted in isolation, and the survey should screen the principal UV filters permit -
ted in Australian sunscreens, chemical and mineral, alongside the target analyte. That 
is the threshold question, and for 4-MBC it is currently unanswered. The second stage 
is exposure characterisation, which a reconnaissance survey cannot deliver. Establishing 
whether 4-MBC concentrations are ecologically relevant would require repeated influ -
ent, effluent, receiving-water and sediment sampling, and potentially biota sampling, 
across seasons, tidal states and periods of differing sunscreen use, together with assess -
ment of the processes governing the fate of 4-MBC between outfall and reef: dilution, 
tidal mixing, hydrodynamic dispersion, sorption to suspended particles and sediments, 
photochemical transformation and residence time. The first stage determines whether 
the second is warranted. The eReefs hydrodynamic modelling system, operated jointly 
by CSIRO, the Australian Institute of Marine Science, the Bureau of Meteorology, and 
Queensland Government partners, is already operational at one-kilometre resolution 
and can generate plume trajectory analysis from each outfall coordinate [26, 28]. No new 
infrastructure is required to to complete the first stage.
The absence of monitoring is not unique to Australia, but Australia’s position is. The 
European Union operates a Watch List mechanism under the Water Framework Direc -
tive requiring Member States to monitor listed substances in surface waters, and has 
applied it to chemical UV filters: ethylhexyl methoxycinnamate was listed in 2015 and 
removed in 2018 once the monitoring data supported a risk assessment, and oxyben -
zone, octocrylene and octisalate have subsequently been listed. 4-MBC was not among 
them. The European prohibition of 4-MBC proceeded on human health grounds and 
was not preceded by environmental monitoring of the compound. Palau, which pro -
hibited 4-MBC in 2018 expressly to protect coral reefs, acted on a precautionary basis 
rather than on the strength of a monitoring programme. No jurisdiction, therefore, has 
monitored 4-MBC in receiving waters before acting on it. What distinguishes Australia 
is not that it has not monitoredprogr, but that it has neither monitored nor acted, while 
continuing to permit the compound at up to 4% w/w. The European Watch List provides 
a direct institutional model for the reconnaissance stage proposed here.
The prohibition recommended here rests on both bases, and they are cumulative 
rather than alternative. The human health basis is already complete. The SCCS, the 
SAG-CS and the TGA each examined 4-MBC and each concluded that a margin of 
Page 15 of 18
Chowdhury and Mizikovsky Discover Water (2026) 6:124 
safety could not be established, and the European Union, the United Kingdom, China 
and ASEAN signatory states acted on that conclusion through binding prohibitions. No 
jurisdiction that has prohibited 4-MBC has subsequently reversed that decision. Aus -
tralia’s continued permission of the compound therefore does not rest on an affirmative 
finding that it is safe. It rests on a finding that removing it carries a supply risk. That is 
a market availability judgement, not a scientific one, and it is the only respect in which 
Australia’s position differs from that of its regulatory peers.
The environmental basis is precautionary and is set out above. A documented dis -
charge pathway into Great Barrier Reef catchment coastal waters, demonstrated coral 
toxicity under experimental exposure, and no measurement of 4-MBC at any point 
along that pathway. The absence of measurement is presently operating as a reason to 
continue permitting the compound. Under a precautionary standard it is a reason to 
restrict it pending measurement. The monitoring campaign proposed here is directed at 
the environmental basis; the regulatory instrument identified below is available on either 
basis independently.
The regulatory route is equally short. The TGA concluded in its January 2026 decision 
(IN-2025-AP-000920) that “a MoS of 4-MBC cannot be reached, and consequently, a safe 
dose of the ingredient cannot be established” [7]. The Delegate nonetheless declined to 
remove 4-MBC from the Permissible Ingredients Determination, citing “the risk of sun -
screen supply shortage , potentially leading to increasing rates of skin cancer” . The Euro -
pean Union, the United Kingdom, China, and ASEAN signatory states reached the same 
scientific conclusion independently and acted on it through binding cosmetic ingredi -
ent prohibitions. The Therapeutic Goods (Permissible Ingredients) Determination under 
section 26BB of the Therapeutic Goods Act 1989 is the existing Australian regulatory 
instrument under which alignment with the international scientific consensus could be 
affected. Alignment would require no new primary legislation.
7 Conclusions
4-MBC remains legal in Australian therapeutic sunscreens at concentrations up to 4% 
w/w. It is prohibited or never permitted across the European Union, the United King -
dom, China, ASEAN signatory states, the United States, and Japan. In January 2026 
the Therapeutic Goods Administration concluded that a safe dose of 4-MBC cannot be 
established ([ 7]; IN-2025-AP-000920). No corresponding restriction on Australian use 
has followed.
The evidence assembled in this Perspective supports three statements, which differ in 
evidential status .
(a) Established. 4-MBC is present in Australian wastewater influent and is incompletely 
removed in treatment, and treated effluent is discharged continuously into Great 
Barrier Reef catchment coastal waters at scale. Whether 4-MBC is present in the 
effluent of the specific Queensland plants identified here has not been measured.
(b) Demonstrated under experimental conditions. Coral toxicity has been shown for 
4-MBC in single-compound laboratory exposure, and amplification of thermal 
bleaching has been shown for a UV filter mixture containing 4-MBC. Neither result 
has been tested at Great Barrier Reef field concentrations, which are unknown .
(c) Established. The Queensland discharge pathway has never been monitored for this 
class of contaminant.

Two actions follow directly. First, a reconnaissance monitoring survey of the two iden-

exposure characterisation if the survey establishes occurrence. Second, amendment of 
the Therapeutic Goods (Permissible Ingredients) Determination under section 26BB of 
the Therapeutic Goods Act 1989 to align Australian regulation with the international 
scientific consensus on 4-MBC. Neither action requires new infrastructure, or new stat -
utory authority. Both are within the capacity of existing Australian institutions to deliver.
