Abstract Anthropogenic activities and hydrocli-
matic variability have intensified hydrochemical 
changes and water-quality degradation in semiarid 
watersheds, increasing the risks of salinization and 
eutrophication. This study evaluated the influence 
of land use and seasonal variability on the hydro-
chemical and microbiological characteristics of sur -
face waters in the lower Piranhas-Açu River basin, 
northeastern Brazil. Water samples were collected at 
seven monitoring points downstream of the Armando 
Ribeiro Gonçalves Reservoir during dry and rainy 
periods between 2019 and 2021. Physical, chemical, 
and microbiological parameters, including pH, elec-
trical conductivity (EC), Na⁺, K⁺, Ca2⁺, Mg2⁺, Cl⁻, 
CO₃2⁻, HCO₃⁻, alkalinity, nitrogen, phosphorus, ther -
motolerant coliforms, and chlorophyll-a, were evalu-
ated using Pearson correlation, principal component 
analysis, cluster analysis, and factor analysis. Multi-
variate analyses identified three distinct water-quality 
groups, while factor analysis retained three factors 
explaining 71.93% of the total variance and represent-
ing trophic conditions, ionic enrichment, and carbon-
ate–bicarbonate dynamics. Dry periods were asso-
ciated with greater salinity, alkalinity, phosphorus 
concentrations, and phytoplankton response, whereas 
rainy periods showed higher N, K⁺, and HCO₃⁻ lev -
els and lower overall mineralization. Urbanized areas 
were mainly associated with phosphorus enrichment

1 Introduction
The Brazilian semiarid region is characterized by 
marked spatial and temporal irregularity in rainfall, 
shallow and often stony soils, and vegetation domi-
nated by the Caatinga biome. Under these condi-
tions, reservoirs play a strategic role in ensuring water 
availability, food security, and socioeconomic devel-
opment. However, increasing anthropogenic pressures 
have progressively compromised both the quantity 
and quality of available water resources. Domestic 
sewage, industrial effluents, agricultural activities, 
and intensive water use contribute to the degradation 
of surface and groundwater, while excessive abstrac-
tion may further affect aquifer recharge and long-term 
water availability (Silva et al., 2024).
Water quantity and quality are also strongly regu-
lated by hydroclimatic processes that control stream-
flow, pollutant transport, dilution, and concentra-
tion within aquatic systems (Mesquita et  al., 2020; 
Wiegand et  al., 2021). Variations in rainfall and 
hydrological regimes can modify pollutant loads 
and concentrations (Couture et  al., 2018; Me et  al., 
2018; Messina et al., 2020), while the increasing fre-
quency and intensity of droughts and floods impose 
additional challenges for water-resource management 
(Shrestha et al., 2017). These effects are particularly 
pronounced in semiarid environments, where pro-
longed droughts reduce stored water volumes and 
enhance evapoconcentration (Rocha Junior et  al., 
2018), whereas intense rainfall following dry peri-
ods can increase surface runoff and rapidly transport 
nutrients and other constituents from the watershed 
into water bodies (Mesquita et al., 2020; Santos et al., 
2014).
In addition to hydroclimatic variability, land use 
and watershed characteristics strongly influence lim-
nological and hydrochemical conditions (Me et  al., 
2018; Menezes et  al., 2016; Soares et  al., 2012). 
Urbanization can increase nutrient and microbial 
inputs through untreated or insufficiently treated 
wastewater, whereas agricultural and livestock activi-
ties may contribute fertilizers, agrochemicals, animal 
wastes, and dissolved salts through surface runoff and 
irrigation return flows (Araújo et al., 2019; du Ples-
sis et al., 2015; Me et al., 2018). In semiarid regions, 
these pressures interact with high evaporation rates 
and seasonal fluctuations in water volume, favoring 
the concentration of salts and nutrients and increas-
ing the susceptibility of aquatic ecosystems to salini-
zation and eutrophication (Macedo & Tavares, 2010; 
Manaouch et  al., 2020; Rocha Junior et  al., 2018). 
Consequently, systematic water-quality monitoring is 
essential for detecting changes associated with land 
use and land cover and for supporting effective plan-
ning and management of water resources (Carvalho 
et al., 2022).
Within this regional context, the Armando Ribeiro 
Gonçalves Reservoir stands out as the largest reser -
voir in the state of Rio Grande do Norte and plays 
a strategic role in water supply, maintenance of per -
ennial river flow, flood control, irrigated agricul-
ture, aquaculture, industrial activities, and tourism. 
Despite its socioeconomic and environmental impor -
tance, studies conducted since 2000 have consist-
ently reported eutrophic conditions, recurrent domi-
nance of potentially toxic cyanobacteria, and marked 
temporal variability in phytoplankton communities, 
particularly in response to seasonal and hydrologi-
cal changes (Câmara et  al., 2015; Chellappa et  al., 
2009; Costa et  al., 2006; Vieira et  al., 2015). Costa 
et  al. (2006) documented high cyanobacterial domi-
nance, including Microcystis spp., Cylindrospermop-
sis raciborskii, and Aphanizomenon spp., together

with the occurrence of microcystins and saxitoxins. 
Chellappa et  al. (2009) subsequently showed that 
seasonal alternation modified phytoplankton compo-
sition, with Planktothrix agardhii prevailing during 
the dry season and Microcystis aeruginosa during the 
rainy season. Under an anomalous rainfall regime, 
Câmara et  al. (2015) observed that increased water 
levels, water-column mixing, and nutrient pulses 
reduced the dominance of potentially toxic cyanobac-
teria and increased phytoplankton diversity. Similarly, 
Vieira et al. (2015), in a longer-term assessment from 
2009 to 2011, reported pronounced temporal changes 
characterized by the succession of cyanobacterial 
species and the persistence of eutrophic conditions. 
Collectively, these findings indicate that water qual-
ity in the Armando Ribeiro Gonçalves Reservoir has 
historically reflected the interaction between nutrient 
enrichment, phytoplankton dynamics, and hydrocli-
matic variability.
Nevertheless, direct comparisons among stud-
ies conducted in the region require caution. Differ -
ences in the geographic location of sampling sites 
expose water bodies to contrasting land-use, land-
cover, and geogenic conditions, which may generate 
distinct hydrochemical, trophic, and microbiologi-
cal responses. Moreover, the irregular distribution of 
rainfall modifies streamflow, runoff, and the concen-
tration or dilution of salts and nutrients, while differ -
ences in sampling periods, analytical procedures, and 
seasonal conditions may further limit direct compari-
sons. Even with these constraints, previous studies 
provide an essential basis for understanding the tem-
poral evolution of water quality and for identifying 
the environmental processes that may control its spa-
tial variability (Câmara et al., 2015; Chellappa et al., 
2009; Costa et al., 2006; Vieira et al., 2015).
Given this complexity, multivariate statistical 
approaches provide a robust framework for integrat-
ing multiple dimensions of water-quality variability. 
Pearson correlation analysis identifies associations 
among measured variables, hierarchical cluster analy-
sis groups sampling sites or periods according to their 
overall similarity, and principal component analysis 
reduces dimensionality while revealing the dominant 
gradients structuring the dataset. Factor analysis com-
plements these approaches by identifying groups of 
variables that exhibit similar patterns of variation and 
by supporting the interpretation of hydrochemical and 
environmental processes potentially associated with 
those patterns (Hair Junior et  al., 2014; Maia et  al., 
2019; Muniz et al., 2019). Together, these techniques 
allow spatial and temporal variability to be evaluated 
in an integrated manner and facilitate the interpreta-
tion of complex interactions among hydroclimatic 
conditions, land use, and water quality.
Although previous investigations of the Armando 
Ribeiro Gonçalves Reservoir have provided impor -
tant information on eutrophication, phytoplankton 
dynamics, cyanobacteria, cyanotoxins, and selected 
physicochemical and microbiological indicators, 
integrated assessments linking spatial and temporal 
changes in downstream water quality with land use, 
land cover, and hydroclimatic seasonality remain 
comparatively limited. Addressing this gap is par -
ticularly important because water-quality assessment 
provides a fundamental basis for identifying environ-
mental pressures, evaluating their potential impacts, 
and supporting mitigation and management strate-
gies (da Silva et al., 2026).
Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether 
land use and land cover downstream of the Armando 
Ribeiro Gonçalves Dam are associated with spatial 
and temporal variations in the physical, chemical, 
and microbiological quality of water in the Piranhas-
Açu River basin. It was hypothesized that spatial 
and temporal variations in water quality are jointly 
shaped by land-use pressures and hydroclimatic sea-
sonality, leading to distinct hydrochemical conditions 
expressed through differences in salinity, nutrient 
dynamics, eutrophic responses, and microbiological 
contamination.
2 Material and Methods
2.1 Characterization of the Study Area
The research was carried out in the lower part of the 
Piranhas-Açu River basin, located in the Vale do 
Açu micro-region, occupying an area of 4756.1 km2 
which corresponds to 9.06% of the area of the state of 
Rio Grande do Norte, Brazil. Seven water collection 
points were established, representative of the pre-
dominant uses and occupation of the lower part of the 
basin, located downstream of the Armando Ribeiro 
Gonçalves Dam (ARGD) with a storage capacity of 
2.4 billion cubic meters (IBGE, 2010), as shown in 
Fig. 1.

Geologically, the study area is located within the 
Borborema Province and is characterized by a het-
erogeneous assemblage of geological units mapped 
by the CPRM. These units include formations asso-
ciated with coastal tablelands, alluvial and colluvial 
deposits, interbedded silt–clay and quartz-rich sandy 
sediments, undifferentiated migmatites, and quartz-
rich sandy and conglomeratic sediments interbed-
ded with silt–clay materials. This geological diver -
sity contributes to spatial differences in sediment 
composition and may influence water–rock and 
water–sediment interactions along the study area 
(CPRM, 2005a, 2005b, 2005c, 2005d, 2005e), as 
shown in Fig. 2.
The soil classes identified in the study area, 
according to the Brazilian Soil Classification Sys-
tem, are Neossolo Flúvico, Neossolo Litólico, Cam-
bissolo Háplico, and Latossolo Vermelho-Amarelo 
(Fig.  3). These classes broadly correspond, under 
USDA Soil Taxonomy, to Fluvents and Orthents 
within the Entisols, Inceptisols, and Oxisols, 
respectively (Santos et al., 2025). Neossolo Flúvico 
is the predominant soil class, occurring mainly 
along the fluvial corridor, whereas Cambissolo 
Háplico, Neossolo Litólico, and Latossolo Ver -
melho-Amarelo occur in more restricted portions of 
the study area. (Santos et al., 2025).
According to the Köppen classification (Köp-
pen, 1918), the study area has a BSh climate, with 
summer and fall rains and an average temperature 
of 26.5  °C (Alvares et  al., 2014). The rainy sea-
son begins in February and lasts until May, with 
the wettest months being March and April, with 
an average annual rainfall of 654 mm. The average 
annual temperature is 28 °C and the relative humid-
ity is 70% (INMET, 2025). The predominant veg-
etation is of the type Caatinga Hiperxerófila, Car -
naubal and Halófita.

2.2 Land Use Diagnosis
Land use was determined by defining the use and occu-
pation of the area downstream of the dam, showing 
areas of forest, non-forest natural formation, agricul-
ture, non-vegetated areas and bodies of water (Fig. 4), 
within a 4-km buffer extending from each margin of 
the main channel of the Piranhas-Açu River. The Land-
sat satellite images, with a spatial resolution of 30 m, 
were taken from MapBiomas, collection 7.0 of 2021, 
and the map was then constructed using QGIS soft-
ware, version 3.16 (MapBiomas, 2021).
2.3 Sampling and Rainfall Seasonality
Water samples were collected in 2019, 2020, and 
2021 at seven sites located downstream of the dam 
(Fig.  3). The sampling sites were selected to repre-
sent predominant land-use and land-cover conditions 
potentially influencing surface-water quality, while 
considering accessibility and field-sampling safety. 
The geographic coordinates of each site were 
recorded using a handheld Global Positioning Sys-
tem (GPS) receiver (Garmin GPSMAP 78 s, Garmin 
Ltd., Xizhi District, New Taipei City, Taiwan). Sam-
pling campaigns were conducted under contrast-
ing hydroclimatic conditions, including drier and 
wetter periods, to capture seasonal changes in the 
magnitude of water-quality parameters. This design 
allowed the assessment of processes such as evap-
oconcentration during drier conditions and dilution, 
hydrological renewal, and enhanced surface runoff 
during wetter periods, thereby improving the inter -
pretation of the combined influence of geogenic char-
acteristics and anthropogenic pressures across the 
study area (Fig. 5).
In 2019, samples were taken in June (C1), Sep-
tember (C2) and November (C3), the months

2.4 Physico-chemical and Microbiological Water 
Analysis
To preserve sample integrity, the vials were stored 
in ice-cooled thermal containers, following the pro-
cedures recommended by APHA (2017), and sub-
sequently transported to two laboratories at the Uni-
versidade Federal Rural do Semi-Árido (UFERSA), 
Mossoró Campus: The Soil, Water and Plant Analysis 
Laboratory (LASAP) and the Semi-Arid Limnology 
and Water Quality Laboratory (LIMNOAQUA). For 
clarity, the procedures adopted for sample collection, 
preservation, storage, and transportation. Water sam-
ples were collected from the surface layer (0–30 cm) 
and stored in polypropylene bottles according to the 
requirements of each analytical procedure. Samples 
collected in 1 L bottles were used for the determina-
tion of pH, electrical conductivity (EC), major ions, 
nitrogen, and alkalinity. The pH and EC were meas-
ured immediately after the samples arrived at the lab-
oratory using a benchtop pH meter and conductivity 
meter, respectively. Sodium (Na⁺) and potassium (K⁺) 
were determined by flame photometry; calcium (Ca2⁺) 
and magnesium (Mg2⁺) by EDTA complexometric 
titration using Eriochrome Black T as indicator; chlo-
ride (Cl⁻) by titration with silver nitrate using potas-
sium chromate as indicator; and carbonate (CO₃ 2⁻) 
and bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) by acid titration using phe-
nolphthalein and methyl orange as indicators, respec-
tively, following Embrapa in triplicates (Silva, 2009) 
(Table 1). Total nitrogen (N) was determined by the 
Kjeldahl method, including digestion, distillation, 
and titrimetric quantification by Standard Methods

2.5 Statistical Analysis
The multivariate statistical analysis was initially per -
formed using a Pearson correlation matrix to assess 
the relationships among the water-quality variables 
and to identify those with sufficient intercorrelation to 
support subsequent multivariate analyses. Pearson’s 
correlation coefficients were considered significant at 
p ≤ 0.05, and variables showing absolute correlation 
coefficients (|r|≥ 0.55) were considered relevant for 
the multivariate approach (Hair Júnior et al. 2014).
Subsequently, principal component analysis (PCA), 
cluster analysis (CA), and factor analysis (FA) were 
applied to investigate similarities and dissimilarities 
among sampling sites and sampling periods. These 
analyses aimed to identify the main patterns of spatial 
and temporal variability in water quality and to pro-
vide insights into potential natural and anthropogenic 
sources affecting the studied river reach, based on the

For PCA, components were retained according to 
the Kaiser criterion, considering eigenvalues greater 
than 1.0 (Kaiser, 1958). Cluster analysis was subse-

sampling periods exhibiting similar water-quality char-
acteristics. Factor analysis was then applied to improve 
the interpretation of the underlying relationships among 
variables. The extracted factors were subjected to Vari-
max orthogonal rotation, which maximizes high factor 
loadings and minimizes low loadings, thereby facilitat-
ing the identification and interpretation of the variables 
most strongly associated with each factor.
All statistical analyses were performed using R v. 
4.6.1 (R Core Team, 2026).

3.4 Factor Analysis
Factor analysis (Table  2) was employed to identify 
sets of physicochemical and microbiological varia-
bles exhibiting similar variation patterns across water 
samples, complementing the interpretation derived 
from Principal Component Analysis (Figs.  8 and 9) 
and Hierarchical Cluster Analysis (Fig.  10). Of the

the dataset.
4 Discussion
The results indicate that both pressures associated 
with land use and land cover and hydroclimatic sea-
sonality contributed to the observed variability in the 
hydrochemical, trophic, and microbiological condi-
tions of the water, thereby corroborating the hypoth-
esis proposed for the study.
Comparing the mean values of the 21 samples 
from the dry period (C1, C2, and C3) with the 14 
samples from the rainy period (C4 and C5) reveals a 
marked seasonal response across the 14 water qual-
ity variables (Table  1). Based on rainfall distribution 
in the study area municipalities (Figs.  1 and 6), C4 
and C5 coincided with the period of highest precipi-
tation, whereas C1, C2, and C3 occurred predomi-
nantly during the dry season. During the rainy period, 
the greatest reductions were observed in P (95.10%) 
and chlorophyll-a (78.90%), indicating a reduced 
phytoplankton response. Levels of Na⁺ (44.70%), 
ALK (41.0%), CO₃ 2⁻ (34.80%), Ca2⁺ (26.30%), Cl⁻ 
(24.10%), thermotolerant coliforms (0.12 log), Mg2⁺ 
(15.20%), and EC (−7.50%) also decreased, while 
pH dropped from 8.17 to 7.21, reflecting less alkaline 
conditions. Conversely, N (64.60%), K⁺ (21.30%), 
and HCO₃⁻ (9.20%) increased, suggesting that rainfall 
promoted not only dilution but also the mobilization

nutrient pulses and water column mixing, as previ-

Reservoir (Câmara et  al., 2015), indicating that the

sity and the stage of the rainy season. Spatially, sites 
P5, P6, and P7 were more strongly associated with 
salinity-related variables, indicating increasing ionic 
enrichment in a downstream direction. This pattern 
may reflect salt concentration during the dry season 
due to intense evaporation, combined with inputs 
from irrigated agriculture, irrigation return flows, 
and shrimp farming. In particular, site P7, located 
downstream of shrimp farms, showed a strong asso-
ciation with EC, Na⁺, Cl⁻, Ca2⁺, and Mg2⁺, recording 
the highest Na⁺ and Cl⁻ concentrations during the dry 
season; this suggests a local contribution to saliniza-
tion, given that shrimp farming effluents can increase 
salt loading into receiving water bodies (de Paiva 
et al., 2024). Conversely, sites P1 through P4 showed 
a stronger association with P, Chlorophyll-a (Chl a), 
and TC, suggesting a more pronounced influence of 
nutrient enrichment and microbiological contami-
nation. Such conditions may be linked to fertilizer 
transport via surface runoff from agricultural areas 
(Baldassarini & Nunes, 2018; Manaouch et al., 2020) 
and the input of untreated domestic wastewater (Bar -
letta et  al., 2019). The higher Chl a concentrations 
observed at P1 and P2 during the dry season are also 
consistent with the increased trophic levels reported 
in semi-arid region reservoirs under low water storage 
conditions, as well as with the known seasonal phy -
toplankton variability in the Armando Ribeiro Gon-
çalves Reservoir (Chellappa et  al., 2009; Wiegand 
et  al., 2021). Similarly, point P4, located within the 
Baixo Açu Irrigated Perimeter, may receive inputs 
from irrigated agriculture, given that evapotranspira-
tion concentrates salts in the soil and irrigation return 
flow can transport these solutes, particularly Cl⁻, into 
adjacent water bodies (Jafari et al., 2012).

area (Figs.  2 and 3). According to approximate cor -
respondence with USDA Soil Taxonomy, the basin is 
dominated by Entisols, particularly Fluvents associ-
ated with fluvial environments, with additional occur-
rences of Inceptisols, Oxisols, and shallow Entisols 
related to lithic substrates. These soils occur over a 
heterogeneous geological framework comprising allu-
vial and colluvial deposits, coastal-tableland materi-
als, interbedded silt–clay and quartz-rich sandy sedi-
ments, undifferentiated migmatites, and quartz-rich 
sandy and conglomeratic deposits. Less developed 
soils, particularly Entisols and Inceptisols, may con-

contribute Ca2⁺, Mg2⁺, Na ⁺, K⁺, and HCO₃⁻ through

of high evaporation and greater concentration of dis-

superimposed on the broader hydrochemical pattern 
captured by PC1.
A joint analysis of Figs.  4 and 10 reveals the fol-
lowing patterns: Group I showed the lowest depend-
ence on seasonality, as it included all five samples 
from P1 and, predominantly, samples from P2 to P5 
collected during C1, C2, and C3. This group repre-
sented a hydrochemical condition characterized by 
low salinity, greater eutrophic expression, and higher 
levels of thermotolerant coliforms. Group II showed 
a predominant association with the dry season and 
consisted mainly of samples from P3 to P7 collected 
during C1, C2, and C3. This group represented a tran-
sitional hydrochemical condition, characterized by 
intermediate levels of eutrophication and microbio-
logical contamination. In contrast, Group III showed 
the strongest and clearest association with the rainy 
season, as it was composed exclusively of samples 
collected during C4 and C5 from points P2 to P7. 
This group was characterized by a distinct hydro-
chemical condition, with no evidence of increased 
salinity and with lower eutrophic expression and, in 
general, lower microbiological contamination.
Finally, Factor 1 (F1) explained 31.32% of the 
total variance (Table 2) and showed high factor load-
ings for pH (0.77), K⁺ (−0.88), alkalinity (0.83), 
N (−0.63), P (0.84), and chlorophyll-a (0.65). The 
opposing factor loadings of N and K⁺ relative to pH, 
alkalinity, P, and chlorophyll-a indicate a trophic-
hydrochemical gradient characterized by contrasting 
nutrient and water chemistry conditions among sam-
ples, consistent with the seasonal changes observed 
between the dry and rainy season sampling events 
(Table  1). Factor 2 (F2) explained 28.36% of the 
total variance and was characterized by high loadings 
for EC (0.76), Na⁺ (0.94), Ca2⁺ (0.85), Mg2⁺ (0.79), 
and Cl− (0.73), reflecting a hydrochemical condition 
associated with increased dissolved ion content in the

additional 12.25% of the variance and was defined 
primarily by CO₃2⁻ (0.89) and HCO₃⁻ (−0.72), repre-
senting variations in carbonate and bicarbonate ani-
ons. Thermotolerant coliforms did not exhibit high 
loadings on any of the retained factors, indicating that 
their variability was independent of the physicochem-
ical structure.
5 Conclusions
The results confirmed that land use and land cover 
downstream of the Armando Ribeiro Gonçalves Dam 
are associated with spatial variations in the physical, 
chemical, and microbiological quality of the Pira-
nhas-Açu River. Areas influenced by irrigated agri-
culture and shrimp farming exhibited greater ionic 
enrichment, particularly regarding electrical conduc-
tivity and concentrations of Na⁺, Ca2⁺, Mg2⁺, and Cl⁻, 
 Water Air Soil Pollut (2026) 237:1329 
 1329 Page 18 of 21
Vol:. (1234567890)
indicating a salinization process that intensifies in the 
downstream reaches. In contrast, upstream and inter -
mediate sites showed a stronger association with P, 
chlorophyll-a, and thermotolerant coliforms, reflect-
ing nutrient enrichment and microbiological pressure.
Seasonality was also a determining factor in water 
quality variability. Samples collected during the dry 
season were characterized by higher levels of salinity, 
alkalinity, P, and chlorophyll-a, whereas wet-season 
samples showed lower values for these parameters 
and a predominance of K⁺, HCO₃⁻, and N; this dem-
onstrates that rainfall altered both solute concentra-
tions and hydrochemical dynamics. Principal compo-
nent analysis made it possible to distinguish gradients 
related to salinity and ionic composition, trophic and 
hydrochemical conditions, and the CO₃ 2⁻–HCO₃⁻ 
equilibrium. Consistently, hierarchical cluster analy -
sis formed three groups with approximately 30% 
dissimilarity, reinforcing climatic seasonality as a pri-
mary factor differentiating sampling conditions.
Overall, the multivariate approach effectively 
integrated spatial and temporal water quality 
responses with land use, rainfall seasonality, and 
the basin’s natural geochemical characteristics. 
Irrigation, shrimp farming, agricultural areas, and 
urban development were linked to changes in indi-
cators for salts, nutrients, and microbiological 
parameters, highlighting the potential for progres-
sive salinization and eutrophication processes in 
the downstream reaches. These results corroborate 
the need for periodic water quality monitoring and 
environmental management actions addressing the 
main anthropogenic pressures identified along the 
Piranhas-Açu River in Rio Grande do Norte, Brazil.
