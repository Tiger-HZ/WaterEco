ABSTRACT 
The movement of water into soil is a critical process influencing irrigation efficiency, 
infiltration processes, groundwater recharge, soil moisture availability, and sustainable 
agricultural production. While soil characteristics have traditionally been regarded as the 
principal factors co ntrolling infiltration, the influence of the physical quality of infiltrating 
water has received comparatively less attention. This study experimentally analyzed the 
effects of viscosity, density, and turbidity, on soil infiltration d ynamics under controll ed 
temperature conditions of 20°C , 25°C, 30°C, and 35°C. The study was co nducted on an 
agricultural soil. The land slope/topography was determined as 1.7%, showing the land area 
to be relatively flat with a climatic condition of high rainfall and atmospher ic moisture. Soil 
samples were collected from three locations within the experimental plot and subjected to 
laboratory analysis with the following results obtained: particle size distribution as 81% 
sand and 18% silt, proving the area to be loam-sand dominated, average moisture contents of 
4.50%, 5.21%, and 4.90% for Locations A, B, and C, respectively, showing that the ar ea has 
low moisture content. Hydraulic conductivity values of 6.505 × 10⁻³ cm/s, 6.513 × 10⁻³ cm/s, 
and 6.505 × 10⁻³ cm/s respectively we re obtained, indicating relatively high permeability. 
Infiltration rates gradually decreased with time from 15.6 cm/h to 7.8 cm/h, 25.2 cm/h to 6.6 
cm/h, and 20.4 cm/h to 7.8 cm/h at Locations A, B, and C, respectively, which shows that the 
area has good i nfiltration characteristics. Water samples were subjected to water test. The 
results showed that v iscosity decreased with increasing temperature. The mean viscosity 
decreased from 5.072 mPa·s at 20°C to 4.298 SmPa·s at 35°C, representing an overall 
reduction of approximately 15.3%. Two -way ANOVA showed that temperature significantly 
affected viscosity (F(3,33) = 36.24, p < 0.001), while water source had no significant effect 
(F(11,33) = 1.31, p = 0.261). Density also decreased progressively with increasing 
temperature, while both temperature (F(3,33) = 47.47, p < 0.001) and water source 
(F(11,33) = 24.48, p < 0.001) significantly affected density, with the statistics explaining 
92.6% of the observed variation (R² = 0.926). Turbidity showed considerable variat ion 
among water sources, with borehole and rainfall water recording relatively low values, while 
surface water, ponds, rivers, and floodwater recorded considerably higher values, with some 
exceeding 1400 NTU. Water source was the dominant factor influencin g turbidity (F(11,33) 
= 4356.1, p < 0.001), while temperature had a statistically significant but relatively small

I. Introduction. 
Water infiltration into soil is one of the most important processes in agricultural w ater 
management, irrigation engineering, groundwater recharge, and environmental sustainability. 
Infiltration refers to the downward movement of water from the soil surface into the soil 
profile where it becomes available for plant uptake, groundwater rech arge, and maintenance 
of soil moisture (Michael & Ojha, 2005). 
From the agricultural definition, according to Schwab, Fangmeier, Elliot, & Frevert (2021), 
infiltration is said to be the process by which precipitation moves downward through the 
surface of the soil to replenish soil moisture, recharge the aquifer and ultimately support 
stream flow during dry periods, as well as support plant growth. 
Infiltration process of fluids (water) always comes into action during precipitation forms, e.g. 
dew, moist, rain fall, reaching the surface of the earth and also during irrigation processes 
(controlled artificial application of fluids (water) to the soil surface, principally for the 
purpose of supplying soil moisture essential for plant growth applied on the soil sur face 
(Michael & Ojha, 2018). However, not all water applied or reaching the surface of the earth 
infiltrates easily or freely into the soil. Some water is ponded on the soil surface, thereby 
resisting free downward movement, while some gradually move downwar ds into the soil, 
causing a delay in the rate or time of infiltration (Ogbu, Nwachukwu, Orakwe, & Chukwuma, 
2015). 
However, research has shown that the cause of this resistance/delay or gradual flow in the 
downward movement of various forms of fluids (water ) from the surface of the earth into the 
soil, is found to be influenced by several factors, including soil texture, soil structure, 
porosity, antecedent moisture content, organic matter content, and the physical properties of 
the infiltrating fluid (water quality), either used for irrigation on that soil. Among the fluid 
properties that influence infiltration are viscosity, turbidity, and density (Arora, 2009; 
Aboukhalesl, Nofal, & Hisham, 2020). 
Water quality refers to the chemical, physical, and biologica l characteristics of water based 
on the standard of its usage, (Cordy, 2001). Water quality is a multidimensional concept that 
describes the physical, chemical, and biological characteristics of water and determines its 
suitability for a specific purpose s uch as drinking, irrigation, industrial processes, 
aquaculture, or environmental conservation. 
Among the numerous water quality parameters, the physical properties of water (viscosity, 
density, and turbidity) are particularly significant because they direc tly influence the 
hydraulic behavior of water, especially during infiltration, seepage, groundwater recharge,

and irrigation. Consequently, understanding the relationship between water quality and these 
three physical properties as well as their influence is fundamental in irrigation engineering, 
hydrology, soil science, and environmental manag ement (WHO, 2022; APHA, AWWA, & 
WEF, 2023). The physical quality of water influences its movement through soil pores, 
interaction with soil particles, and ability to tr ansport dissolved and suspended materials. 
Variations in these water qualities (viscosity, density, and turbidity) often alter the resistance 
of water to flow, the gravitational forces acting on water within the soil profile, and the extent 
to which suspen ded particles obstruct soil pore spaces. These changes ultimately affect soil 
infiltration rate, hydraulic conductivity, soil aeration, groundwater recharge, and crop 
productivity (Hillel, 1998; Dingman, 2015). 
Viscosity, density, and turbidity are presume d to be some of the factors affecting infiltration 
during the irrigation period on the agricultural soil. Most infiltration studies focus mainly on 
soil characteristics as factors that influence the downward movement of water into the soil, 
giving limited attention to the influence of these selected water quality parameters (viscosity, 
density, and turbidity parameters). Usually, Variations in viscosity, turbidity, and density may 
significantly alter infiltration behaviour, affecting soil moisture replenish ment, groundwater 
recharge, irrigation efficiency, and crop productivity.Therefore, there is a need to investigate 
how these water quality parameters influence infiltration rates under varying temperature 
conditions to provide useful information for irriga tion planning and water resource 
management. 
Objective of the Study 
The main objective of this study is to experimentally analyze the effects of selected water 
quality parameters (viscosity, turbidity, and density) on soil infiltration dy namics under 
controlled temperature conditions of 20, 25, 30°C, and 35°C. 
Other specific objectives includes to: 
i. analyze the viscosity, density and turbidity of the different water samples at 20,25, 30, and 
35oC and their effects on soil infiltration. 
ii. determine the i nfiltration characteristics of different water samples at different 
temperatures. 
iii. statiscally analyze the temperature relationship between the selected water quality 
parameters and soil infiltration. 
iv. specify the water quality parameter with the greatest influence on soil infiltration under 
the experimental conditions. 
II. Literature Review 
Water quality refers to the physical, chemical, and biological characteristics of water that 
determine its suitability for a particular purpose, such as drinking , irrigation, industrial use, 
recreation, or environmental sustainability. The quality of irrigation water is of particular 
importance because it directly affects soil physical properties, infiltration characteristics, 
nutrient availability, crop performan ce, and long -term soil productivity. Water containing 
excessive suspended solids, dissolved salts, organic matter, or contaminants may gradually 
alter soil structure, reduce hydraulic conductivity, clog soil pores, and decrease infiltration 
rates (WHO, 2022; APHA, AWWA, & WEF, 2023). 
Physical water quality refers to the observable and measurable physical characteristics of 
water which determine its appearance, movement, and hydraulic behaviour. The major 
physical parameters include temperature, colour, odou r, taste, turbidity, total suspended

solids (TSS), total dissolved solids (TDS), electrical conductivity (EC), density, and 
viscosity. 
Viscosity is one of the most important physical properties of water affecting fluid flow. In 
fluid mechanics, viscosity r epresents the friction that exists between adjacent layers of a 
moving fluid. It is defined as the internal resistance of a fluid to deformation or flow under an 
applied force. Fluids with high viscosity resist movement, whereas fluids with low viscosity 
flow more readily (Potter, Wiggert and Ramadan, 2025). 
Temperature has a significant effect on the viscosity of fluids (water). As water temperature 
increases, the cohesive forces between molecules reduce, resulting in reduced viscosity. 
Thus, at high tempe ratures water loses its viscosity, while at low temperatures its viscosity 
increases.The viscosity of water varies with temperature. The values presented by Rumble 
and John (2018) show that dynamic and kinematic viscosity decrease as temperature 
increases. For example, the dynamic viscosity of water decreases from 1.002 × 10⁻³ Ns/m² at 
20°C to 0.798 × 10⁻³ Ns/m² at 30°C and 0.653 × 10⁻³ Ns/m² at 40°C. Thus, water at low 
temperatures has higher viscosity, while water at higher temperatures has lower viscosity. 
Water, which is the material used in this work, falls under the Newtonian fluid classification, 
but may change with slight changes in environmental conditions such as temperature and 
pressure. The viscosity coefficient of a liquid decreases with increas ing temperature, while 
the coefficient of viscosity of gases increases with increasing temperature. 
Fluid viscosity controls the resistance to flow within interconnected pore spaces; 
consequently, an increase in viscosity decreases hydraulic conductivity ( Assouline & Or, 
2013; Wang, Ma, & Zhu, 2022). As viscosity decreases, hydraulic resistance is reduced, 
resulting in increased infiltration rates and more rapid water movement under both saturated 
and unsaturated conditions (Kirkham, 2014). 
Density is one of the fundamental physical properties of fluids and is defined as the mass of a 
substance per unit volume. It is an important parameter in fluid mechanics, hydrology, 
irrigation engineering, and soil-water movement because it influences the gravitational for ces 
acting on water during flow and infiltration processes.Under standard atmospheric conditions, 
pure water has a density of approximately 998 kg/m³ at 20°C. As temperature increases, 
water molecules become more energetic and move farther apart, resulting in a slight decrease 
in density. Density plays a significant role in determining the energy state and movement of 
water within soil pores. Water infiltration is driven primarily by gravitational forces a nd 
capillary action. 
Turbidity is also one of the mos t important physical indicators of water quality .Turbid water 
usually appears cloudy, milky or otherwise coloured, thereby affecting the physical look of 
the water. Turbidity is also a measure of the degree to which water loses its transparency 
because of the presence of suspended and colloidal particles that scatter and absorb light 
passing through the water column (Lipps, Braun -Howland, & Baxter, 2023). The major 
source of turbidity is total suspended solids (TSS). These suspended solids come from two 
sources: one is a natural source, and the other is an anthropogenic source. The natural sources 
are sediments such as silts, clay, organic materials, algae, dead organisms, etc., while 
anthropogenic sources are wastewater (sewage), agricultural runoff and ero sion from rainfall. 
Turbidity is expressed in Nephelometric Turbidity Units (NTU) and is measured using 
instruments known as nephelometers or turbidimeters. 
In irrigation systems, water turbidity directly influences soil infiltration because suspended 
sediments are deposited on the soil surface and within soil pore spaces during irrigation. Over 
time, these fine particles form a thin, low -permeability layer (commonly referred to as a 
surface seal or clogging layer), which disrupts pore continuity, reduces sa turated hydraulic

Infiltration is the process by which precipitation or any form of water moves downward 
through the surface of the soil into the soil in o rder to replenish soil moisture, recharge 
aquifer, and ultimately support stream flow during dry periods. The maximum rate at which 
the water enters the soil in a given time is known as the infiltration capacity while a measure 
of the rate at which the soi l is able to absorb the water is known as the infiltration rate and it 
is measured in millimeter per hour (mm/hr) or inches per hour (Kirkham, 2014). The rate of 
infiltration is usually measured using an infiltrometer (Nimmo & Shilliti, 2023). 
The infiltration rate of water into soil is affected by soil characteristics, storage capacity, soil 
texture, vegetation type and cover, moisture content, soil temperature, rainfall intensity, and 
frost (Huffman et al., 2013). 
III. Materials and Methods 
Study Area 
The study was conducted at the Teaching and Research Farm of the School of Agriculture 
and Agricultural Technology, Federal Polytechnic Nekede, located in Nekede, Owerri West 
Local Government Area of Imo State, southeastern Nigeria ( Federal Polytechnic Nekede, 
n.d.). The institution lies approximately between Latitude 5°27′ –5°30′ N and Longitude 
6°58′–7°02′ E, at an elevation of about 75 –90 m above mean sea level. The study area 
experiences a humid tropical climate characterized by two distinct seasons: a rainy seaso n 
extending from April to October and a dry season from November to March. Mean annual 
rainfall ranges between 2,000 and 2,500 mm, while average temperatures vary from 25°C to 
35°C. Relative humidity ranges from about 80% during the dry season to over 100% during 
the rainy season. The soils are predominantly sandy loam to loamy sand, well -drained, 
porous, and characterized by moderate to high infiltration capacity, making them highly 
suitable for studies involving soil-water movement.

Soil Analysis 
The different soil analyses conducted in the course of this research were necessary to 
determine the important physical properties and nature of the soil, which influence th e 
downward movement of water from the soil surface into the soil (infiltration) (Brady & Weil, 
2016). Among the properties tested for in the course of the analysis are the following: grain 
size analysis, hydraulic conductivity (permeability), available moi sture content (MC) and the 
infiltration rate of the soil. 
The wet sieving method was selected for the grain size determination, and this method was 
selected based on visual inspection of the texture of the soil, which was found to be fine –to 
medium–sized s and(Dane, &Topp, 2020). Soil samples were collected using the soil auger 
borer, from three different locations in the test plot, at a required de pth. The locations were 
tagged as Location A, B, and C for proper identification. 
The moisture content determin ation was done using the gravimetric method (oven drying 
method) on samples collected from the three different locations A, B and C, so as to ensure 
accurate determination of the initial moisture content of the soil from the test plot (ASTM, 
2021). 
Hydraulic conductivity is the rate of flow of water through the soil (Michael and Ojha, 2005). 
It greatly influences the infiltration possibilities of an area and depends on the properties of 
the soil and fluid. The falling head laboratory method was used to det ermine the hydraulic 
conductivity of the soil samples collected from Locations A, B, and C. 
The infiltration test for this research work was done in-situ in the test plot using a double ring 
infiltrometer. It was done under a favourable climatic condition to ensure accuracy. The test 
was carried out to determine the velocity or speed at which water enters the soil from the 
surface and the depth in cm of water level that can enter the soil per hour is usually measured.

Statistical Analysis 
A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for each response variable, with 
Water Source (12 levels) and Temperature (4 levels) as the two independent factors, to 
determine whether each variable had a statistically significant influen ce on each 
physical/quality variable. The Source × Temperature interaction parameter could not be 
separated from the residual error because each Source × Temperature combination was 
expressed by one measurement (i.e., no replicate measurements). The two ma in effects 
(Source and Temperature) were thus evaluated against a pooled residual (interaction/error) 
term with 33 degrees of freedom in a two -way ANOVA without replication (an additive, 
randomized-block-type model). The means were separated using Tukey's Honestly 
Significant Difference (HSD) post -hoc test when a main effect was significant (p < 0.05). 
Every test was performed at the significance level of 5% (α = 0.05).The Shapiro -Wilk test 
was used to determine residual normality. Viscosity and density residuals were examined on 
their raw scale and showed a normal distribution (p > 0.05). As is customary for positively -
skewed turbidity/concentration data, a log10 transformation was applied before the ANOVA 
and post-hoc comparisons because turbidity values breached the normality assumption on the 
raw scale (Shapiro –Wilk p = 0.003) and ranged over nearly three orders of magnitude (2.0 –
1437 NTU).

Discussions 
The topographic surv ey conducted showed that the plot has a total area of 10,000m 2 with a 
maximum flow length of 1180m. Also, the elevation of the farthest point was determined to 
be 46.5m, while the elevation of the outlet point was 26.5m, which resulted in a total 
elevation difference of 20m across the test plot. From the values obtained, the average slope 
of the plot was calculated to be 1.7%. The calculated slope value shows that the experimental 
plot has a gentle slope/gradient, which conforms to the classification of a r elatively flat 
terrain as reported by Schwab et al. (2021). 
The climatic characteristics of the study area show that the area lies within the sub -equatorial 
climatic zone, which is typical of many regions in southern Nigeria. The recorded relative 
humidity ranges between 80% -100%, while the tempera ture ranges between 25 0C and 35 0C. 
Also, it was observed that the area experiences a mean annual rainfall of between 2000mm 
and 2500mm, with a rainfall intensity of approximately 22mm/hr. 
The calculated time of co ncentration ( 𝑇𝐶) of 21.18 minutes suggests that the experimental 
plot has a relatively rapid hydrol ogic response. Therefore the ( 𝑇𝐶) is suitable for infiltration 
experiments because it allows sufficient observation of the initial and transitional stages of 
infiltration. 
The moisture content analysis conducted on the soil samples from the three different 
locations (A, B, and C) in the study area, showed the average moisture as 4.45%, 5.21% and 
4.90%, respectively. These results show that the soil within the expe rimental plot area 
possesses relatively low moisture content and are fairly uniform. 
The wet sieve analysis conducted on the soil samples collected from the three different 
locations showed that the soil comprised an average of approximately 81% sand and 1 8% 
fines. Based on the USDA soil textural classification system, this particle size distribution 
classifies the soil as loamy sand. This textural composition provides the soil with relatively 
high permeability while retaining sufficient fine particles to i mpart moderate cohesion and 
limited water-holding capacity. 
The hydraulic conductivity results obtained at the three sampling locations are nearly 
identical, with only negligible differences among Locations A, B, and C. This close 
agreement indicates that the soil within the experimental plot is relatively homogeneous with 
respect to its hydraulic properties. Such uniformity is advantageous because it minimizes 
spatial variability during infiltration experiments and ensures that differences observed in 
infiltration behaviour can be attributed primarily to the experimental variables —namely 
water viscosity, density, turbidity, and temperature rather than to inherent variations in soil 
properties. 
The infiltration tests conducted at the three sampling locations within the experimental plot 
showed a similar infiltration pattern despite differences in their initial infiltration rates. At 
Location A, the infiltration rate decreased from 15.6 cm h⁻¹ to approximately 7.8 cm h⁻¹ after 
150 minutes. Similarly, Location B recorded the highest initial infiltration rate of 25.2 cm h⁻¹, 
which gradually declined to 6.6 cm h⁻¹, while Location C exhibited an initial infiltration rate 
of 20.4 cm h⁻¹, eventually stabilizing at approximately 7.8 cm h⁻¹. 
The observed decline in inf iltration rate with time followed by the attainment of a relatively 
constant value is characteristic of infiltration processes in natural soils. The infiltration 
behaviour observed in this study agrees with the infiltration theory proposed by Horton 
(1940), who explained that infiltration capacity is initially high at the beginning of rainfall or 
water application but decreases progressively as the soil becomes wetter until a constant or 
equilibrium infiltration rate is attained.

The results obtained for th e kinematic viscosity of the various water samples showed a 
consistent decrease in viscosity with increasing temperature. Similar trends were observed for 
all the water samples analysed, indicating that increasing temperature reduces the resistance 
of wate r to flow regardless of its source. The statistical analysis further confirmed that 
viscosity was significantly influenced by temperature (F(3,33) = 36.24, p < 0.001), but not by 
water source (F(11,33) = 1.31, p = 0.261). 
The mean viscosity decreased progr essively from 5.072 mPa·s at 20°C to 4.298 mPa·s at 
35°C, representing an overall reduction of approximately 15.3% across the 15°C temperature 
range. The lower viscosity values obtained at higher temperatures imply that water would 
experience less resistance while moving through the interconnected pore spaces of the loamy 
sand soil used in the experiment. 
The density measurements obtained for the various water samples showed a slight decrease 
with increasing temperature. For example, the density of the bore hole water sample collected 
from Owerri Municipal decreased from 0.9989 g cm⁻³ at 20°C to 0.9922 g cm⁻³ at 35°C. The 
statistical analysis further showed that both temperature (F(3,33) = 47.47, p < 0.001) and 
water source (F(11,33) = 24.48, p < 0.001) signi ficantly influenced water density. The model 
explained 92.6% of the total variation in density (R² = 0.926). 
Although density contributes to the gravitational driving force responsible for water 
movement through soil, the variations observed between 20°C a nd 35°C were relatively 
small. Consequently, these density differences are unlikely to produce a significant 
independent effect on the movement of water through soil pores or on infiltration under 
normal irrigation conditions. 
The turbidity measurements ob tained for the various water samples revealed considerable 
differences in water clarity among the sources. Borehole water and direct rainfall recorded 
relatively low turbidity values, indicating comparatively clear water with low concentrations 
of suspende d particles. In contrast, surface water sources such as the Imo River, Otamiri 
River, floodwater, and earth pond water exhibited very high turbidity values, with some 
samples exceeding 1400 NTU. 
The statistical analysis showed that water source contributed the largest portion of the 
variance in turbidity (F(11,33) = 4356.1, p < 0.001), while temperature also had a statistically 
significant but very small influence (F(3,33) = 17.27, p < 0.001). This demonstrates that the 
physical source and exposure of the w ater body, rather than the temperature at which it was 
analysed, was the primary factor influencing turbidity. 
The mean turbidity increased moderately with temperature, from 270.3 NTU at 20°C to 297.9 
NTU at 35°C. Although temperature had a statistically significant effect, none of the pairwise 
temperature comparisons remained significant after Tukey correction, confirming that the 
temperature effect was minor compared with the influence of water source. 
From the perspective of soil infiltration, the result s indicate that water samples with high 
turbidity possess greater potential to reduce infiltration than clearer water. During irrigation 
or infiltration, suspended particles may accumulate on the soil surface and within pore 
spaces, gradually forming a thi n sealing or clogging layer that restricts water movement into 
the soil. Consequently, infiltration rates and saturated hydraulic conductivity decrease as pore 
continuity is progressively reduced. 
The findings therefore reveal that the three water quality parameters do not contribute equally 
to infiltration under varying thermal conditions. Among the parameters investigated, viscosity 
exhibited the greatest sensitivity to temperature, density showed only minor temperature -
dependent changes, whereas turbidit y exerted its influence largely through sediment -induced 
pore clogging independent of temperature.

Accordingly, the present study refines the conclusions of Arora (2009), Hillel (2013), and 
Michael (2018) by demonstrating that water quality parameters shou ld not be evaluated 
independently of temperature when assessing infiltration behaviour. Rather than considering 
viscosity, density, and turbidity as isolated variables, the present research shows that 
temperature modifies the behaviour of these properties —particularly viscosity —and 
consequently influences the infiltration process. 
V. Conclusion and Recommendations 
Conclusion 
This study successfully evaluated the effects of selected water quality parameters under a 
controlled temperature range on soil infilt ration characteristics, using viscosity, density, 
turbidity, and infiltration analysis on various water samples obtained from rivers, ponds, 
boreholes, wells, rainfall, and surface water sources. 
The experimental results revealed that viscosity and density decreased with increasing 
temperature for all water samples, while turbidity generally increased with temperature. The 
three two -way ANOVAs revealed a physically cohesive and uniform trend. Viscosity was 
mostly controlled by temperature and unaffected by the source of water, confirming that it is 
an inherent thermophysical characteristic of water. Temperature and water source had a 
substantial impact on density, reflecting the combined effects of thermal expansion and 
dissolved mineral/solute loading. Temp erature had only a small, secondary impact on 
turbidity, while standing or pond waters were far more turbid than groundwater. 
The infiltration tests showed that infiltration rates were initially high and gradually decreased 
with time until a constant infil tration rate was attained. This behaviour was due to gradual 
soil saturation and a reduction in soil suction forces. Water samples with low turbidity, such 
as direct rainfall and borehole water, produced more stable infiltration characteristics because 
they contained fewer suspended particles capable of clogging soil pores. Highly turbid water 
samples such as earth pond water, concrete pond water, and surface water from Nekede 
contained large amounts of suspended solids, which contributed to pore blockage a nd 
reduced infiltration efficiency during the later stages of infiltration. 
The study further establishes the fact that among the selected water quality parameters, 
turbidity significantly influences infiltration processes and hydraulic behaviour of soils, while 
temperature variation also played an important role by altering viscosity and density 
characteristics of the water samples. Among the parameters investigated, viscosity was the 
most temperature-sensitive, density exhibited only minor temperature-induced variations, and 
turbidity influenced infiltration primarily through sediment deposition rather than thermal 
effects. 
The findings show that even though the twelve water samples were taken from the same 
common area, their physicochemical characteristic s varied significantly depending on the 
hydrological origin. They also show that temperature is an important control factor whenever 
viscosity or density measurements are compared across studies or treatment processes like 
filtration, sedimentation, and pu mping design, where both variables directly affect hydraulic 
performance and flow behaviour. 
Conclusively, giving much focus to the turbidity nature of water samples, and minor 
considerations to viscosity and density, cleaner water sources are more suitabl e for 
infiltration and groundwater recharge processes, as highly turbid water may gradually reduce 
soil permeability and infiltration efficiency over time during irrigation processes.

Recommendation 
1. Particular attention should be given to turbidity and temperature because these parameters 
significantly influence soil infiltration and irrigation efficiency. 
2. Where practicable, infiltration/irrigation should be carried out during periods when water 
temperatures favour lower viscosity. 
3. Farmers and irri gation managers should incorporate water quality monitoring into 
irrigation management programmes. 
4. Water int ended for drip and sprinkler irrigation systems should possess low turbidity 
levels. This will reduce emitter clogging, improve water distribution uniformity, minimize 
maintenance costs, and enhance irrigation system performance. 
5. Government agencies, ir rigation authorities, and water resource managers should develop 
practical guidelines for water quality that include physical parameters such as viscosity, 
density, and turbidity, in addition to conventional chemical quality standards. 
6. Agricultural extension services should educate farmers on the influence of water quality on 
soil infiltration and irrigation efficiency. 
7. Further studies should evaluate a wider temperature range and additional water quality 
parameters. 
8. Mathematical and numerical models capable of predicting soil infiltration from measurable 
water quality parameters should be developed and validated. 
The findings of this study should be considered in the design and management of irrigation 
schemes.
