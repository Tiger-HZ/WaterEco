If a fictional typology can be articulated beyond reductive accounts of postcolonial sovereignty—on the premise that material boundaries are themselves porous—then our literacy of the decolonial border might be relocated by apprehending water as the generative medium of political geography. Such a reorientation foregrounds movements in and through water as forces that unsettle the presumed solidity and authority of the nation-state. In this sense, the border emerges less as a line to be policed than as a fluid field of relation, contingency and drift. This chapter considers Gurnah's writing (into) the world from Zanzibar in By the Sea by constantly deferring any notion of terrestrial centrality to the water that surrounds it. The novel gives a panoramic view of bordering practices in the Indian Ocean world in the second half of the 20th century through the memories of Gurnah's co-narrators—Saleh Omar, a former furniture dealer, and Latif Mahmud, a literary studies professor—who arrive in England separately as refugees from Zanzibar during the 1980s and 1990s.
