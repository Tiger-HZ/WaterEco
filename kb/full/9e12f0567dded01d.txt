 Abstract 
 Cyanobacterial blooms and the associated hepatotoxins produced (e.g., microcystins, MCs) create a significant human health risk in freshwater lakes around the world, including Lake Erie. Though various physical and chemical treatment options are utilized, these are costly and their effectiveness decreases when other organics are present. Laboratory studies have identified a remediation option based on a mlr gene operon that can systematically degrade this toxin; however, studies on Lake Erie have been unable to amplify mlr genes from MC-degrading bacteria. These results suggest that either existing primers may be inefficient for broad identification of the mlr genes or that MC degradation genes and/or pathways may vary among bacterial taxa. To investigate the dynamics of the Lake Erie microbial community involved in the degradation of microcystin-LR (MC-LR), a flow-through column experiment using collected beach sand was conducted over a period of six weeks. Increasing concentrations of lake water spiked with MC-LR were continuously delivered to both biotic and abiotic (sterilized) sand columns, with influent and effluent MC-LR concentrations measured by LC-MS/MS. Despite the toxin concentrations far exceeding natural conditions during a bloom event (maximum dosage = 15.4 μg/L), MC-LR was completely removed within 21 h of contact time in the biotic columns. Stimulation of community taxa during the degradation process included Burkholderiaceae, Illumatobacteraceae, Pseudomonadaceae, Rhodocyclaceae and Nitrosomonadaceae. The overall results suggest several critical species may be required for the most complete and effective degradation of MC-LR. 
 Keywords: Microcystin, Biodegradation, Sand-column, 16S rRNA, Lake erie, CyanoHABs Graphical Abstract 
 1. Introduction 
 Changes in both climate (e.g. precipitation and temperature) and nutrient loading into receiving water bodies are increasing the prevalence and toxicity of cyanobacterial harmful algal blooms (CyanoHABs) on a global scale ( Bullerjahn et al., 2016 ). The most common cyanotoxin associated with HABs are microcystins (MCs), a family of monocyclic heptapeptide hepatotoxins [cyclo-(D-Ala 1 -X 2 -D-MeAsp 3 -Z 4 -Adda 5 -D-Glu 6 -Mdha 7 )] produced by cyanobacteria genera such as Microcystis , Anabaena , Nostoc and Planktothrix ( Carmichael 1992 ; Keil et al., 2002 ; Tillett et al., 2000 ; Watanabe et al., 1992 ). Although over 200 variants have been identified ( Zastepa et al., 2015 ), the most studied, and one of the most toxic analogues, is microcystin-LR, where L and R represent leucine and arginine, respectively. The degradation of MCs is difficult due to their cyclic structure that renders them stable and resistant to degradation via high temperatures, extreme pH, sunlight and common enzymes ( Rastogi et al., 2014 ; Wormer et al., 2010 ). Municipal water treatment plants have implemented a number of treatment options including ozonation, powdered activated carbon, sedimentation, sand filtration, and chemical coagulation to remove MCs from the finished product ( Chang et al., 2014 ; Lahti et al., 1997 ; Miao et al., 2010 ; Szlag et al., 2015 ). However, these methods substantially increase water treatment costs and their effectiveness can be compromised by the presence of other naturally occurring organic materials in the water ( Lahti et al., 1997 ; Steffen et al., 2017 ). The severe risks that MCs pose to plants, animals, and humans makes the elimination of this toxin from aquatic environments of utmost concern and priority. 
 Biotic degradation of the MC toxin has been a rapidly expanding field of study in the last two decades. To date, only one biodegradation pathway for MC-LR has been characterized and occurs through a gene cluster containing the genes mlrA , mlrB , mlrC , and mlrD, which is mostly found in species belonging to Sphingomonas and Sphingopyxis ( Bourne et al., 2001 ; Li et al., 2017 ). Multiple studies have identified microbial species that can degrade MCs, but without the simultaneous detection of the mlr gene cluster ( Kormas and Lymperopoulou, 2013 ; Li et al., 2017 ). This suggests a greater diversity of bacterial genera might be able to degrade MCs with yet to be characterized degradation mechanisms. However, while several studies have identified individual species capable of degrading MCs, there is a lack consideration of the entire community response during the biodegradation process, which would be more indicative of the key microbial species that are actively involved in the degradation of the MC toxins. As a result, despite the many advancements made in identifying MC degraders, alternate mechanisms of MC biodegradation have rarely been described ( Ame et al., 2006 ; Dziga et al., 2017 ; Edwards et al., 2008 ; Zhang et al., 2010 ). 
 Lake Erie has been vulnerable to CyanoHABs for decades, and this has negatively impacted ecosystems and human health by diminishing habitat for plants and animals, disrupting food web dynamics, creating hypoxic zones, and producing toxins ( Backer et al., 2013 ; Carmichael, 2001 ; Conroy et al., 2005 ; Hernandez et al., 2009 ; Miller et al., 2010 ; Mohamed et al., 2019 ). Recent studies indicate bacteria capable of degrading MC-LR are present in Lake Erie, though are unique as they do so without the mlr degrading gene ( Krausfeldt et al., 2019 ; Krishnan et al., 2018 ; Mou et al., 2013 ; Thees et al., 2019 ). In 2014, a severe CyanoHAB event released high concentrations of MCs into the municipal water inlet of Toledo, Ohio, which resulted in the city issuing a 3-day “do not drink” advisory as the finished drinking water exceeded the 1.0 μg/L guideline level for safe drinking water set by the World Health Organization ( Bullerjahn et al., 2016 ; Steffen et al., 2017 ; WHO, 2020 ). Pelee Island, Ontario, Canada, located in the western half of Lake Erie ( Fig. 1 ), was similarly exposed to the same CyanoHAB. However, unlike the City of Toledo, the municipality of Pelee Island observed an absence of MCs in their drinking water treatment facilities upon routine testing during and after the bloom event. This region extracts water from ground water wells, suggesting physical adsorption and/or microbial activity are responsible for the removal of MC as lake water flows through sand channels to these wells ( Wang et al., 2018 ).

Current knowledge suggests microbial degradation of MC-LR in Pelee Island sand within Lake Erie is performed by an unknown mechanism, different from the known pathway involving the mlr gene operon. We hypothesize that the bacterial species potentially involved in biodegradation can be determined by monitoring the changes in the bacterial community upon exposure to MC-LR. The aim of this study was to enrich and isolate MC-LR degrading bacteria from Pelee Island beach sand during a CyanoHAB event to determine the species involved and additionally measure biodegradation efficiency. A stepwise, increasing dosage of MC-LR was spiked into flow-through sand columns, with effluent concentrations measured by liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Metataxonomic surveys of active (RNA) microbial communities were performed to understand the temporal bacterial community structure associated with degradation activity. Cumulatively, these data contributed to a better understanding of the natural degradation kinetics of MC-LR and determined how the indigenous community within sand from Pelee Island responds to MC exposure, which can potentially identify bacterial species involved in the degradation of MC-LR. 2. Materials and methods 
 2.1. Site characteristics and collection 
 Lake Erie is the southernmost of all Laurentian Great Lakes and is situated on the international boundary between the Ontario Peninsula and the U.S. states of Michigan, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and New York. Lake Erie is also the warmest, shallowest, and most biologically productive of all the Great Lakes, but has become known for its susceptibility to CyanoHABs ( Watson et al., 2016 ). Pelee Island, Ontario, Canada is affected by CyanoHABs that similarly plague the coast of Michigan, Ohio, and southern Ontario. Residents of Pelee Island receive their water either through a municipal water supply or though ground water sourced from residential wells. Shore wells and infiltration galleries have their water filtered through sand trenches on the embankments of the island. The residence time within the sand channels is ~7 h (unpublished). Lake water (20 L) and sand (15 kg) were collected in 20-L plastic buckets from the western shoreline of the island in September of 2018 and transported to the University of Windsor. Subsets of both the lake water and sand were sent to the McMaster Institute of Applied Radiation Services (McIARS) in Hamilton, Ontario where they were sterilized by gamma-irradiation (GI). Specifically, buckets were sterilized at 28 kGy for 100 h. All samples were stored at 4 °C at the Great Lakes Institute for Environmental Research (GLIER) until the time of the experiment in June 2019. 2.2. Laboratory column flow-through setup and sample collection 
 The bench-scale microcosm experiment consisted of a simple column flow-through system designed to measure the efficiency and degradation capacity of the microbial community in response to MC-LR exposure. The flow-through system consisted of six polycarbonate columns (L = 20 ± 0.05 cm, ID = 6.6 ± 0.05 cm) filled to a height of 17 ± 0.05 cm with Pelee Island sand. For the purpose of replication, three columns (C1, C2, and C3) contained biotic sand (unmodified), and three columns (C4, C5, and C6) contained GI sand (sterilized) as the negative control. Separate reservoirs containing GI water and biotic water were initially set up to flow through the control and biotic columns, respectively. A Masterflex L/S Variable Speed Pump (Cole-Parmer) was set at its lowest speed, which translated to an approximate residence time of 21 h within the sand column. To prevent phototropism, the experiment was conducted in a fume hood with blackout curtains used to block incoming light. 
 Increasing concentrations of MC-LR were added to the reservoirs every 7 d for a total of 6 weeks. MC-LR (provided by Wayne State University) was supplied as 0.5 mg dry mass and dissolved in 1 mL of MiliQ water for a final stock concentration of 500 μg/mL. The volume of toxin added to each reservoir was calculated so that the final concentration in each reservoir was equal to the intended concentration ( Table 1 ). Reservoir water samples were collected each week after a new concentration was established. Following the initial spike of MC and 24 h of equilibration/incubation, water samples were collected three times a day for 4 d for temporal measurements of degradation kinetics. Influent samples were collected in duplicate using sterile plastic syringes; effluent samples were collected directly from the tubing that dripped into the collection beakers. All samples were stored in 5.55 mL Borosilicate glass vials (VWR) at −20 °C. At the time of analysis, LC/MS/MS (Lumigen Instrument Centre, Wayne State University) was used to determine the concentration of MC congeners in the water as described ( Birbeck et al., 2019 ; Appendix A ). Water chemistry of the influent reservoirs was measured daily to ensure conditions such as pH, dissolved oxygen (DO), and temperature remained consistent throughout the experiment.

 Open in a new tab Sand samples were collected before beginning the experiment from the biotic and GI sand to determine the starting (i.e., in situ ) microbial composition. Each week, except for Week 1, sand was collected from the columns 24 h after the addition of MC-LR to the reservoir water. Samples were collected from the top 2 cm of each column into sterile cryotubes. The tubes were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen to prevent degradation, then stored at −80 °C until nucleic acid extraction. 2.3. Nucleic acid extraction and library preparation 
 Total RNA was extracted from the sand using Qiagen RNeasy Powersoil Total RNA isolation kits following the manufacturers’ instructions with slight modifications. Specifically, the sample weight was increased from 2 to 5 g, and the extractions began with sand still in a semi-frozen state to minimize RNA degradation. RNA precipitation was extended to >12 h at −20 °C to increase yield, and the final pellet was resuspended in 60 μL of RNase-free water to increase concentration. 
 The extracted RNA was immediately converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Applied Biosystems High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Additional library preparations for sequencing of cDNA on the Ion Torrent have been previously described ( Falk et al., 2018 ; VanMensel et al., 2020 ). 2.4. Bioinformatics analysis 
 Metataxonomic analyses of the bacterial community were performed on RNA (cDNA) samples using the QIIME2 pipeline ( Hall and Beiko, 2018 ). Following standard QIIME2 protocols, individual demultiplexed raw sequence files were imported using the fastq manifest format. Standard parameters were used to filter low-quality sequences, and trimming of barcode adapters, linkers, and primer sequences was performed within the DADA2 pipeline within QIIME2. Taxonomy classifications of each amplicon sequence variant (ASV) were assigned within QIIME2 utilizing a pre-trained Naïve-Bayes classifier from the Silva v138 taxonomy database including only those sequences bound by the specific 16 S rRNA gene primers used in this study. The ASV table was exported from QIIME2, at which point diversity metrics (Shannon), relative abundance, and differential abundance analyses were performed within MicrobiomeAnalyst ( Chong et al., 2020 ), a comprehensive web-based analysis tool for amplicon-based and metagenomic datasets. The raw ASV table was rarified at a base cutoff of 3000 reads per sample to normalize unequal sequence depths and normalized using total sum scaling (TSS). Beta diversity was performed using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity metric and plotted using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Differential abundance was performed using the DESeq2 parametric model ( Love et al., 2014 ) with a p-cutoff value of 0.05. 3. Results and discussion 
 3.1. Quantification of influent and effluent water for Microcystin-LR using LC/MS/MS 
 Corresponding influent and effluent water samples were collected temporally over the course of the study to understand the removal efficiency of MC-LR within the biotic and abiotic sand columns. The weekly concentrations of dosed MC-LR used for each microcosm are provided in Table 1 , and the resulting concentrations determined using LC-MS/MS for selected weeks are presented by week in Fig. 2 (see Appendix B for complete dataset). Over the six-week experiment, concentrations of MC-LR were not detected in any of the triplicate biotic columns 21 h after inoculation, indicating complete elimination. This result remained consistent regardless of the increasing concentrations of MC-LR introduced, except for minor detections during Week 1 (21 h) and Week 5 (21, 24 and 52 h), though >99% removal efficiency was still maintained ( Table 1 ).

In the abiotic columns, no change between the effluent and influent concentrations was demonstrated in Week 1 ( Fig. 2a ); this result indicated no physical adsorption of MC-LR to the sand sediment took place. An unexpected onset of degradation activity was observed at Week 2 within the abiotic columns ( Appendix B ), and by Week 3 ( Fig. 2b ) had become very prominent. Though the sand in the abiotic columns and influent water had been sterilized by GI, the results suggest a possible biotic inoculation initiated this degradation activity after the first week. Some radiation-tolerant bacterial species and spore formers can survive in a diverse array of environmental conditions, including exposure to high levels of radiation ( Checinska et al., 2015 ; Lees et al., 2018 ; Setlow, 2006 ; Sinha and Hader, 2002 ). As such, some bacterial species were likely resistant to the radiation and, from a chemical perspective, were able to adapt over the first three weeks to this new, specialized environment. Therefore, the term “abiotic” does not accurately describe the control columns; the GI treatment appears to have eliminated the entire original community except for a few very resilient members that were able to utilize the MC-LR or other GI by-products for their metabolic gain. For the remainder of the experiment, the controls demonstrated a strong ability to degrade MC-LR, though rarely were able to eliminate the full concentration of incoming toxin ( Table 1 ; MC-LR concentrations in Appendix A ). 
 A substantial decrease in the concentration of MC-LR in the biotic water reservoir was observed in Week 3, indicating internal degradation within the reservoir ( Fig. 2b ). Consequently, less toxin was entering C1, C2 and C3, although normalizing input concentrations still resulted in increased degradation in the biotic columns. Several bacterial genera are capable of degrading MC, many of which have been isolated from lake water ( Kormas and Lymperopoulou, 2013 ; Li et al., 2017 ). The lake water was collected during peak algal bloom season (late summer), and planktonic bacteria with the ability to break down MC toxins were likely present. Due to this observed degradation pattern, the biotic water reservoir was eliminated beginning in Week 5 and the influent water for all six columns was instead sourced from a single reservoir of sterilized water. This negated any further biotic degradation within the reservoir itself and ensured equivalent concentration inputs for all six columns thereafter. The sand within the biotic columns and tubing were replaced to eliminate any residual degradation effects from bacteria that may have still been present. The influent concentration was decreased back down to 2.0 μg/L in Week 5 to minimize any adverse effects from immediately introducing ≥10 μg/L MC-LR to the new bacterial community. 3.2. 16 S rRNA gene community diversity and distribution 
 In total, 45 samples were sequenced to an average sequence depth of 17,934 per sample (one failed quality control prior to sequencing). Sequenced samples with fewer than 3000 reads were discarded prior to analysis, resulting in a total of 37 RNA files. 
 A Bray-Curtis β-diversity PCoA revealed a compact cluster among the biotic samples, while the abiotic samples demonstrated greater variation ( Fig. 3a ). The lack of any spatial variation on a weekly basis in the biotic samples clearly indicates the bacterial community from these columns did not change throughout the course of the study ( Gower, 1966 ), even when exposed to high concentrations of MC-LR. The results suggest prior exposures have equipped the community with the functional components required to degrade MC-LR, and to do so with high degree of efficiency.

In contrast, the abiotic samples displayed a clear temporal progression largely corresponding to the 13.1% variation associated along PC 2 ( Fig. 3a ). The final (Week 5 and later) biotic and abiotic communities remained distinct and statistically different (p < 0.05). The chemical data supports this diversity metric, showing the abiotic columns evolved and diversified, resulting in the increasingly effective capability to degrade MC-LR over the duration of the study. 
 The Shannon index ( Fig. 3b ) shows an increasing diversity in the abiotic columns over time while the biotic α-diversity remained fairly consistent. The concomitant increase in diversity with increasing MC-LR concentration suggests community richness is positively correlated with the community’s ability to degrade MC-LR. This could be attributed to direct degradation of MC-LR (directly involved in biodegradation or benefitting from secondary metabolites) and/or a result of species emergence and adaptation to this high MC environment. Although the abiotic community was able to degrade 82–99% of input concentrations in the final two weeks ( Table 1 ; Appendix A ), it was unable to degrade all MC-LR (100% degradation). This ~20% loss of degradation efficiency may be associated with a lack of diversity in the abiotic microbial community. This suggests multiple species may be required for full degradation to occur, especially at high MC-LR concentrations and key features may be absent from a less diverse abiotic community. 
 Contrasting results have been reported regarding the relationship between taxonomic diversity and MC degradation. Some studies have found the most robust MC degradation occurs in culture containing multiple bacterial isolates and therefore suggest two or more bacterial species may be necessary for MC-LR degradation ( Jones and Orr, 1994 ; Thees et al., 2019 ), while others have found that communities become more specified to be better equipped to degrade the toxin ( Li et al., 2016 ; Morón-López et al., 2020 ). The latter studies however, looked at the entire DNA pool as opposed to the active taxa (16s RNA). Our results agree with the former by demonstrating a more complete breakdown of MC-LR as the community diversified. The statistical difference in the microbial compositions of the biotic and abiotic communities provides a unique opportunity to compare the taxonomy of communities that can both degrade MC-LR, but with different efficiencies. Thus, the taxonomic differences between these communities may reveal integral species that greatly contribute to the complete degradation of MC-LR. 3.3. Bacterial taxa identification in biotic and abiotic columns 
 Compositional shifts responding to MC-LR exposure in biotic and abiotic columns at the family level are shown in Fig. 4 . Consistent trends are evident in the abiotic and biotic microbial compositions across each week at the phylum ( Appendix C ) and family level. Specifically, biotic taxa compositions remain relatively stable throughout the study (in agreement with the results from the diversity metric analyses), while the abiotic taxa composition changes from Week 0 to Week 6. The top phyla for the biotic RNA samples consisted of Proteobacteria (44–65%) and Actinobacteria (12–32%). In contrast, top phyla within the abiotic samples included Proteobacteria (6–91%) and Deinococcous-Thermus (5–89%), however prominent growth in the prevalence of Proteobacteria occurred throughout the course of the experiment. The dominance of Proteobacteria, specifically β-, and γ-Proteobacteria in both the abiotic and biotic samples supports other studies that report similar taxonomical composition for MC-degrading communities ( Dziga et al., 2019 ; Krishnan et al., 2018 ; Li et al., 2016 ; Mou et al., 2013 ) and suggests species within Proteobacteria meaningfully contribute to the degradation process ( Dziga et al., 2013 ).

At the family level, the prevalence of Burkholderiaceae, belonging to β- Proteobacteria, remained dominant in the biotic community throughout the duration of the experiment, with an abundance ranging from 27 to 41%. The same family exhibited pronounced growth in the abiotic columns, from a low of 2% to values as high as 84% in latter weeks ( Fig. 4 ). The concomitant increase in abundance of this family with increasing MC-LR concentration strongly suggests species within Burkholderiaceae are involved in the degradation pathway. The dominance of Burkholderiaceae is also consistent with other studies performed in the natural environment and various biological filters and have been suggested as important MC degraders in Lake Erie ( Krishnan et al., 2018 ; Morón-López et al., 2020 ; Mou et al., 2013 ). 
 Differential abundance analyses between the “stable” Weeks 5–6 was performed to identify statistically differing taxa during the most efficient degradation periods ( Table 2 ). The phylum Actinobacteria was statistically more abundant (p = 3.84 × 10 -−20 ) within the biotic treatment, ranging from 12 to 32%, whereas the initial abundance of Actinobacteria in the abiotic community was 2% and by Week 6 was undetectable. The identification of several MC-degrading species from this phylum and its association with cyanobacterial species in recent years suggests its members may be important for complete degradation to occur ( Dziallas and Grossart, 2011 ; Dziga et al., 2019 ). Increased abundance of Actinobacteria in response to a bloom event was also observed during an in situ field experimental survey conducted in 2017 and performed within a drinking well trench on Pelee Island (unpublished; photos provided in Supplementary Data ). During this experiment, samples were collected before and during a CyanoHAB (late summer) that produced measurable concentrations of MC-LR within the well trench gradient. Thus, corroborating evidence from that in situ study and this microcosm study strongly supports the involvement (be it primary or secondary) of Actinobacteria. However, the role of Actinobacteria in recent studies has had contrasting results. For example, a study by Li et al. (2016) found the abundance of Actinobacteria decreased after biodegradation commenced, suggesting members of this phylum were outcompeted by Proteobacteria. Additionally, Mou et al. (2013) found significantly fewer Actinobacteria in MC metagenomes, suggesting this taxon was insignificant in MC degradation in the water samples examined in Lake Erie.

 Open in a new tab In our results, the abundance of Actinobacteria did not increase in the abiotic community, suggesting either members of this phylum are not involved in biodegradation in Lake Erie sand or that a critical taxa member was removed/depleted during the GI treatment and was not able to recover. Given that Actinobacteria was one of the most abundant phyla in the biotic samples, our results support the latter. Among this phylum, Illumatobacteraceae (p = 2.52 × 10 −8 ) was the most differentially abundant family in the biotic columns compared to the abiotic columns due to its presence in the former and almost complete absence in the latter. While little is known about this family, its potential involvement in the degradation process should be considered. The diversity of members within Actinobacteria, combined with the fact other members within this phylum have MC degrading species, makes Illumato-bacteracea a potentially integral contributor to the degradation of MC-LR. 
 Among Proteobacteria, Pseudomonadaceae (γ-Proteobacteria) was statistically more abundant (p = 1.13 × 10 −3 ) in the abiotic samples; however, its presence in this treatment group emerged concurrent with the onset of degradation capability and was consistently abundant in the biotic samples. Members of this family have been reported to be important taxa in biodegradation of MC-LR due to their ability to degrade recalcitrant aromatic compounds (including one species being a known MC-LR degrader) as well as their reported increase in abundance during MC-LR exposure in several studies ( Guerin and Boyd, 1995 ; Krishnan et al., 2018 ; Morono et al., 2004 ; Takenaka and Watanabe, 1997 ; Yang et al., 2014 ). Rhodocyclaceae (β-Proteobacteria, now also a member of γ-Proteobacteria due to recent database revisions) was similar to Pseudomonadaceae in its abundance in biotic samples and its emergence in the abiotic community, although was not determined to be differentially abundant (p > 0.05). However, its members show broad metabolic capabilities and are able to utilize a number of organic acids, alcohols and aromatic compounds ( Smalley et al., 2015 ). Further research is required to confirm the identities of members involved in the functional metabolic pathways that are active; however, the results from this experiment demonstrate the potential for Illumatobacteraceae, Pseudomonadaceae, Rhodocy-claceae, and, in particular, Burkholderiaceae to be involved in the degradation of MC-LR in Lake Erie sand. 
 The differential abundance of Nitrosomonadaceae (β-Proteobacteria) (p = 7.67 × 10 −4 ) in the biotic community is notable and suggests a possible link to secondary chemical cycling of nitrogen. Nitrosomonadaceae are comprised of lithoautotrophic ammonia oxidizers which play major roles in the control of the nitrogen cycle in terrestrial, freshwater, and marine environments and in waste-water treatment processes ( Caffrey et al., 2007 ). When we consider the sequential degradation of MC-LR controlled by the mlr gene operon, the first two cleavage sites occur at peptide bonds ( Dziga et al., 2013 ). Cooperative metabolic strategies of the bacterial community may target and cleave these peptide bonds not only for initiating MC-LR degradation, but also providing an energy gain of mutualistic community benefit. Thus, the susceptibility of peptide bonds to cleavage may be an advantageous means to gain access to the amine groups which could then be exploited by other species for their metabolic gain. As a result, the presence Nitrosomonadaceae may suggest a secondary role of nitrogen in the complete degradation of MC-LR. 
 Sphingomonadaceae (p > 0.05) was detected in both treatment groups (0–1.1% in biotic, 0–1.4% in abiotic), however its low abundance indicates the Sphingomonadaceae species present were not integral in the degradation process. Still, the presence of the mlr gene pathway in either community cannot be ruled out. Although amplification of mlr genes was not performed in this experiment, we note other studies on Lake Erie have been unable to amplify mlr genes from MC-degrading bacteria ( Mou et al., 2013 ; Thees et al., 2019 ); this suggests that either existing primers may be inefficient for broad identification of mlrA in non-Sphingomonas taxa ( Hu et al., 2009 ) or MC degradation genes and/or pathways may vary among bacterial taxa ( Manage et al., 2009 ; Mou et al., 2013 ). Future studies using metatranscriptomics (shotgun gene expression analysis) could determine which genes belonging to which species are actively expressed during MC-LR degradation. 4. Conclusion 
 This study demonstrates the natural bacterial community living within Pelee Island sands is highly effective at degrading MC-LR. Evidence of this natural, microbially-mediated degradation is an important step forward in our understanding of how to control the persistent and reoccurring CyanoHABs within Lake Erie that threaten the health of many organisms including humans. Results from this experiment demonstrate a clear increase in abundance of Burkholderiaceae (β-Proteobacteria), which strongly supports evidence of its involvement in the MC degradation pathway. Additionally, the observed emergence of ammonia oxidizing bacteria in later weeks may reveal their relevance in the degradation of MC-LR through the cycling of amine groups. The greater species diversity in the biotic columns, paired with the more complete degradation of all traces of MC-LR with great efficiency compared to the abiotic columns, implies that degradation capability is positively correlated with community diversity. Thus, we propose critical MC-degrading species may be required within the broader community structure to allow complete elimination of the toxin, especially at extreme concentrations (15.4 μg/L) not observed in natural environments. This information significantly advances current understanding of the complexity of the MC biodegradation pathway present in Lake Erie. Future studies can use these results as a resource to identify potential species involved in this pathway. Supplementary Material
