Measurement‑Driven Low‑Label Adaptation for Signal‑Based Areal 
Surface Roughness Prediction After Tool Replacement in Ti–6Al–4V 
Milling

Abstract
In precision milling, areal surface roughness ( Sa ) is commonly verified through offline surface topography measurement. 
However, tool replacement can change process signals and reduce the reliability of signal-based Sa prediction. This study 
formulates Sa prediction in Ti–6Al–4V milling as a measurement-driven low-label target-tool adaptation problem. Three 
tools were machined under fixed cutting parameters, and each steady-state pass was paired with one confocal white-light 
Sa measurement. Force and vibration signals were organized using a one-pass–one-sample strategy to avoid overlapping 
window sample inflation. Seventy-two statistical descriptors were extracted to describe amplitude, energy, fluctuation, dis-
tribution, and spectral response characteristics. Descriptor-based regressors and a hierarchical signal statistical fusion model 
were evaluated using the same leave-one-tool-out (LOTO) protocol. Unlabeled target-tool descriptors were used for per-tool 
input normalization, and limited target-tool Sa labels were used only for affine output calibration. Raw cross-tool predic-
tion remained unreliable for all models, with negative average R2 values. After calibration, random forest achieved the best 
numerically calibrated performance in the present controlled setting, with average RMSE/ R2 values of 0.0925 µm/0.4658, 
0.0956 µm/0.3331, and 0.0973 µm/0.2908 under pass-uniform calibration, layer-all-pass calibration, and layer-one-pass 
calibration, respectively. Under the present controlled three-tool LOTO setting, the results indicate that reliable statistical 
descriptors, unsupervised input alignment, and few-shot affine correction provide a practical route for improving Sa predic-
tion after tool replacement.

despite fixed cutting parameters.

ured Sa values.
3. Few-shot affine calibration improves cross-tool Sa pre-

1 Introduction
Surface topography metrology plays an essential role in 
precision manufacturing because the measured surface state 
directly affects friction, wear, fatigue resistance, sealing per-
formance, and assembly contact quality [1 , 2]. Areal sur -
face roughness ( Sa ), provides a three-dimensional descrip-
tion of machined surface texture, making it more suitable

than a single profile-based index for evaluating the quality 
of milled surfaces. Ultimately, the cutting process is highly 
nonlinear and unstable due to milling of Ti–6Al–4V, low 
thermal conductivity, high chemical activity, and severe 
thermomechanical coupling [3 ]. Progressive tool wear 
further changes the tool–chip–workpiece contact state and 
cutting dynamics, which may alter the machined surface 
topography and the process signals acquired during cutting. 
Consequently, reliable Sa prediction after tool replacement is 
important for surface-quality consistency and measurement-
driven process control.
In practical machining, Sa is usually obtained through 
offline contact or optical surface topography measurement. 
These measurement methods provide reliable roughness 
values, but they require additional measurement planning, 
machine stoppage, and dedicated instruments. For complex 
surfaces or high-throughput production, measuring every 
machined pass at a sufficiently high frequency can be dif-
ficult. Therefore, signal-based prediction provides a useful 
complement to surface metrology: force and vibration sig-
nals collected during cutting can be used to estimate the 
measured Sa value and support timely quality assessment 
before full offline inspection is completed [4]. In this sense, 
the prediction task is not merely a data regression problem 
but also a measurement-driven process-quality estimation 
problem.
Surface roughness prediction methods can be broadly 
divided into physics-based modeling, traditional machine 
learning, signal-feature-based modeling, and deep represen-
tation learning. Physics-based models are usually established 
from geometric kinematics, material-removal mechanisms, 
and dynamic cutting responses. Moreover, they provide clear 
physical interpretability. However, their accuracy may be 
affected by idealized assumptions, parameter uncertainty, 
boundary conditions, tool runout, and transient disturbances 
in real machining [5]. Therefore, traditional machine learn-
ing methods have been widely used to learn nonlinear map-
pings from machining variables to roughness. For example, 
Zhou et al. [6] combined genetic optimization with gradient 
boosting regression trees, Chen et al. [7 ] introduced cut-
ting forces and vibrations into a nested artificial neural net-
work (ANN) framework, Lu [8 ] reviewed surface quality 
prediction in terms of process parameter effects, and Wu 
and Lei [9] used vibration signal analysis with an ANN for 
milling roughness prediction. These studies demonstrated 
the feasibility of data-driven roughness prediction. However, 
models based mainly on nominal process parameters or low-
dimensional handcrafted variables may be insufficient for 
describing real-time cutting-state variation [10].
With advances in sensing and data acquisition tech-
nologies, dynamic cutting signals such as force, vibration, 
acoustic emission, and temperature have been further inte-
grated into roughness prediction models. Researchers have 
extracted time-domain, frequency-domain, and time–fre-
quency-domain features to characterize machining-state 
variation more directly  [9 , 11, 12]. These approaches 
improve the representation of cutting dynamics, but their 
performance may still greatly depend on manually designed 
signal processing and feature extraction procedures. When 
cutting conditions, noise patterns, or data distributions 
change, the stability and transferability of handcrafted fea-
tures may deteriorate [13]. To reduce dependence on feature 
engineering, deep learning models have also been introduced 
for roughness prediction from process signals. Convolu-
tional and sequence-aware models have shown potential in 
learning latent representations from acoustic emission or 
vibration signals [14, 15], while long short-term memory 
(LSTM)- and convolutional neural network (CNN)-based 
structures have been used to model temporal signal evolu-
tion in machining-related prediction tasks [16, 17]. How-
ever, increasing model complexity alone does not necessar-
ily guarantee stable prediction when the tool state or data 
distribution changes.
More recently, structural fusion, multi-task learning, 
transfer learning, and wear-aware modeling have been 
explored to improve model usability under complex machin-
ing distributions. Cheng et al. [18] developed a multi-task 
learning model for simultaneous estimation of surface 
quality and tool wear. Wang et al. [12] incorporated tool 
wear information into a stacked autoencoder (SAE)-LSTM-
based online roughness prediction framework, and Cooper 
et al. [ 19] used generative adversarial network (GAN)-
synthesized power signals as process signatures to support 
surface roughness prediction. In the broader field of tool 
state perception and transfer modeling, Qiang et al. [20, 21] 
investigated cross-condition transfer learning for tool health 
monitoring and tool wear prediction, Shi et al. [22] incor -
porated wear information into cutting-force prediction, Ma 
et al. [23] proposed an end-to-end deep learning method for 
tool-wear monitoring, Li et al. [24] introduced a condition-
adaptive framework for multi-condition tool wear prediction, 
and Fan et al. [25] analyzed tool-state-related deformation 
from a mechanics perspective. These studies suggest that 
the tool state is an important source of process variation 
and can reshape the mapping between cutting signals and 
machining responses.
Nevertheless, reliable Sa prediction after tool replacement 
remains insufficiently addressed from a measurement adap-
tation perspective. Many roughness prediction studies have 
implicitly assumed that training and test samples are drawn 
from the same or similar distributions. In real production, 
however, a newly installed tool may produce different force/
vibration amplitudes, spectral responses, and output rough-
ness distributions even when the cutting parameters remain 
unchanged. Consequently, a model learned from source 
tools may suffer from input distribution shifts, output mean

bias, scale mismatches, and goodness-of-fit degradation 
when applied to a target tool [20]. Since repeatedly collect-
ing many surface topography measurements for every new 
tool is usually unrealistic, a more practical strategy is to 
use unlabeled target-tool signal/statistical distributions for 
input-level alignment and only a few measured target-tool 
Sa values for output-level correction [21].
To address this problem, this study constructs a pass-
level Sa prediction framework that links steady-state force/
vibration signals with measured Sa values. Descriptor-based 
regressors, including support vector regression (SVR), ran-
dom forest (RF), Gaussian process regression (GPR), and 
least-squares boosting (LSBoost) are compared with a hier-
archical signal statistical fusion model under identical leave-
one-tool-out (LOTO), per-tool unsupervised normalization, 
and affine-calibration protocols. This fusion model serves 
as a structured comparison baseline instead of a predefined 
optimal solution. This design enables the study to determine 
whether compact statistical descriptors and conventional 
regressors offer a more reliable approach than increasing 
model complexity under the present small-sample, meas -
urement-limited cross-tool setting [20, 21].
This study takes a different approach compared with gen-
eral transfer learning and domain adaptation algorithms. 
Instead of focusing on a generic feature alignment model, 
this work formulates tool replacement as a measurement-
driven, low-label target-tool adaptation problem for pass-
level Sa prediction. The target response is the measured sur-
face topography parameter Sa , and each valid steady-state 
cutting pass is paired with one measured Sa value. Under 
this setting, unlabeled target-tool signal/statistical descrip-
tors are used for input-level alignment, while only a few 
measured target-tool Sa labels are used for output-level affine 
calibration. This design addresses the practical metrology 
constraint that target-tool process signals are easily acquired, 
whereas dense target-tool surface topography measurements 
are costly. Therefore, the contribution of this work lies in the 
measurement-driven problem formulation, pass-level data 
organization, low-label target-tool calibration protocol, and 
systematic comparison of descriptor-based and fusion-based 
predictors under identical LOTO settings.
The main contributions of this work are summarized as 
follows: 
1. A measurement-driven pass-level Sa prediction frame-
work is established for Ti–6Al–4V milling after tool 
replacement. Each steady-state cutting pass is linked to a 
single measured Sa value, providing a direct correspond-
ence between process signals and surface-topography-
derived quality labels.
2. A one-pass–one-sample data organization strategy is 
used to avoid overlapping window sample inflation. The 
proposed organization explicitly distinguishes pass-level 
Sa labels from layer-level tool-life progression and pro-
vides a reproducible basis for evaluating prediction reli-
ability under a tool-to-tool shift.
3. A measurement-driven low-label target-tool adapta-
tion setting is formulated for Sa prediction after tool 
replacement. Unlike generic source-only transfer or 
conventional domain-adaptation settings, the target-tool 
unlabeled descriptors are used only for input-level nor -
malization, while a few measured target-tool Sa values 
are used only for affine output calibration. This design 
reflects practical machining conditions in which full 
target-tool roughness measurement is costly, but limited 
metrology feedback can be obtained.
4. Statistical-descriptor-based regressors and a hierarchical 
signal statistical fusion comparison model are evaluated 
under identical LOTO partitions, normalization strate -
gies, and calibration protocols. The comparison clarifies 
whether compact descriptors and conventional regres-
sors are more suitable than a more complex fusion archi-
tecture under the present controlled three-tool small-
sample setting.
5. Pass-uniform, layer-uniform all-pass, and layer-uniform 
one-pass calibration protocols are systematically com-
pared to clarify differences in label availability across 
target-tool scenarios. These protocols distinguish a con-
trolled full-life calibration upper bound from more con-
servative layer-wise adaptation settings, helping avoid 
overly optimistic conclusions in low-label target-tool 
evaluation.
2 Experiments and Data
2.1 Experimental Setup and Signal Acquisition
End-milling experiments on TC4 (Ti–6Al–4V) were 
conducted using a five-axis CNC machining center 
(Jingdiao JDGR400T). The workpiece size was 
50 mm × 50 mm × 30 mm , and a four-flute shank radius end 
mill (RM-4R-D10.0R1.0) was used. The main tool specifi-
cations and cutting parameters are summarized in Table  1.
During milling, cutting force and vibration signals were 
synchronously acquired. The cutting forces were measured 
using a triaxial dynamometer (ME-Systeme K3D160), pro-
viding the cutting force components Fx , Fy , and F z . The 
vibration signal Vib was collected using an IEPE single-
axis piezoelectric accelerometer [26, 27]. After signal con-
ditioning, all channels were recorded by an MI-7016 data 
acquisition system at a sampling frequency of 5 kHz [28, 
29]. The dynamometer was mounted between the fixture and 
the machine table, while the accelerometer was attached to 
the fixture to capture the dynamic response during cutting.

2.2 Surface Roughness and Tool‑Wear 
Characterization
Surface topography was measured using a laser confocal 
white-light interferometer (Sensofar S neox), and Sa was 
adopted as the pass-level surface-quality label [30, 31]. For 
each machined pass, the measurement region was selected 
from the middle stable cutting area of the corresponding 
milled surface, avoiding the entry edge, exit edge, and 
boundary region. A stitched surface topography region of 
approximately 12.34 mm × 5.21 mm was acquired using a 
50× objective. Before the areal roughness evaluation, the 
acquired topography was processed by tilt correction in the 
SensoVIEW software. The parameter Sa was then evalu-
ated following ISO 25178. No additional task-specific 
S-filter or L-filter cutoff was manually specified beyond 
the standard evaluation settings used in the instrument 
software. For each pass, three repeated surface topography 
measurements were performed, taking their average value 
as the final pass-level Sa label. The average Sa value was 
then aligned with the corresponding pass-level steady-state 
force/vibration signal sample. Although repeated measure -
ments were used to reduce random measurement variation, 
the repeated-measurement uncertainty was not separately

To characterize tool wear, the flank wear land width VB 
was measured using a tool measuring instrument (Omega 
PG1000–400–4K). Because non-uniform wear may occur 
on multi-flute tools, VB was measured for all four cutting 
edges and recorded at the layer level [32]. Since wear was 
measured once for each machined layer, the corresponding 
VB value was shared by the valid passes within each layer. 
In this study, tool replacement and progressive tool wear 
are treated as related but different sources of machining 
state variation. Tool replacement mainly refers to the dis -
tribution shift between different tools, whereas progressive 
tool wear describes the within-tool cutting state evolution 
over the tool’s lifetime. Therefore, the measured VB values 
were used to characterize tool-life progression, verify that 
the dataset spanned a broad wear range, and support the 
physical interpretation of changes in surface morphology. 
However, VB was not used as a supervised input or auxiliary 
prediction target in the final selected prediction model. This 
choice was made because Sa was measured at the pass level, 
whereas VB was measured at the layer level. Directly using 
layer-level VB as a pass-level model input would repeat-
edly assign identical wear values to multiple passes within a 
layer and could introduce an artificial form of repeated layer-
level supervision. Therefore, the modeling task examines 
pass-level Sa prediction using signal statistical descriptors 
and low-label target-tool adaptation.
Representative examples of tool wear and corresponding 
surface topography are shown in Fig.  4. As the tool wear 
progresses from slight to more severe, VB becomes more 
evident, and the surface morphology tends to deteriorate. 
This observation supports the physical relevance of tool-
life progression to surface generation. However, this figure 
is used only to characterize the experimental dataset and 
provide machining context interpretation; the final selected 
prediction model does not use VB or wear stage labels as 
supervised inputs.

3 Method
3.1 Task Definition and Low‑Label Adaptation 
Pipeline
The objective of this study is to predict pass-level Sa from 
force/vibration signals under a tool-replacement-induced 
distribution shift. Since Sa is obtained from offline surface 
topography measurement, the prediction model is expected 
to provide a signal-based estimate of the measured surface 
quality value rather than replace the metrology process 
itself [30, 31]. In practical machining, a model trained on 
source tools may perform poorly when applied directly 
to a newly introduced target tool, even when the cutting 
parameters remain unchanged. This degradation is mainly 
caused by tool-to-tool differences in signal scale, spectral 
response, and output Sa distribution [20]. Therefore, this 
work does not formulate the task as a strict source-only 
generalization. Instead, it examines a low-label target-tool 
adaptation setting for measurement-driven Sa prediction after 
tool replacement [21].
In each LOTO fold, two tools serve as source tools, and 
the remaining tool serves as the unseen target tool. The 
source tools provide labeled pass-level samples for model 
training. The target tool provides unlabeled signal/statistical-
feature distributions for input-level normalization, and only a 
few labeled Sa samples are used for affine output calibration. 
No target-tool Sa labels are used during source model train-
ing, descriptor ranking, or per-tool feature normalization.
The overall pipeline is summarized in Fig. 7 and contains 
five sequential stages. First, experimental measurements 
provide synchronized force/vibration signals and pass-level 
measured Sa labels. Second, steady-state pass-level samples 
are extracted from the multi-channel signals using a one-
pass–one-sample strategy. Third, each valid pass is rep-
resented by all 72 statistical descriptors, layer-level mean 
descriptors, and pass-index information. Fourth, descrip -
tor-based regressors and the hierarchical signal statistical 
fusion comparison model are trained on source tools under 
the LOTO setting, while unlabeled target-tool descriptors 
are used for per-tool input normalization. Fifth, few-shot 
affine calibration is applied using limited target-tool Sa label 
measurements, and the calibrated model is evaluated on the 
remaining target-tool samples.

avoids imposing layer-level wear information as repeated 
pass-level supervision and keeps the prediction task focused 
on pass-level Sa.
3.3 Pass‑Layer Organization and Layer Context 
Descriptors
The dataset has a natural pass-layer structure. A tool con-
tains multiple machined layers, each layer with several pass-
level samples. Although Sa is measured at the pass level, the 
passes within a layer are not completely independent because 
they share similar tool-life states, machining environments, 
and layer-level wear conditions. Therefore, the pass-layer 
organization is explicitly retained in the data representation.

operation does not use measured target-tool Sa labels and 
cannot correct the target-tool output response itself. In other 
words, per-tool normalization can reduce input distribution 
shift, but it cannot remove systematic prediction offset or 
scale error in the predicted Sa . Thus, an additional output-
level calibration step is required.

3.9 Few‑Shot Affine Calibration

put bias and scale mismatch after per-tool unsupervised 
normalization. This outcome arises because input-level 
descriptor alignment only reduces differences in the statisti-

aligned descriptors and the measured Sa response can still 
vary across tools. In the present measurement-driven set-
ting, collecting a dense set of target-tool surface topography 
measurements is costly. However, a few measured target-tool 
Sa values can be obtained for calibration. Therefore, affine 
calibration is used as a simple and interpretable output-level 
correction strategy for low-label target-tool adaptation. The 
affine form corrects the dominant offset and scale errors in 
the transferred prediction without introducing a complex 
target-domain model that may overfit the limited calibration

4 Results and Comparative Analysis
4.1 Evaluation Setting
All models are evaluated using a three-fold LOTO proto-
col. In each fold, one tool is used as the unseen target tool, 
while the other two tools are used for source-tool training 
and validation. The final fold assignment is defined as Fold 1 
targeting C1, Fold 2 targeting C2, and Fold 3 targeting C3. 
This setting is designed to examine tool-to-tool shifts under 
fixed cutting parameters, rather than generalization across 
different machining conditions. Similar cross-domain and 
cross-tool distribution shift issues have also been empha-
sized in recent tool condition monitoring and machining 
transfer studies [43].
The comparison includes conventional machine learn-
ing regressors and the hierarchical signal statistical fusion 
model. For the conventional regressors, the input features 
comprise all 72 available pass-level statistical descriptors, 
the corresponding layer-level mean descriptors, and pass-
index one-hot encoding. The compared regressors include 
SVR-RBF, SVR-Linear, RF, GPR, and LSBoost. For the 
fusion model, the CNN waveform branch, statistical feature 
branch, per-tool unsupervised normalization, masked layer 
aggregation, and mean-of-all-seeds ensemble are used. All 
models are evaluated using the same LOTO partition, per-
tool unsupervised normalization strategy, and affine-calibra-
tion protocols.
Because direct raw cross-tool prediction may suffer from 
systematic output bias and scale mismatch, few-shot affine 
calibration is further evaluated as a low-label target-tool 
correction strategy. This evaluation design is used to test 
whether the proposed affine calibration can correct output-
level mismatch under different amounts and organizations of 
target-tool metrology feedback. Similar few-shot calibration 
ideas have been used in other sensor-based transfer scenarios 
to rapidly correct target-domain response shifts with limited 
labeled samples [44]. Three fixed-budget target-tool calibra-
tion protocols are considered:
• Pass-uniform calibration: Npass = 30 pass samples are 
uniformly selected from the target-tool life for affine cali-
bration, and these calibration samples are excluded from 
the final evaluation set. Because the selected labels are 
distributed over the whole available target-tool life, this 
Nanomanufacturing and Metrology (2026) 9:30 
 Page 15 of 23 30 
protocol should be interpreted as a controlled full-life 
adaptation upper bound. It estimates the potential cali-
brated performance achievable when limited but repre-
sentative target-tool labels are available, rather than the 
performance that can be obtained immediately after a 
new tool is installed.
• Layer-uniform all-pass calibration: Nlayer = 10 target-
tool layers are uniformly selected, and all valid passes in 
these layers are used for calibration. The selected cali-
bration layers are excluded from the final evaluation set. 
This protocol corresponds to a stage-wise inspection sce-
nario, in which several representative layers are measured 
during the target-tool life, and all valid passes in those 
layers are used to fit the affine correction.
• Layer-uniform one-pass calibration: Nlayer = 10 target-
tool layers are uniformly selected, and only one valid 
pass from each selected layer is used for calibration. The 
selected calibration layers are excluded from the final 
evaluation. This protocol is the most conservative among 
the three settings because it uses fewer pass-level rough-
ness labels and reduces the possible optimistic effect 
caused by within-layer pass correlation. Therefore, it is 
used to evaluate sparse low-label target-tool adaptation 
more strictly.
Notably, these three protocols are not intended to represent 
the same deployment condition. Instead, they define com-
plementary calibration scenarios with different levels of tar-
get-tool label availability: pass-uniform calibration provides 
an idealized full-life upper-bound estimate, layer-uniform 
all-pass calibration represents representative stage-wise 
inspection, and layer-uniform one-pass calibration provides 
a stricter sparse label evaluation. In all cases, the samples 
used for affine calibration are excluded from the final evalu-
ation set.
In the following analysis, RF is used for detailed visuali-
zation because it achieves the best numerically calibrated 
performance among the compared models in the present 
controlled LOTO setting. However, this selection should not 
be interpreted as universal RF dominance, because LSBoost 
and the hierarchical signal statistical fusion model also show 
competitive calibrated performance. This setting allows the 
analysis to focus not only on model accuracy but also on the 
relative roles of statistical descriptors, waveform representa-
tion, target-tool calibration, and input-level alignment.

systematic output bias and scale mismatch that can be effec-
tively corrected using a few labeled target-tool samples.
Notably, that pass-uniform calibration is interpreted as a 
controlled full-life adaptation protocol rather than a strictly 
online early-stage deployment setting. Since the calibration 
samples are uniformly selected across the entire target-tool 
life, this protocol models the potential performance when 
limited but representative target-tool labels are available. 
Therefore, stricter layer-wise calibration protocols are 
further analyzed in the following subsection to provide 
a more conservative evaluation of low-label target-tool 
adaptation [44].
4.4 Layer‑Wise Calibration Protocol Comparison
Although pass-uniform calibration provides the best average 
RF performance, it should be considered a controlled full-
life adaptation setting. In practical deployment, calibration 
labels uniformly spanning the entire target-tool life may not 
always be available at the initial stage of a new tool. There-
fore, two layer-wise calibration protocols are further evalu-
ated, and the corresponding prediction-error distributions 
are compared in Fig. 10.
The layer-uniform all-pass protocol selects Nlayer = 10 
target-tool layers uniformly across tool life and uses all valid 
passes in these selected layers for affine calibration. Under 
this setting, the RF model achieves an average RMSE of 
0.0956 µm and an average R2 of 0.3331. The layer-all-pass 
protocol shows slightly weaker performance than the pass-
uniform result of 0.0925 µm and 0.4658, but it still provides 
a substantial improvement over raw cross-tool prediction. 
This comparison indicates that representative layer-level 
calibration samples can effectively correct part of the output 
bias and scale mismatch caused by tool-to-tool shift.
The layer-uniform one-pass protocol is stricter. In this 
setting, only one valid pass from each selected calibration 
layer is used to fit the affine mapping, and these layers are 
excluded from the final evaluation set. This design reduces 
the possibility that within-layer pass correlation leads to

4.5 Discussion and Limitations
The proposed framework should be distinguished from 
conventional transfer learning or domain adaptation meth-
ods. Its main purpose is not to learn a generic domain-
invariant representation but to provide a practical meas-
urement adaptation procedure after tool replacement. In 
this procedure, target-tool unlabeled descriptors are first 
used to reduce input-scale mismatch, and a few measured 
target-tool Sa values are then used to correct output bias 
through affine calibration. This separation between unsu-
pervised input alignment and low-label output correc -
tion is consistent with the practical metrology workflow 
in which cutting signals can be continuously collected, 
whereas surface topography measurements are available 
only for a few target-tool samples.
The results further support the rationale of this two-
stage adaptation design. Per-tool normalization and affine 
calibration address two types of tool-replacement-induced 
mismatch. Per-tool normalization uses unlabeled target-tool 
descriptors to reduce differences in input-level scale and dis-
tribution. Conversely, affine calibration uses a few measured 
target-tool Sa values to correct output-level offset and scale 
mismatch. The negative raw R2 values in Table  3 indicate 
that input-level alignment alone is insufficient for reliable 
target-tool prediction. In contrast, the improved calibrated 
results in Table 3 and Fig. 9 show that a simple affine map-
ping can effectively correct the remaining dominant error 
component. Therefore, the proposed framework is inten-
tionally designed as a lightweight measurement adaptation 
procedure rather than a complex target-domain retraining 
strategy, which would be more prone to overfitting across 
limited target-tool Sa labels.
The results indicate that Sa prediction after tool replace-
ment is mainly limited by target-tool output bias and scale 
mismatch. Although per-tool unsupervised normalization 
reduces input-level descriptor differences, all compared 
models still produce negative average R2 values before affine 
calibration. Therefore, raw source-trained prediction should

A key implication for surface quality metrology is that 
compact statistical descriptors provide a robust represen-
tation of the cutting state under the present small-sample 
setting. In the present controlled three-tool LOTO set-
ting, RF with all 72 descriptors achieves the best numeri-
cally calibrated performance. However, the performance 
margins between RF, LSBoost, and the hierarchical signal 
statistical fusion model are modest under some calibrated 
protocols. For example, under pass-uniform calibration, RF, 
LSBoost, and the fusion model obtain RMSEs of 0.0925 µm, 
0.0952 µm, and 0.0949 µm, respectively. Therefore, this 
result should not be interpreted as universal RF dominance. 
Instead, it suggests that compact statistical descriptors 
provide a reliable and practical route for target-tool adap-
tation, especially when combined with tree-based ensem-
ble regressors and low-label affine calibration. The fusion 
model remains a meaningful structured comparison model. 
However, with the present three-tool small-sample dataset, a 
more complex waveform fusion architecture may not consist-
ently outperform compact descriptor-based regressors [44].
Moreover, the calibration protocol affects the interpre-
tation of the results. Pass-uniform calibration provides the 
best average performance because the selected calibration 
samples span the entire life of the target tool. Therefore, it 
should be considered a controlled full-life adaptation upper 
bound rather than a strict early-stage online deployment 
condition. This result should not be interpreted as the per -
formance achievable immediately after installing a new tool, 
because uniformly distributed labels over the full target-tool 
life are generally unavailable at the beginning of machining. 
The layer-wise protocols provide more conservative evalua-
tions. In particular, the layer-one-pass protocol uses only one 
valid pass from each selected layer, excluding the selected 
calibration layers from final evaluation. This approach 
reduces the influence of within-layer pass correlation [44]. 
Therefore, the three protocols should be considered comple-
mentary evaluation settings: pass-uniform gives an idealized 
upper-bound estimate, layer-all-pass represents representa-
tive stage-wise inspection, and layer-one-pass provides a 
stricter sparse-label assessment.
The role of per-tool normalization should also be clearly 
defined. In this study, per-tool normalization uses only unla-
beled target-tool signal/statistical feature distributions and 
does not use target-tool Sa labels, so it is not label leakage. 
Specifically, the target-tool normalization statistics are esti-
mated using all valid unlabeled passes collected over the 
target-tool life. Therefore, the present raw setting should 
be interpreted as an offline unsupervised input-level align-
ment setting, rather than a strict source-only transfer or 
early-stage online deployment. This setting should be inter-
preted as cross-tool prediction with unsupervised input-level 
alignment, followed by limited-label affine calibration [41, 
44]. This distinction is important for avoiding overly opti-
mistic claims about source-only generalization.
From an industrial-use perspective, the proposed frame-
work can be interpreted as a measurement-assisted adapta-
tion procedure after tool replacement. First, a source predic-
tion model can be trained on historical labeled data from 
previously used tools, where each valid pass-level force/
vibration sample is paired with a measured Sa value. Second, 
after a new target tool is installed, force/vibration signals 
can be collected during normal cutting, and the resulting 
unlabeled target-tool descriptors can be used to estimate 
per-tool normalization statistics for input-level alignment. 
Third, a few target-tool passes, or representative layers, can 
be selected for offline surface topography measurement. 
These limited measured values of target-tool Sa are then used 
to fit the affine calibration parameters. Finally, the calibrated 
model can be applied to the remaining target-tool passes to 
provide signal-based Sa estimates. In this workflow, dense 
roughness measurements of every target-tool pass are unnec-
essary, while limited metrology feedback is still used to cor-
rect target-tool output bias and improve prediction reliability.
Several limitations remain. First, the dataset contains only 
three tools under a single fixed cutting parameter setting. 
This design enables a strict, three-fold LOTO evaluation in 
which each tool is held out once as an unseen target tool, and 
the remaining two tools are used as source tools. Therefore, 
the current dataset is suitable for examining whether low-
label target-tool adaptation is feasible under controlled cut-
ting conditions and for isolating tool-replacement-induced 
shift from cutting-parameter variation [20]. However, three 
tools cannot support broad generalization claims across dif-
ferent machines, tool batches, cutting parameters, materi-
als, or industrial production environments. Consequently, the 
present results should be interpreted as evidence of feasibil-
ity within the current controlled experimental setting rather 
than a complete proof of universal tool-replacement gener -
alization. Future work should include more tools, repeated 
tool replacements, different tool batches, and broader cutting 
conditions to further evaluate the robustness and industrial 
generalizability of the proposed framework.
Second, the calibration protocols still require limited 
target-tool Sa measurements. In particular, pass-uniform 
calibration uses target-tool labels distributed throughout the 
available tool life and may be unavailable at the early stage 
of a new tool life. Therefore, it should be interpreted as a 
controlled full-life adaptation upper-bound protocol rather 
than a strictly online early-stage deployment protocol [44]. 
The layer-all-pass and layer-one-pass protocols offer more 
conservative layer-wise calibration scenarios, but they still 
depend on target-tool surface topography measurements. 
Future work should further investigate early-life calibration 
strategies with smaller target label budgets and more realistic

online measurement schedules. For practical deployment, 
the layer-wise protocols and future early-life calibration 
strategies are more relevant than the pass-uniform upper-
bound protocol because they require fewer, more localized 
target-tool measurements.
Third, the current per-tool normalization uses the avail -
able unlabeled target-tool descriptor distribution in an 
offline adaptation manner. This setting is useful for evaluat-
ing whether unlabeled target-tool signals reduce input-level 
descriptor mismatch, but it should not be interpreted as 
strictly source-only or fully online. Future online deploy -
ment should further investigate rolling or incremental nor -
malization strategies, in which target-tool normalization sta-
tistics are updated progressively as new unlabeled cutting 
signals are collected [41].
Fourth, tool wear is physically related to surface genera-
tion, but the final selected prediction model does not use 
VB or wear stage labels as supervised inputs because Sa is 
measured at the pass level, whereas VB is measured at the 
layer level. Thus, VB should be interpreted as an experi-
mental tool-life characterization variable and a mechanism 
interpretation variable in this study, rather than as an online 
input variable of the final prediction model. This choice also 
reflects the intended deployment scenario, in which force/
vibration signals and limited Sa measurements are more 
readily available for target-tool adaptation than frequent 
direct VB measurements.
Finally, although three repeated surface topography 
measurements were averaged to obtain each pass-level Sa 
label, the repeated-measurement uncertainty was not inde -
pendently quantified as a separate uncertainty component. 
Therefore, the reported prediction errors may still include 
model prediction error and residual surface measurement 
variability. Future work should further quantify the metrol-
ogy uncertainty in repeated Sa measurements and separate 
it from model prediction error.
5 Conclusions
This study investigated measurement-driven low-label 
target-tool adaptation for signal-based Sa prediction in 
Ti–6Al–4V milling. A one-pass–one-sample dataset was 
constructed from steady-state force/vibration signals, and 
each valid pass was associated with one measured Sa value 
obtained from surface topography measurement [30, 31]. 
Seventy-two statistical descriptors were retained to charac-
terize the cutting state. Descriptor-based regressors and a 
hierarchical signal statistical fusion model were compared 
under identical three-fold LOTO protocols, per-tool unsu-
pervised normalizations, and fixed-budget affine calibration 
protocols [41, 44]. The main conclusions are as follows: 
1. Direct raw cross-tool Sa prediction remains unreliable 
after tool replacement. All compared models produce 
negative average R2 values before affine calibration, indi-
cating that input-level alignment and model representa-
tion alone cannot fully remove target-tool output bias 
and scale mismatch.
2. Few-shot affine calibration substantially improves the 
reliability of target-tool Sa prediction. By fitting a simple 
affine mapping with a limited number of measured tar -
get-tool Sa samples, prediction errors are reduced under 
pass and layer-wise calibration protocols.
3. In the present controlled three-tool LOTO setting, 
RF with all 72 statistical descriptors achieves the 
best numerically calibrated performance. Its average 
RMSE/R2 values are 0.0925 µm/0.4658 under pass-uni-
form calibration, 0.0956 µm/0.3331 under layer-all-pass 
calibration, and 0.0973 µm/0.2908 under layer-one-pass 
calibration. However, the margins over LSBoost and the 
fusion comparison model are modest under some proto-
cols. Therefore, this result should not be interpreted as 
universal RF dominance. Instead, it suggests that com-
pact statistical descriptors offer a reliable and practi-
cal route for low-label target-tool adaptation, especially 
when combined with tree-based ensemble regressors 
and few-shot affine calibration.
4. The hierarchical signal statistical fusion model remains 
competitive but does not consistently outperform RF. 
This comparison suggests that, under the current three-
tool small-sample setting, reliable statistical descriptors 
and affine output correction are more important than 
increasing model complexity.
5. The proposed framework clarifies the boundary between 
strict source-only prediction and practical low-label 
target-tool adaptation. Per-tool normalization should 
be interpreted as unsupervised input-level alignment, 
whereas affine calibration provides output-level correc-
tion using a limited number of measured target-tool Sa 
values.
Overall, the most reliable approach in the present con-
trolled Ti–6Al–4V milling dataset is to combine inform-
ative statistical descriptors, robust descriptor-based 
regression, and few-shot affine calibration. These results 
demonstrate the feasibility of the proposed low-label tar -
get-tool adaptation framework under the current three-tool 
LOTO setting, but further validation with more tools, dif-
ferent tool batches, and broader cutting conditions remains 
necessary before broad industrial generalization can be 
claimed. Future work will expand the dataset to include 
more tools and cutting conditions and further investigate 
early-life calibration strategies for online deployment after 
tool replacement.
