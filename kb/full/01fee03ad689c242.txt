Climate warming can restructure ecological communities because of differential physiological responses of organisms and/or altered species interactions. In eutrophic aquatic ecosystems, temperature elevation may promote cyanobacterial bloom and thus reduce the diversity of eukaryotic phytoplankton as well as other types of plankton. We tested this hypothesis in a mesocosm experiment involving both temperature warming and algal bloom mitigation treatments. Temperature elevation of ~4.5℃ increased the severity of algal bloom, and caused shift of taxonomical composition in both cyanobacteria and eukaryotic phytoplankton. Warming showed a negligible impact on cyanobacterial taxonomical richness, and reduced eukaryotic phytoplanktonic richness. Algal bloom mitigation treatment did not affect taxonomic composition or richness of those two groups of producers, nor showed an interaction effect with temperature elevation. The response of both non-cyanobacterial prokaryotes and non-phytoplanktonic eukaryotes to temperature elevation manifested as taxonomical turnover, not net diversity loss. The planktonic taxa that occurred more frequently under warming identified in our experiment were not phylogenetically clustering; and the proportion of taxa that occurred less frequently under warming was higher in eukaryotic phytoplankton. We also found that planktonic communities from warmed mesocosms showed greater resistance under glyphosate (a widely used herbicide) pollution. Our finding that hotter summers may impair eukaryotic phytoplanktonic diversity regardless of severity of agal bloom highlights the importance of planktonic conservation biology.
