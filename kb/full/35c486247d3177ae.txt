Abstract
Background The present study evaluated the biological parameters (parasitism rate, fertility, emergence rate, adult 
longevity, and offspring sex ratio) of seven generations (F₀, F₁, F₃, F₅, F₇, F₈, and F₁₀) of the parasitoid wasp Telenomus 
busseolae Gahan (Hymenoptera: Scelionidae) reared on eggs of the sugarcane stem borer Sesamia nonagrioides 
Lefèbvre (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) under laboratory conditions.
Results A host colony was established from field-collected S. nonagrioides, and fresh eggs were provided to T. 
busseolae females from generations F₀ (field-collected), F₁, F₃, F₅, F₇, F₈, and F₁₀; 30 female wasps per generation were 
randomly selected for the parameter recording. Significant declines were observed across generations: parasitism rate 
decreased from 85.2% (F₀) to 61.3% (F10), fertility from 42.1 to 25.7 parasitized eggs/female, emergence rate from 92.3 
to 71.8%, female longevity from 9.2 to 5.8 days, representing an a approximately 37% reduction. The offspring sex ratio 
shifted from 75% female (significantly female-biased) to 45% female, which did not differ significantly from a 1:1 ratio.
Conclusions The results demonstrate a gradual decline in the biological quality of T. busseolae under laboratory 
rearing. Consequently, periodic introduction of field populations, optimization of rearing diets, and stringent 
environmental control are recommended to sustain the efficacy of T. busseolae in biological control programs.

 Background
As a cornerstone of industrial agriculture in tropical 
and subtropical regions worldwide, sugarcane ( Saccha-
rum officinarum L. hybrids) fulfills a vital role, supply -
ing approximately 70% of global sugar (Hunsigi 2012). 
The crop’s economic significance extends far beyond 
sugar; it holds remarkable potential for generating a 
diverse portfolio of industrial outputs, including bagasse, 
straw, molasses, filter cake, and ethanol (Paturau 1989). 
Iran’s sugarcane industry is predominantly centered in 
Khuzestan province, a region whose very name (“the land 
of sugar”) reflects its agricultural identity as the country’s 
primary cultivation area (Hamdi 2016).
Sesamia spp. (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), known as the 
sugarcane stem borers, ranks as a primary threat to sug -
arcane cultivation in Khuzestan province, with additional 
impacts on maize and rice (Agah-Manesh et al. 2021). 
Larval damage manifests as “dead heart” in young plants 
and structural compromise or death in mature plants 
through stem boring. Consequently, larval infestations 
cause substantial reductions in sugarcane juice volume 
and quality. The pest exhibits four yearly generations in 
this region. Its nocturnal adult females deposit eggs spe -
cifically behind the leaf sheaths of living sugarcane plants 
(Khanjani 2009).
Chemical control proves ineffective against Sesamia 
nonagrioides Lefèbvre (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) because 
its larvae are protected from chemical exposure by dint 
of feeding within the sugarcane stems (Halabian et al. 
2013). This limitation has led to the adoption of inocu -
lative releases of the egg parasitoid Telenomus busseolae 
Gahan (Hymenoptera: Scelionidae) as the principal con -
trol measure in Khuzestan’s sugarcane agroecosystems 
(Nikpay and Goebel 2016). However, this parasitoid wasp 
does not consistently suppress pest populations below 
damaging levels across a range of sugarcane cultivars 
(Halabian et al. 2013).
Given the vital role of this wasp in controlling the 
insect pest, rearing this parasitoid under laboratory con -
ditions is essential and essential component of the bio -
logical control program. However, the biological quality 
of wasps in successive generations may deteriorate due to 
rearing conditions. Declines in parasitism rate, longev -
ity, fecundity, and optimal sex ratio in later generations 
can reduce field efficacy. Therefore, rigorous assessment 
of T. busseolae quality indicators across generations and 
comparison with wild populations is critical for opti -
mizing biological control strategies. Studies on various 
egg parasitoid wasps, including Telenomus remus Nixon 
(Hymenoptera: Platygastridae) (Pomari-Fernandes et al. 
2015) and Trichogramma brassicae Bezdenko (Hyme -
noptera: Trichogrammatidae) (Ghaemmaghami et al. 
2021a; b.), have shown that rearing conditions signifi -
cantly impact the biological quality and biological control 
efficacy of individuals reared over successive generations. 
Specifically, without rigorous quality assessment and 
the selection of robust isolines, parasitoids frequently 
exhibit reduced parasitism rates, fertility, and optimal sex 
ratios, which directly compromise sustained field efficacy 
against target pests (Chen et al. 2023). Therefore, rigor -
ous assessment of T. busseolae quality indicators across 
generations and comparison with wild populations is 
critical for optimizing biological control strategies.
The present study aimed to assess the impact of suc -
cessive rearing on the biological characteristics and effi -
cacy of biological control by T. busseolae, with the goal of 
applying the findings to the development of the biological 
control of sugarcane stem borers.
Methods
To establish a laboratory colony, S. nonagrioides lar -
vae were collected from infested sugarcane fields in 
the Imam Khomeini (31.801993, 48.767391) and Haft 
Tappeh (32.086303, 48.402481) agro-industrial com -
plexes. Infested stems were identified by desiccated api -
cal buds or larval entry holes containing darkened frass. 
The stems were ten dissected to extract 4th and 5th larval 
instars, thereby reducing the duration of the pupal stage 
before colony establishment. Larvae were reared in cylin -
drical plastic containers (25  cm height × 16  cm diam -
eter) and provided with fresh sugarcane stems under 
controlled laboratory conditions. After pupation, indi -
viduals were sexed based on genital slit morphology and 
isolated in separate cylindrical containers (5 cm height × 
10  cm diameter). For each oviposition cage, 10 pairs of 
newly emerged adults (sexed as pupae) were introduced. 
Mating was confirmed by observation of copulation, and 
only egg masses laid after confirmed mating were used in 
experiments. All developmental stages were maintained 
in a germinator at 28 ± 1  °C, 60 ± 5% relative humid -
ity, and a 14:10 (Light: Dark) photoperiod. Throughout 
the experiments, the same host colony was used as the 
source of eggs. While strict environmental and dietary 
controls were applied to maintain host quality, wild-type 
moths were not periodically introduced. We acknowl -
edge this as a study limitation, as any untracked decrease 
in host colony quality or potential artificial genetic bot -
tleneck effects from the initial field collection could have 
compounded the observed decline in parasitoid biologi -
cal parameters.
The parasitoid, T. busseolae was reared on its natural 
host, S. nonagrioides eggs. The initial culture was estab -
lished from parasitized S. nonagrioides egg masses col -
lected from the sugarcane fields. In the laboratory, host 
eggs (< 24  h old) were exposed to newly emerged para -
sitoid species. For oviposition, S. nonagrioides eggs were 
glued onto 15 × 2 cm strips of radiographic polyester film 
using a 14% honey-water solution and presented to the

parasitoid. The egg-bearing strips and parasitoids were 
maintained in cylindrical glass tubes (17  cm length × 
3  cm diameter), which were sealed with cotton plugs. 
Adult wasps were provided with filter paper strips satu -
rated with a 20% honey-water solution. All tubes were 
maintained in a germinator at 28 ± 1 °C, 60 ± 5% relative 
humidity, and a 16:8 (Light: Dark) photoperiod.
Biological quality assessment of Telenomus busseolae
Quality control parameters of T. busseolae were evalu -
ated across seven laboratory-reared generations (F0, F1, 
F3, F5, F7, F8, and F10), where F0 represented the field-
collected generation. For each generation, 30 randomly 
selected female wasps (mated for 24  h post-emergence) 
were individually housed in glass tubes. Each female was 
provided daily with 30 fresh host eggs (< 24 h old) until 
her death. The number of parasitized eggs was recorded 
daily, and the total parasitism rate was calculated as the 
proportion of eggs parasitized during the first 24  h of 
exposure (to standardize across generations). Fecundity 
was calculated as the total number of eggs parasitized 
over the female’s lifetime.
Statistical analysis
Data normality and homogeneity of variances were 
checked using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respec -
tively. For normally distributed data, one-way ANOVA 
followed by Tukey’s HSD test was applied. For propor -
tional data (emergence rate), data were arcsine-square-
root transformed before ANOVA. Sex ratio data were 
analyzed using a generalized linear model (GLM) with a 
binomial error distribution and logit link function (logis -
tic ANOVA), with generation as the fixed factor, follow -
ing the recommendations of Wilson and Hardy ( 2002). 
While arrhenotoky provides mated females with the 
physiological mechanism to control sex allocation, it is 
the population structure and mating system that primar -
ily drive the evolutionary expectation of female-biased 
sex ratios in parasitoid wasps (Hardy and Boulton 2024); 
therefore, deviations from a 1:1 ratio were interpreted 
within this ecological context. All analyses were per -
formed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, 
USA). Each experiment was repeated 30 times.
Results
Results indicated that the F0 generation (field-collected) 
exhibited the highest parasitism rate (Table  1). A grad -
ual decline in parasitism rate was observed across labo -
ratory-reared generations (F1 to F10). Tukey’s HSD test 
revealed significant differences in parasitism rates among 
generations (df = 6, 203; F = 31.2; P < 0.05). The lowest 
parasitism rate was observed in F10 (61.3%), which was 
28.1% lower than F0 (85.2%).
Female wasp fertility was assessed by counting the 
number of eggs parasitized by each female. There was sig-
nificant difference between wasp fertility in different gen-
erations (df = 6, 203; F = 27.6; P < 0.05) (Table 1). The early 
generations (F0 and F1) exhibited higher mean fertility 
with 42.1 and 39.5 eggs per female, respectively, com -
pared to subsequent generations. Furthermore, a decline 
in fertility was observed across successive generations. 
The lowest fecundity was observed in F10 (25.7 eggs per 
parasitoid), which was 39.0% lower than F0 (42.1 eggs). 
These findings indicated a gradual decline in reproduc -
tive capacity in laboratory-reared generations.
Results indicated a significant gradual decrease in 
the lifespan of female T. busseolae (df = 6, 203; F = 19.4; 
P < 0.001), with mean lifespan decreasing from 9.2 days in 
the F₀ generation to 5.8 days in the F₁₀ generation, repre -
senting a 37.0% reduction. (Table 1).
The emergence rate was calculated by dividing the 
number of parasitized eggs with exit holes relative to the 
total number of parasitized eggs. The emergence rate was 
the highest in F0 generation and decreased across sub -
sequent generations. The difference in emergence rates 
between generations was statistically significant (df = 6, 
203; F = 24.8; P < 0.001). Emergence rate of the parasit -
oid decreased by 22.2% from F0 (92.3%) to F10 (71.8%) 
(Table 1).
Logistic ANOVA (GLM with binomial distribution) 
revealed a significant overall effect of generation on off -
spring sex ratio (Wald χ² = 18.45, df = 6, P = 0.005). Pair-
wise comparisons against the 1:1 expectation showed 
that the sex ratio was significantly female-biased in the 
F0 ( P = 0.006) and F1 ( P = 0.033) generations. From F3 
onward, the offspring sex ratio did not differ signifi -
cantly from 1:1 ( P > 0.05), indicating a progressive loss of 
female-biased allocation in later laboratory generations 
(Table 2).

Laboratory rearing of T. remus has been reported to 
reduce foraging and flight capacity, compromising field 
efficacy in biological control programs (Naranjo-Guevara 
2020). These impairments are consistent with broader 
evidence that mass-reared egg parasitoids frequently 
exhibit reduced parasitism rates, fertility, host searching 
efficiency, and flight activity (de Paula et al. 2023). The 
decline in fecundity and parasitism rate may result from 
multiple interacting factors, including reduced foraging 
ability, inbreeding depression, host quality variation, and 
potential adaptation to laboratory conditions that may 
compromise field-relevant traits.
Fecundity is a critical determinant mass production 
costs and serves as a key quality indicator for parasit -
oids, however, it often declines during prolonged labora -
tory rearing rearing. In this study, T. busseolae exhibited 
a reduction in fecundity over 10 generations, mirroring 
with declines reported in T. embryophagum (Sadat et 
al. 2021) and T. brassicae reared on lepidopteran eggs 
(Ghaemmaghami et al. 2021a). Notably, the biological 
quality of T. busseolae, as reflected by fecundity and para-
sitism rate, declined within only 10 generations, a dete -

other parasitoid wasps such as T. brassicae, which main-
tained quality for over 40 generations under similar rear -

robust isolines of T. remus that require careful selection 
to avoid quality loss (de Lacerda et al. 2023). This sug -
gests heightened sensitivity of T. busseolae to artificial 
rearing conditions and inbreeding, necessitating colony 
restoration through wild-type supplementation.
Our findings align with Naranjo-Guevara et al. ( 2020), 
who demonstrated that prolonged laboratory rearing can 
select for parasitoids with reduced fecundity, aggression,

Discussion
Biological fitness parameters (reproductive output, 
lifespan, emergence rate, and sex ratio) and parasitism 
rates of T. busseolae declined significantly across suc -
cessive generations under laboratory rearing conditions. 
This progressive decline could significantly compromise 
the effectiveness of T. busseolae as a biological control 
against S. nonagrioides.
Preserving the quality of natural enemies across mul -
tiple generations is critical, as prolonged mass rearing 
under stable insectary conditions may reduce their field 
efficacy (Bertin et al. 2017). Large-scale parasitoid pro -
duction for augmentative biological control demands 
significant labor resources while risking deterioration in 
agent quality over successive generations (Parra 2010). 
This degradation manifests as reduced environmental 
tolerance and altered host specificity, ultimately compro -
mising pest management efficacy (Sørensen et al. 2012). 
Consequently, rigorous quality control throughout rear -
ing and deployment progress is essential for biologi -
cal control program success. The observed reduction in 
parasitism rate and fertility can be attributed to multiple 
interacting factors, including adaptation to artificial labo-
ratory conditions, reduced host-searching efficiency, and 
nutritional deficiencies associated with continuous rear -
ing on the same host species (Chen et al. 2023). Recent 
studies on T. remus and Trichogramma spp. have demon-
strated that without rigorous quality assessment and the 
selection of robust isolines, parasitoids frequently exhibit 
reduced parasitism rates and fertility, which directly 
compromise their long-term efficacy under field condi -
tions (de Paula et al. 2023).
Furthermore, the significant decrease in emergence 
rate and female longevity observed in later generations 
indicates a loss of physiological vigor and developmen -
tal stability. This deterioration is often a consequence of 
inbreeding depression and metabolic changes induced 
by the artificial rearing environment (Ghaemmaghami 
et al. 2021). Reduced emergence rates may result from 
increased embryonic mortality due to the expression of 
deleterious recessive alleles, while shortened longevity 
reflects a decline in stress tolerance and overall metabolic

A major concern in the mass-rearing natural enemies 
is inbreeding, definedas the mating between genetically 
related individuals, which triggers inbreeding depression. 
This evolutionary process manifests as reduced offspring 
fitness. In diploid females, inbreeding increases homo -
zygosity, thereby increasing the likelihood of deleterious 
recessive deleterious recessive alleles. In haploid males, 
deleterious alleles are inherently and always expressed, 
collectively compromise survival, reproduction, and 
adaptive traits (Charlesworth and Willis 2009). Recent 
genomic and ecological studies confirm that prolonged 
mass rearing of parasitoid wasps can rapidly reduce 
genetic diversity and host- searching efficiency unless 
these traits are actively monitored and managed (Li et al. 
2024; de Melo et al. 2024). The significant generational 
effect on sex ratio confirmed by logistic ANOVA further 
supports the progressive loss of female-biased allocation 
as a consequence of inbreeding. Although the specific 
molecular mechanism of sex determination in T. bus -
seolae has not been explicitly characterized in the litera -
ture, this rapid deterioration and sex ratio distortion are 
highly consistent with the effects of complementary sex 
determination (CSD). Under CSD, inbreeding increases 
the production of homozygous diploid males, which are 
often sterile or exhibit reduced fitness, thereby imposing 
a severe genetic load on mass-reared colonies (Cook and 
Crozier 1995). In arrhenotokous parasitoids, sex ratio 
manipulation is primarily controlled by females through 
differential fertilization of eggs; however, this ability 
may be disrupted by inbreeding depression (Russell and 
Stouthamer 2010). The male-biased shift observed in T. 
busseolae is likely a consequence of increased homozy -
gosity, which exposes deleterious recessive alleles that 
disproportionately affect female offspring survival or 
impair female reproductive capacity, including sperm 
storage and utilization (Charlesworth and Willis 2009). 
Recent studies further emphasize that such sex ratio dis -
tortions in mass-reared colonies can substantially reduce 
population growth potential and biological control effi -
cacy, underscoring the need for periodic genetic supple -
mentation with wild populations (Li et al. 2023).
Concern about the decline in the female sex ratio is 
another major challenge in using T. busseolae against 
the sugarcane stem borer. As emphasized in biological 
control literature, a shift in the sex ratio towards male-
bias and a reduction in females (the individuals directly 
responsible for parasitizing the host pest) will lead to a 
severe decrease in field efficacy (Russell and Stouthamer 
2010). Offspring sex ratios in parasitoid species are influ -
enced by multiple factors (e.g., parental age, temperature, 
host traits, density), primarily through female-mediated 
control of egg fertilization and differential survival).
Overall, the rapid deterioration of T. busseolae biologi-
cal quality within only 10 generations—a rate dispropor -
tionately faster than in other parasitoid species such as T. 
brassicae, which maintained quality for over 40 genera -
tions under similar rearing conditions (Ghaemmaghami 
et al. 2021a)—suggests a heightened sensitivity of this 
species to artificial rearing and inbreeding. The combined 
declines in parasitism, fertility, emergence, longevity, and 
sex ratio directly threaten the operational viability of T. 
busseolae in augmentative biological control programs. 
To mitigate these effects, rigorous quality control proto -
cols, including the periodic introduction of wild genetic 
material, optimization of rearing diets, and the potential 
use of iso-female line selection, are essential for sustain -
ing the efficacy of this parasitoid against S. nonagrioides 
in sugarcane agroecosystems (Fortes et al. 2023).
Although the arcsine-square-root transformation fol -
lowed by ANOVA provided robust statistical compari -
sons in this study, future research could further enhance 
the analysis of proportional data (e.g., parasitism rate, 
emergence rate) by employing logistic regression (GLM 
with binomial distribution). The models directly account 
for the binomial nature of the response variable and may 
provide a more appropriate framework for analyzing pro-
portion-based outcomes. (Hardy and Smith 2023).
Conclusions
The findings indicate that the biological quality of T. 
busseolae may deteriorate over successive generations 
(F₀–F₁₀) under laboratory rearing conditions. Critical 
parameters, including parasitism rate, fecundity, emer -
gence rate, adult longevity, and female-biased sex ratio, 
appeared to decline progressively, with the F₁₀ genera -
tion exhibiting reductions of 28–39% compared to the 
wild-derived F₀. This relatively rapid degradation (within 
10 generations) suggests that T. busseolae may be par -
ticularly sensitive to prolonged laboratory rearing and 
the inbreeding effects, although direct comparisons to 
other parasitoid species require standardized experimen -
tal protocols. Notably, these findings contrast with pre -
vious studies in which key quality parameters remained 
relatively stable across multiple generations. Recom -
mended interventions include periodic introduction of 
wild genetic material, optimization of rearing diets, and 
and strict control of rearing conditions. These measures 
may be critical for sustaining the operational viability of 
T. busseolae as a biological control agent and for ensuring 
consistent pest suppression in sugarcane agro-systems.
