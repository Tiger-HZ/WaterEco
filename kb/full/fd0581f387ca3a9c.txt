REVIEW ARTICLE
Algal bioactive compounds for next‑generation biopesticides: 
a multi‑target resource for sustainable pest management
Adegoke Isiaka Adetunji1 · Jeff David Ojwach2,3 · Rahul Kumar Goswami1 · Ismail Rawat1 · Faizal Bux1 
Received: 17 March 2026 / Accepted: 9 September 2026 
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract
The rapid growth in global population and the incessant demand for food warrant greater attention to sustainable agricultural 
practices. To ensure adequate food security, protection of crops from exposure to biotic agents (e.g., pathogens and insects) 
is imperative. Over the decades, synthetic pesticides have been used to control pests in agriculture. However, excessive and 
uncontrolled use of these chemical compounds causes serious environmental and health hazards. Therefore, algal biopes-
ticides are used as biological agents to control soil-borne diseases and plant pests. The algae secrete a broad spectrum of 
metabolites, including phenolic compounds, terpenoids, alkaloids, peptides, and others. These bioactive compounds exhibit 
insecticidal, nematocidal, herbicidal, antibacterial, antifungal, and antiviral activities, thereby enhancing crop protection 
and productivity. Algal biopesticides are effective, eco-friendly, biocompatible, sustainable, and biodegradable, with low 
or no toxicity to the environment and beneficial off-target organisms. Thus, the present review focuses on the mechanisms 
of action and biological activities of various bioactive compounds produced from macroalgae and microalgae for exploita-
tion as biopesticides. In addition, it presents the much-needed techno-economic analysis (TEA) and conceptual scenarios 
for cost-effective algal biopesticide production by integrating wastewater-based cultivation systems and designing multiple 
biorefinery approaches. The regulatory pathways for algal biopesticides, as well as some limitations hindering their com-
mercial production, are also discussed.
Keywords Bioactive compounds · Algal biopesticides · Biological activities · Sustainability · Circular bioeconomy
Introduction
The rise in global population growth and the incessant 
demand for food are worrisome, necessitating urgent atten-
tion to sustainable agricultural practices to ensure adequate 
food security (Poveda and Díez-Méndez 2023). Exposure of 
crops to biotic agents (e.g., pests and pathogens) results in 
significant agricultural losses. Consequently, plant diseases 
are recognized as a challenge to achieving global food secu-
rity amid a growing human population (Velásquez et al. 
2018; Mohammad-Razdari et al. 2022). The use of pesti-
cides in agriculture is crucial for preventing, controlling, or 
destroying pathogens, insects, weeds, and other pests that 
affect plant growth (Costa et al. 2019; Ahmad et al. 2024). 
Pesticides are classified based on their sources, chemical 
structures, application methods, hazards, and mechanisms 
of action (Ahamad and Kumar 2023). They exist in both 
synthetic and biological forms. Synthetic pesticides are 
chemical substances used to control pests, with common 
classes including carbamates, organophosphates, synthetic 
pyrethroids, and organochlorines (Ahamad and Kumar 
2023). The extensive and irrational use, overspray, and acci-
dental spillage of these chemical substances have resulted 
in the widespread and persistent presence of pesticides in 
the environment, causing serious genotoxic, carcinogenic, 
and mutagenic effects on aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems 
and human health (Mohanty and Mohanty 2023a). In addi-
tion, chemical pesticides hurt soil biodiversity, reduce soil

Therefore, the development of sustainable, environmentally

Biopesticides are naturally derived substances from liv -
ing organisms (e.g., plants, animals, and microbes) or their 
metabolites that suppress the growth and proliferation of 
plant pathogens and pests (Parmar et al. 2023). Compared 
to synthetic pesticides, these biocontrol agents are effective, 
target-specific, biocompatible, sustainable, and biodegrad -
able, offering minimal environmental toxicity and lower pest 
resistance risks. They effectively function by inhibiting the 
growth, reproduction, or nutrition of phytopathogens or pests 
(Aioub et al. 2024). Additionally, biopesticides inhibit pro-
tein synthesis and disrupt plasma membrane permeability, 
ultimately resulting in cell death (Kumar et al. 2021). The 
worldwide market value of biopesticides was $5.75 billion 
(2022), and this is projected to reach $17.57 billion (2030) 
at a compound annual growth rate of 15% (Akmukhanova 
et al. 2023). However, biopesticides face high production 
costs, short shelf life, and limited field efficacy. Based on 
their sources, biopesticides are divided into microbial biope-
sticides, biochemical biopesticides, and botanical biopes-
ticides. Microbial pesticides are produced from microbes, 
including bacteria, fungi, and algae. However, algae-based 
pesticides are prominent owing to the potential of the photo-
synthetic organisms to produce and accumulate a vast array 
of bioactive metabolites with potent and varying biological 
activities.
Algal biopesticides are biological agents used to control 
pathogens, pests, and soil-borne diseases in plants. Algae 
(including microalgae and macroalgae) secrete a plethora of 
metabolites, including polyphenols, tocopherols, oils, alle-
lochemicals, sesquiterpenes, saponins, fatty acids, polysac-
charides, and nitrogen-rich peptides, which are extracted and 
utilized to produce biopesticides (Sekar et al. 2025). These 
bioactive compounds exhibit insecticidal, nematocidal, her-
bicidal, and antimicrobial activities, thereby enhancing crop 
protection and productivity (Kumar et al. 2021; Mohanty 
and Mohanty 2023b). In addition, the metabolites inactivate 
enzymes, disrupt cytoplasmic membranes, and inhibit pro-
tein synthesis in targeted microbes or pests.
Biopesticide production from microalgae (e.g., Chlo-
rella vulgaris, Chlorella fusca, Chlorella sorokiniana, 
Tetradesmus obliquus, Scenedesmus sp., and Mono-
raphidium sp.), cyanobacteria (including Anabaena sp., 
Nostoc sp., Spirulina platensis, and Tolypothrix sp.), and 
macroalgae (e.g., seaweeds) has been studied for sustain-
able pest management in agriculture (Costa et al. 2019; 
Ferreira et al. 2021; Hernández-Fernández et al. 2021; 
Jokel et al. 2023; Mohanty and Mohanty 2023a & b; 
Sekar et al. 2025). The broad biochemical characteristics 
of algae offer opportunities to discover new, highly bio-
active compounds with a distinctive mode of action 
against pests and phytopathogens (Sekar et al. 2025). 
These organisms are a sustainable resource that can be 
harvested as a feedstock for producing biopesticides. 
However, the requirement for large amounts of nutrients 
and water for the mass cultivation of algae poses a sig-
nificant challenge to the commercial production of algal 
biopesticides.
Algal bioactive compounds are a multi-functional 
and multi-target alternative to conventional pesticides, 
but their transition from promising laboratory agents to 
commercial biopesticides depends on cost optimization, 
regulatory clarity, and large-scale validation. Within 
the algal biorefinery field, numerous techno-economic 
analysis (TEA) studies have been reported for biofuel 
and bioplastic production, CO₂ sequestration, wastewa-
ter treatment, and pigment extraction (Bhatt et al. 2022; 
Figueroa-Torres and Theodoropoulos 2023). However, 
TEA studies focused specifically on biopesticide pro -
duction from algal biomass remain scarce. Integrating 
biological properties and mechanisms with techno-eco-
nomic and regulatory realities is imperative to ascertain 
the commercial viability of algal biopesticides. Thus, 
this review provides insights into the mode of action 
and biological activities of algal bioactive compounds 
for utilization as biopesticides. Additionally, it presents 
the TEA and conceptual scenarios for cost-effective algal 
biopesticide production by integrating wastewater-based 
cultivation systems and multiple biorefinery approaches. 
Furthermore, it discusses the regulatory frameworks for 
algal biopesticides and highlights the limitations that 
hinder their commercial production.
Environmental and health effects 
of synthetic pesticides
Synthetic pesticides are chemical substances that are toxic 
to pests, phytopathogens, animals, humans, and the environ-
ment (Costa et al. 2019). These pesticides inhibit enzyme 
activity, modify the signaling system, and disturb electro-
lytic equilibrium. In addition, they degrade lipophilic mem-
branes and generate free radicals, destroying organisms’ 
DNA, proteins, and tissue (Fang et al. 2020). Excessive and 
uncontrolled use of these compounds results in air, agricul-
tural, and aquatic pollution, as well as food contamination 
(Cech et al. 2023).
Based on comparative toxicity studies, synthetic pes-
ticides exhibit higher acute toxicity (lower lethal dose 
(LD) 50). For instance, Vehedi et al. (2024) assessed 
the comparative toxicity of three insecticides, namely 
malathion, deltamethrin, and fenitrothion, on Blattella 
Environmental Science and Pollution Research 
germanica, an urban pest. Using bioassay tests, after 1 h 
of exposure, malathion had the lowest LD50 of 4.29 ppm, 
making it more potent at lower concentrations. Acute tox-
icity of the biopesticide (neem gold) and two synthetic 
pesticides (permethrin and monocrotophos) was evaluated 
in juvenile Cyprinus carpio Linn at 96 h. The toxicity 
tests gave lethal concentration (LC) 50 values of 75.49, 
72.26, and 56.89 mg/L for neem gold, monocrotophos, 
and permethrin, respectively, suggesting the efficacy and 
toxicity of synthetic pesticides (permethrin followed by 
monocrotophos) (Davoodi and Abdi 2012).
According to a report by the United Nations Environ-
ment Programme (UNEP) and World Health Organiza-
tion (WHO), 3 million people are exposed to pesticide 
poisoning, while 200,000 die globally, particularly in 
low- and middle-income countries (Ahmad et al. 2024; 
Desye et al. 2024). The increased exposure of humans 
to these substances has been linked to acute and chronic 
diseases, including endocrine disruption, neurotoxicity, 
type 2 diabetes, cancers, Parkinson’s Disease, Alzhei-
mer’s disease, infertility, and leukemia (Foucault et al. 
2021; Perrin et al. 2021 ; Rani et al. 2021 ; Frisoni et al. 
2022; Hernández-Mariano et al. 2022; Rafeeinia et al. 
2022; Wang et al. 2024). Lower amounts of synthetic 
pesticides that are applied to crops get to the target organ-
isms, thereby causing greater threats to the sustainability 
of the ecosystems. The pesticide residues destroy natural 
vegetation, deteriorate surface and groundwater quality, 
and kill non-target organisms (e.g., livestock, bees, and 
wildlife) (Ahmad et al. 2024). In addition, these chemi-
cal agents leach into the subsoil and pollute groundwater. 
The incessant use of chemical pesticides permits greater 
resistance to pests, warranting the use of higher doses 
of the substances, which also pose greater toxic effects, 
revival, and epidemics of new pests. The unceasing and 
unrestricted use of synthetic pesticides leads to the accu-
mulation of toxic chemicals in the soil, which harm the 
beneficial organisms (e.g., earthworms) and disrupt soil 
fertility. The contamination of water bodies by chemical 
pesticides poses significant hazards by reducing the dis-
solved oxygen levels required for aquatic organisms (e.g., 
fish) to survive. The quantitative data on the impacts of 
pesticide toxicity are shown in Table  1.
Algal metabolites with pesticidal properties
Algae are a prolific source of a broad spectrum of second-
ary metabolites, which are secreted in response to abiotic 
or biotic stresses within the ecosystems. These metabolites 
exhibit diverse biological activities, making algae an excel-
lent biofactory in agrobiotechnology (Akmukhanova et al. 
2023). The major classes of algae-derived bioactive com -
pounds are elucidated in Table  2 and discussed in detail 
below.
Terpenoids
Terpenoids (also known as isoprenoids) constitute the largest 
class of bioactive natural products, comprising more than 
50,000 identified compounds (Breitmaier 2006; Akmukh-
anova et al. 2023). Based on the number of isoprene units, 
they are classified as hemiterpenoids (1 isoprene unit, C5), 
monoterpenoids (2 isoprene units, C10), sesquiterpenoids (3 
isoprene units, C15), diterpenoids (4 isoprene units, C20), 
sesterterpenoids (5 isoprene units, C25), triterpenoids (6 iso-
prene units, C30), tetraterpenoids (8 isoprene units, C40), 
and polyterpenoids (> 8 isoprene units, > C40). (Abdallah 
and Quax 2017; Singh et al. 2017). Structurally, terpenoids 
may occur as monocyclic, bicyclic, or linear molecules, 
depending on the number and arrangement of their cyclic 
components. These compounds are produced as second-
ary metabolites by numerous algal species, with red algae 
representing particularly prolific terpenoid producers (Wei 
et al. 2019). In algae, terpenoid biosynthesis occurs via the 
methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) and mevalonate (MVA) 
pathways. The MEP pathway occurs in the algal chloroplast 
by utilizing glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and pyruvate for 
the synthesis of isopentenyl diphosphate and dimethylal-
lyl diphosphate, serving as precursors for carotenoids and 
phytol. Conversely, the MVA pathway takes place in the 
cytosol of microalgae. In the presence of acetyl-CoA, it gen-
erates isopentenyl diphosphate, which is then converted to 
dimethylallyl diphosphate. This pathway provides precursors 
for sesquiterpenes and sterols (Bergman et al. 2019; Huang 
et al. 2021). Tetraterpenoids (carotenoids), including astax-
anthin, zeaxanthin, and β-carotene, are especially abundant 
in phototrophic organisms, such as cyanobacteria, where

Alkaloids
Alkaloids are well-known allelochemicals with significant 
pesticidal properties. These bioactive compounds are nitro-
gen-rich heterocyclic molecules and are widely produced 
by dinoflagellates and cyanobacteria (Casanova et al. 2023). 
In algae, alkaloids occur predominantly as indole alkaloids, 
including velvitindolinones, ambigines, hapalindolinones, 
fishambigines, and hapalindoles (Walton and Berry 2016). 
However, the presence of hapalindole alkaloids and tjipana-
zoles contributes to the strong fungicidal activity observed 
in several cyanobacterial taxa (Vasas et al. 2010). Algae-
derived alkaloids are promising natural biocides that induce 
severe oxidative stress in target pests, pathogens, and com-
peting organisms. These compounds excessively produce 
reactive oxygen species (ROS), causing cell structural dam-
age, DNA damage, and inducing programmed cell death 
in target pests (Rezayian et al. 2019; Karakoti et al. 2023). 
Additionally, these bioactive compounds exhibit potent her-
bicidal, insecticidal, and antibacterial activities against pests 
through various mechanisms, including cytotoxicity, induc-
tion of plant defense responses, and neurotoxicity (Asimikis 
et al. 2022; Refaay et al. 2025). However, alkaloids exhibit 
low toxicity and greater selectivity for beneficial organisms 
(Beev et al. 2025). Examples include 12-epi-hapalindole E 
isonitrile (Fischerella sp.), domoic acid (Pseudo-nitzschia 
sp.), calothrixin A (Calothrix sp.), and saxitoxin (diatoms) 
(Doan et al. 2000; Murray et al. 2011; Orr et al. 2013; Brun-
son et al. 2018).
Phenolic compounds
Phenolic compounds are a broad category of compounds 
that contain at least one aromatic ring bonded to one or 
more hydroxyl (OH) groups. These bioactive compounds 
are grouped into polyphenols and phenols, with phenols 
subdivided into phenylpropanoids, flavonoids, and phenolic 
acids (Singh et al. 2017; Gonçalves 2021). The polyphe-
nols are found mostly in seaweeds and exist as bromophe-
nols, mycosporine-like amino acids, and phlorotannins. 
They offer several advantages over plant-derived phenolics, 
including higher antioxidant potency, a broad spectrum of 
biological activities, and high tolerance to environmental 
stress (Cichoński and Chrzanowski 2022). Algal phenolic 
compounds act as potent biopesticides by targeting struc-
tural and biochemical constituents in pests and pathogens, 
including plant defense pathways, metabolic enzymes, and 
cell membranes, as well as exhibiting high genotoxicity and 
cytotoxicity (Pereira et al. 2026). They exhibit strong antiox-
idant, antifungal, and antibacterial properties. Furthermore,

Polyketides
Polyketides derived from algae are highly significant in the 
development of biopesticides due to their diverse chemical 
structures and potent bioactive properties. These compounds 
serve as a defensive mechanism against plant pathogens and 
pests by exhibiting antioxidant, herbicidal, insecticidal, lar-
vicidal, and antimicrobial properties (Kizhakkekalam et al. 
2020; Chakraborty et al. 2022; Beev et al. 2025). They are 
synthesized from carboxylic acid precursors via a series of 
condensation, reduction, and dehydration reactions, cata-
lyzed by polyketide synthases (Casanova et al. 2023). They 
exist as type I, type II, and type III polyketides. They are 
a major class of secondary metabolites in algae, account-
ing for almost 25% of all well-known bioactive compounds 
from marine macroalgae (Sasso et al. 2012). Polyketides 
are characterized by high oxygen content and complex mac-
rolide structure (Baerson and Rimando 2007). These bioac-
tive compounds are usually found in cyanobacteria and toxic 
blooms (Sasso et al. 2012; Demay et al. 2019). A typical 
example is trichophycin A, a linear polyketide derived from 
Trichodesmium (Bertin et al. 2017; McManus et al. 2020). 
Other polyketides include amphidinins (Amphidinium sp.) 
and cryptophycins (Nostoc sp.) with potential antifungal 
and herbicidal activities (Casanova et al. 2023; Wang et al. 
2023).
Peptides
Peptides constitute a major class of algal allelochemicals 
and are produced through two distinct pathways, namely 
non-ribosomal peptide synthesis and ribosomal synthesis, 
followed by post-translational modification (Qoms et al. 
2025). The non-ribosomal peptides are biochemically active 
compounds produced via an enzymatic complex referred to 
as non-ribosomal peptide synthetases, found in cyanobacte-
ria (e.g., Nostoc sp.). Their biosynthesis involves sequential 
enzymatic reactions acting on a diverse set of precursor mol-
ecules (Nikolouli and Mossialos 2012; Akmukhanova et al. 
2023). This group includes microcystins, which are pro-
duced by cyanobacterial genera, such as Anabaena, Micro-
cystis, and Oscillatoria. In contrast, ribosomally synthesized 
peptides, including cyanobactins such as patellamide A and 
tenuecyclamide C, are found in Lyngbya, Trichodesmium 
erythraeum , Prochloron, and Nostoc , and exhibit notable 
antimicrobial activity (Sivonen et al. 2010; Micallef et al. 
2014; Rojas et al. 2020). Additionally, algae-derived pep-
tides possess insecticidal, nematocidal, and repellent proper-
ties (Park et al. 2011; Chanthini et al. 2024). Algal peptides 
exert antioxidant activity through ROS scavenging, inhibi -
tion of lipid peroxidation cascades, and direct metal chela-
tion (Nimalaratne et al. 2015). A peptide from Chlorella 
vulgaris showed antioxidant capacity, found to be 26-fold 
higher (197 ng/mL Trolox equivalent) than Trolox after test-
ing using the oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC) 
test (Sheih et al. 2009). However, the stability of these com-
pounds is a crucial challenge since they are sensitive to pro-
teolytic degradation and environmental factors. This can be 
improved through encapsulation and chemical modifications 
(Pereira et al. 2019).
Extraction of bioactive compounds 
from algae
The extraction of bioactive compounds from algae is impera-
tive for the proper utilization of the secondary metabolites 
for a broad spectrum of applications, including the synthe-
sis of biopesticides. The selection of suitable and optimal 
techniques for extracting bioactive compounds from algae 
is crucial to attain higher concentrations of desired bioactive 
compounds and obtain extracts devoid of toxic substances 
(Pereira et al. 2026). This is achieved using an array of 
techniques, including aqueous, microwave-assisted, ultra-
sound-assisted, enzyme-assisted, and supercritical extrac-
tion (Sekar et al. 2025). Some of the extraction methods are 
discussed below.
Supercritical fluid extraction
The supercritical fluid extraction method is a modern and 
effective technique for extracting lipophilic compounds 
from algae. This technique uses CO₂ at a critical point 
above 30 °C and 7380 kPa as a supercritical fluid owing to 
its low toxicity, chemical stability, cost-effectiveness, and 
 Environmental Science and Pollution Research
nonflammability (Saeed et al. 2024). It is suitable for recov-
ering fatty acids, terpenoids, and other nonpolar compounds. 
Remarkably, Mehariya et al. (2019) used supercritical fluid 
extraction to isolate lutein from Scenedesmus almeriensis. 
In addition, this technique is environmentally benign, highly 
selective, requires low temperatures, generates no solvent 
residues, and maintains the integrity of thermostable mol-
ecules (Uwineza and Waśkiewicz 2020). In comparison to 
conventional extraction methods, supercritical fluid extrac-
tion requires less organic solvent, short extraction times, 
and offers high extract selectivity and compatibility with 
automation. However, the high cost of the equipment, cou-
pled with significant expenses in its industrial application, 
surpasses its technical advantages (Putra et al. 2024). In 
addition, the efficiency of this technique is dependent on 
extraction duration, temperature, pressure, and target com-
pound solubility (Hu et al. 2025).
Enzyme‑assisted extraction
Enzyme-assisted extraction involves the application of 
specific enzymes for the degradation of the algal cell wall, 
thereby enhancing the release of intracellular bioactive 
compounds in the cell. This approach is operated under 
mild conditions, thus preserving the structural integrity of 
the sensitive target compounds. However, further purifica-
tion of the extracts is needed for the removal of residual 
enzymes and impurities (Lubek-Nguyen et al. 2022; Pereira 
et al. 2026). During the extraction process, little energy and 
low temperatures are used, thereby preventing degradation 
of sensitive bioactive compounds. This technique has been 
used for extracting many bioactive compounds, including 
allophycocyanin (Arthrospira platensis), polyphenols (Asco-
phyllum nodosum), carotenoids (Ulva latuca), and phloro-
tannins (Sargassum dupplicatum) from their respective algae 
(Tavanadi et al. 2019; Bio et al. 2020; Costa et al. 2022; 
Periaswamy Sivagnanam et al. 2024). In general, enzyme-
assisted extraction is a green and sustainable technology 
for extracting bioactive compounds for potential industrial 
applications.
Microwave‑assisted extraction
The microwave-assisted extraction process requires micro-
wave heat to enhance the extraction of bioactive compounds 
from algal biomass in the presence of suitable solvents. It 
is operated under mild, non-photolytic conditions that help 
preserve the structural and functional integrity of bioactive 
compounds (Gil-Martin et al. 2022). This technique is useful 
for the recovery of polyphenols and sulfated polysaccharides 
from algae. For instance, bioactive compounds possessing 
insecticidal and larvicidal properties were extracted from 
Gracilaria sp. using a microwave-assisted extraction method 
(Kumar et al. 2019). When compared to traditional extrac-
tion methods, this method is reproducible, high-through-
put, rapid, energy-efficient, requires less solvent, and has a 
favorable temperature gradient to recover diverse bioactive 
compounds from algae. In addition, it offers uniform heat-
ing, efficient cell disruption, high yields of bioactive com-
pounds, and shorter processing times (Maskur et al. 2023; 
Mungwari et al. 2025; Pereira et al. 2026). These character-
istics qualify microwave-assisted extraction as a scalable and 
eco-friendly extraction strategy for the sustainable recovery 
of high-value algal metabolites.
Mechanisms of action of algal bioactive 
compounds
Phototrophic organisms, including microalgae and macroal-
gae, have emerged as promising and environmentally sus-
tainable biocontrol agents in agriculture due to their diverse 
biological activities. Their protective effects are mediated 
through multiple mechanisms, including the direct suppres-
sion of plant pathogen growth, the indirect activation of sys-
temic plant resistance, and the modulation of soil microbial 
communities (Sadvakasova et al. 2025 ) (Table 3) (Fig. 1). 
In direct mechanisms of action, algal bioactive compounds 
function as biopesticides through multiple pathways, includ-
ing the disruption of pest physiological processes (neuro-
toxicity, metabolic inhibition), antimicrobial action via cell 
membrane destruction, herbicidal activity through photo-
synthesis inhibition, and the stimulation of systemic plant 
immunity against pathogens (Shah et al. 2021 ; Akmukh-
anova et al. 2023; López-Arellanes et al. 2025).
In the indirect mode of action, algal bioactive compounds 
elicit systemic resistance in plants, activate defense enzymes, 
and enhance tolerance to biotic stress through pathways, 
including the phenylpropanoid pathway for strengthening 
cell walls, jasmonic acid and salicylic acid signaling, and 
the activation of antioxidant defenses to combat pathogen 
and pest attack (Chanthini et al. 2024 ). Algal metabolites 
suppress plant pathogens by stabilizing the structure and 
diversity of the rhizosphere microbiome and by enhancing 
nutrient cycling processes (Berthon et al. 2021; Shah et al. 
2021). In some instances, algae form symbiotic associations 
with plants, leading to the release of bioactive compounds 
that activate plant defense pathways and promote systemic 
resistance to phytopathogens (Rachidi et al. 2021; Ouaddi 
et al. 2025).
Additionally, allelochemicals produced by microalgae, 
including flavonoids, antioxidants, alkaloids, phenols, pep-
tides, and other metabolites, contribute to indirect protec-
tion by inhibiting the growth of a wide range of phytopatho-
gens and functioning as natural herbicides and insecticides 
(Casanova et al. 2023; Righini et al. 2025). Algae also act 
Environmental Science and Pollution Research 
as biopesticides through the secretion of secondary metabo-
lites that inhibit photosynthetic processes in higher plants 
(Makepa et al. 2025). For example, cyanobacterin, produced 
by certain cyanobacteria, exhibits toxicity toward terrestrial 
angiosperms and aquatic Lemna species and disrupts pho-
tosynthetic electron transport in pea chloroplasts (Glea-
son 1986; Asimakis et al. 2022). Similarly, Fischerellin A, 
derived from Fischerella muscicola, inhibits photosynthetic 
electron transport in the aquatic macrophyte Lemna minor 
and suppresses the growth of several fungal pathogens, 
including Pyricularia oryzae, Uromyces appendiculatus, 
and Phytophthora infestans (Hagmann and Jüttner 1996). 
Therefore, algal biopesticides operate through an integrated 
molecular, microbial, and plant-mediated process, which 
collectively suppresses phytopathogens and enhances plant 
resilience.
Furthermore, some algal metabolites exhibit pesticidal 
effects by acting as potent enzyme inhibitors in plants. This 
is typical of microcystin-LR, nodularin, and oscillamide B 
and C, which are secondary metabolites secreted by cyano-
bacteria and inhibit protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) and protein 
phosphatase 2 A (PP2A). Both PP1 and PP2A are major 
regulatory enzymes that govern cellular processes, including 
gene expression, ion channel activity, and nitrogen and car-
bon metabolism (Toroser and Huber 2000). Anabenopeptins 
from algae inhibit the activity of zinc-containing carboxy -
peptidases A and B, thereby disrupting cell function and 
reproduction (Harada et al. 1995).
Bioactive compounds from algae can hamper the quo -
rum-sensing (QS) system in plant pathogens. For instance, 
lignic acid (Lyngbya mujuscula), doscadenamide A, pitinoic 
acid A (marine cyanobacteria), and honaucins A, B, and 
C (Leptolyngbya crossbyana) have been reported to hinder 
the QS systems of pathogens, such as Vibrio harveyi and 
Escherichia coli (Montaser et al. 2011; Choi et al. 2012). 
QS inhibitors have also been discovered in Chlorella sac-
charophila and Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (Teplitski et al. 
2004; Natrah et al. 2011). The insecticidal properties of

Biological properties of algal bioactive 
compounds
Macroalgae and microalgae represent an expanding frontier 
in biopesticide development due to their capacity to synthe-
size diverse secondary metabolites with potent bioactivities 
(Poveda and Díez-Méndez 2023). Beyond their well-estab-
lished functions as biofertilizers and biostimulants, many 
algal taxa, including cyanobacteria, green microalgae, red 
and brown seaweeds, produce compounds with antifungal, 
antibacterial, antiviral, nematocidal, insecticidal, and her -
bicidal properties (Sithole et al. 2023). These bioactivities 
underpin their emerging role as environmentally benign 
alternatives to synthetic agrochemicals (Gonçalves 2021; 
Bhattacharjee 2022). Many species produce a wide range 
of secondary metabolites with antimicrobial, antifungal, 
and antiviral properties. These compounds can inhibit 
the germination of fungal spores, suppress the growth of 
phytopathogenic bacteria, and reduce the spread of viral 
diseases in crops (Asimakis et al. 2022).
Certain cyanobacteria also produce allelopathic com-
pounds that disrupt pest development and reproduction, 
thereby contributing to natural crop protection (Chaïb et al. 
2021). Overall, the integration of microalgae- and cyano-
bacteria-based biopesticides into pest management pro-
grams offers a sustainable approach to reducing reliance on 
chemical pesticides while enhancing agroecosystem resil-
ience. Incorporating these algae-derived products into crop 
protection frameworks not only minimizes ecotoxicity but 
also supports long-term agricultural sustainability (Verma 
et al. 2024). Recent literature underscores the significant 
promise of algae-based biopesticides as eco-friendly alterna-
tives to synthetic agrochemicals (Gonçalves 2021; Casanova 
et al. 2023; Mohanty and Mohanty 2023a, b; Sharanappa 
et al. 2024; Verma et al. 2024). The biological activities 
of selected algal bioactive compounds are summarized in 
Table 4 and discussed in detail below.

such as Sargassum sp. induce systemic resistance in plants,

phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (Gonçalves 2021). These sys-
temic effects provide a dual antibacterial mechanism: direct 
pathogen inhibition and indirect plant immune priming.
Although algal extracts are often described as broad-
spectrum antibacterial agents, their activity spectrum 
remains incompletely resolved because many studies test 
only a limited number of organisms under varying experi-
mental conditions. Importantly, resistance development 
has rarely been systematically investigated. The presence 
of multiple bioactive compounds within algal extracts, 
targeting membranes, QS pathways, oxidative stress 
responses, and enzymatic systems, may reduce the likeli-
hood of rapid resistance emergence compared to single-
target antibiotics. However, this hypothesis remains largely 
untested, as few studies have conducted serial-exposure 
experiments or evaluated adaptive responses in target path-
ogens. Consequently, the long-term sustainability of algae-
derived antibacterial agents requires further investigation. 
Additionally, in the above discussion, in vitro MIC values 
provide a baseline for antimicrobial potential; the transi-
tion to field-scale biopesticide application faces challenges 
such as photodegradation by ultraviolet (UV) radiation, 
microbial degradation in the soil, and poor rainfastness on 
the plant surface. However, the success of in vivo trials on 
tomato and zucchini suggests that these metabolites can 
maintain efficacy under simulated agricultural conditions 
(Ambrico et al. 2020). However, more large-scale field 
trials need to validate these laboratory outcomes.

agricultural relevance remains less established than their 
in vitro antiviral activity. Their performance in plant sys -

leaf surfaces, resistance to UV degradation and rainfall, and 
compatibility with existing virus-management strategies, 
such as resistant cultivars, vector control, and sanitation. 
Therefore, future studies should compare algal polysaccha-
rides with existing antiviral or virus-management products 
under greenhouse and field conditions.
Antifungal activity
Antifungal properties of algal metabolites have been widely 
recognized against major phytopathogens, including Botrytis 
cinerea, Fusarium oxysporum, and Phytophthora infestans 
(Shah et al. 2021; Bhardwaj et al. 2025). Active compounds 
include phlorotannins and phenolic acids, which disrupt 
fungal membranes; fatty acids and aldehydes, which impair 
mitochondrial function and ergosterol biosynthesis; and 
cyclic peptides from cyanobacteria that inhibit protein syn-
thesis. Algal bioactive compounds exhibit promising anti-
fungal activity in vitro and in controlled environments and 
are increasingly being explored as sustainable alternatives 
to synthetic fungicides (Lu et al. 2021; Shah et al. 2021). 
Phenolics, phlorotannins, and fatty acids from brown and 
red algae also exert fungicidal effects by damaging cell 
membranes, inhibiting spore germination, and interfering 
with ergosterol biosynthesis (Mol and Purushothaman 2020; 
Casanova et al. 2023).
The transition from laboratory potential to agricultural 
utility is supported by consistent performance across vari-
ous application methods. For instance, extracts from marine 
macroalgae and microalgae prepared with solvents such as 
water, methanol, acetone, ethanol, chloroform, ethyl acetate, 
and benzene have demonstrated strong inhibitory effects 
against a wide range of phytopathogenic fungi (O’Keeffe 
et al. 2019; Jönsson et al. 2020). Cyanobacteria, such as 
Nostoc sp., produce metabolites capable of suppressing mul-
tiple fungal pathogens, with ethanolic extracts completely 
inhibiting Sclerotinia sclerotiorum in tomato plants (Esserti 
et al. 2017). The efficacy of these extracts demonstrates that 
bioactive metabolites can maintain functionality despite the 
complex chemical environment of the plant phyllosphere. 
Furthermore, aqueous extracts of brown algae, including 
Cystoseira myriophylloides, Fucus spiralis, and Laminaria 
digitata, demonstrate significant protection of tomato seed-
lings against Verticillium dahliae infection when applied 
as foliar sprays or drenches (Ibraheem et al. 2017). These 
diverse application methods (sprays vs. drenches) highlight 
the ability of algal compounds to operate across different 
agricultural matrices, including soil and leaf surface.
Organic extracts from Lessonia innamomic reduced 
necrotic lesion formation caused by Botrytis cinerea. In 
contrast, red algal extracts (e.g., Gracilaria chilensis) sup-
pressed Phytophthora cinnamomi in a dose-dependent 
manner (Kumar et al. 2008). Brown seaweeds, such as Sar-
gassum latifolium and Padina gymnospora, also exhibit 
significant antifungal activity against Fusarium solani and 
Rhizoctonia solani (Ansari et al. 2012).
The functional success of these extracts is deeply linked 
to their biochemical composition and dual-action mecha-
nisms. Fatty acids, including palmitic acid, linoleic acid, 
and arachidonic acid, often present in high abundance, exert 
direct fungitoxic effects and can inhibit spore germination in 
pathogens such as Phoma tracheiphila (Paulert et al. 2007; 
Sarwar 2015). Beyond direct toxicity, polysaccharides, 
including laminarin, fucoidan, and alginate, also play key 
roles as immunity elicitors. Laminarin from Laminaria digi-
tata stimulates host defense responses and protects grapevine 
and tomato plants against Botrytis cinerea and Plasmopara 
viticola (Paulert et al. 2007; O’Keeffe et al. 2019). Similarly, 
polysaccharides from Anabaena, Ecklonia, and Jania spe-
cies inhibit Botrytis cinerea in strawberries (Jönsson et al. 
2020), while ulvan, a sulfated heteropolysaccharide from 
Ulva sp., reduces the severity of Colletotrichum gloeospori-
oides infection in apple seedlings by enhancing peroxidase 
activity and activating defense pathways (Shah et al. 2021). 
Nevertheless, direct comparison with commercial fungicides 
remains limited, and standardized greenhouse and field trials 
are needed before practical replacement claims can be made.
Recently, microalgae have gained increasing attention due 
to their rich diversity of bioactive secondary metabolites. 
Environmental Science and Pollution Research 
Various microalgal compounds, such as carotenoids, phe-
nolic compounds, and fatty acids, demonstrated significant 
antifungal activity (Eckstein et al. 2024; Lage et al. 2024). 
Research by Senousy et al. (2022) identified two microalgal 
strains from soil and brackish water habitats in Egypt and 
studied their antifungal potential against phytopathogenic 
fungi. Their results showed that among the isolated microal-
gae, Anabaena sp. HSSASE11 (83.94%) showed the highest 
inhibition percentage against three fungal pathogens, B. the-
obromae, F. solani, and P. ultimum, respectively, followed 
by Dunaliella sp. HSSASE13 (81.94%) and Oscillatoria 
nigroviridis HSSASE 15 (63.42%). The authors suggested 
that flavonoids and phenolic compounds in microalgae 
are the major contributors to antifungal activity. A recent 
report by Eckstein et al. (2024) shows that different spe-
cies of the microalga Desmodesmus demonstrate good anti-
fungal activity, with inhibition effectiveness values ranging 
between 1.27 and 2.24. Given the growing trend of using 
microalgae as antifungal agents, Horvath et al. (2023) also 
isolated 280 microalgal strains (including Cyanophyceae, 
Chlorophyceae, Trebouxiophyceae, Klebsormidiophyceae, 
and Zygnematophyceae), and prepared water extracts, which 
were tested against six phytopathogenic fungi and three 
oomycetes. Overall, 45% of the strains inhibited at least 
one pathogen. Cyanobacteria showed the highest activity 
(64%), followed by Chlorophyceae (49%) and Trebouxi-
ophyceae (30%). Nineteen strains exhibited fungicidal or 
oomyceticidal activity, mainly Cyanobacteria, which also 
had a broader spectrum of inhibition. Notably, Trichormis 
variabilis MACC-304 and Tolypothrix tenuis MACC-205 
inhibited six pathogens, while Nostoc linckia MACC-612 
inhibited four. Chlorophyta strains were generally active 
against only 1 to 2 pathogens. In general, these findings 
highlight algae as a rich source of antifungal agents that act 
directly on pathogens or indirectly through the induction of 
plant immunity. Their biochemical diversity, biodegradabil-
ity, and agricultural efficacy position algal extracts as strong 
candidates for next-generation biopesticides. Despite these 
promising findings, many studies remain limited to con-
trolled laboratory or greenhouse conditions. Environmental 
factors such as UV exposure, rainfall, microbial degradation, 
and variability in plant surfaces can significantly influence 
the stability and efficacy of algae-derived compounds in field 
settings. Therefore, further research is required to assess 
formulation stability, delivery mechanisms, and large-scale 
performance under agricultural conditions.
In conclusion, while the majority of existing research 
has been conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, 
these laboratory-derived MIC values provide an essential 
baseline for assessing antimicrobial potential. However, 
the true agricultural efficacy of algal extracts is driven by 
a dual mechanism: direct pathogen toxicity and indirect 
plant immune priming. Transitioning from these laboratory 
findings to large-scale greenhouse and field studies is critical 
for validating real-world performance.
Although large-scale reports remain limited, success-
ful field and greenhouse trials have already demonstrated 
significant disease reduction in crops such as strawberries 
and tomatoes (Gonçalves 2021). These results indicate a 
strong potential for incorporating algal extracts into inte-
grated pest management (IPM) systems. Furthermore, the 
systemic induction of resistance offered by these extracts 
helps overcome common environmental challenges, such 
as UV-induced photodegradation and soil adsorption, that 
typically limit the field performance of traditional, direct-
contact biopesticides. By bridging the gap between in vitro 
data and in vivo application, algal metabolites emerge as 
highly promising candidates for sustainable, real-world agri-
cultural solutions.
Nematocidal activity
Marine and freshwater algae exhibit strong nematocidal 
potential. Phlorotannins, alkaloids, sulfated polysaccharides, 
and fatty acids from species such as Sargassum, Gracilaria, 
Nostoc, and Anabaena variabilis demonstrate nematocidal 
activity against root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne sp.) and 
free-living nematodes (Hamrouni et al. 2025). These algae 
produce metabolites capable of immobilizing or killing 
plant-parasitic nematodes (Table  6). Additionally, microal-
gae, such as Anabaena variabilis and Nostoc sp., enhance

Insecticidal activity
Algae are a rich source of insecticidal and acaricidal com-
pounds, making them valuable candidates for environmen-
tally sustainable pest management. Marine macroalgae and 
microalgae produce diverse bioactive metabolites (Cadar 
et al. 2025; Monroy-García et al. 2025). These include poly-
saccharides, phenolics, proteins, terpenoids, alkaloids, fatty 
acids, and halogenated compounds that act through multiple 
biochemical pathways to suppress arthropod pests (Sithole 
et al. 2023; Tran et al. 2024; Izydorczyk et al. 2025).
Unlike synthetic insecticides, which often rely on single-
site toxicity, algae-derived metabolites exhibit multimodal 
mechanisms, thereby reducing the likelihood of resistance 
development (Poveda and Díez-Méndez 2023; Hamrouni 
et al. 2025). Algae-derived insecticides exhibit activity 
against a range of agricultural pests, including Spodoptera 
litura, Aedes aegypti larvae, aphids, and stored-grain bee-
tles. Compounds responsible for insecticidal effects include 
terpenoids, sterols, peptides, polyphenols, and volatile alde-
hydes (Mol and Purushothaman 2020; Casanova et al. 2023). 
Mechanisms of insecticidal action include disruption of chi-
tin synthesis, neurotoxicity via octopaminergic pathways, 
midgut epithelial disruption leading to larval mortality, and 
inhibition of detoxification enzymes, such as glutathione 
S-transferases (GSTs) and esterases (Ranganathan et al. 
2022; Chen et al. 2025). Extracts from Laurencia sp. and 
Prasiola crispa have shown potent larvicidal activity, while 
cyanobacteria produce cyclic peptides with evidenced toxic-
ity to mosquito larvae (Verma et al. 2024).
Sargassum vulgare and Arthrospira platensis contained 
phenols, notably quercetin, kaempferol, and resorcinol, 
tannins, and alkaloid compounds strongly associated with 
insecticidal activity (Asimakis et al. 2022). Similarly, etha-
nolic extracts of Caulerpa sertularioides, Sargassum hor-
ridum, and Laurencia johnstonii exhibited both insecticidal 
and repellent effects against the Asian citrus psyllid Diapho-
rina citri (Lu et al. 2021; Poveda and Díez-Méndez 2023). 
The efficacy of algal biopesticides is often species- and 
stage-specific. In Ulva lactuca, acetone extracts were par -
ticularly potent against Culex pipiens larvae, while ethanolic 
and chloroform extracts of the same alga suppressed larval 
development of Spodoptera littoralis, and methanolic frac-
tions inhibited pupation and adult emergence (Costa et al. 
2019; Jönsson et al. 2020). Broad-spectrum effectiveness 
has also been reported against major agricultural pests of the 
genera Dysdercus and Spodoptera, where extracts of Padina 
pavonica, Caulerpa scalpelliformis, Sargassum tenerrimum, 
Ulva fasciata , and U. lactuca induced nymphal mortality, 
adult mortality, and reduced fecundity, hatchability, and 
lifespan (Ansari et al. 2012; Sarwar 2015; Ramkissoon et al. 
2017). Remarkably, Sharanappa et al. (2024) investigated the 
insecticidal activity of cyanobacterial extracts against Spo-
doptera frugiperda. The petroleum ether extract of Nostoc 
muscorum had the lowest LC50 value of 155.22 ppm.
Additionally, microalgae and cyanobacteria have demon-
strated valuable arthropod management potential. Extracts 
of Arthrospira sp., Nostoc sp., and other genera possess 
proteinaceous and phenolic compounds known to disrupt 
insect metabolism, feeding behavior, and hormonal regula-
tion (Esserti et al. 2017; Ibraheem et al. 2017; Sharanappa 
et al. 2024). In some cases, algal extracts display activity 
 Environmental Science and Pollution Research
comparable to synthetic insecticides, offering viable replace-
ments within IPM systems (Ahmadi et al. 2015; Ngala et al. 
2016). Long-term interest in the insecticidal properties of 
algae is well documented, with early studies identifying 
toxic effects of algal metabolites on stored-product insects 
and agricultural pests (Morgan and Tarjan 1980; Featonby-
Smith and Van Staden 1983; Radwan et al. 2011, 2012; 
Baloch et al. 2013). Collectively, these findings emphasize 
that algal insecticidal and acaricidal biopesticides offer ver-
satile, multi-target, and environmentally compatible tools 
for sustainable pest management. The chemical diversity, 
low ecological footprint, and demonstrated effectiveness 
across stages of multiple pest species support the integra-
tion of algae-derived insecticides into modern agricultural 
IPM frameworks.
Herbicidal activity
Natural bioherbicides have emerged as an important 
tool in the integrated management of herbaceous weeds, 
which represent a major agricultural challenge due to their 
ability to suppress crop growth and significantly reduce 
yields (Dayan et al. 2009). In this regard, microalgae and 
cyanobacteria provide a rich source of allelochemicals 
and cyanotoxins with potent herbicidal properties. Algal 
metabolites (e.g., cyanotoxins) that exhibit cytotoxicity are 
promising candidates for herbicidal activity (Singh et al. 
2017). Additionally, other cyanobacterial antimetabolites, 
such as 7-deoxysedoheptulose (7dSh), have demonstrated 
herbicidal potential by inhibiting the shikimate pathway 
enzyme dehydroquinate synthase, which is crucial for aro-
matic amino acid biosynthesis in plants (Casanova et al. 
2023). Many cyanotoxins function ecologically as allelo-
chemicals, inhibiting competing macrophytes, algae, and 
microbes in aquatic ecosystems (Berry et al. 2008; Rastogi 
and Sinha 2009). Several of these compounds have demon-
strated strong phytotoxicity toward terrestrial weeds. For 
example, the indole alkaloid norharmane (Fig.  4), isolated 
from Synechocystis aquatilis, inhibits the cyanobacteria 
Microcystis aeruginosa and Oscillatoria limnetica, as 
well as the green algae Chlorella vulgaris and Ulothrix 
sp., and suppresses germination and growth in terrestrial 
weeds such as Avena fatua , Portulaca oleracea , Echino-
chloa crus-galli, Plantago lanceolata, and Amaranthus

Formulation of algal biopesticides
Macroalgae or microalgae cultivation occurs in a liquid 
medium (e.g., water) in the presence of adequate nutrients 
and optimized environmental factors for high biomass pro-
duction. After harvesting from the growth medium, the bio-
mass undergoes further downstream processing (e.g., dry -
ing). The biomass is a rich source of biochemically active 
compounds (such as terpenoids and polyphenols), which 
can be recovered using appropriate extraction techniques 
(e.g., aqueous, solvent, or microwave-assisted extraction) 
to produce biopesticides with varying biological activities 
(Sekar et al. 2025). In the formulation of biopesticides, the 
biocontrol agents should be economical, shelf-stable, user-
friendly, target-specific, and effective in the destruction of 
desired pests (Mishra et al. 2020). A biopesticide is prepared 
by mixing an algal extract component of a known biological 
activity with a carrier and adjuvants (e.g., stabilizers and 
binders) to ensure the survival of the biological agent and 
improve the lifespan and efficiency of the finished product 
(Mishra et al. 2020) (Fig. 5). Biopesticides exist in two major 
types, namely dry and liquid-based formulations (Knowles 
2008). Generally, biopesticides exist as powders, emulsions, 
hydrogels, aqueous suspensions, capsules, or nano-capsules 
(Hernandez-Tenorio et al. 2022). Numerous techniques, such 
as direct application, dilution in water, and seed dressing 
formulations, are employed to deliver biopesticides to crops, 
depending on the type of biocontrol agents.
Techno‑economic feasibility of algae‑based 
biopesticides
TEA is a critical tool used to assess the technical feasibil-
ity and commercial viability of any industrial bioprocess. 
A standard TEA evaluates both the economic perfor -
mance and the cost–benefit profile of a process, enabling 
informed decision-making for scale-up and commerciali-
zation. A typical TEA framework begins by defining sys-
tem boundaries, which generally encompass both upstream 
and downstream processing steps. The input assessment 
covers two major cost components, namely capital expend-
iture (CapEx) and operational expenditure (OpEx). The 
CapEx includes initial investments such as cultivation 
systems (medium, reactors, and infrastructure), harvest-
ing technologies, extraction units, bioproduct develop-
ment facilities, electricity requirements, and solvent usage, 
whereas the OpEx involves the recurring costs associated 
with running the process, including labor, maintenance, 
utilities, and consumables.
The output of a TEA usually provides estimates of 
product cost, net profit, payback period, interest, and 
potential carbon-credit gains. These indicators collec-
tively determine whether the process is commercially 
viable. In many bioprocesses, direct profitability may not 
be achieved in the first cycle; however, consecutive pro-
duction cycles often stabilize costs and may yield profit. 
Additionally, indirect economic benefits such as carbon 
credits or tax incentives can further improve feasibility 
(Vázquez-Romero et al. 2022). Within the algal biorefin-
ery field, numerous TEA studies have been reported for 
biofuel production, bioplastics, CO₂ sequestration, waste-
water treatment, and pigment extraction (Bhatt et al. 2022; 
Figueroa-Torres and Theodoropoulos 2023). However, 
TEA studies focused specifically on biopesticide produc-
tion from microalgal biomass remain scarce, with only a 
few preliminary assessments available.
In this section, we critically examine the limited TEA 
studies on algae-based biopesticide production, highlight 
existing gaps, and emphasize the need for comprehensive 
economic evaluations to support future commercializa-
tion. Further, Duarte et al. (2021) evaluated the potential 
of Asparagopsis armata as a biopesticide for weed con-
trol and conducted an economic analysis. The outcomes 
showed significant biopesticide efficiency against Thel-
lungiella halophila and impaired photosynthesis, carot-
enoid metabolism, and induced oxidative stress. They 
also suggested that using this approach can be aligned 
with the circular bioeconomy framework. However, TEA 
studies for specific biopesticides are lacking, and only 
a few experimental reports exist for biofertilizers. For 
example, Romero-Garcia et al. (2022) conducted a TEA

Advanced techno‑economic analysis approach 
for cost‑effective algal biopesticide production

the integration of wastewater and the design of multiple 
biorefinery approaches. For example, the production of 
biofuel with the addition of biopesticides can be a more 
economical approach compared to a single biorefinery 
system. Recent literature also suggested similar thoughts. 
For example, Mohanty and Mohanty (2023b) proposed 
the dual strategy to produce biofuels and biopesticides

remaining biomass was utilized for biocrude oil produc-

proposed a biorefinery model for commercial possibili-
ties. The results showed that the produced biopesticides 
from algal biomass reduce the growth of Pantoea agglo-
merans, Xanthomonas oryzae, and two phytopathogens. 
Moreover, a 28% biocrude oil yield was reported. The 
economic assessment of this approach suggests possible 
implementation on a commercial scale.

variation in MSP when fluctuated by ± 25%, whereas all 
other variables collectively influenced MSP by 0.2–3%. 
Overall, their TEA outcomes concluded that integrat-
ing wastewater-derived microalgal biomass with TSP 
yields a technically feasible and economically attractive 
biofertilizer pathway, especially when carbon credits, 
waste valorization benefits, and improved nutrient-use 
efficiency are considered, positioning the process as a 
competitive and sustainable alternative to conventional 
phosphate fertilizers (Junior et al. 2025).

improving product diversification or conversion efficiency.

wastewater integration reduces costs, producing only a sin-

for commercial viability.

grated with wastewater systems
Scenario III builds upon scenario II but adopts a multi-

of extracted products generates $923.70 across 10 cycles. 
Even when accounting for variability in biopesticide yield

buffers economic risk by reducing reliance on a single con-
version factor, thereby improving overall process robustness. 
Under this sensitivity range, the total revenue varies between 
$859.01 and $1007.83, indicating that scenario III is com-
paratively less sensitive to yield fluctuations due to the 
contribution of multiple value-added products. This multi-

uncertainties in single-product yields, thereby improving the

this approach still requires approximately 10–20 additional

(iv) Scale-up constraints and process limitations
Despite promising conceptual TEA outcomes, the 
industrial translation of algal biopesticide production sys-
tems faces several critical scale-up challenges. At larger 
cultivation volumes, factors such as light distribution, tem-
perature control, water availability, and nutrient supply 
become increasingly difficult to manage. In addition, self-
shading effects and mass-transfer limitations significantly 
reduce photosynthetic efficiency and biomass productivity 
compared with laboratory-scale systems (Samoraj et al. 
2024). For wastewater-based cultivation (scenarios II and 
III), variability in nutrient composition and the presence 
of inhibitory compounds can further compromise process 
stability and reproducibility.
Downstream processing represents a major bottle-
neck, particularly in multi-product biorefinery systems. 
Sequential extraction of biopesticides, pigments, and anti -
oxidants increases solvent consumption, energy demand, 
and separation complexity, thereby impacting overall pro -
cess economics (Okeke et al. 2022). Moreover, integrated 
biorefinery designs for biopesticides and other intracellu-
lar biomolecules require multistep processing strategies, 
which add operational complexity and cost.
From an application perspective, microalgae-based 
biopesticides have been reported to enhance soil fertility 
and plant productivity by suppressing harmful microorgan-
isms. However, most studies remain limited to laboratory-
scale investigations, and there is a lack of comprehensive 
field-scale validation and long-term agronomic studies. 
In addition, challenges related to high CapEx and OpEx, 
technological limitations, regulatory approvals, and lim-
ited societal awareness further constrain large-scale adop-
tion (Samoraj et al. 2024).
Overall, the integrated TEA (Table  8 and Fig.  6) indi-
cates that economic feasibility improves progressively

from scenario I to scenario III, driven by (i) reduced cul-
tivation costs, (ii) enhanced biomass productivity, and (iii) 
multi-product valorization. However, scale-up and down-
stream processing constraints remain key barriers to indus-
trial deployment. Although scenario III approaches com-
mercial benchmark revenue levels, its practical feasibility 
will depend on overcoming these challenges through opti-
mized cultivation system design, efficient downstream pro-
cessing, and validated pilot-to-field-scale implementation.
Regulatory pathways for algal biopesticides
The development and application of algal biopesticides 
are regulated by intricate legal frameworks established to 
guarantee their efficacy, safety, and minimal environmen-
tal impact (Deeksha et al. 2025). Biopesticides undergo 
a strict, multi-phase regulatory evaluation before com-
mercialization to guarantee safety for humans, non-target 
organisms, and the environment. The registration process 
involves submitting comprehensive dossiers, encompassing 
product identity, efficacy, and toxicology, to major regula-
tory authorities, such as the United States Environmental 
Protection Agency (USEPA) under the Federal Insecticide, 
Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act (FIFRA) or the European 
Commission under Regulation EC 1107/2009. Upon review 
and approval, a certificate is issued, authorizing the product 
for market release. However, gathering the data requirements 
for a dossier is a cumbersome process in the development 
of biopesticides. The specific data requirements that need to 
be satisfied for biopesticide registration include toxicity and 
safety data, efficacy data, environmental impact data, and 
public perception and acceptance data (Kumar 2012; Dar 
et al. 2021; Ahmed et al. 2022; Butu et al. 2022).
The regulatory agencies conduct rigorous scientific 
assessments to ensure products meet safety and efficacy 
standards before entering the market (Arora et al. 2016). 
Additionally, the agencies have distinct legislation and pro-
cesses for approving and monitoring biopesticides (Deek -
sha et al. 2025). The law and policies regulating the use 
and development of biopesticides differ significantly across 
countries, and there is no universal regulatory model to 
streamline their regulation and registration process (Arora 
et al. 2016). These inconsistencies pose critical challenges 
to the development pipeline, regulatory approval processes, 
and large-scale commercialization of algae-based biopesti-
cides in global agriculture (Varghese et al. 2026). Remark -
ably, in the USA, the EPA requires less data for registra -
tion when compared to chemical pesticides (USEPA 2010). 
Under Regulation (EC) No. 1107/2009, the European Union 
(EU) biopesticide registration process is considered highly 
challenging, often perceived as more rigorous than in other 
jurisdictions. This is largely because biopesticides must 
fulfill comprehensive data requirements, including assess-
ments of efficacy, environmental impact, and toxicology, like 
those for conventional chemical pesticides (Hauschild 2012).
Limitations in the application of algal 
biopesticides in agriculture
Algal biotechnology provides an efficient means of control-
ling plant pathogens and pests by producing bioactive com-
pounds through the cultivation of macroalgae and microal-
gae. Nevertheless, the global acceptance of this technology 
for commercialization remains a challenge due to some 
techno-economic and environmental factors, highlighted 
below.
 (i) The cultivation and harvesting of algae require a large 
amount of water and synthetic nutrients, posing a sig-
nificant financial burden for large-scale biopesticide 
production. Therefore, the development of innova -
tive cultivation and harvesting systems is crucial for 
enhanced biomass and biopesticide productivity at 
lower cost. Integration of wastewater (medium) can 
reduce cultivation costs, but the social acceptance of 
using wastewater-grown algae for bioproduct devel-
opment still faces a major challenge.
 (ii) The extraction of bioactive compounds from algae 
for the synthesis of biopesticides requires expensive 
technologies and a large volume of organic solvents, 
resulting in higher costs of the obtained biopesti-
cides. Consequently, the development of efficient, 
eco-friendly, and economical techniques for extract-
ing bioactive compounds will pave the way for sus-
tainable and cost-effective biopesticide production.
 (iii) Algal extracts may contain metabolites that are haz-
ardous to some crop species. As a result, the safety 
and effectiveness of algal biopesticides are crucial to 
avoid the accidental spread of harmful compounds. 
Therefore, adoption of a metagenomic approach for 
the selection of suitable target phytopathogens or 
pests and algal strains with selective antimicrobial 
or pesticidal activity is imperative, as this technique 
provides information on the composition and func -
tional activity of phototrophic communities as well 
as their potential ecosystem threats.
 (iv) Algal biopesticides are unstable due to changes in 
environmental factors, such as humidity and temper-
ature, thereby compromising the effectiveness and 
stability of the biocontrol agents under field condi-
tions.
 (v) The slow action and inconsistent performance of the 
algal biopesticide discourage farmers and make their 
Environmental Science and Pollution Research 
field application undesirable when fast pest control is 
required.
 (vi) The high specificity of algal biopesticides to patho-
gens and pests will allow multiple use of different 
biopesticides for the concurrent control of a vast 
array of pests, thus making the process capital-inten-
sive and laborious.
 (vii) While algal biopesticides exhibit multi-target bio-
activity, challenges persist in scaling, formulation, 
and field performance. Variability in metabolite 
profiles due to culture conditions and extraction 
methods hampers reproducibility. Developing a user-
friendly product requires suitable carrier materials 
to ensure stability. However, finding compatible and 
low-cost ingredients that do not deactivate the algae 
is challenging. In addition, the specific bioactive 
compounds responsible for pesticidal activity are 
sometimes not fully identified, making it difficult 
to standardize the product for consistent quality and 
efficacy. Emerging formulation techniques such as 
microencapsulation and polymer-based delivery sys-
tems improve compound stability. The use of marker 
compounds, metabolic profiling, and advanced, con-
sistent extraction methods is crucial to ensure effec-
tive product standardization and quality.
 (viii) The lack of specialized, harmonized guidelines for 
algae-based products and stringent safety and envi-
ronmental impact assessments, coupled with high 
variability in efficacy and complex, expensive reg-
istration processes, affects the regulation of algal 
biopesticides in the agricultural sector. Addressing 
these challenges requires clearer, tailored regulatory 
pathways that recognize the unique biological prop-
erties of algae while balancing safety with the urgent 
need for sustainable pest control solutions.
 (ix) Algal biopesticides encounter significant storage sta-
bility challenges, primarily due to the rapid degrada-
tion of bioactive compounds, induced by heat, light, 
oxygen, and moisture, leading to short shelf-lives 
and reduced field efficacy. These can be mitigated 
through advanced formulations and preservation 
techniques, including encapsulation and coating, the 
addition of stabilizing agents, lyophilization, spray 
drying, and wet storage.
Conclusions and future perspectives
Macroalgae and microalgae are rich sources of a wide 
range of bioactive compounds (e.g., terpenoids, alka-
loids, polysaccharides, phenolics, and peptides) that 
play key roles in crop protection and productivity. These 
biochemically active compounds exhibit varying degrees 
of biological activities, including antibacterial, antivi-
ral, antifungal, nematocidal, and herbicidal activities, 
through multiple mechanisms. Algal bioactive com-
pounds are eco-friendly, effective, biodegradable, and 
sustainable biopesticides with a lower risk of pest resist-
ance. Compared with established commercial biopesti-
cides such as Bacillus thuringiensis and spinosad, algal-
derived biopesticides currently face major barriers, 
including limited field-efficacy data, sparse regulatory 
precedent, variable extract composition, and uncertainty 
regarding scale-up and formulation stability. Therefore, 
their most realistic short-term role may not be to replace 
established products directly, but to complement them 
within IPM. Algal products may be particularly valu-
able where multifunctionality is advantageous, includ -
ing simultaneous pathogen suppression, plant immune 
priming, stress tolerance enhancement, and resistance-
management strategies. The following future opportuni-
ties can be explored for efficient and sustainable algal 
biopesticide production.
 (i) Further studies should delve into improving the effi-
cacy and stability, as well as easing the regulatory 
burdens and economic feasibility of algal biopesti-
cides.
 (ii) Substantial field trials are imperative to ascertain the 
efficiency of algal biopesticides on different crops 
under varying environmental conditions, as well as 
provide adequate application techniques, formula-
tions, and dosages for maximum benefits of the bio-
control agents.
 (iii) Genetic engineering, including clustered regularly 
interspaced short-palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/Cas 
nuclease system, can be adopted for improving the 
production and potency of a particular algal bioactive 
compound against a specific phytopathogen.
 (iv) Nano-formulation of algal bioactive compounds rep-
resents a transformative research area for the future 
of sustainable agriculture, enhancing the stability, 
solubility, and targeted delivery of natural biope-
sticides while reducing the required dosage. Algal 
metabolites can be encapsulated in nano-carriers or 
green-synthesized as metallic nanoparticles, effec-
tively tackling pests with minimal environmental 
impacts.
 (v) The synergistic effects of a cocktail of different algal 
extracts could be explored for the development of 
efficacious and environmentally safe biopesticides.
 (vi) Government policies and collaboration among 
researchers, industry stakeholders, farmers, and 
academia can expedite the development, adoption, 
and integration of algal biopesticides into modern 
agricultural practices.

 (vii) Future research should focus on genomic and metab-
olomic profiling to identify biosynthetic pathways 
and optimize algal biopesticide production.
 (viii) Further TEA and LCA analyses are needed to justify 
the economic and environmental feasibility of algal 
biopesticides.
