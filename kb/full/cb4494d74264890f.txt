Article in Press
A Reference Dataset of 66 High-
Quality Plastid Genomes across 
Three Algal Phyla
Jieqiong Wang, Kexin Liu, Xiaolei Wang, Zhengjie Yu, Chaoyong Wu, Shenglong Kan 
& Wei Zhang ✉
Algae represent a diverse group of photosynthetic organisms, and their plastid genomes provide 
valuable molecular resources for investigating plastid evolution, photosynthetic adaptation, and 
phylogenetic relationships. However, algal plastomes exhibit remarkable evolutionary diversity, with 
substantial variation in genome structure and organization across different lineages. Currently, high-
quality algal plastid genomes and a standardized and reliable workflow for generating high-quality 
algal plastid genomes is still lacking. To address these challenges, we successfully assembled 66 high-
quality, complete algal plastid genomes, including 7 green, 19 brown, and 40 red algal genomes. 
During this process, we developed and established a standardized workflow for plastid genome 
assembly and quality assessment. Notably, these newly assembled genomes enrich existing NCBI 
resources by contributing 29 species, 13 genera, four families, and one order for the first time, offering 
both expanded taxonomic coverage and a standardized foundation for subsequent comparative and 
evolutionary analyses.
Background & Summary
Algae represent a major component of global primary production and play fundamental roles in marine carbon 
cycling and ecosystem functioning1. Cyanobacteria are considered the common ancestor of extant green plant 
plastids, having formed distinct plastid structures in green and red algae through primary endosymbiosis 2. In 
contrast, brown algae underwent more complex secondary endosymbiotic events 3,4. The diverse evolutionary 
origins of plastids across algal lineages, together with their complex endosymbiotic histories, have shaped exten-
sive variation in plastid genome structure, genome size, and gene content, thereby increasing the challenges of 
comparative analyses of plastid genome evolution across lineages5–10. Currently, most algal plastid genomes have 
been generated through independent studies, resulting in heterogeneous assembly and annotation strategies and 
consequently variable data quality 11. Common issues include inconsistent genome annotations 12, unresolved 
structural errors, and inaccurate identification of inverted repeat (IR) regions, all of which reduce the consist-
ency and reliability of available plastome resources.
Advances in sequencing technology have greatly accelerated research on plant chloroplast genomes. 
Although the numbers of algal plastid genomes have also increased, their expansion remains relatively limited 
compared with that of land plants. For instance, over 74,101 complete chloroplast genomes from Embryophyta 
have been publicly released. In comparison, the number of algal plastomes remains limited, with only 2,878 
entries recorded as of November 2025, covering merely about 2% of algal species diversity13. Therefore, expand-
ing high-quality algal plastid genome resources is essential for addressing the current gaps in available algal 
plastome data.
To address this gap, this study assembled 90 plastoms (66 complete circular genomes and 24 linear drafts) 
from 110 samples species, with 20 datasets excluded due to unresolved assembly graphs (Fig. 1A). These newly 
generated datasets add 29 species, 13 genera, four families, and one order previously absent from NCBI to the 
public repository (Fig. 1B). Together, these data establish an expanded plastome resource for downstream com-
parative genomic and evolutionary studies of algae.

Methods
Data Sources. Raw sequencing reads were sourced from publicly available macroalgal genome datasets 
deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA), including whole-genome sequencing data from 110 algal 
individuals and additional datasets reported in previous studies 14–18. These datasets correspond to BioProject 
accessions PRJNA924561, PRJDB15746, PRJEB39309, PRJNA32771, and PRJEB21674. Detailed information 
on species, source publications, BioProject accessions, and sequencing-run accessions is provided in Figshare 
repository at https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.32983835.v819. To perform plastome assembly, annotation, and 
comparative analysis, we downloaded the publicly available Illumina short-read data for all taxa.
Plastomes Assembly and Annotation. Raw sequencing reads were quality-checked using FastQC v0.12.1 
1 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) with default analytical settings and trimmed 
with Trimmomatic v0.3920 to remove low-quality bases and adapter sequences with the following parameters: 
-phred33, ILLUMINACLIP:TruSeq. 3-PE.fa:2:30:10, HEADCROP:10, LEADING:20, TRAILING:24, CROP:140, 
SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15, AVGQUAL:28, MINLEN:36, and TOPHRED64. Cleaned reads were assembled 
with GetOrganelle v1.7.7.1 with -F other_pt, -R 10, and an appropriate plastid genome seed sequence specified 
using -s; other assembly parameters were left at their default settings. Assembly graphs were visualized using 
Bandage v0.8.121 to inspect contig connectivity, coverage depth, and circularity, and were manually simplified 
based on depth and connectivity. Datasets were excluded when their assembly graphs could not be resolved into 
either a single uninterrupted circular plastome or a characteristic dumbbell-shaped structure associated with 
inverted repeats. Assembly quality was evaluated by remapping reads to the assembled plastomes using the eval-
uate_assembly_using_mapping.py script from GetOrganelle, which assesses per-site coverage, mismatch rates,

Plastomes were annotated with MFannot v1.3.6 (https://megasun.bch.umontreal.ca/apps/mfannot/) 
using the bacterial/plastid genetic code (code 11) 22. The resulting annotations in ASN format were converted 
to GenBank format using asn2gb from the NCBI toolkit 23. Annotations were manually curated, and circular 
genome maps were generated using OGDRAW v1.3.124 with default settings. In addition, key genomic features 
were manually compiled for 90 species.
Comparative plastomes analysis. Inverted repeat (IR) region loss, contraction, expansion, and bound-
ary shifts were analyzed using IRplus 25 (irscope.shinyapps.io/IRplus/) with default settings and CPGView 26,27 
(http://47.96.249.172:16085/cpgview/home) with default settings (Figures S2–S4). Protein-coding genes were 
categorized by functional groups based on phylogenetic relationships, and their presence/absence patterns was 
recorded, with ORF-classified genes excluded from the analysis28,29.
Phylogenetic Analyses. A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree of algae was constructed using 
IQ-TREE multicore version 2.4.0 30 under the LG + F + I + G4 model, with Synechocystis sp. (CP007542) and 
Prochlorococcus marinus (BX548175) as outgroups. The procedure was as follows: (a) For all 92 species span-
ning three algal phyla, the nucleotide and corresponding protein sequences of every protein-coding gene were 
retrieved using the script extract_all_genes_from_gb.py. A set of 117 protein-coding genes present in at least 
80% of species was selected and concatenated for tree construction. (b) Protein sequences of these genes were 
aligned using MAFFT31 with default parameters. (c) Unreliable alignment regions were removed using Gblocks 
with default parameters in PhyloSuite v1.2.332. (d) PhyloSuite v1.2.3 was used to concatenate the 117 single-gene 
alignments into a supermatrix with default parameters. (e) IQ-TREE was applied to construct the ML tree under 
the LG + F + I + G4 model with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. (f) The tree was visualized and annotated using iTOL 
(https://itol.embl.de/)33.

Data Overview
For chloroplast genome assembly, we obtained 66 complete plastomes and 24 linear draft assemblies, with cir-
cular maps of the complete genomes presented in Fig. 1C. The canonical quadripartite structure was displayed 
in all Ochrophyta plastid genomes, whereas it was observed only in a subset of Chlorophyta and Rhodophyta 
plastid genomes. In Rhodophyta, the repeat regions consisted of either inverted repeats or direct repeats (Fig. 2).
Among the Chlorophyta plastid genomes, large single-copy (LSC), small single-copy (SSC), and inverted 
repeat (IR) regions lengths ranged from 53,789–88,291 bp, 10,447–23,760 bp, and 316–5,266 bp, respectively. In 
contrast, Ochrophyta and Rhodophyta plastomes exhibited relatively conserved structural partitions within each 
lineage, with average LSC, SSC, and IR/repeat region lengths of 78.87/42.92/11.70 kb and 152.60/31.12/9.60 kb, 
respectively (Fig.  3A). The complete plastome length ranged from 81,997 to 113,888 bp in Chlorophyta and 
from 124,224 to 143,684 bp in Ochrophyta and from 164,327 to 194,981 bp in Rhodophyta (Fig. 3B). The aver-
age GC content varied among the three algal lineages, with mean values of 28.31% in Chlorophyta, 30.81% in 
Ochrophyta, and 29.98% in Rhodophyta (Fig.  3C). Correlation analysis showed that LSC length was signifi -
cantly correlated with genome size (Fig.  3D), whereas SSC and IR lengths showed no significant correlations 
(Fig. 3E,F). Among the functional partitions, the protein-coding region (CDS) exhibited the strongest corre -
lation with genome size, while tRNA and intergenic regions showed moderate correlations. In contrast, rRNA, 
introns, and pseudogenes showed little or no correlation (Fig. 3G–L).
Protein-coding gene numbers differed among the major algal lineages, averaging approximately 99 in 
Chlorophyta, 142 in Ochrophyta, and 186 in Rhodophyta. Compared with Ochrophyta and Rhodophyta, 
Chlorophyta exhibited reduced gene numbers across all functional categories, whereas the differences between 
Ochrophyta and Rhodophyta were primarily associated with genes involved in carbon assimilation and biosyn-
thesis, transcription and translation, and light harvesting and chlorophyll biosynthesis (Fig. 4).
Technical Validation
Raw Data Quality Control. To ensure technical reliability, raw sequencing reads underwent quality control 
and trimming. All samples retained high Q30 scores, low error rates, and stable sequence quality.
Validation Criteria. Standard 1–Assembly Contiguity . Assembly graphs were visualized and evaluated 
using Bandage to assess their contiguity and circularity. A plastome was considered complete only when the 
final resolved graph formed one or more fully closed circular structures without gaps. Specifically, the graph was 
required to be continuous and fully resolved, without unconnected terminal nodes, dangling ends, open-ended 
paths, or fragmented structures with ambiguous connectivity.
Standard 2–Assembly Accuracy. Reads were remapped to the assembled plastomes to evaluate coverage. The 
sequencing-depth profile showed no abrupt or unexplained interruptions. Uniform depth distribution without 
significant fluctuations indicated correct and stable assembly.

Standard 3–Core Gene Set Completeness. Core gene set completeness was evaluated separately for Chlorophyta, 
Ochrophyta, and Rhodophyta, with the reference gene sets for each phylum detailed in Table S1. Plastome 
annotations retaining ≥80% of the lineage-specific core genes were considered to have acceptable annotation 
completeness. Following automated annotation, all identified protein-coding genes, rRNAs, and tRNAs were 
manually curated against reference sequences from closely related species in GenBank.

Data availability
The 66 assembled plastid genomes and their functional annotations have been submitted to DDBJ/ENA/
GenBank34 and figshare (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.30943982.v4) 35. All detailed plastome features, 
associated sequence data, including both raw and trimmed alignments, as well as the tree file are publicly available 
on figshare (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.32983835.v8)19.
Code availability
The custom Python script extract_all_genes_from_gb.py used in this study is publicly available in figshare 
(https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.30943982.v4)35 together with the plastid genome assemblies and annotation 
files.
