contamination?
Water reuse in the food industry .T h ew a t e r– food nexus is becoming a
deﬁning challenge for sustainable development since societal growth and
food security heavily depend on how water is managed across food pro-
duction systems1,2. The food industry is increasingly reusing water and
treated wastewater ef ﬂuent to support critical growing, harvesting and
processing functions with water treatment targets being tailored to the
intended end-use. The use of treated wastewater efﬂuent for crop irrigation
is a common practice globally, with regulator-set water quality standards
that vary by jurisdiction and often focus on disinfection and micro-
biological water quality3– 5. However, water reuse for water-intensive pro-
cesses within the food industry is also becoming increasingly common.
These water-intensive processes are shown in Fig. 1 and include post-
harvest activities, such as: raw product washing (e.g., grain or produce
sorting, cutting, washing, chilling); dunk and immersion tanks; dehusking,
shelling and peeling tanks; poultry and meat processing; clean-in-place and
sanitizing equipment; and produceﬂumes and conveyors2,6.M a n yo ft h e s e

and restore equipment performance, can account for 30 – 80% of opera-
tional costs and can consume up to 70% of water, while also requiring
substantial chemical use8,9.
Given how volume-intensive and unsustainable these practices are, the
food industry has increasingly relied on water reuse and treatment (also
known as water conditioning) within its operations to reduce or remove
microbiological, chemical, or physical contaminants to allow reuse for one
to more thanﬁfty cycles. By reconditioning the water, the same water can be
reused for multiple, different batches of products instead of being disposed
of after minimal use 6. While this is highly desirable from a water con-
servation perspective, it introduces the potential for cross-contamination of
food products with foodborne pathogens and chemical contaminants
through contact with contaminated process water10. Another unexamined
aspect of water conditioning in food systems is the potential cross-

Relevance of food allergies from a public health perspective
Food allergies are an important public health problem that impacts 1– 10%
of the global population14. Food allergy frequency and severity are reported
to have increased over recent decades15. In the United States (US), about 33
million people suffer from food allergies, including 5.6 million children, with
childhood food allergies alone resulting in an estimated annual cost of $24.8
billion16– 18. The World Health Organization (WHO) forecasts that nearly
50% of the global population will e xperience allergies by 2050, making
allergies the fourth most common chronic disorder19.
Food allergies result in an acute Immunoglobulin E (IgE) mediated
reaction that occurs in response to speciﬁc food components. The allergic
reaction is triggered after sensitization occurs as a result of either oral
exposure to a food allergen induces an IgE-sensitizing response that primes
the host immune system to react, or as a result of maladaptive immune
responses in the skin that are now known to also contribute to the devel-
opment of food allergy14.S h e l lﬁsh, ﬁsh, tree nuts, and peanuts have been
reported to have high rates of allergenic cross-reactivity (Kamath et al.20).
Cross-reactivity occurs when structurally similar proteins or allergenic
epitopes from different foods are recognized by the same allergen-speciﬁc
IgE antibodies, potentially resulting in an allergic reaction (Kamath et al.20).
However, IgE cross-sensitization does not necessarily result in clinically
relevant allergic reactions, and the degree of clinical cross-reactivity varies
among food groups and individuals. Allergenic proteins with greater than
70% amino acid sequence identity are generally considered more likely to
exhibit IgE cross-reactivity, but cross-reactivity is inﬂuenced by the struc-
tural similarity and conservation of IgE-binding epitopes and cannot be
predicted from sequence identity alone21. This implies that food processing
operations that reuse water between many different food types could carry
over food allergens that trigger some cross-reactivity.

Approximately 7– 14% of patients with peanut allergies experience acci-
dental peanut exposure annually22. Similarly, in a prospective 1-year study of
157 adults with physician-diagnosed food allergy, 46% experienced at least
one accidental allergic reaction, corresponding to an average of approxi-
mately two reactions per affected in dividual per year; 28% of reported
reactions were classiﬁed as severe23. Avoidance remains a key intervention in
the management of food allergies24. Several international initiatives attempt
to reduce exposure through labeling and testing. Precautionary allergen
labeling (PAL) refers to voluntary statements such as“may contain” or “may
be present” that communicate the potential unintended presence of aller-
gens resulting from cross-contact during food production. In Australia and
New Zealand, the VITAL® (Voluntary Incidental Trace Allergen Labelling)
Program provides a standardized, r isk-based methodology for assessing
allergen cross-contact and determining whether PAL is appropriate. VITAL
uses scientiﬁcally derived reference doses and action levels to support
consistent labeling decisions while reducing unnecessary use of precau-
tionary statements. Still, the presence of undeclared allergens makes com-
pliance difﬁcult with studies in the European Union (EU) estimating that up
to 7.6% of “free-from-allergen” products do contain undeclared allergens25.
In addition, a study by Soon et al. that reviewed 2,932 food safety incidents
between 2008 and 2023 in Canada, the EU, the United Kingdom (UK) and
the US, reported the primary reason for food recalls being allergens
(28– 48%) followed by microbial contamination26,27. The four most com-
monly reported allergen recalls were for undeclared milk (36%), egg (14%),
wheat (13%), and peanut (12%) allergens12,28. To mitigate food recalls,ﬁrms
that manufacture products both withand without major allergens need to
design proper labels, place the correct product in the correct package, and
have a concrete understanding of potential allergen cross-contact pathways
during processing29.
Allergen cross-contamination is the unintentional incorporation of a
major food allergen in a food during processing. Previous research focused
on employee training, cleaning procedures, and managing cross-
contamination and found these important for allergen management
efforts for companies13. Cross-contamination of allergens often results from
carryover of allergen-containing material on shared equipment (70% of
facilities) or bulk tanks (<40%), which cannot always be prevented especially
in small and medium sized facilities26. Very little has been published on the
effectiveness of cleaning procedures for removing allergenic materials from
processing equipment30. A survey of 50 quality control and allergen man-
agement food industry specialists showed that despite 96% of companies
having detailed food allergen control plans in place in alignment with best
practices, nearly half (42%) had at least one food allergen-related recall
within the past 5 years 13. For food companies, the economic and reputa-
tional costs resulting from recalls as a result of mislabeling and cross-
contamination are major, which is why companies create programs and
practices to ensure that food productswith and without major allergens are
kept separated in produ ction and distribution29. There are currently no
standard water conditioning practices or water reuse guidance in the food
industry. There is no data or methods to validate whether allergen cross-
contamination through water reuse or ineffective treatment is a concern.
Given how recent water reuse in food processing is, it is not surprising that
potential of cross-contaminatio n has not been explored for condi-
tioned water.
Allergens’ stability and persistence in water systems
Allergen properties in biological systems that result in stability and
persistence. Abundance and stability are common features of allergens in
biological systems31, which have implications for removal or persistence of
allergens in water systems. Researchers have proposed that greater abun-
dance of potential allergens may increase the likelihood of human exposure
and, consequently, the probability of sensitization 31.A l t h o u g ha l l e r g e n
abundance may contribute to allergenic risk, sensitization is multifactorial
and is inﬂuenced by additional characteristics, including protein stability,
exposure duration, and immunological factors.
Pekar et al. deﬁne allergen stability as the ability of proteins to preserve
their native, three-dimensional structure over time after chemical, physical
or protease exposure 32. Depending on their routes of exposure, food
allergens possess molecular and structural properties that can in ﬂuence
their stability, persistence, and ability to survive physiological barriers
and enzymatic degradation. For orally ingested allergens, stability to
gastrointestinal conditions, including acidic pH and proteolytic and
hydrolytic degradation, can allow intact proteins or immunologically
relevant peptide fragments to persist and become available for uptake
across the intestinal epithelium and subsequent interaction with antigen-
presenting cells. Following uptake, allergens undergo intracellular pro-
cessing and presentation that can contribute to the development of an
IgE-mediated immune response. Therefore, allergen stability is one of
several factors inﬂuencing sensitization rather than a sole determinant of
allergenic potential31,32. Respiratory allergens, such as pollen or dust mite
proteins, are not exposed to low pH levels or enzymes, but exhibit
extensive solubility in the aqueous environments of the mucosal surfaces
of the alveoli and have the correct particle size to enter and escape mucus
binding33. Allergens entering the body through the skin must cross the
epidermal barrier, where ﬁlaggrin and its degradation products

Other factors that result in allergen stability and persistence
While food allergens are diverse proteins, they often do share common
structural features, such as strong disul ﬁde bonds or oligomeric
structures35,36.D i s u lﬁde bonds make proteins more resistance to chemical or
biological degradation, inﬂuence stability under varying pH and inﬂuence
protein 3-D structure. Studies showed that peanut allergens Ara h 2 and Ara
h 6 are extremely resistant to proteolytic digestion due to their four highly
stable disulﬁde bonds37. Higher abundance of β-sheet structures results in
more thermal tolerance32.T h u s ,d i s u lﬁde bonds and 3-D protein folding can
impact charge, hydrophobicity and protein partitioning in water treatment
processes, which impact whether a protein ultimately partitions into solids
or liquid phases.

many are relatively small proteins with molecular masses below approxi-

Challenges and opportunities
As the food industry continues to optimize water reuse to reduce water
consumption, the potential for higher allergen cross-contamination and risk
to public health increases56. There is a need to better understand the impacts
of traditional and emerging food processing and water treatment technol-
ogies on protein structures and immunoreactivity (e.g., thermal, radiation,
light, high-pressure, transient pressure, mechanical wave, plasma, electric
ﬁeld) and cross-contamination potential.
Quantiﬁcation of food allergens in food and other complex matrices
remains challenging. Immunoassay-based methods, particularly enzyme-
linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs), are widely used for routine allergen
analysis; however, their quantitative performance can be affected by matrix
composition and processing history. Protein denaturation, aggregation,
reduced solubility, epitope masking, non-speciﬁc interactions, and chemical
modiﬁcations can reduce allergen extraction ef ﬁciency or alter antibody
recognition, potentially resulting in inaccurate estimates of allergen
concentration52,57. ELISA, western blotting and immunochromatography all
exhibit cross-reactivity and result in false positives 58.A l t h o u g ht h e s e

Conclusions
 The food industry is increasingly reusing water and treated wastewater
efﬂuent to support critical growing, harvesting and processing func-
tions with water treatment targets being tailored to the intended
end-use.
 Food allergies are an important public health problem that impacts
1– 10% of the global population and their frequency and severity is
reported to have increased over recent decades.
 Research showed that 92% of companies produced food products
containing one or more of the nine major food allergens and despite
96% of companies having detailed food allergen control plans in place
in alignment with best practices, nearly half had at least one food
allergen-related recall within the past 5 years.
 Water treatment and conditioning for reuse in the food industry is a
common practice, but treatment trains, disinfectant dosing and
monitoring systems are not standardized.
 To our knowledge, there has been no research on the occurrence of
allergens in food systems processing water, and the potential for
allergen cross-contamination has not been explored.
 The same properties that allow stability and persistence of allergens in
biological systems can also potentially result in persistence and impact
fate and transport in water reuse system in the food industry.
 Given the large number of food allergens, the use of certain allergens as
