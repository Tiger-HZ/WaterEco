 Abstract 
 Cyanophages are emerging as key regulators of freshwater cyanobacterial blooms, affecting host mortality, community succession, nutrient cycling, and cyanotoxin dynamics. Understanding how climate change impacts these interactions is crucial for predicting bloom persistence and developing robust ecological frameworks to forecast how freshwater ecosystems will respond as the climate warms. 
 Keywords: bloom management strategies, climate change impacts, cyanobacterial blooms, cyanophage infections, freshwater ecosystems 1. Cyanobacterial Blooms in the Era of Climate Change: Unraveling the Layers of Complexity 
 Global climate change and the unprecedented rise in air temperatures over recent decades have been correlated with increased reports of confirmed blooms (algal and cyanobacterial) in freshwater environments (Favot et al.  2023 ; Chang et al.  2026 ). Cyanobacterial blooms, characterized by visible water discoloration due to elevated cyanobacterial biomass, pose numerous challenges to freshwater ecosystems. These blooms degrade water quality by producing metabolites that affect taste and odor, complicating their removal from drinking water due to high costs and complex chemistries (O'Neil et al.  2012 ). This decline in water quality impacts recreation, drinking water availability, fisheries, and human health (O'Neil et al.  2012 ). 
 Many cyanobacterial taxa produce a diverse array of harmful toxins that significantly elevate ecological and public health concerns during blooms. These toxins include microcystins and nodularins (cyclic hepatotoxic peptides), saxitoxins (neurotoxins), and cylindrospermopsin (a cytotoxic alkaloid with hepatotoxic effects) (Pearson et al.  2010 ; Saha et al.  2022 ). Genera such as Aphanizomenon , Cylindrospermopsis , Dolichospermum , Microcystis , Nodularia , and Planktothrix are among the principal contributors to cyanobacterial harmful algal blooms (CyanoHABs). Despite decades of research, the full diversity of cyanobacterial toxins remains poorly understood, suggesting that their ecological and toxic effects may be underestimated. The recent discovery of guanitoxin, a highly potent neurotoxin produced by cyanobacteria such as Sphaerospermopsis (Lima et al.  2022 ), reveals previously overlooked toxic metabolites and highlights gaps in our knowledge. Consequently, the environmental distribution, factors influencing toxin production, and risks related to many cyanobacterial toxins require further study. 
 Climate change factors, including elevated temperatures, reduced wind speeds, and altered precipitation patterns, intensify phytoplankton blooms, accelerate their growth, affect their motility, and alter lake stratification (Hou et al.  2022 ). For example, over the last 31 years, increased stratification in 20 reservoirs in the USA due to high summer temperatures has led to higher cyanobacteria cell densities in the environment (Smucker et al.  2021 ). Moreover, cyanobacterial blooms in the Moselle River were most strongly associated with low discharge, elevated water temperatures, and reduced nitrate concentrations. Recent bloom events have similarly been linked to dry, warm weather, which has intensified low‐flow conditions and further increased water temperatures in this regulated river system (Kleinteich et al.  2024 ). 
 Cyanobacterial bloom events result from multiple complex factors, typically not driven by a single environmental driver but by multiple factors co‐occurring (O'Neil et al.  2012 ; Massey et al.  2020 ). Among the factors influencing cyanobacterial bloom dynamics, some are directly linked to climate change, including rising water temperatures, more extreme wet–dry cycles, altered light regimes, and increased carbon dioxide availability (Preece et al.  2025 ). Others, although not directly driven by climate change, result from anthropogenic activities and also play a critical role in promoting bloom development and persistence, particularly through eutrophication (Chang et al.  2026 ). Importantly, the interaction between climate warming and eutrophication synergistically increases bloom risk (Rodgers 2021 ). Elevated temperatures, along with increased phosphorus and nitrogen inputs from agriculture and aquaculture, create optimal conditions for cyanobacterial growth and dominance (Zepernick et al.  2023 ). 
 As a result, cyanobacterial harmful algal blooms (CyanoHABs) are expected to become more frequent, longer‐lasting, more severe, and more widespread. Recent global analyses of two decades of satellite observations from more than 4000 lakes found that bloom intensity and timing often respond differently to environmental drivers. Bloom intensity was primarily associated with human population density and agricultural pressure, whereas shifts in bloom timing were more strongly linked to temperature and wind conditions, particularly in colder regions (Xue et al. 2026 ). These findings suggest that anthropogenic nutrient enrichment and climate change can independently and interactively regulate distinct aspects of bloom dynamics, with potentially significant consequences for lake ecosystem functioning and food‐web structure. 
 Given the complexity of these interacting drivers, improving our capacity to predict, prevent, and mitigate CyanoHABs will require multidisciplinary research integrating ecological, physiological, biogeochemical, evolutionary, and modeling approaches (Song et al.  2023 ; Chang et al.  2026 ). 
 Current strategies for mitigating blooms typically involve reducing nutrient inputs and implementing physical and chemical methods, such as applying algaecides (hydrogen peroxide) and nutrient flocculation/coagulation (Kibuye et al.  2021 ). However, these approaches are often developed without considering trade‐offs among cost, ecosystem safety, and health risks, and primarily focus on controlling or preventing abiotic factors (Song et al.  2023 ). While certain biological traits of CyanoHABs, such as their buoyancy regulation and adaptability to changes in CO 2 levels, are recognized as significant factors in bloom occurrence, the importance of biotic interactions as potential causes or enhancers of blooms is often overlooked. 
 The importance of biotic interactions in shaping and triggering cyanobacterial blooms is increasingly recognized (Crevecoeur et al.  2025 ; García et al.  2026 ). These interactions can either promote or constrain bloom development. On the one hand, bloom‐associated microbial communities often harbor diverse metabolic and nutritional capabilities that can support cyanobacterial growth through the exchange of metabolites and bloom‐derived exudates (Johnson et al.  2020 ; Crevecoeur et al.  2025 ; Samuel and Lopez  2025 ). On the other hand, cyanobacteria actively compete with co‐occurring microorganisms, particularly under nutrient‐enriched conditions, thereby reducing bacterial alpha diversity and reshaping microbial community composition (Du et al.  2022 ; Samuel and Lopez  2025 ; Kang et al.  2026 ). Beyond these microbe–microbe interactions, cyanobacterial populations are also subject to top‐down regulation by grazers, including zooplankton, invertebrates, and certain fish species, as well as by parasitic microorganisms such as fungi, bacteria, and viruses (Gerphagnon et al.  2015 ). Together, these antagonistic interactions play a critical role in controlling bloom dynamics, often contributing to bloom decline, collapse, and subsequent decomposition (van Wichelen et al.  2016 ; Zimmerman et al.  2020 ; Morón‐López et al.  2023 ). 
 Viruses have been recognized as important trophic drivers, potentially surpassing the influence of bottom‐up control, a regulatory process in which ecosystem processes are driven by the availability of abiotic resources, such as nutrients and light, which influence the growth and activity of primary producers (Cissell and McCoy  2022 ). By exerting top‐down control over cyanobacterial populations, viruses can influence microbial community structure, biodiversity, and ecosystem functioning, ultimately shaping bloom dynamics (Chen et al.  2025 ). Field observations suggest that viral activity drives phytoplankton succession through the lysis of eukaryotic microorganisms, increased lysogeny in cyanobacteria, and nutrient recycling from lysed cells. This process supports the shift toward Microcystis dominance and sustains cyanobacterial blooms (Peng et al.  2023 ). While environmental factors remain the main influences on bloom dynamics, cyanophages play a crucial supplementary role by affecting host mortality, microbial interactions, nutrient availability, and cyanotoxin dynamics. Evidence from recurring Microcystis ‐dominated blooms in Western Lake Erie shows that cyanophage‐induced lysis can substantially increase dissolved microcystin levels by releasing intracellular toxins into the water column, with direct implications for drinking water treatment and public health (McKindles et al.  2020 ). These results show that cyanophages affect bloom impacts not just by controlling host populations but also by changing how cyanotoxins move and become available in the environment. Nevertheless, the mechanisms behind these interactions remain poorly understood, limiting our ability to forecast how climate‐induced environmental changes will influence cyanobacteria–cyanophage relationships. This, in turn, impacts bloom duration, toxicity levels, and overall ecosystem health. Understanding how climate change reshapes trophic interactions, especially predator–prey dynamics between cyanobacteria and cyanophages in freshwater systems, is crucial for better predicting bloom patterns. It also provides a mechanistic basis for developing more effective mitigation strategies in the context of future climate conditions (Zhang, Liu, et al. 2022 ). 2. Environmental Drivers of Cyanobacteria‐Cyanophage Interactions and Bloom Dynamics 
 Cyanophages are viruses that exclusively infect cyanobacteria. They were traditionally classified into three families, previously recognized as Myoviridae, Podoviridae, and Siphoviridae, which vary in morphology and host infectivity (Table  1 ). Recently, these families were abolished, and all tailed bacterial and archaeal viruses possessing icosahedral capsids and double‐stranded DNA genomes were reclassified within the class Caudoviricetes. The terms “myovirus,” “podovirus,” and “siphovirus” are now recognized solely as morphological, non‐taxonomic identifiers (Turner et al.  2023 ). Like other bacteriophages, cyanophages adopt either lytic or lysogenic infection strategies. In the lytic cycle, the virus replicates and causes the host cell to burst, releasing new virions. Conversely, during lysogeny, the viral genome remains dormant within the host until environmental factors, such as temperature shifts, UV exposure, heavy metals, or nutrient changes, induce a switch to the lytic cycle (Li et al.  2025 ). Both lifestyles have been observed in freshwater cyanophages (Figure  1 ).

 Schematic representation of the lytic and lysogenic life cycles of freshwater cyanophages and their ecological consequences. Following infection, cyanophages can either enter the lytic cycle, leading to viral replication, host‐cell lysis, and the release of progeny virions that infect new cyanobacterial cells, or establish lysogeny, in which the viral genome integrates into the host chromosome as a prophage and remains dormant until induced by environmental or physiological cues. Beyond regulating cyanobacterial abundance through top‐down control, cyanophage infections contribute to horizontal gene transfer, shape microbial community composition and diversity, drive nutrient recycling through host‐cell lysis, and promote the release of intracellular cyanotoxins into the aquatic environment. Some cyanophages exhibit a broad host range, infecting multiple cyanobacterial genera (Table  1 ). For instance, the cyanophage MinS1, initially described as infecting 
 M. aeruginosa 
, has been shown to infect members of several cyanobacterial orders, including Chroococcales, Nostocales, Oscillatoriales, Hormogonales, and Synechococcales (Zhang, He, et al. 2022 ). In contrast, other cyanophages display a narrow host range and high specificity, infecting only particular strains or phylotypes within a single cyanobacterial species. An example is the cyanophage MVG_NODE620, which infects only specific phylotypes of 
 M. aeruginosa 
 (Morimoto et al.  2023 ). Variation in host range has significant ecological consequences for freshwater ecosystems. Cyanophages with broad host ranges can promote horizontal gene transfer among cyanobacterial taxa, contributing to genetic diversification and the dissemination of key ecological traits. This is supported by findings such as the identification of a Planktothrix ‐like phycocyanin gene in a cyanophage isolated from Anabaena (Deng and Hayes  2008 ), as well as the presence of auxiliary metabolic genes like pho H and elements of the queuosine biosynthesis pathway in broad‐host‐range cyanophages infecting Pseudanabaena spp. (Zhu et al. 2023 ). In contrast, narrow‐host‐range cyanophages tend to influence strain‐level selection, shaping cyanobacterial diversity and succession more specifically. Recent studies demonstrate that even closely related cyanobacterial strains can exhibit markedly different infection outcomes. For instance, cyanophages infecting the filamentous, nitrogen‐fixing Anabaena sp. PCC 7120 exhibits varied infection patterns, with neighboring cells within the same filament responding differently (Shi et al. 2026 ). Similarly, Microcystis blooms previously considered a single morphospecies have been shown to comprise multiple coexisting genospecies with different microcystin genotypes and phenotypes (Johansson et al. 2026 ). These findings suggest that phage‐mediated selection may contribute to temporal shifts in bloom toxicity. However, the respective roles of broad and narrow cyanophages in structuring host–phage interaction networks and shaping bloom dynamics remain largely unresolved, representing an important gap in our understanding of freshwater viral ecology. 
 Cyanophages are widely distributed across aquatic environments, occurring in both marine and freshwater systems. Nevertheless, much of our current understanding of their diversity and ecological functions derives from studies in marine ecosystems (Lin et al.  2020 ), despite the greater direct relevance of freshwater environments to human and ecosystem health. In freshwater habitats such as lakes, ponds, reservoirs, streams, and sewage outlets, cyanophages play key roles in regulating cyanobacterial populations and shaping community structure (Xia et al.  2013 ; Zhou et al.  2023 ). 
 Evidence indicates that freshwater cyanophages differ from their marine counterparts. Although both groups exhibit high genetic diversity, freshwater cyanophages generally display narrower host ranges and greater specificity, often infecting only particular cyanobacterial species or even specific strains (Morimoto et al.  2020 ). This high level of specialization could have significant ecological impacts since cyanobacterial blooms are usually dominated by a single species or even a particular strain (Peng et al.  2023 ; Song et al.  2023 ; Johansson et al. 2026 ). As blooms develop, shifts in species and strain composition trigger phytoplankton succession, leading to dynamic changes in host availability. These changes are reflected in associated microbial and viral communities. Metagenomic studies of eutrophic freshwater lakes reveal that viral communities experience marked seasonal succession driven by variations in host composition and environmental factors like nutrients and temperature. These shifts involve changes in viral lifestyles, host range, and the distribution of virus‐encoded auxiliary metabolic genes (vAMGs). The greater presence of vAMGs before bloom onset suggests viruses may influence host metabolism during the early stages of bloom development (Yuan et al.  2024 ). Overall, these results demonstrate that due to their high host specificity, freshwater cyanophages can selectively suppress dominant cyanobacteria populations, influence competitive dynamics, and thereby affect bloom progression, stability, and ecosystem functions. 
 Genomic analyses further reinforce the ecological distinction between marine and freshwater cyanophages. Comparative studies have revealed limited genomic similarity and few viral lineage transitions between these aquatic biomes, suggesting that cyanophages have largely evolved independently in each environment (Zhang et al.  2021 ). Consistent with this pattern, freshwater cyanophages carry a much higher proportion of prophage integrase‐related genes compared to marine cyanophages (37.97% vs. 7.42%), indicating a greater capacity for lysogeny. Similarly, freshwater cyanobacteria encode a higher density of antiviral defense systems per million base pairs, implying more robust or frequent virus–host interactions and a prolonged co‐evolutionary relationship with cyanophages (Lin et al.  2024 ). 
 However, the host range appears to be influenced not only by habitat but also by phage taxonomy and evolutionary lineage. This is exemplified by the isolation and genomic characterization of two cyanophages, S‐SRP01, which infects Synechococcus sp. strain SR‐R4S1, and S‐SRM01, which infects Synechococcus sp. strain SR‐C6, from a tropical eutrophic freshwater reservoir in Singapore, located adjacent to the sea but separated by a dam. These viruses share core genes and gene order with their marine counterparts, indicating that Synechococcus ‐infecting cyanophages from marine and freshwater environments may have originated from a common gene pool (Zhang et al.  2021 ). More recently, described host‐derived transcription factor binding sites in the genome of the freshwater cyanophage A‐4(L), suggesting that the virus exploits host regulatory machinery to control its gene expression, a mechanism likely reflecting coevolution between the virus and its host. Altogether, these findings reveal that freshwater cyanophages, though less studied than marine ones, constitute an evolutionarily distinct and ecologically significant group. Their ability to modulate cyanobacterial community dynamics and their potential application as biological control agents highlight the importance of expanding viral ecology research within freshwater environments (Grasso et al.  2022 ). 
 The relationship between cyanobacteria and cyanophages is highly dynamic and shaped by the interplay between host specificity, infection strategy, host defense mechanisms, and environmental conditions (Zhang, Liu, et al. 2022 ). Environmental conditions strongly influence cyanobacteria–cyanophage interactions. Yet, the impact of climate change on these relationships has only recently gained attention, mostly through laboratory studies focusing on cyanophage isolation and characterization. These studies consistently show that abiotic factors such as temperature, light, nutrients, pH, salinity, ultraviolet radiation, and elevated pCO 2 significantly influence cyanophage infectivity, replication, and life cycle, though responses are often highly host–phage‐specific. For example, low light extended the latent period of the cyanophage CrV infecting Raphidiopsis raciborskii , while higher temperatures shortened this period and increased burst size (Steenhauer et al.  2016 ). Similarly, warming promoted the transition from lysogenic to lytic infection in cyanophage AS‐1 (Chu et al.  2011 ), and elevated pCO 2 accelerated viral replication in 
 Plectonema boryanum 
 and boosted cyanophage production in Phormidium spp. (Cheng et al.  2017 , 2019 ). In addition, the freshwater cyanophage MinS1 showed tolerance to a wide range of temperatures, pH, salinity, and UV exposure (Zhang, Liu, et al. 2022 ), while cyanophage A‐4(L) altered host nitrogen metabolism and photosynthesis to enhance viral replication (Liu et al.  2026 ). Other freshwater cyanophages displayed marked differences in replication efficiency, latent period, and burst size across temperature and pH gradients (Yadav and Ahn  2021 ). 
 In contrast, evidence from natural freshwater ecosystems is limited and mostly correlational. Observations have shown that pH is associated with declines in free cyanophage abundance, although the underlying mechanisms remain unresolved. Likewise, genes associated with lytic replication tend to increase with dissolved nitrogen, ammonium, and salinity, whereas lysogeny‐associated genes are more common at higher pH and phosphorus levels (Stough et al.  2017 ). These results suggest that environmental factors significantly influence cyanophage activity. However, it is still uncertain whether these responses are general ecological patterns or specific to particular host–phage systems. This knowledge gap hinders our ability to predict how climate change could affect cyanophage infection processes and, in turn, cyanobacterial blooms and their persistence. 
 In freshwater ecosystems, cyanophages can affect bloom toxicity via two main pathways: by triggering the release of intracellular toxins when host cells lyse and by selectively infecting specific cyanobacterial strains, which shifts the balance between toxic and non‐toxic populations (Yoshida et al.  2008 ). As a result, the overall impact of viral infection on bloom toxicity depends on the interplay of infection dynamics, host resistance development, and environmental factors. 
 Experimental and field studies support both mechanisms. Cyanophage infection increases the release of nodularin and other cyanopeptides from 
 Nodularia spumigena 
 (Šulčius et al.  2018 ), while experiments infecting 
 M. aeruginosa 
 showed over a fourfold rise in extracellular microcystin‐LR within two days (Lee et al.  2025 ). In Western Lake Erie, recurring Microcystis blooms have been linked to higher dissolved microcystin levels during cyanophage lytic events (McKindles et al.  2020 ). Additionally, natural bloom succession has shown shifts between toxin‐ and non‐toxin‐producing Microcystis populations that align with increased cyanophage abundance (Yoshida et al.  2008 ), implying that viral infections may influence bloom toxicity both directly, via cell lysis, and indirectly, by altering cyanobacterial community composition. Nevertheless, the importance of these mechanisms and whether cyanophages ultimately increase or decrease bloom toxicity under varying environmental conditions remains unclear. 
 An additional, but even less explored, mechanism by which cyanophages might impact bloom dynamics is through phage‐mediated genetic exchange (Bhaya et al. 2025 ). Horizontal gene transfer could allow cyanophages to spread toxin biosynthesis genes among cyanobacteria, potentially reshaping the genetic basis of bloom toxicity. Additionally, when integrated as prophages in host genomes, viral sequences may influence host metabolism and gene regulation, impacting toxin production and other traits relevant to the ecosystem. While these mechanisms remain largely theoretical, they offer promising directions for future investigation. 3. Ecological and Evolutionary Theories Explaining Cyanobacteria–Cyanophage Interactions 
 In the previous section, we discussed the bidirectional relationship between cyanobacteria–cyanophage interactions and how environmental factors influence one another, emphasizing how abiotic factors shape infection patterns and how viral infections affect ecosystem functioning through their effects on bloom dynamics and cyanotoxin release. Building on this, the current section discusses ecological theories that may explain these patterns and provide a theoretical basis for predicting the occurrence, persistence, and collapse of cyanobacterial blooms in freshwater systems under climate change. Cyanobacteria–cyanophage relationships are primarily parasitic, with viruses reproducing by exploiting their hosts. As a result, cyanobacteria have developed several defense strategies to counter infections, including intracellular antiviral defenses like CRISPR–Cas, restriction–modification systems, toxin–antitoxin modules, and other protective pathways (Morimoto et al.  2020 ). 
 The development of host resistance plays a key role in shaping the dynamics of cyanobacterial populations and bloom formation. In 
 M. aeruginosa 
, ecological success and bloom potential are linked to viral defense genes in the genome, along with variation at the strain level in phage recognition sites (Rodriguez‐Valera et al.  2009 ; Papoulis et al.  2021 ). Since resistance can quickly develop under viral attack, cyanophage infections can cause shifts from susceptible to resistant host genotypes, changing clonal makeup and infection patterns (Winter et al.  2010 ). Supporting this, a field study showed that applying cyanophages to a lake with frequent 
 M. aeruginosa 
 blooms reduced host abundance by 95% in six days, with populations recovering after three weeks as resistant strains likely emerged (Tucker and Pollard  2005 ). 
 However, resistance is often associated with fitness trade‐offs. Cyanobacterial strains resistant to cyanophages may exhibit reduced growth rates, increased susceptibility to other phages, diminished buoyancy, shorter filament lengths, and impaired nitrogen fixation or heterocyst formation (Kolan et al.  2024 ). These costs may influence the long‐term coexistence of cyanobacteria and cyanophages and contribute to the maintenance of diversity within bloom‐forming populations. 
 These defense strategies not only enhance cyanobacterial survival but also support the long‐term coexistence of hosts and cyanophages in natural environments. One key mechanism thought to contribute to this coexistence is lysogeny, in which phages integrate their genomes into the host's DNA and remain during times when active replication is unfavorable. Besides maintaining viral presence during low host abundance, lysogeny can affect host fitness, population dynamics, and horizontal gene transfer as previously discussed. While lysogeny has been observed in freshwater cyanophages, evidence remains limited compared with well‐documented lytic infections (Dillon and Parry  2008 ). 
 The ecological importance of long‐term host–phage coexistence was first highlighted by Waterbury and Valois ( 1993 ), who found that the acquisition and maintenance of resistance in marine Synechococcus populations enabled stable coexistence with diverse cyanophages despite high viral loads. Rather than significantly reducing total host numbers, cyanophages primarily reshaped host populations through selective pressure. Although these insights come from marine environments, they offer a valuable framework for understanding how resistance, lysogeny, and viral pressures similarly influence cyanobacterial coexistence, diversity, and bloom dynamics in freshwater ecosystems. 
 Several eco‐evolutionary theories have been proposed to explain changes in cyanobacterial populations driven by cyanophage infections. Among the most widely discussed ecological theories are the Kill‐the‐Winner (KtW) and Piggyback‐the‐Winner (PtW) hypotheses. The KtW hypothesis predicts that the most abundant and metabolically active microbial populations experience the strongest viral pressure, resulting in selective mortality of dominant hosts and preventing competitive exclusion (Winter et al.  2010 ; Yang et al.  2023 ; Peng et al.  2023 ). In contrast, the PtW hypothesis proposes that high host abundance favors lysogenic rather than lytic infections. Under this framework, reduced virus‐to‐microbe ratios (VMR) are associated with increased lysogeny, allowing viruses to persist within successful host populations and potentially enhancing the competitive advantage of dominant taxa, thereby reducing community diversity (Figure  2 ) (Chen et al.  2021 ).

 Conceptual overview of the eco‐evolutionary processes underlying cyanobacteria–cyanophage interactions. Cyanophage infection can follow either a lytic or a lysogenic pathway, while cyanobacteria have evolved multiple defense mechanisms, including CRISPR–Cas systems, restriction–modification systems, and other antiviral strategies. The continuous coevolution between hosts and cyanophages alters host susceptibility, often involving fitness trade‐offs, and may drive shifts in the relative abundance of susceptible versus resistant and toxic versus non‐toxic cyanobacterial populations. The figure illustrates three major eco‐evolutionary hypotheses proposed to explain these interactions: Kill‐the‐Winner (KtW), in which viruses preferentially infect dominant host populations; Piggyback‐the‐Winner (PtW), in which lysogeny is favored under high host abundance, promoting host–phage coexistence; and the Red Queen Hypothesis (RQH), which describes the continuous evolutionary arms race between host resistance and viral counter‐adaptation. Together, these processes influence bloom collapse or persistence, community succession, and long‐term host–phage coexistence. Together, these mechanisms highlight the potential of cyanophages to regulate cyanobacterial community structure and succession. More broadly, virus–host interactions are thought to promote biodiversity and drive evolutionary change through reciprocal adaptation, as hosts evolve resistance mechanisms while viruses acquire strategies to overcome them. This continuous coevolutionary process is consistent with the Red Queen Hypothesis (RQH), which predicts that interacting species must constantly adapt to maintain their relative fitness in the face of ongoing evolutionary change (van Valen  1974 ; Morimoto et al.  2020 ) (Figure  2 ). 
 During cyanobacterial blooms, different eco‐evolutionary scenarios may emerge. Cyanobacteria often outcompete other phytoplankton groups, decreasing community diversity and frequently leading to the dominance of a single species or genotype (Amorim and Moura  2021 ). From the KtW perspective, cyanophages preferentially infect dominant cyanobacterial populations, weakening their competitive advantage and promoting community turnover (Haraldsson et al.  2018 ). Such selective pressure may drive shifts in strain dominance and transitions between toxin‐producing and non‐toxic populations, ultimately influencing bloom composition and toxicity (Šulčius et al.  2018 ). 
 Alternatively, the PtW hypothesis suggests that high host densities promote lysogeny rather than lytic infection. Under these conditions, temperate cyanophages integrate into the genomes of rapidly growing hosts, allowing viral persistence without causing immediate host cell lysis. Additionally, prophage‐mediated homoimmunity may protect lysogenic cells from infection by related phages, allowing dense Microcystis populations and abundant cyanophages to coexist and potentially contributing to bloom persistence (Chen et al.  2021 ; Martin et al.  2025 ). This view aligns with the high prevalence of lysogeny reported among Microcystis ‐infecting cyanophages in the Nile River (Ahmed et al.  2022 ). However, understanding of lysogeny in freshwater cyanobacterial blooms remains limited, mainly due to methodological challenges in detection, especially in Microcystis ‐dominated systems (Martin et al.  2025 ). As a result, the roles of PtW and KtW during bloom development remain unclear and likely depend on factors such as host abundance, nutrient levels, environmental conditions, and the balance between lytic and lysogenic strategies. An often‐overlooked factor is host quality: suboptimal hosts, such as strains with partial resistance or reduced metabolic performance, may support slower viral replication or favor lysogeny. These hosts could act as reservoirs, maintaining cyanophage populations when highly susceptible hosts are scarce. Such dynamics may aid long‐term host–phage coexistence, influence bloom succession, and explain the persistence of cyanophages during periods of low infection efficiency (Tokodi et al.  2025 ). 
 Beyond KtW and PtW, the ongoing cyanobacteria–cyanophage interactions might also be explained by the RQH, which suggests continuous reciprocal adaptation between hosts and parasites. Support for this idea comes from observations that bloom‐forming cyanobacteria often have enhanced antiviral defense systems, such as CRISPR–Cas and restriction–modification (RM), which likely provide advantages under high viral pressure and contribute to bloom persistence (Morimoto et al.  2020 ; Papoulis et al.  2021 ). Recent genomic studies further support this view by revealing that 
 M. aeruginosa 
 harbors multiple CRISPR–Cas systems with spacers targeting cyanophage genes, indicating ongoing coevolution of host defenses and viral genomes (Zeng et al.  2025 ). Conversely, picocyanobacteria and other non‐bloom taxa tend to have fewer or less specific antiviral defenses and may therefore be more susceptible to phage control, indirectly supporting the prevalence of bloom‐forming cyanobacteria (Soukup et al.  2022 ; Krausfeldt et al.  2024 ). 
 The hypotheses and theories discussed above shed light on possible mechanisms behind cyanobacteria–cyanophage coexistence in freshwater systems. Clarifying these ecological patterns is crucial for understanding bloom dynamics. Many of these theories lack full support from experimental data and environmental observations, highlighting a need for further research and for filling a significant gap in studies of cyanophages and cyanobacteria. This gap is particularly important because cyanobacteria–cyanophage interactions are heavily influenced by environmental factors. As climate change causes shifts in temperature, hydrology, and nutrient levels, these interaction patterns are likely to change, affecting the conditions that promote bloom formation. 4. Effects of Global Change on Cyanobacteria and Cyanophage Interactions 
 The complexity of aquatic habitats can be defined by their spatial and physical structures and degree of stratification, which vary over space and time, and these features are affected by global climate change (Soukup et al.  2022 ). An increase and/or decrease in habitat complexity driven by climate fluctuations may disrupt biotic interactions, as different species respond differently to environmental changes (Soukup et al.  2022 ; Winder and Schindler  2004 ). In an epoch of rapid environmental change, scientists are increasingly being asked to predict the impact of these changes on biodiversity (Kharouba and Williams  2024 ). Given the inherently chaotic nature of prey–predator and host–parasite relationships (Sella et al.  2024 ), such as those between cyanobacteria and cyanophages, forecasting their behavior is often exceedingly challenging. 
 Freshwater ecosystems are particularly susceptible to climate change due to their sensitivity to atmospheric warming and to multiple anthropogenic pressures, including agriculture, urbanization, transportation, and industry. Increasing temperatures affect not only water temperature but also essential physicochemical properties that are crucial for water quality, ecosystem health, and biodiversity. Consequently, warming is increasingly seen as a key factor affecting cyanobacteria–cyanophage interactions, altering host physiology and viral infection processes (Harvey et al.  2011 ; Capon et al.  2021 ). 
 Experimental studies demonstrate that cyanophages remain infective across environmental temperatures (15°C–35°C) (Grasso et al.  2022 ). Increasing temperature enhances several stages of the infection cycle, including adsorption efficiency and burst size, and shortens the latency period, partly because reduced water viscosity increases host–virus encounters (Cheng et al.  2017 ). Temperature also affects infection outcomes by altering host physiology. For instance, 
 M. aeruginosa 
 cultures acclimated to 19°C were substantially more resistant to cyanophage Ma‐LMM01 than those acclimated to 26°C, indicating that thermal history influences host susceptibility (Hambrick et al.  2026 ). Similarly, increasing temperature from 24°C to 35°C triggered a switch from lysogeny to lytic infection in cyanophage AS‐1 (Chu et al.  2011 ). 
 Evidence from natural systems is more limited but points in the same direction. Field studies identify water temperature as one of the main factors structuring both bacterial and viral communities, highlighting its central role in bloom–virus dynamics (Stern et al.  2025 ). Collectively, these findings suggest that warming may alter the balance between KtW and PtW dynamics. Conditions that enhance viral performance relative to the host are expected to strengthen top‐down control and favor KtW‐like interactions, increasing the likelihood of bloom decline. Conversely, when warming sustains host dominance while promoting lysogeny or reducing lytic efficiency, PtW‐like dynamics may prevail, contributing to bloom persistence. 
 Although interest in cyanobacteria–cyanophage interactions is increasing, predicting their behavior in natural settings is still challenging. This difficulty arises from the complexity of host–virus relationships and the limitations of current methods for studying ecosystem‐scale processes. Such studies often require combining various environmental, ecological, and molecular data collected across different places and times (Baum and Worm  2009 ). Our understanding of how cyanobacteria and cyanophages interact and their ecological impacts is still limited, making it challenging to predict their responses to environmental change. This knowledge gap is worsened by the lack of direct experimental research on the effects of climate warming on these interactions. Most existing data are from correlative field studies or laboratory experiments in simplified settings, which might not reflect the full complexity of natural freshwater environments, where cyanobacterial blooms are growing more frequent and severe. 
 Nevertheless, viruses are estimated to account for approximately half of bacterial deaths in aquatic ecosystems and are crucial in controlling microbial communities and phytoplankton populations (Zhang et al. 2020 ). Given their ecological significance, future ecosystem models should explicitly incorporate ecosystem models aimed at predicting ecosystem responses to climate change in marine and freshwater settings. 5. Concluding Remarks 
 Over recent decades, cyanobacterial blooms have become more frequent, intense, and widespread, especially in freshwater habitats, posing risks to water quality, ecosystems, and human health. While these blooms have long been recognized as an environmental concern, climate change is projected to further exacerbate their occurrence by affecting temperature patterns, nutrient flows, hydrological cycles, and species interactions. Today, cyanobacterial blooms are increasingly seen not only as a consequence of climate change but also as a factor that can amplify climate impacts by influencing biogeochemical cycling and ecosystem processes (Griffith and Gobler  2020 ). A key process driving this feedback is the change in carbon cycling. During bloom development, intense primary production leads to high inorganic carbon uptake and an increase in water pH, temporarily making aquatic systems CO 2 sinks. However, when blooms decay, microbial breakdown of the accumulated biomass releases large amounts of CO 2 , which lowers pH and contributes to water acidification (Zhang, Liu, et al. 2022 ). Additionally, this influx of organic matter also stimulates heterotrophic activity and sediment methanogenesis, increasing CH 4 emissions, especially in eutrophic soda lakes during droughts. This trend has become more apparent in the Nhecolândia subregion of the Brazilian Pantanal, where rising cyanobacterial blooms indicate that these lakes could become significant sources of greenhouse gases under future climate change scenarios (Pellegrinetti et al.  2024 ). 
 The development and persistence of blooms are driven by a complex interplay between environmental factors and biological relationships (Figure  3 ). Although significant advances have been made in understanding how abiotic factors like temperature and nutrients influence blooms, the impact of biotic interactions is still relatively less studied. Some initiatives have begun to incorporate biological information, such as measuring biodiversity changes in response to temperature variations along elevation gradients, to forecast the effects of global climate change on biodiversity (Kharouba and Williams  2024 ). However, these initiatives and current biodiversity models often overlook species interactions, which are crucial for environmental performance and survival (Schleuning et al.  2020 ). Moreover, these initiatives and current biodiversity models rely heavily on past and present data about organisms and their relationships, especially long‐term field evaluations (Kharouba and Williams  2024 ).

 Conceptual model illustrating how climate change may influence cyanobacteria–cyanophage interactions and, consequently, cyanobacterial bloom dynamics. Cyanophages regulate cyanobacterial populations through top‐down control, but infection dynamics are strongly modulated by environmental drivers associated with global change, including warming, eutrophication, altered hydrological regimes, and their potential synergistic effects. The figure also highlights two complementary research priorities: (i) broad‐scale field studies, aimed at identifying long‐term ecological patterns and major environmental drivers across freshwater ecosystems, and (ii) detailed mechanistic studies, designed to elucidate the molecular, physiological, and ecological processes governing cyanobacteria–cyanophage interactions under environmentally realistic conditions. Our perspective highlights that the few long‐term studies on cyanobacteria–cyanophage interactions focus on marine ecosystems, with freshwater environments largely neglected (Carlson et al.  2022 ; Cai et al.  2024 ). This gap is especially important considering climate change, as viral infections are known to affect host mortality, community shifts, nutrient cycling, cyanotoxin levels, and host evolution, which in turn influence bloom formation, persistence, and toxicity. Although cyanobacteria–cyanophage interactions could play a key role in freshwater systems, they remain poorly understood, as current knowledge largely relies on marine studies and laboratory experiments. Consequently, fundamental questions remain regarding how environmental changes impact infection patterns, the roles of lytic and lysogenic cycles, and how cyanophages influence bloom succession, biodiversity, and toxin production. 
 Addressing these knowledge gaps will require a coordinated, multi‐scale research agenda (Figure  3 ). First, broad‐scale field studies are needed to characterize cyanobacteria–cyanophage interactions across environmental gradients and over long temporal scales, thereby identifying recurring patterns, key environmental drivers, and ecosystem‐specific responses. Second, mechanistic laboratory studies should investigate the molecular, physiological, and ecological processes underlying infection, host resistance, and viral life‐history strategies. Third, experimental approaches conducted under environmentally realistic conditions, including relevant temperature ranges, nutrient concentrations, and natural fluctuations, are essential to determine how climate change influences infection outcomes, host fitness trade‐offs, and bloom toxicity. Finally, ecological and eco‐evolutionary concepts such as Kill‐the‐Winner, Piggyback‐the‐Winner, and Red Queen dynamics should be explicitly incorporated into predictive frameworks aimed at forecasting bloom responses under future climate scenarios. 
 Ultimately, advancing our understanding of cyanobacteria–cyanophage interactions will require moving beyond correlative observations toward mechanistic and predictive approaches that integrate environmental, ecological, and evolutionary processes. Such efforts are essential not only for improving our understanding of freshwater ecosystem functioning but also for developing more robust strategies to predict, prevent, and mitigate harmful cyanobacterial blooms in a rapidly changing world.
