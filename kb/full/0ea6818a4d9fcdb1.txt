Abstract
Coffee is one of the most important global agricultural commodities and plays a key economic role in Ethiopia. However, 
productivity remains low in many coffee-growing areas due to limited use of improved technologies. This study assessed 
the current status of coffee production technologies in the Afalach watershed. From 113 total population 73 farmers were 
randomly selected based on their willingness and involvement in coffee production. Data were collected through semi-
structured questionnaires, field observations, transect walks, key informant interviews, and focus group discussions. Both 
quantitative and qualitative data were analyzed using SPSS software. The results showed that most farmers had only 1–10 
years of experience in coffee production, indicating that coffee farming is relatively recent in the area. The majority of 
households allocated less than 0.125 ha of land to coffee and produced yields below the national average (1 quintal per 
hectare). Most farmers relied on local coffee varieties and did not apply organic or inorganic fertilizers. Selective hand -
picking was the dominant harvesting method. The study highlights limited adoption of improved coffee technologies in the 
watershed. Promoting improved coffee varieties, strengthening farmer training on production practices, and encouraging 
compost preparation are recommended to enhance productivity.

1 Introduction
Coffee ( Coffea arabica L.) is one of the world’s most 
important agricultural commodities and plays a major role 
in Ethiopia’s economy and rural livelihoods. Ethiopia is the 
center of origin and genetic diversity of C. arabica , with 
diverse genetic resources and agro-ecological conditions 
that contribute to the distinctive quality of Ethiopian cof -
fee (Alemayehu et al., 2023). Recent studies also highlight 
the importance of conserving Ethiopia’s coffee genetic

of extension education, farmer-group membership, com -
munication technologies, and access to climate information 
in strengthening farmers’ access to agricultural information 
(Kaske et al., 2023). Similarly, recent research on coffee 
technology adoption in the Jimma area has applied a mul -
tivariate probit framework to examine factors associated 
with adoption of multiple coffee technologies (Temesgen & 
Debeb, 2024).
Coffee agroforestry systems can maintain tree cover and 
contribute to biodiversity conservation, soil fertility, car -
bon storage, and climate resilience (Chemeda et al., 2024). 
Recent experimental evidence also shows that canopy and 
fertilizer management influence soil nitrogen dynamics in 
coffee agroforestry systems (Kurniawan et al., 2024). More 
recently, research in southern Ethiopia found that structur -
ally complex coffee–enset agroforestry systems can main -
tain substantially greater carbon stocks than monocrop 
coffee systems (Gabiso et al., 2026).
The role of institutions and markets is equally important. 
Market information can affect coffee sales, marketed sur -
plus, and coffee revenue, particularly where market partici -
pation is relatively weak (Lee et al., 2025). Recent evidence 
from southwestern Ethiopia further shows that coffee mar -
ket supply is associated with production quantity, landhold-
ing size, livestock assets, non-farm income, and household 
characteristics (Yadeta et al., 2026).
The Afalach watershed provides an important case 
for understanding these issues because coffee production 
occurs within a mixed-farming and watershed-management 
context. However, information on the current status of cof -
fee production technologies and farm-level management 
practices in the watershed is limited.
Therefore, this study aimed to assess the current status of 
coffee production technology dissemination in the Afalach 
watershed, with particular emphasis on farmers’ experience, 
land allocation, productivity, planting materials, agronomic 
practices, soil fertility management, disease management, 
harvesting, drying, and handling practices. The study also 
discusses the implications of these practices for productiv -
ity, agroforestry sustainability, biodiversity, soil fertility, 
climate resilience, household food security, and market 
development.
2 Methodology
2.1 Description of the study area 
Assessment of the dissemination of coffee technology was 
done in the Afalach watershed at Shishoende district of Kaffa 
Zone. The geographical location of the study area is shown 
in Fig. 1. The area receives rainfall almost throughout the 
year, with the main rainy season extending from March to 
October. The mean annual rainfall is about 1877 mm, while 
the average minimum and maximum temperatures are 12 °C 
and 26 °C, respectively. The main economic activity of the 
local people is mixed farming system.Coffee production is 
a major component of the farming system and serves as the 
primary source of income for most households.
2.2 Research design and sampling procedure
A cross-sectional survey design was used to assess the dis -
semination of coffee production technologies. Both qualita-
tive and quantitative data collection methods were applied 
to ensure comprehensive analysis.
A total of 113 eligible coffee-producing farmers were 
identified in the study watershed based on predefined eli -
gibility criteria. After identifying eligible participants, The 
required sample size was determined using Yamane’s for -
mula (Yamane, 1967):

collected from the selected farmers using a structured ques-
tionnaire administered through face-to-face interviews. 
A semi-structured questionnaire, transect walks, and field 
visits (home gardens, cultivated fields), key informants and 
focus group discussions are used for collecting information 
from selected farmers. Data gathered from transect walks, 
field visits and key informants are used for provision and 
confirmation of the information obtained from the semi-
structured questionnaire.
2.3 Data collected
The questionnaire focused on: Household characteristics 
related to coffee production, Cropping systems and farm 
management practices, Coffee production technologies and 
packages used, Gender roles in coffee production, Seasonal 
calendar (planting, harvesting, and major constraints). Field 
observations were conducted to assess: Cropping patterns, 
Disease and insect pest incidence and Severity of produc -
tion constraints.

2.4 Data analysis
Quantitative and qualitative social survey data collected 
was coded and analyzed using IBM Statistical Package for 
Social Science (SPSS) software, version 20 (Field, 2024). 
Cross-tabulation tables are constructed, and descriptive sta-
tistics was generated to summarize data from the question -
naires.To make statistical inferences, descriptive statistics, 
frequencies and percentages are conducted to analyze the 
variables.
These methods are commonly used in agricultural and 
social science research to interpret survey data (Field, 2024).

a large proportion of farmers in the Afalach watershed have 
relatively limited experience in coffee production, which 
may affect their familiarity with improved management 
practices and production technologies. Recent evidence 
from Southwest Ethiopia shows that farmer characteris -

3.2 Land allocation for coffee production
As shown in Table 2, more than half of the farmers (52.1%) 
allocated less than 0.125 hectares for coffee production. 
Only a few farmers (4.1%) used larger plots.
This indicates that coffee is produced on a small scale, 
despite the area being suitable for coffee production. Recent 
evidence indicates considerable variation in the area allo -
cated to coffee production among Ethiopian coffee-growing 
households, reflecting differences in farming systems and 
local conditions (Tikuneh et al., 2023). Similarly, Girma 
Asefa and Ayalew Muluken (2024) reported that farm size 
is an important factor influencing the efficiency of small -
holder farmers. Therefore, the predominance of small cof -
fee plots in the study area may be associated with limited 
land availability and the need to allocate land to other crops. 
The difference may be due to better institutional support and 
market access in cooperative-based systems.
3.3 Coffee productivity
Table 3 shows that 45.2% of farmers produced 1 quintal of 
coffee per hectare, indicating relatively low productivity in 
the study area compared with the reported national average 
of 8.4 q/ha − 1 (Tikuneh et al., 2023). This low productiv -
ity may be related to limited adoption of improved coffee 
varieties and inefficient farm management practices. Recent 
evidence from Ethiopia indicates that factors such as access 
to extension services, inputs, and improved coffee variet -
ies influence coffee production (Tamirat & Tadele, 2023). 
Similarly, Tesfay (2023) reported that adoption of improved 
coffee varieties significantly increased coffee productivity 
among smallholder farmers. These findings suggest that 
the low productivity observed in the study area may partly 
reflect limited access to improved planting materials and 
inadequate adoption of recommended coffee production and 
management practices.
3.4 Coffee varieties and sources
Table 4 shows that 56.2% of farmers used local coffee vari-
eties, compared with 43.8% who used improved varieties, 
indicating relatively low adoption of improved varieties in 
the study area. Recent studies in Ethiopia found that adop -
tion of improved coffee varieties can increase productivity 
and is influenced by access to extension services, training, 
and improved planting materials (Tesfay, 2023). The rela -
tively high use of local varieties in the study area may there-
fore be associated with limited access to improved planting 
materials and support services.
The source of coffee varieties planted in the Afalach 
watershed is presented in Table 5. More than half of the 
respondents (56.16%) could not identify the source of their 
coffee varieties, while 37.0% obtained them from agricul -
tural offices, 5.47% from NGOs, and 1.37% from research 
centers. This pattern suggests limited farmer awareness and 
gaps in the dissemination of improved planting materials.

Table 6 indicates that 68.49% of farmers did not use the

adoption of improved coffee technologies in the study area.

3.6.3 Weed control, pruning, and disease management
Weed control was dominated by slashing (82.2%), while 
mulching (12.3%) and cover crops (5.5%) were rarely used 
(Table 12); similarly, only 24.7% of farmers practiced prun-
ing (Table 13). This limited adoption of improved man -
agement practices may constrain coffee productivity. This 
result is consistent with the finding of Daba et al. ( 2023), 
who reported the negative effects of weeds on coffee yield, 
and Mohammed and Worku (2025), who found that appro -
priate pruning significantly improved coffee yield and yield 
components in southwest Ethiopia.
The knowledge of stakeholders regarding coffee diseases 
was assessed and presented in Table 14 below. Diseases 
is one of yield reduction factor. From total of 73 (100%) 
respondents 45 (61.6%) of the respondent assess diseases 
and reported for respective bodies. The rest 28 (38.4%) 
farmers never assess diseases from their coffee plantation 
farm (Table 14).
3.6.4 Fertilizer use
Fertilizer use was generally low in the Afalach watershed: 
71.2% of farmers did not apply organic fertilizer, while 
only 28.8% used it, and all farmers reported that they did 
not use inorganic fertilizer (Table 15). This limited use of 
soil-fertility inputs may constrain coffee productivity, as 
Gebisa (2024) reported that combining coffee-husk organic 
fertilizer with mineral fertilizer significantly improved cof -
fee yield in southern Ethiopia. Similarly, Takala and Chali 
(2025), emphasized the importance of integrated soil-
fertility management for improving coffee productivity in 
Ethiopia.
3.7 Harvesting and post-harvest practices
3.7.1 Harvesting practices
As shown in Tables 9 and 167.3% of farmers practiced 
selective picking, while only 2.7% collected fallen or unripe 
cherries. This high use of selective harvesting is impor -
tant because picking ripe cherries helps reduce defects and 
maintain coffee quality.
Banti, & Atlaw, 2024,also emphasized the importance of 
using fully matured red-ripe cherries in coffee quality evalu-
ation in Jimma, Ethiopia. Thus, the high level of selective 
picking in the Afalach watershed indicates good adoption of 
a quality-oriented harvesting practice.
3.7.2 Drying practices

fee on locally constructed raised beds, while 21.9% dried 
it directly on the ground, 2.7% used wire-mesh beds, and 
6.8% sold fresh cherries without drying. Raised-bed dry -
ing is preferred because it improves air movement, reduces 
contact with soil and other contaminants, and helps achieve

3.7.3 Coffee handling practices
The study also examined materials used for handling and 
storage (Table 18). Sacks were the main coffee-handling 
material (43.8%), followed by bags (30.1%), plastic 
(13.7%), and other materials (12.3%) (Table 18). This result 
agrees with Ummeta ( 2025), who reported widespread use 
of jute sacks in southwestern Oromia. However, Eshete et 
al. (2024) found that hermetic PICS and GrainPro bags pre-
served coffee quality better than jute sacks during storage.
In contrast, Gutema et al. ( 2025) reported that 70.8% of 
farmers in Ari and South Omo used polyethylene bags, with 
49.1% experiencing mold during storage. These differences 
may be related to packaging availability and storage con -
ditions. Promoting clean, moisture-protective packaging, 
especially hermetic bags, could therefore help maintain cof-
fee quality in the Afalach watershed.
3.8 Coffee production systems and agroforestry 
context
Table 19 shows that garden coffee was the dominant pro -
duction system (68.5%), followed by semi-forest coffee 
(20.5%), forest coffee (6.8%), and plantation coffee (4.1%). 
The dominance of garden coffee indicates that farmers com-
monly grow coffee with enset and fruit crops around their 
homes. This agrees with Urugo et al. ( 2025), who reported 
that garden coffee is widely practiced in Ethiopia as a diver-
sified agroforestry system. Similarly, Kebebew and Ozanne 
(2024) highlighted the importance of forest-based coffee 
systems in southwestern Ethiopia. The low proportion of 
plantation coffee suggests that intensive, single-crop coffee 
production is less common in the watershed. Overall, coffee 
production in Afalach is mainly based on diversified agro -
forestry systems, which can support both farm livelihoods 
and environmental conservation.
3.9 Coffee marketing and market information
Table 20 shows that local collectors/traders were the main 
coffee marketing channel (60.3%), followed by private 
washing stations (19.2%), cooperatives (13.7%), and local 
consumers (6.8%), while none sold directly to wholesal -
ers. The high dependence on local traders indicates limited 
use of alternative market outlets. This finding agrees with 
Mihret (2026), who examined coffee farmers’ market out -
let choice in Gimbo Woreda, Kaffa Zone, Southwest Ethi -
opia, and showed that farmers’ choice of market outlet is 
influenced by factors related to market access and farmer 
characteristics. Therefore, strengthening cooperatives and 
improving farmers’ access to market information and direct 
buyers could help increase their bargaining power and mar-
keting benefits.

coffee production. However, the overall productivity is very 
low due to several factors. These include limited farming 
experience, small land allocation, low use of improved

Most farmers rely on traditional methods and do not 
follow recommended agronomic practices such as proper 
spacing, pruning, fertilizer use, and disease management. 
In addition, the absence of a clear production calendar and 
limited awareness of improved technologies further reduce 
productivity.
To improve coffee production in the area, a combined 
approach is needed. Farmers should be supported through 
regular training on improved coffee production practices, 
including pruning, compost preparation, and disease con -
trol. Improved coffee varieties should be widely distrib -
uted and demonstrated under local conditions to increase 
adoption.
Extension services need to be strengthened to provide 
clear guidance on planting schedules, spacing, and farm 
management practices. Awareness programs should also 
be conducted to improve farmers’ knowledge of pests and 
diseases.
Furthermore, promoting the use of organic fertilizers 
and improving post-harvest handling practices will help 
increase both yield and quality. Coordinated efforts among 
agricultural offices, research institutions, and development 
organizations are essential to bring sustainable improve -
ments in coffee production.
