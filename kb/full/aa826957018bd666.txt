Probiotics as a Promising Strategy for Preventing Urinary 
Tract Infections: Mechanisms, Efficacy, and Clinical 
Evidence
Temiyefunolu Akuchukwu Ngwu1, Oluwaseun Oyetunji Olasunkanmi1, Victor Akpe2, Ian Edwin Cock2,*
¹Department of Microbiology, Faculty of Science, Lagos State University, Lagos, NIGERIA.

ABSTRACT
Urinary tract infections (UTIs) remain one of the most prevalent bacterial infections globally, 
with Escherichia coli accounting for most cases and women disproportionately affected due to 
anatomical and hormonal factors. Recurrent UTIs, which occur in up to 25% of initially affected 
women, continue to impose a substantial clinical burden despite the widespread use of antibiotics. 
However, increasing antimicrobial resistance has exposed the limitations of antibiotic-centric 
management and intensified interest in alternative strategies such as probiotics. Although 
strains including Lactobacillus crispatus, L. rhamnosus , and L. reuteri are frequently cited as 
promising candidates, the evidence supporting their efficacy remains heterogeneous and often 
strain-specific. Proposed mechanisms ranging from competitive exclusion and antimicrobial 
metabolite production to pH modulation and immune enhancement are biologically plausible, 
yet their consistency in vivo is not fully established. Clinical studies suggest that probiotics, 
particularly vaginally administered L. crispatus, may reduce recurrence by restoring urogenital 
microbial balance; however, variability in colonisation success, dosing regimens, and product 
standardisation complicates interpretation. Despite these limitations, probiotics offer theoretical 
advantages over antibiotics, including reduced adverse effects and no contribution to resistance. 
Future progress will depend on refining strain selection, optimising delivery systems, and 
strengthening mechanistic and clinical evidence. Overall, probiotics represent a potentially 
valuable adjunct or alternative for UTI prevention, but their widespread adoption requires more 
rigorous, standardised, and mechanistically informed evaluation.

resistance, Urobiome.
INTRODUCTION
Urinary tract infections (UTIs) remain among the most prevalent 
bacterial infections globally and continue to impose a substantial 
clinical and economic burden despite decades of research 
and therapeutic refinement. 1 Although UTIs are traditionally 
described as resulting from the ascension of intestinal bacteria 
into the urinary tract, this simplified model does not fully capture 
the complexity of host-microbe interactions or the variability in 
disease susceptibility.2 The infection can involve any segment of the 
urinary system, yet the lower tract is disproportionately affected, 
reflecting both microbial dynamics and host anatomical factors. 
Whilst Escherichia coli remains the dominant uropathogen, the 
involvement of Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis , and 
Staphylococcus saprophyticus underscores the polymicrobial and 
context-dependent nature of UTI pathogenesis.3
The disproportionate burden of UTIs in women is well 
recognised, but the underlying mechanisms, ranging from 
anatomical predisposition to hormonal modulation of the 
urogenital microbiota are still incompletely understood. Notably, 
up to 25% of women experience recurrence following an initial 
episode, highlighting persistent gaps in preventive strategies. 4 
Although antibiotic prophylaxis can reduce recurrence by as 
much as 85%, 5 its long-term utility is increasingly undermined 
by escalating antimicrobial resistance. The emergence of resistant 
strains, driven by both clinical overuse and ecological disruption 
of commensal microbiota, has complicated treatment algorithms 
and reduced the reliability of standard therapies. 6,7 These 
limitations have catalysed interest in non-antibiotic interventions, 
with probiotics frequently positioned as a biologically plausible 
alternative or adjunct.8

Probiotics, defined as live microorganisms that confer health 
benefits when administered in adequate amounts, 9 have 
gained traction due to their potential to modulate microbial 
communities and influence mucosal immunity. 10 However, the 
evidence supporting their role in UTI prevention is nuanced. 
While strains such as Lactobacillus crispatus have demonstrated 
promising reductions in recurrence, particularly when used 
vaginally or in combination with antibiotics, 11 outcomes remain 
highly strain-specific and dependent on successful colonisation. 
Furthermore, the assumption that probiotics uniformly 
restore microbial balance or inhibit uropathogen colonisation 
oversimplifies the complex ecological and immunological 
processes involved.12
This review critically evaluates the mechanistic rationale, 
clinical evidence, and translational challenges associated with 
probiotic-based UTI prevention. By interrogating both the 
strengths and limitations of current research, we aim to clarify 
the potential role of probiotics as adjuncts or alternatives to 
antibiotics, identify gaps that hinder clinical adoption, and outline 
future directions necessary to advance probiotic strategies within 
the broader context of antimicrobial resistance and urogenital 
health.
The Growing Challenge of Antimicrobial Resistance 
in UTI Management 
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as one of the 
most formidable obstacles in contemporary UTI management, 
fundamentally undermining the reliability of conventional 
therapeutic strategies. 13 Although the link between antibiotic 
misuse and resistance is well established, the persistence 
and acceleration of resistance patterns suggest that current 
stewardship efforts remain insufficient. The combined effects of 
inappropriate prescribing, incomplete treatment courses, and the 
efficient horizontal transfer of resistance genes have facilitated the 
rapid dissemination of multidrug-resistant uropathogens across 
diverse clinical settings.14 This trend is particularly concerning for 
UTIs, where E. coli, the predominant pathogen, has increasingly 
acquired resistance to multiple frontline antibiotics, thereby 
diminishing the effectiveness of standard empirical regimens.15
The growing prevalence of resistant UTIs has, paradoxically, 
intensified reliance on broad-spectrum antibiotics, despite 
their well-documented drawbacks. Whilst these agents may 
offer short-term therapeutic benefit, their use often exacerbates 
the very resistance pressures they are intended to overcome 
and exposes patients to significant adverse effects. 16 Moreover, 
broad-spectrum therapy indiscriminately disrupts the gut 
microbiota, a disruption that not only contributes to dysbiosis but 
also heightens susceptibility to subsequent infections, creating a 
self-perpetuating cycle of antimicrobial exposure and microbial 
imbalance.17
Clinically, antibiotic-resistant UTIs are associated with higher 
rates of treatment failure, prolonged hospitalisation, and 
increased healthcare costs, reflecting both the direct burden of 
resistant infections and the indirect consequences of limited 
therapeutic options.18 In cases where resistance severely restricts 
available treatments, clinicians are often forced to resort to 
combination regimens, escalated dosing, or last-line antibiotics. 
These approaches carry substantial toxicity risks and may further 
accelerate resistance development, highlighting the fragility of 
current treatment paradigms. 19 These challenges highlight the 
urgent need for alternative or adjunctive strategies capable of 
reducing antibiotic dependence and mitigating the escalating 
threat of AMR in UTI management.
Insights into the Urinary Microbiome: Composition 
and Function 
The concept of a urinary microbiome or urobiome has 
fundamentally reshaped long-standing assumptions about 
urinary tract sterility, but the understanding of this microbial 
community remains incomplete and methodologically 
constrained.20 For decades, urine was presumed sterile, a belief 
rooted more in the limitations of culture-based diagnostics than 
in biological reality.21 The advent of high-throughput sequencing 
technologies has overturned this paradigm by revealing a diverse, 
low-biomass microbial ecosystem within the urinary tract. 22 
However, despite these technological advances, the field is still 
in an early developmental stage. Current datasets are limited by 
small cohort sizes, inconsistent sampling methods, and a lack 
of representation from clinically relevant subgroups, including 
individuals with systemic diseases or recurrent UTIs. 23 As a 
result, the true diversity, stability, and functional significance of 
the urinary microbiota remain only partially characterised.
Compared with other microbial niches such as the gut, the urinary 
microbiota is markedly less abundant and less diverse, with 
typical bacterial loads in women ranging from 10⁴ to 10⁵ CFU/
ml, several orders of magnitude lower than the gut microbiome.24 
This low-biomass environment complicates both detection 
and interpretation, increasing susceptibility to contamination 
and methodological artefacts. Nonetheless, emerging evidence 
indicates that the urobiome exhibits structured variation 
influenced by gender, race, and geography. Firmicutes dominate 
in both sexes, though with notable quantitative differences, 
followed by Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria. 25 
Gender-specific patterns further highlight the biological 
relevance of this community: Corynebacterium and Streptococcus 
are more prevalent in men, whereas Lactobacillus predominates 
in women, reflecting hormonal and anatomical influences on 
microbial composition.26 However, these observations are largely 
descriptive, and the extent to which such differences translate 
into functional or clinical consequences remains insufficiently 
explored.

Functionally, the urinary microbiota appears to contribute to 
mucosal homeostasis, but mechanistic insights are still emerging. 
Beneficial genera such as Lactobacillus and Streptococcus possess 
adaptations that facilitate colonisation, including type 1 fimbriae 
for urothelial adhesion and glycosaminoglycan-degrading 
enzymes that enhance nutrient acquisition.27 These traits suggest 
an active ecological role rather than passive presence. However, 
the degree to which these mechanisms operate in vivo and how 
they interact with host immunity, urine chemistry, and pathogen 
dynamics remains poorly defined. Environmental factors such as 
pH, oxygen tension, and nutrient availability undoubtedly shape 
microbial behaviour, yet their specific contributions to urobiome 
stability and resilience are not well quantified.28
Current evidence supports the existence of a structured urinary 
microbiome with potential relevance to urinary tract health, 
but substantial gaps persist. A more nuanced understanding of 
its composition, functional capacity, and interactions with host 
physiology is essential before the urobiome can be fully integrated 
into diagnostic or therapeutic frameworks.
The Role of Beneficial Bacteria in Urinary Tract 
Health 
The urinary tract plays a central role in maintaining systemic 
homeostasis through the elimination of metabolic waste, but 
its microbial ecology has only recently been recognised as a 
contributor to urogenital health. 29 Although a balanced urinary 
microbiota is frequently described as essential for maintaining pH 
stability, epithelial integrity, and immune readiness, much of this 
understanding is extrapolated from higher-biomass niches such 
as the gut and vagina. Consequently, the specific contributions of 
urinary commensals remain less clearly defined than commonly 
assumed.
Beneficial genera, including Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, 
Streptococcus, and Staphylococcus, are often cited as key 
components of a healthy urinary ecosystem, but their roles are 
supported by uneven and sometimes indirect evidence. 30 For 
instance, Lactobacillus is widely regarded as protective due to its 
acidifying and antimicrobial properties, but its prevalence and 
functional dominance vary substantially across individuals and 
demographic groups. Similarly, the presence of Bifidobacterium 
and Streptococcus in the urinary tract is documented, but their 
mechanistic contributions to pathogen suppression or immune 
modulation remain poorly characterised. Even Staphylococcus 
species, typically associated with pathogenicity, may include 
commensal strains whose ecological roles are not fully understood.
Overall, whilst the concept of “beneficial bacteria” in the urinary 
tract is biologically plausible and supported by emerging evidence, 
the field still lacks comprehensive functional studies capable 
of distinguishing correlation from causation. A more rigorous 
understanding of how these taxa contribute to urinary tract 
resilience and how their disruption predisposes individuals to 
infection is essential for advancing microbiome-based preventive 
strategies.
Lactobacillus: A Key Guardian of Urinary Tract Health 
Lactobacillus species are the most abundant beneficial bacteria in 
the female urinary microbiota in maintaining a healthy urinary 
tract.31 These bacteria produce lactic acid, which lowers the 
pH of the urine, creating an acidic environment that is hostile 
to uropathogens such as Escherichia coli , the leading cause of 
UTIs.32 Additionally, Lactobacillus species secrete bacteriocins 
and other antimicrobial compounds that inhibit the adhesion and 
proliferation of harmful bacteria.
Lactobacillus species also contribute to urinary tract health 
through biofilm formation. These biofilms act as physical barriers 
on the epithelial lining of the urinary tract, preventing pathogenic 
bacteria from adhering to and colonising the area.33 Furthermore, 
Lactobacillus stimulates the production of immunoglobulins and 
other immune responses, enhancing the body's ability to combat 
infections.34 However, not all Lactobacillus species are associated 
with a healthy microbiota. For instance, whilst Lactobacillus 
crispatus is linked to a state of health, other species, such as 
Lactobacillus gasseri , have been associated with pathological 
conditions like urgency urinary incontinence (UUI). 35 A 
reduction in Lactobacillus abundance has been correlated with 
an increased risk of uropathogen Colonisation and subsequent 
infections.36
Maintaining Microbial Balance in the Urinary Tract 
The maintenance of a balanced urinary microbiota is widely 
regarded as essential for urogenital health, but the mechanisms 
governing this balance and the factors that disrupt it remain 
incompletely understood. Dysbiosis, broadly defined as a 
disturbance in the composition or function of the microbial 
community, is frequently attributed to antibiotic exposure, 
hygiene practices, and hormonal fluctuations, but the relative 
contribution of each factor is still debated.37 Antibiotics, although 
indispensable for treating acute infections, exert broad and 
often indiscriminate effects on microbial communities. Their 
capacity to eliminate both pathogenic and beneficial bacteria can 
precipitate substantial shifts in microbial diversity, weakening 
colonisation resistance and creating ecological niches that 
favour the expansion of opportunistic or antibiotic-resistant 
organisms.38 This unintended consequence highlights a paradox 
in UTI management that the very agents use to treat infection 
may predispose individuals to future episodes by destabilising the 
urinary and adjacent microbiota.39
Hormonal influences add an additional layer of complexity. 
Fluctuations associated with pregnancy, menopause, and the 
menstrual cycle can alter the physicochemical environment 
of the urinary tract, with downstream effects on microbial 
composition.40 For example, estrogen deficiency during

Dysbiosis and Increased Susceptibility to UTIs 
Dysbiosis of the urinary microbiota is strongly associated with 
heightened susceptibility to UTIs, yet the causal pathways linking 
microbial imbalance to infection risk remain only partially 
understood. A healthy urinary microbiota, typically enriched 
with protective taxa such as Lactobacillus, is thought to inhibit 
uropathogen colonisation through multiple mechanisms, but 
much of this evidence is correlative rather than mechanistically 
resolved.43 Declines in Lactobacillus abundance are frequently 
interpreted as a loss of protective capacity, particularly given 
the associated reduction in lactic acid and hydrogen peroxide 
production, both of which contribute to an acidic and 
antimicrobial urinary environment. However, the extent to 
which these metabolites exert sustained in vivo effects remains 
uncertain, especially in the context of urine flow and dilution.44
Microbial imbalance may also compromise mucosal immunity, 
with dysbiosis linked to reduced production of antimicrobial 
peptides and immunoglobulins that normally help neutralise 
invading bacteria. 45 However, the directionality of this 
relationship—whether dysbiosis weakens immunity or impaired 
immunity drives dysbiosis—has not been fully established. 
Inflammatory responses triggered by microbial disruption 
may further exacerbate vulnerability, creating a permissive 
environment for pathogen ascension and persistence. 46 
Additionally, the loss of beneficial bacteria may destabilise 
protective biofilm structures on the uroepithelium, increasing 
epithelial exposure to pathogen adhesion and invasion.47 Despite 
these proposed mechanisms, direct evidence demonstrating 
stable, functional biofilms formed by commensal species in vivo 
remains limited.

Addressing Dysbiosis through Probiotics 
Probiotics are increasingly promoted as a strategy to restore 
microbial balance and reduce UTI risk, however, the 
evidence supporting their efficacy remains variable and often 
strain-dependent. The rationale for their use is grounded in 
the assumption that reintroducing beneficial bacteria can 
reconstitute key protective functions of the urinary microbiota, 
including lactic acid production, biofilm formation, and immune 
modulation but the extent to which these mechanisms operate 
reliably in vivo is still under investigation. 48 Whilst these 
proposed functions are biologically plausible, many studies rely 
on indirect markers of microbial restoration rather than direct 
demonstration of functional recovery within the urinary tract.
Probiotics are also positioned as a countermeasure to 
antibiotic-induced dysbiosis, with the potential to mitigate 
microbiota disruption and limit the emergence of resistant 
strains through competitive exclusion of uropathogens. 49 
However, the degree to which probiotics can meaningfully 
reverse antibiotic-associated microbial shifts remains uncertain, 
particularly given the transient colonisation observed with 
many strains and the variability in host receptivity. Moreover, 
the assumption that probiotics uniformly promote competitive 
exclusion oversimplifies the complex ecological interactions that 
govern microbial community structure.
Despite these limitations, ongoing research into strain 
optimisation, dosing strategies, and delivery systems continues 
to refine the therapeutic potential of probiotics. Advances in 
formulation and targeted delivery may enhance colonisation 
efficiency and mechanistic consistency, addressing some of the 
shortcomings observed in earlier studies. As such, probiotics 
represent a promising but still evolving adjunct or alternative to 
conventional antibiotic therapies. Their successful integration 
into UTI prevention frameworks will depend on more rigorous 
mechanistic validation, standardised clinical protocols, and 
clearer identification of patient populations most likely to benefit.
Probiotics: Mechanisms of Action in UTI Prevention 
The proposed mechanisms through which probiotics prevent 
UTIs are diverse and conceptually compelling, but the strength of 
empirical support varies considerably across studies. Probiotics 
are often described as acting through direct antagonism of 
uropathogens, modulation of host immune responses, and 
restoration of microbial balance within the urinary and vaginal 
microbiota.50 Whilst these mechanisms provide a biologically 
plausible framework, much of the evidence remains fragmented, 
strain-specific, or derived from experimental systems that do 
not fully replicate the complexity of the human urogenital 
environment.

Direct interactions with uropathogens, such as competitive

pathways. However, the consistency and durability of these 
effects in vivo remain uncertain, particularly given the transient 
colonisation observed with many probiotic strains. Similarly, 
claims of immune modulation, including enhanced mucosal 
immunity and increased production of antimicrobial peptides, 
are supported primarily by preclinical data, leaving important 
gaps regarding their clinical relevance and reproducibility in 
human populations.
The restoration of microbial balance is often positioned as a 
central mechanism; however, this concept assumes a level of 
ecological stability and host receptivity that may not be universally 
present. The urinary and vaginal microbiota are dynamic systems 
influenced by hormonal, behavioural, and environmental factors, 
and the ability of probiotics to meaningfully reshape these 
communities remains variable. Moreover, the extent to which 
microbiota restoration directly translates into reduced UTI risk 
has not been conclusively demonstrated.
Overall, whilst probiotics exhibit multiple mechanistic pathways 
that could theoretically reduce UTI incidence and mitigate 
antibiotic resistance, the current evidence base is still evolving. A 
more rigorous, mechanistically grounded understanding of these 
pathways is essential for determining which probiotic strains, 
delivery routes, and dosing strategies are most likely to yield 
clinically meaningful benefits.
Competitive Exclusion: Blocking Pathogen 
Adherence 
Competitive exclusion is frequently cited as a central mechanism 
through which probiotics may prevent UTIs, yet the robustness 
and consistency of this mechanism in vivo remain subjects of 
ongoing debate. The concept that beneficial bacteria outcompete 
uropathogens for adhesion sites and nutrients is biologically 
plausible and supported by in vitro observations, particularly 
against E. coli , the predominant uropathogen. 51 Lactobacillus 
species are often highlighted for their ability to bind uroepithelial 
receptors, thereby occupying niches that would otherwise 
be exploited by pathogens. However, the extent to which 
these interactions occur reliably within the dynamic urinary 
environment is not fully established, and evidence of sustained 
epithelial colonisation remains limited.52

properties mediated by surface proteins and exopolysaccharides, 
which may facilitate the formation of protective biofilms. These 
biofilms are proposed to stabilise the microbial community and

Antimicrobial Activity: Neutralizing Uropathogens 
Probiotics are widely described as exerting antimicrobial effects 
through the production of compounds such as bacteriocins, 
hydrogen peroxide, and lactic acid, but the strength and consistency 
of these effects in vivo remain incompletely characterised. 54 
Whilst these metabolites can inhibit uropathogen growth 
under controlled laboratory conditions, their concentrations 
and stability within the urinary tract, an environment marked 
by continuous dilution, variable pH, and rapid turnover are far 
less certain. The commonly cited role of lactic acid in lowering 
urinary pH, for example, may overstate its physiological impact, 
given that urine acidity is influenced by host metabolism and diet 
as much as by microbial activity.
In addition to acidification, some probiotic strains are reported 
to secrete anti-adhesion metabolites capable of interfering with 
pathogen attachment mechanisms, including fimbriae and pili 
used by E. coli and other Uropathogens.55 Although these findings 
provide a compelling mechanistic rationale, most supporting 
evidence derives from in vitro assays that do not fully replicate 
the complexity of host-pathogen interactions in the urinary tract. 
The extent to which these anti-adhesion effects translate into 
meaningful clinical protection therefore remains uncertain.
Whilst antimicrobial metabolite production represents a plausible 
pathway through which probiotics may neutralise uropathogens, 
the current evidence base is limited by experimental constraints 
and a lack of robust in vivo validation. More rigorous studies 
are needed to determine whether these mechanisms operate at 
physiologically relevant levels and contribute substantively to 
UTI prevention.

Immune System Modulation: Enhancing Host 
Defenses 
Immune modulation is frequently proposed as a key mechanism 
through which probiotics may enhance host defence against UTIs. 
Again, much of the supporting evidence remains preliminary and 
derived from experimental systems rather than robust clinical 
studies. Probiotics are reported to influence both innate and 
adaptive immune responses, potentially strengthening mucosal 
defences against Uropathogens. 56 For example, certain strains 
can stimulate the production of antimicrobial peptides such as 
defensins and cathelicidins, which disrupt bacterial membranes. 
They may also enhance the activity of phagocytic cells, including 
macrophages and neutrophils, thereby promoting more efficient 
clearance of invading pathogens. However, the extent to which 
these immunomodulatory effects occur at physiologically 
meaningful levels within the urinary tract remains uncertain.
Interactions between probiotics and toll-like receptors (TLRs) 
on uroepithelial cells are also proposed to trigger cytokine 
release and immune cell recruitment, theoretically bolstering 
early responses to infection. Similarly, increased secretion of 
secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) has been suggested as a 
mechanism by which probiotics could neutralise pathogens and 
prevent epithelial adhesion.57 However, most evidence for these 
pathways originates from in vitro or animal models, and direct 
confirmation in human urinary tissues is limited. Moreover, the 
immune effects of probiotics are highly strain-specific, raising 
questions about the generalisability of these findings.
Probiotics are also thought to modulate T-cell responses, 
potentially reducing excessive inflammation that can damage 
uroepithelial tissues and facilitate infection. 58 Whilst this 
anti-inflammatory potential is appealing, the balance between 
protective and suppressive immune modulation is delicate and 
insufficiently characterised in the context of UTIs. Without 
clearer mechanistic evidence, it remains difficult to determine 
whether these immunological effects contribute meaningfully to 
clinical outcomes.
Overall, although immune modulation represents a plausible 
mechanism of probiotic action, the current evidence base is 
limited by reliance on preclinical models and a lack of direct in vivo 
validation. More rigorous human studies are needed to clarify the 
extent, consistency, and clinical relevance of probiotic-mediated 
immune enhancement in UTI prevention.

strain-specific and often transient, raising questions about the 
long-term stability of probiotic-induced microbial changes.
In addition to their ecological effects, probiotics produce 
metabolites such as lactic acid, which may contribute to 
pathogen suppression by lowering urinary pH and creating a 
less favourable environment for Uropathogens.61,62 However, the 
physiological relevance of these pH-modulating effects in vivo 
is not fully established, particularly given the dynamic nature 
of urine production and flow. As such, the contribution of lactic 
acid to sustained pathogen inhibition may be more limited than 
commonly assumed.

an appealing, systems-level framework for UTI prevention, 
current evidence remains largely associative and mechanistic 
validation is incomplete. Probiotics may indeed support microbial 
recovery and reduce dysbiosis-associated vulnerability, but their 
effectiveness depends on strain selection, host factors, and 
delivery strategies that are not yet optimised. A more rigorous

DISCUSSION
Probiotics continue to attract interest as a potential strategy for 
UTI prevention, yet the evidence supporting their use requires 
careful interpretation. Although the urinary tract harbours a 
structured microbiome with demonstrable roles in mucosal 
integrity and immune regulation, 63 the functional significance 
of many detected taxa remains incompletely understood. As 
illustrated in Figure 1, the urinary microbiome is less diverse than 
the gut microbiome, but includes genera such as Lactobacillus, 
Gardnerella, Streptococcus , and Corynebacterium, which may 
contribute to epithelial barrier maintenance and pathogen 
exclusion.64,65 However, the stability of this community is 
highly sensitive to perturbations, including antibiotic exposure, 
hormonal fluctuations, and pathogen invasion, highlighting the 
fragility of urinary tract homeostasis and the potential value of 
interventions that support microbial resilience.66
Figure 2 summarises the mechanistic pathways through which 
Lactobacillus species may counteract dysbiosis, including 
competitive exclusion, acidification, bacteriocin production, 
immune modulation, and interference with pathogenic biofilms.67 
Whilst these mechanisms provide a coherent biological rationale, 
their consistency in vivo remains variable, and many studies 
rely on surrogate markers rather than direct demonstration of 
functional effects within the urinary tract. The distinction between 
mechanistic plausibility and clinical reliability is therefore critical 
when evaluating probiotic efficacy.
The mode of probiotic administration further complicates 
interpretation. As shown in Figure 3, oral and vaginal delivery 
routes differ markedly in their mechanistic pathways and practical 
limitations. Oral probiotics offer convenience and may influence 
the gut-urinary axis, but their indirect action and susceptibility 
to gastrointestinal degradation can delay or diminish therapeutic 
impact.68 Vaginal delivery provides more targeted exposure but 
raises challenges related to colonisation consistency and patient 
acceptability. Figure 4 reinforces the importance of microbial 
balance by depicting the progression from eubiosis to dysbiosis, 
acute infection, and recurrence. 69,70 This cyclical pattern 
underscores the need for interventions that restore, rather than 
further disrupt, microbial ecology.

of probiotic therapy beyond conventional strains. Figure 5 
highlights innovations such as engineered probiotics expressing

comparisons with antibiotic prophylaxis. 76,77 Future research 
should prioritise personalised probiotic therapies informed by 
urinary and vaginal microbiome profiling, combination strategies 
integrating probiotics with non-antibiotic interventions such as 
D-mannose or immunomodulators, and long-term safety and 
efficacy assessments of engineered strains and advanced delivery

probiotic benefits are generalisable or context-specific.79-81
In summary, the integrated evidence presented in this review 
demonstrates that probiotics represent a biologically plausible 
and increasingly sophisticated approach to UTI prevention. 
Their capacity to restore microbial balance, inhibit uropathogens, 
and modulate host immunity, combined with advances in 
delivery technologies positions them as a viable complement 
or alternative to antibiotic prophylaxis. However, realising their 
full potential will depend on continued innovation, rigorous 
mechanistic validation, and well-designed clinical trials capable 
of resolving current uncertainties and guiding evidence-based 
implementation.

CONCLUSION
Probiotics have emerged as a biologically plausible and 
increasingly sophisticated strategy for preventing recurrent 
urinary tract infections, supported by growing evidence that 
the urinary, vaginal, and gut microbiota play integral roles in 
maintaining urogenital health. Their capacity to restore microbial 
balance, inhibit uropathogen colonisation, and modulate host 
immune responses positions them as a compelling complement 
or, in selected cases, an alternative to antibiotic prophylaxis. 
However, the clinical effectiveness of probiotic interventions 
remains influenced by multiple factors, including strain 
specificity, dosing regimen, delivery route, and host-dependent 
microbial ecology. As highlighted in recent comparative analyses, 
the choice between oral and vaginal administration should be 
guided by individual clinical needs and the underlying drivers 
of dysbiosis, with oral delivery offering systemic modulation and 
convenience, and vaginal delivery providing more targeted action 
for localised microbial disruption.
Advances in biotechnology are accelerating the evolution 
of probiotic therapies beyond conventional formulations. 
Microencapsulation platforms that enhance viability and 
sustained release, along with engineered strains capable of 
expressing anti-adhesive or antimicrobial molecules, offer 
promising avenues for improving colonisation efficiency, 
mechanistic consistency, and therapeutic durability. These 
innovations address long-standing limitations associated with 
probiotic survival, mucosal adherence, and variability in clinical 
outcomes, and they hold the potential to significantly strengthen 
the role of probiotics in UTI prevention.
Realising this potential will require rigorous, standardised 
clinical trials, microbiome-guided patient stratification, and 
long-term safety assessments, particularly in populations 
with heightened vulnerability to recurrent UTIs. Integrating 
probiotics with other non-antibiotic strategies, such as 
D-mannose, immunomodulators, or targeted vaginal delivery 
systems may further enhance therapeutic outcomes. As the 
field advances, probiotics are poised to contribute meaningfully 
to a more sustainable, microbiome-informed approach to UTI 
management, reducing reliance on antibiotics and helping to 
mitigate the global challenge of antimicrobial resistance.
