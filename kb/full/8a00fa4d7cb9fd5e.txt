Excessive alcohol consumption among the male labor force poses a significant threat to public health, human capital development, and sustainable economic growth. Rising educated unemployment, vulnerable employment, economic uncertainty, and environmental stressors have intensified concerns regarding alcohol-related health risks worldwide. This study examines the determinants, causal dynamics, and long-run implications of alcohol consumption across 84 countries. Using Panel Robust Least Squares (PRLS), Granger causality analysis, and an Innovation Accounting Matrix, the study provides comprehensive evidence on the socioeconomic and environmental drivers of alcohol use. The findings reveal that educated unemployment, vulnerable employment, and human capital deterioration significantly increase alcohol consumption, whereas economic growth reduces alcohol use by expanding economic opportunities and alleviating socioeconomic distress. The results further suggest that labor market vulnerabilities and environmental degradation exacerbate alcohol-related risks across countries. Granger causality estimates indicate unidirectional causal relationships running from vulnerable employment to alcohol consumption and human capital formation, from economic growth to alcohol consumption, and from alcohol consumption, vulnerable employment, and economic growth to carbon emissions. Moreover, dynamic forecasting results demonstrate that socioeconomic and environmental stressors exert persistent yet heterogeneous effects on alcohol consumption over time. The study concludes that reducing labor market precarity, strengthening human capital, promoting inclusive economic growth, and enforcing effective environmental policies are essential for mitigating alcohol-related risks and advancing global health and sustainability objectives. These findings offer valuable policy insights for enhancing workforce well-being and fostering long-term socioeconomic resilience.
