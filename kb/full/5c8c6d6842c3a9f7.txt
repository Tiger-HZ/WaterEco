DigVentures was invited by Pro Corda (an educational charity providing ensemble musical training for young people) to undertake a crowdfunded community-based archaeological research project at Leiston Abbey. This report presents the comprehensive findings of a five-year community-based archaeological research project conducted by DigVentures at two sites associated with Leiston Abbey: the original foundation at Minsmere Nature Reserve (Site 1 - Scheduled Monument 1015687) and the later foundation at Theberton (Site 2 - Scheduled Monument 1014520). The project comprised four years of fieldwork (2013-2016) followed by analysis and reporting. The primary aim was to define and characterise the physical extent and condition of both sites through integrated non-intrusive investigations and targeted excavation to facilitate their future management. Specific objectives included identifying 'lost' structures, understanding the Abbey's water management system, refining chronology and phasing, assessing preservation conditions, and investigating linkages between the two sites. Using a mixed methodology of remote sensing, metric survey, augur survey, and targeted excavation, the project recorded all data through a web-accessible relational database (Digital Dig Team system). Specialist analyses were conducted on faunal remains, ceramics, building materials, lithics, registered finds, and environmental samples. Key Findings At Minsmere (Site 1), investigation of the claustral precinct provided evidence of the original construction phase, demolition, and reuse. An augur survey identified evidence indicative of a small estuary or channel nearby, crucial to understanding why flooding eventually necessitated relocation. Archaeological features showed good preservation despite some agricultural disturbance. At Leiston Abbey (Site 2), remote sensing and excavation successfully identified several 'lost' structures including the infirmary and the original gatehouse. Investigation of earthworks revealed a moat and extensive water management systems, suggesting the site incorporated existing landscape features rather than being built on a blank canvas. This challenges traditional assumptions about Premonstratensian isolation. The project established a refined chronology spanning from Mesolithic flint work through the Medieval occupation of both sites to post-dissolution adaptation and modern use. Analysis of building materials indicated the high status of the original Abbey, which employed cutting-edge brick and tile technology. The unusually high proportion of imported pottery and presence of elite food remains (deer, rabbit, swan) suggested the monastery functioned as more than just a place of worship and seclusion. Comparative analysis with other Premonstratensian sites revealed both conformity to standard layouts (as with the kitchen's position near the refectory) and adaptations to local conditions (such as extensive water management systems).
