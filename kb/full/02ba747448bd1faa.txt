sediment blue carbon assessment in the Western Irish Sea
Anthony Grey · Ciara Ní Mhaoldomhnaigh · 
Mark Chatting · Phoebe Walsh · Alan Lee · 
Brian Kelleher · Mark Coughlan

Abstract Continental-shelf sediments (< 200  m 
water depth; ~ 9% of the global seafloor) store large 
amounts of organic carbon (OC) and accumulate OC 
annually, but management and accounting applications 
require regional evidence and transparent uncertainty. 
Loss-on-ignition (LOI) is a rapid, low-cost proxy 
for organic matter and OC, but LOI-to- total organic 
carbon (TOC) conversions vary with sediment type 
and analytical protocol. This study used multiagency 
Western Irish Sea data to test whether region- and 
sediment-specific LOI-to-TOC regressions improve 
OC estimation relative to a pooled conversion. Paired 
LOI and TOC measurements were strongly corre-
lated (R 2 = 0.948). Structural-break and analysis of 
covariance (ANCOVA) tests identified changes in the 
LOI-TOC relationship associated with mud content, 
iron (Fe) concentration and water depth. Splitting 
samples into high-mud and low-mud groups produced 
group-specific models (R 2 = 0.97 and 0.72, respec-
tively) and improved fit for high-mud sediments, while 
the low-mud model retained greater uncertainty. The 
results support the use of separate sediment-class-spe-
cific LOI-to-TOC calibration models for Western Irish 
Sea sediments and comparable settings, provided that 
paired LOI–TOC calibration, the range of sediments to 
which each model applies, and propagated uncertainty 
are reported. By integrating archived samples, rou-
tine geotechnical measurements, grain-size data, LOI 
results and targeted TOC analyses from multiple sur -
vey and research sources, the study also demonstrates 
how existing marine monitoring and seabed-mapping

Introduction
Subtidal, non-vegetated marine sediments hold a 
major fraction of ocean OC, with the top metre 
estimated to contain > 2.3 Tt OC and accumulate 
126–350 Mt OC yr − 1 (Atwood et al., 2020; Epstein 
& Roberts, 2022). Continental shelves cover ~ 9% 
of the global marine area and include OC-rich sedi-
ments that can hold ~ 2% total organic carbon (TOC) 
and are exposed to climate and anthropogenic pres-
sures (Diesing, 2020; Epstein et  al., 2025; Legge 
et al., 2020; Smeaton & Austin, 2022; Smeaton et al., 
2020). Fine-grained shelf deposits, including those 
in the Irish Sea, Fladden Grounds and Celtic Sea, are 
recognised OC accumulation hotspots (Roseby et al., 
2026; Ward et al., 2025). In these areas, OC-rich sedi-
ments are found in low-energy depositional settings 
where fine-grained sediments composed primarily 
of silts and clays (i.e. < 63 µm), are allowed to set-
tle from suspension and accumulate on the seafloor. 
Their carbon content reflects mineralogical and geo-
chemical controls, including clay minerals and Fe 
oxides that can protect organic matter (Babakhani 
et al., 2025; Kennedy & Wagner, 2011; Moore et al., 
2023; Zhang et al., 2025).
Reliable OC measurements are needed to sup-
port stock estimates, spatial predictive models and 
monitoring. To address this, collaboration among 
researchers, policymakers, and international organi-
zations is essential to develop and implement con-
sistent approaches and standardized methodologies 
for assessing and reporting OC stock, accumulation 
and burial rates, thereby supporting the develop-
ment of a rigorous evidence base. Mapping stud-
ies depend on local measurements propagated 
into unsampled areas using statistical or machine-
learning approaches (Chatting et  al., 2025; Dies-
ing, 2020; Diesing et al., 2017; Wilson et al., 2018). 
Although databases such as the Modern Ocean Sed-
iment Archive and Inventory of Carbon (MOSAIC), 
EURO-CARBON and PANGAEA improve data 
access (Felden et al., 2023; Graversen et al., 2025; 
Paradis et  al., 2023), inconsistent methods for OC 
stock, accumulation and burial-rate estimation still 
limit comparability across studies (Dahl et al., 2025; 
Graves et al., 2022; Ratefinjanahary et al., 2025).
Direct OC measurement by combustion analysis 
after inorganic carbon removal is widely used and 
can be accurate and reproducible when preparation, 
acidification and calibration are controlled (King 
et al., 1998; Serrano et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2011). 
However, elemental analysis is relatively costly and 
requires specialist equipment, consumables and 
routine calibration, which can restrict high-density 
monitoring. This is especially relevant for agen-
cies with limited budget and developing nations. 
Alternative approaches include Walkley–Black wet 
oxidation, which can overestimate OC in anoxic 
sediments and underestimate it in oxic sediments 
(Leong & Tanner, 1999; Tuffour et al., 2014). LOI 
is widely used by researchers, monitoring agencies 
and commercial laboratories as a semi-quantitative 
OC proxy (Breithaupt et al., 2019; Hoogsteen et al., 
2015; Plater et al., 2015) and is included in BS EN 
15935:2021 for soil, waste, treated biowaste and 
sludge (British Standards Institution, 2021). LOI 
measures mass loss during ignition and can include 
organic matter (OM), structural water, salts and car -
bonate-related losses; therefore, conversion to OC 
requires local calibration.
Published LOI-to-TOC conversions differ among 
arid soils, mangroves and littoral sediments (Jensen 
et al., 2018; Ratefinjanahary et al., 2025; Touch et al., 
2017), partly because sediment composition, OM 
content and ignition temperature affect regression 
slopes (Sleutel et  al., 2007; Breithaupt et  al., 2023; 
Table S3). Ireland’s Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) 
covers roughly 450,000 square kilometres (~ 10 times 
its land mass), yet its seabed carbon inventory remains 
poorly constrained despite the extensive degree of 
mapping and sampling that has occurred there (Chat-
ting et al., 2025). Here, we compile LOI, TOC, grain-
size and iron (Fe) data for Western Irish Sea (WIS) 
non-vegetated marine sediments from research, com-
mercial and national monitoring sources, and test 
whether stratified LOI-to-TOC models provide a prac-
tical framework for regional OC assessment. We also 
provide recommendations for methodological aspects 
of sediment carbon analysis and recommendations for

Regional setting
The Western Irish Sea study area (Fig.  1) connects to 
the Atlantic Ocean through the North Channel and St. 
George’s Channel (Knight et al., 1999). Sample sites 
span mud, sand and gravel sediments and were retro-
spectively grouped in Fig.  1 as High mud (> 19.80% 
mud) or Low mud (≤ 19.80% mud) as described by 
the results of statistical testing from this study.
Water depths range from coastal shallows (< 5 m) 
to approximately 115  m in the central basin. The 
western region is generally deeper, less tidally ener -
getic and more saline (Gowen & Stewart, 2005), sup-
porting a seasonal summer gyre and offshore Summer 
Stratified Region (Woods et al., 2019; Fig.  1) distinct 
from the Coastal and Southern Mixed Regions (Hill 
et  al., 1994, 1997; Horsburgh et  al., 2000; O’Reilly 
et al., 2014). North of 53.5°N, weaker hydrodynam-
ics favour fine-grained deposition (i.e. silts and clays), 
whereas southern areas are dominated by sands, grav-
elly sands and high-energy bedforms (Coughlan et al., 
2021; Creane et al., 2022; Trimmer et al., 2003; Ward 
et al., 2025). The Irish Sea catchment is ~ 43,000 km2, 
with major freshwater input in the eastern Irish Sea 
(Bowden, 1980). Previous work documented anthro-
pogenic impacts on Western Irish Sea Mud Belt sedi-
ments, including trawling and radionuclide inputs 
from Sellafield (Coughlan et  al., 2015; Muir et  al., 
2026).
Data and methods
Samples and existing data were collated from sur -
veys between 2010 and 2023: CV10_28 (O’Reilly 
et  al., 2010), KRY22_03 (Furey & Cullen, 2022) 
and CE23_016 (QUEST; grant no. PBA/CC/21/01). 
Data variables and source agencies are summarised 
in Table  1. Where data had been published previ-
ously, the relevant reference is provided. CV10_28 
surface sediments were sampled in June 2010 dur -
ing INFOMAR (Integrated Mapping for the Sustain-
able Development of Ireland’s Marine Resource) 
survey CV10_28 aboard the RV Celtic Voyager using 
a Day Grab. Box cores were taken using a Reineck 
box corer, and subsequently push cores were taken 
from the box using pre-cut and edge filed piping 
(50  cm length and 7  cm internal diameter), capped, 
sealed and frozen at −20  °C. For survey Kry22_03, 
the Marine Institute (MI) collected surface grab 
samples from Dublin Bay in spring 2022 using a 
Day Grab aboard the RV Keary (Geological Survey 
Ireland), and samples were refrigerated until analy -
sis. In October 2023, the QUEST project team led 
survey CE23_016 aboard the RV Celtic Explorer 
(MI) to execute a sampling programme in the West-
ern Irish Sea. Surface grab samples were captured 
using a Day Grab and stored at −20  °C. A Reineck 
box corer was used similarly to survey CV10_28 
where duplicate push cores (hereafter referred to as 
box cores) were extracted from the sediment box on 
deck before storage (1* frozen). Gravity (1–3 m) and 
Vibrocores (4–5.5  m) were used to collect deeper 
sediments where sediments were collected within 
plastic piping inserted into respective coring devices 
before deployment to the seabed. Onboard cores 
were sectioned into 1  m lengths before storage at 
−20 °C. The processing of frozen cores was carried 
out at Dublin City University. Workflow of grab sam-
ples included defrosting, complete homogenisation 
using a stainless-steel trowel before subsampling for 
respective analysis. The box cores, gravity cores, and 
vibrocores were split manually while frozen using 
an angle grinder power tool. Briefly, the two deep 
lines were scored along the surface of the piping at 
opposing sides. A flat-blade screwdriver and hammer 
were used to gently tap along the score lines until the 
outward pressure split the pipe to reveal the frozen 
and physically intact core. The outer core was QC 
assessed for any possible contaminants from the pipe. 
Stainless steel saws were used immediately while the 
core remained frozen to further section the core into 
selected horizons before subsampling.

TOC was measured consistently across surveys on 
the < 2 mm sediment fraction after freeze-drying, pow-
dering by manual grinding with a mortar and pestle, 
and storage in a desiccator. A 125 µm sieve was used 
only after powdering samples as a post grain size test 
to ensure that all powdered sediments passed the same 
size cutoff to obtain a uniform fine fraction prior to 
weighing, i.e. sediment that did not pass through was 
further powdered and combined with the rest of the 
sample before homogenising. CV10_28 samples were 
analysed at the University College Dublin Microana-
lytical Laboratory KRY22_03 and CE23_016 samples 
were analysed in the Dublin City University environ-
mental laboratory. TOC concentrations are reported 
as weight percent of dry sediment (wt.%). TOC data 
used in this study for CV10_28 was carried out on 
the 0–2 cm depth horizon of cores, while cores ana-
lysed for this study were sampled 0–3 cm to increase 
sample availability. CE23_016 core sediments used 
0–5  cm depths. Briefly, powdered sediment samples 
were weighed into silver capsules (~ 10  mg) using 
a calibrated balance (QUINTIX35-1S- readability 
0.01  mg). Sediments were treated to remove carbon-
ate (Verardo et al., 1990). The serial addition (20 µl*5) 
of 1 M HCl was pipetted into the silver capsule until, 
followed by drying overnight at 40 °C, and storage in 
a desiccator until analysis. Immediately prior to analy-
sis, silver capsules were closed and lightly compacted 
using stainless stell tweezers and analysed by combus-
tion using a Fisons NCS 1500 NA elemental analyser, 
calibrated with a certified soil standard (CE Instru-
ments; mean C = 2.3%, % RSD < 1%) before each 
batch and after every nine samples. % RSD for sample 
triplicates ranged from 1.02 to 5.5 with the higher var-
iability occurring in sediments with higher sand and 
shell content.
To perform LOI analysis, sediment samples 
(~ 40–50  g wet weight) were placed on glass petri 
dishes and dried at 105  °C for 24  h to remove all 
moisture, until constant mass was achieved. Dried 
sediments were sieved to < 2  mm and manually 
powdered using a mortar and pestle. Samples were 
re-dried at 60  °C for an additional 4  h, cooled, and 
stored in a vacuum desiccator prior to ignition 
(ensuring complete dryness after processing). Rep-
licate sample masses of 10–15  g were placed in 
pre-weighed ceramic crucibles. The samples were 
furnaced at 450 °C for 4 h, allowed to cool in the fur -
nace, and immediately placed in a desiccator until re-
weighing for mass loss. LOI (wt.%) was calculated 
as: LOI (wt.%) = (Y − X/Y) × 100 where, Y is the initial 
dry sediment mass, and X is the post-ignition mass 
(determined as total crucible + ash mass—crucible 
tare). Replicate analyses and desiccation were used 
to reduce precision and rehydration artefacts. In most 
cases duplicate analysis (some samples had triplicate 
runs) was carried out for LOI with % RSD ranging 
from 1.3% in homogenous mud sediments to 13.6% in 
heterogeneous sandy and lower—OM sediment.
Fe concentrations were measured in triplicate using 
a Thermo Scientific Niton XL3t 900 portable X-ray 
fluorescence (XRF) analyser with Niton NDTr 6.5.2 
software (Radu et al., 2013). Quantification used fun-
damental parameters to reduce matrix effects, with 
internal calibration before and after each run. Accu-
racy was checked against certified soil reference mate-
rials (NCSDC 73308, TILL-4 and GBW 07411) fol-
lowing US EPA Method 6200 (U.S. EPA., 2007).

Group differences were evaluated using analysis 
of covariance (ANCOVA): TOC = ([a + b] × LOI) + (
c × Group) + (d × [LOI × Group]). A significant inter-

effects indicated intercept differences. Final group 
models were assessed using regression fit, residuals

explored variable relationships, and Mann–Whitney 
U tests were used for non-normal group comparisons 
(Nachar, 2008).
Results
XRF calibration checks indicated accept-
able Fe accuracy across the calibration range: 
measured-versus-certified slopes were 0.97 
(R2 = 0.9469) for NCSDC 73308, 0.90 (R 2 = 0.8069) 
for TILL-4 and 0.99 (R 2 = 0.9897) for GBW 07411. 
The dataset covered wide sediment and carbon ranges 
(Table 2 and Table  S1). LOI ranged from 0.33% to 
4.95% (mean 1.23%; mean Relative standard devia-
tion (RSD) 3.53%), and measured TOC ranged from 
0.03% to 2.02% (mean 0.40%; mean RSD 2.65%). 
Water depths ranged from 3 to 114  m; mud con-
tent ranged from 0% to 97.66%; and Fe ranged from 
0.41% to 3.78%, generally increasing in finer sedi-
ments. The paired LOI-TOC subset was smaller than 
the full metadata set, so external validation remains 
required.
Across groups, TOC correlated strongly with 
LOI (r = 0.85–0.98), with the strongest relationship 
in mud-rich sediments (r = 0.98; Fig.  2). Mud con-
tent correlated with TOC and LOI in the full data-
set (r = 0.66 and 0.80) and particularly in the high-
mud group. Fe also correlated with TOC and LOI 
(r = 0.69–0.79), especially in high-mud sediments, 
but these associations are interpreted as covariation 
with depositional setting rather than direct causation.

were also higher in fine sediments (low mud = 0.20; 
high mud = 0.34), supporting sediment-class-specific 
conversion.
Discussion
Improving LOI-TOC conversion in marine sediments
The WIS data are not well represented by one pooled 
LOI-TOC relationship. Chow tests and ANCOVA 
revealed structural breaks associated with mud con-
tent, water depth and Fe concentration: mud-rich 
or Fe-rich sediments had steeper TOC/LOI slopes 
than coarse, Fe-poor sediments. Calibrated slopes 
were ~ 0.41 percentage points TOC per 1LOI (wt.%) 
in deep or mud-rich beds and ~ 0.24 percentage points 
TOC per 1 LOI (wt.%) in shallow or coarse sites. 
These findings are consistent with the ability of fine 
mineral matrices, clay interlayers and Fe/Mn oxides 
to retain and protect organic matter (Barber et  al., 
2017; Chen et al., 2020; Johnson et al., 2015; Lalonde 
et  al., 2012; Moore et  al., 2023; Wei et  al., 2025). 
In coarse sediments, low organic content and small 
absolute LOI values can make TOC estimates more 
sensitive to minor inorganic mass losses or seasonal/
anthropogenic organic inputs (Ahrens et  al., 2020; 
Kubo & Kanda, 2017).
The Fe split indicates a geochemical association 
rather than a direct causal test. Fe phases, including iron 
(oxyhydr)oxides and sulfides, can co-accumulate with 
TOC in low-energy, fine-grained or low-oxygen set-
tings (Egger et al., 2017; Taylor & Macquaker, 2011). 
However, Western Irish Sea sediments are affected by 
bioturbation by Nephrops norvegicus and by trawl-
ing, with reported Irish Sea trawling intensities of 
2.70–11.90  yr−1 and measurable benthic impacts (Jones 
et al., 2002; Hinz et al., 2017; O’Sullivan et al., 2014; 
Hiddink et  al., 2016). These processes can alter sedi-
ment structure, biogeochemical cycling and OM-min-
eral relationships (Lønborg et al., 2024; Paradis et al., 
2021). The TOC/mud ratio may help separate grain-
size effects from disturbance effects, as shown for inten-
sively trawled North Sea sediments (Zhang et al., 2024).
Fe and water depth can therefore help flag shifts in 
TOC/LOI where grain-size data are incomplete, but 
they should be used as proxies for depositional set-
ting. Mineral associations remain important mecha-
nistic context: nitrogen-rich OM may bind closely 
to Fe-rich minerals, whereas carbon-rich OM may 
be preserved within aggregate pores (Zhang et  al., 
2025), consistent with distinct mineral protection 
pathways for different OM pools (Blattmann et  al., 
2019; Yedema et al., 2024; Bauer et al., 2013; Moore 
et al., 2023; Barber et al., 2017; Wei et al., 2025).

A further implication of this study is that national 
sediment carbon datasets need not rely solely on stan-

integrates archived samples, routine geotechnical 
data, particle-size measurements, LOI results and 
targeted TOC analyses from multiple research, moni-
toring and seabed-mapping activities. This approach 
could be expanded through collaboration among 
national mapping programmes, research institutions, 
environmental regulators, local authorities, offshore 
renewable-energy developers, ports, fisheries-man-
agement bodies and commercial survey providers. 
Shared study design, sample archiving, infrastruc-
ture, metadata standards and co-funding would allow 
routine marine surveys and infrastructure projects 
to strengthen national seabed carbon assessment, 
improve spatial coverage, reduce duplicate sampling 
and support more robust carbon-stock mapping and 
policy evaluation.
Limitations and uncertainty
This regional case study should not be interpreted 
as providing a universal LOI-to-TOC conversion. 
The paired LOI-TOC dataset is smaller than the 
full metadata set, group sizes are uneven (18 high-
mud and 59 low-mud samples), and the 19.8% mud, 
0.99% Fe and 29.6  m water depth thresholds were 
selected using exploratory structural-break analysis 
without independent validation. Application to other 
regions or sedimentary settings therefore requires 
new paired LOI–TOC measurements to calibrate and 
independently validate locally appropriate conver -
sion relationships and any associated sediment-class 
thresholds. LOI provides an indirect estimate of 
sediment organic matter and cannot be expected to 
achieve the analytical specificity or accuracy of direct 
TOC determination by combustion-based elemental 
analysis. LOI at 450  °C can include organic matter, 
clay structural water and other volatile or mineral-
associated losses. Results can also vary with igni-
tion temperature, sample mass, sieving, drying, car -
bonate content and desiccation. These limitations do 
not preclude the use of LOI for estimating TOC, but 
they require conversion relationships to be empiri-
cally calibrated against direct TOC measurements 
and interpreted within the sedimentological and geo-
chemical context of the samples. Fe and water depth 
co-vary with grain size and depositional environment 
in this dataset and should be treated as practical prox-
ies unless supported by independent geochemical 
and mineralogical evidence. Prediction uncertainty 
is greatest in low-OC coarse sediments for samples 
close to classification thresholds.
Importantly, this study evaluates the conversion of 
LOI measurements to estimated TOC concentration 
only. It does not directly quantify sediment carbon 
stocks, accumulation or burial rates, carbon perma-
nence or climate-mitigation potential. Where cali-
brated LOI-to-TOC relationships are applied to addi-
tional sediment samples, the resulting TOC estimates 
may subsequently contribute to stock calculations, 
but these require additional information including 
dry bulk density, sediment thickness or defined water 
depth intervals, grain-size and spatial data, together 
with appropriate propagation of conversion, meas-
urement and spatial uncertainties. Estimates of accu-
mulation or burial additionally require independent 
chronological constraints such as radionuclide dating.
Conclusion
This study demonstrates that the principal value of 
LOI-to-TOC conversion is not to replace direct ele-
mental analysis, but to extend the spatial value of 
existing and future marine sediment datasets where 
LOI is available, but direct TOC measurements are 
limited. For the WIS dataset, sediment-class-specific 
conversion improved model fit and reduced conver -
sion bias relative to a single pooled LOI-to-TOC

Marine sedimentary blue carbon is increasingly rel-
evant to marine protected area (MPA) assessment and 
marine spatial planning (Allcock et al., 2024; Bergagna 
et al., 2024; Laffoley & Baxter, 2022; Marine Protected 
Area Advisory Group, 2020; Visalli et  al., 2020).

accumulation-rate data limit stock accuracy (Graversen 
et al., 2025), so national seabed mapping programmes 
such as INFOMAR and MAREANO (Norwegian sea-
bed mapping programme) provide valuable sampling 
platforms (Bøe et al., 2022; Guinan et al., 2020). The 
framework presented here can increase OC data avail-
ability where LOI is the best available option. Indeed, 
retrospective application of validated conversion mod-
els could increase the spatial density of estimated TOC 
data where archived LOI measurements are accompa-
nied by sufficient metadata, particularly grain-size and 
location information, but it should not be used alone 
to infer accumulation, burial, permanence or mitiga-
tion value. Beyond the specific LOI–TOC calibration, 
this study demonstrates the value of collating data 
from multiple marine survey, monitoring and research 
sources. Coordinated sample archiving, shared infra-
structure, common metadata standards and targeted 
collaboration among mapping agencies, research insti-
tutions, regulators, local authorities and offshore infra-
structure stakeholders could improve spatial coverage 
while reducing duplication of sampling effort. This 
approach would support more robust national seabed 
carbon assessment, monitoring and policy evaluation 
while merging multidisciplinary knowledge to enhance 
interpretation of data.
