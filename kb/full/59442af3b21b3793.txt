Abstract This study evaluates Palm Oil Mill Effluent 
(POME) wastewater from palm oil production through a 
preliminary, site-specific feasibility screening of the 
Wastewater Treatment Plant (WWTP) at PT Bintara Tani 
Nusantara (BTN) in West Sumatra, Indonesia. Water 
samples were collected from the WWTP inlet (Sludge Pit), 
WWTP outlet (Buffer Pond), one community monitoring 
well, and three surface water locations. Because all water-
quality results were obtained from a single sampling event, 
the findings are interpreted as screening evidence rather 
than definitive proof of treatment performance or 
environmental impact. Laboratory parameters included 
Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD)₅, Chemical Oxygen 
Demand (COD), oil/grease, and total nitrogen (as N), while 
soil conditions were characterized through water content 
testing and direct shear testing at nine embankment points. 
The influent exhibited high temperature (46.2°C), BOD₅ 
(10,155 mg/L), oil/grease (3,044 mg/L), and total N (1,118 
mg/L). The reported influent COD value (51.4 mg/L) is 
treated as a data-quality anomaly because it is lower than 
the influent BOD₅ value and therefore was not used for 
COD-removal or overall treatment-performance 
calculations until verified against the original laboratory 
record. At the effluent , BOD₅ decreased to 303 mg/L and 
oil/grease to 2.2 mg/L; however, the effluent remained non-
compliant for BOD₅, COD, and total N under the 
referenced discharge limits. Surface water results showed 
low BOD₅ values, COD below the detection limit (<9.91 
mg/L), and oil/grease below detection (<0.345 mg/L). 
These surface water and groundwater findings are 
interpreted only as preliminary screening indicators, not as 
causal evidence of environmental impact or absence of 
impact. Overall, the study identifies efflue nt-compliance 
constraints and monitoring priorities for Palm Oil Mill 
Effluent (POME) management, especially verification of 
the anomalous COD record, repeated seasonal/event-based 
monitoring, and inclusion of control wells.

1. Introduction
The palm oil industry generates Palm Oil Mill Effluent 
(POME), a high -strength wastewater characterized by 
substantial organic loading and a typically acidic pH.

Although raw POME consists predominantly of water 
(approximately 95 –96%), it still contains residual solids 
and oil/grease fractions. As a result, it commonly exhibits 
elevated Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) and 
Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD), which can severely 
degrade rec eiving waters if discharged without adequate 
treatment [1]. The combination of high and fluctuating 
loads requires treatment systems that can buffer shock 
variations, maintain the stability of biological processes, 
and consistently deliver compliant efflue nt quality [2]. In 
wastewater engineering practice, BOD, COD, total 
suspended solids (TSS), and pH are routinely used as key 
performance indicators because they are directly linked to 
oxygen requirements, solids and sludge generation, and 
potential ecological risks in the receiving environment [3]. 
In the Indonesian context, water pollution control is 
largely driven by effluent standards that require wastewater 
to meet regulatory thresholds before it can be released into 
the environment. Government Regulati on (GR) No. 
30/2024 [4] clarifies that "water" includes both surface 
water and groundwater. It distinguishes two closely 
connected operational domains, namely water pollution 
control (preventing pollutants from entering water bodies) 
and water quality mana gement (maintaining and/or 
improving water quality at water sources and related 
infrastructure). At the discharge level, the Minister of 
Environment Regulation (MoE Reg.) No. 5/2014 [5] 
remains a principal reference for wastewater quality 
standards. Howeve r, in plantation operations, POME is 
often reused through land application, meaning that 
evaluation is insufficient if it focuses solely on compliance 
at the Wastewater Treatment Plant (WWTP) outlet. 
Because land -applied POME may interact with 
surrounding soil and water, monitoring should extend 
beyond the WWTP outlet to groundwater and nearby 
surface waters, as supported by studies of POME land 
application across soil and receiving-water contexts [6–9]. 
Wood et al. [6] argue that the outcomes of land 
application are highly context-dependent. Raw POME may 
clog soil pores and induce waterlogging, thereby reducing 
infiltration and aeration, indicating that critical concerns 
are not only chemical, but also related to physical 
degradation of soil structure. At the landscape scale, Comte 
et al. [7] further show that soil responses to long -term 
organic amendments can vary with soil class, topography, 
and application history, meaning that "safe/unsafe" 
conclusions should not be generalized from a single 
sampling poi nt. Sukisno and Arianto[8] report that soil 
chemical changes tend to diminish with depth, implying 
that evaluation designs should distinguish intensively 
applied zones, transition areas, and control zones to 
minimize bias arising from spatial heterogeneity . 
Conversely, benefits may emerge when effluent is properly 
treated, and application rates are controlled. Hamdan et al. 
[9] found that monitoring of digested POME showed no 
evidence of significant contamination in receiving media 
over a defined observatio n period. Treated POME 
application has also been reported to increase soil microbial 
biomass and to correlate with plantation productivity 
through nutrient recycling mechanisms [10]. Therefore, 
what is required is not merely the claim that an effluent 
"meets discharge standards", but evidence linking 
treatment performance to measurable responses in 
receiving soils and waters under site -specific land 
conditions. 
This study gap arises from two dominant tendencies in 
the literature and in POME management practice. First, 
technical studies on POME treatment often judge the 
performance of a WWTP primarily through effluent 
parameters measured at the outlet, treating these values as 
the main indicators of process success [1, 2]. Second, 
within regulatory compliance practice, assessments 
frequently rely on whether wastewater meets the applicable 
discharge standards, so feasibility conclusions commonly 
stop at the "outlet numbers" [4, 5]. Yet available evidence 
indicates that land application outcomes are context-
specific and shaped by soil physical properties and spatial 
heterogeneity. Consequently, "safe/unsafe" conclusions 
cannot be credibly generalized from a single point and 
should be tested directly in the receiving media [6 –8]. An 
integrated, cross-compartment evaluation is therefore 
needed, one that links effluent performance to surface 
water and groundwater quality while situating those results 
within site-specific land conditions and observed responses 
in receiving soils and waters [7–10]. Building on this need, 
the present study evaluates the feasibility of POME 
treatment at the WWTP of PT Bintara Tani Nusantara 
(BTN), located in Pasaman Barat Regency, West Sumatra, 
Indonesia by testing water quality across three media 
(influent–effluent wastewater, surface water, and 
groundwater) and by characterizing site-specific soil 
physical conditions (direct shear testing and water content) 
as contextual indicators of water movement pathways. 
Operationally, the evaluation framework is aligned with 
GR No. 30/2024 [4], so that the study does not end at 
"outlet compliance", but also examines indications of 
potential risk and/or responses in the receiving 
environment surrounding the POME management and 
utilization area. 
This study evaluates POME wastewater from palm oil 
production through a preliminary, site -specific feasibility 
screening of the WWTP at PT BTN in West Sumatra, 
Indonesia, through three interrelated objectives. First, it 
compares available influent and effluent parameters against 
the relevant regulatory standards while explicitly 
separating verifiable removal indicators from parameters 
affected by data inconsistency. Second, it screens surface 
water and groundwater conditions around the operational 
area without making causal impact claims because no 
temporal basel ine, repeated water sampling, or external 
control-well design is available. Third, it characterizes 
local soil physical conditions through direct shear testing 
and water content measurements, providing contextual 
information that may influence infiltration, water retention,

and dissolved -solute migration pathways in land

2. Methods 
This study is an evaluative assessment of the feasibility 
of the WWTP at the palm oil mill of PT BTN, located in 
Pasaman Barat Regency, Indonesia. Because the available 
water-quality dataset was obtained from a single sampling 
event, this study is designed as a preliminary screening 
assessment rather than a causal environmental -impact 
study. The evaluation focuses on treatment performance 
and preliminary indications of receiving-media conditions. 
The terms "impact", "influence", and "risk" are therefore 
used cautiously and only as screening -level indications 
unless supported by repeated measurements, baseline data, 
and control locations. Cross -media testing cov ered 
influent–effluent wastewater, surface water, and 
groundwater, while local soil physical conditions were 
characterized using direct shear testing and water content 
measurements. The soil data are used as contextual 
evidence to interpret possible water movement pathways 
and potential contaminant migration around the WWTP 
under a land -application setting, not as proof of pollutant 
migration. 
2.1. Study Area, Sampling Locations, Sampling 
Layout, and Sample Collection 
This study was conducted in two stages, namely an 
initial site reconnaissance and field data collection, 
including water and soil sampling within the WWTP area 
of PT BTN and its surrounding environment. Water 
samples were collected from six locations representing 
three evaluation compartments: 1) wastewater at the 
WWTP inlet (Sludge Pit) and outlet (Buffer Pond); 2) 
groundwater from one community monitoring well; and 3) 
surface water at upstream, downstream, and receiving -
water locations (Lubuk Buaya River). The water-sampling 
design was limited to one sampling event and did not 
include a time -series baseline, wet -season/dry-season 
replication, or a dedicated control well outside the potential 
influence zone. Therefore, the dataset is suitable for 
feasibility screening and compliance -oriented 
interpretation, but not for definitive source attribution or 
causal impact assessment. The water quality parameters 
evaluated followed those available from laboratory test 
results: temperature, BOD₅, COD, oil/grease, total nitrogen 
(as N), dissolved oxygen (DO), TSS, and pH. The map and 
coordinates of the six water -quality sampling points are 
presented in Figure 1 and Table 1 . Figure 1 was prepared 
to show the relative positions of the sampling points, the 
WWTP units, nearby river reaches, and the surrounding 
environmental setting. 
The PT BTN treatment system evaluated here is a pond-
based POME treatment train. In operational sequence, 
wastewater enters the Sludge Pit/inlet, passes through acid 
and anaerobic pond units, continues to the facultative pond, 
and is retained in the Buffer Pond/outlet before discharge 
or further management under the site land -application 
setting. This pond sequence is intended to reduce organic 
load, oil/grease, and nitrogen progressively through settling, 
anaerobic degradation, facul tative biological processes, 
and final retention; however, the present dataset evaluates 
only the available inlet -outlet and receiving -media 
measurements and does not quantify hydraulic residence 
time or unit-by-unit removal [11]. 
Sampling was conducted in situ at nine locations (Points 
1 to 9) positioned on the embankments of the WWTP ponds 
and/or on the partition embankments between ponds. As 
shown in Figure 1, Points 1 and 2 were located on the 
southern perimeter embankment, Points 3 to 7 were 
distributed along the western embankment, Point 8 was 
placed on the northern embankment near the river corridor, 
and Point 9 represented the internal inter -pond partition 
embankment. This layout was intended to represent 
embankment materials in zones relevant to wat er 
movement and potential seepage pathways. Soil samples 
were collected using a steel sampling pipe at depths of 
approximately 2 to 3 m, adjusted to field conditions and 
pond positions. The collected samples were used for direct 
shear testing and water con tent determination. The layout 
of the WWTP effluent pond system and the soil sampling 
locations are shown in Figure 2. 
2.2. Types and Sources of Data 
The data comprised secondary and primary sources. 
Secondary data included administrative and location maps 
(plantation area, PT BTN site, and land -application or 
related activity areas) as well as operational information on 
effluent routing and land -application schedules, which 
were used to contextualize field activities and to structure 
the sampling design an d sequence. Primary data were 
obtained from water sampling at six locations representing 
the WWTP influent–effluent compartments, surface water 
(upstream, downstream, and receiving water body), and 
groundwater (monitoring well). Water samples were 
analyzed in a laboratory for the parameters reported in the 
results: BOD₅, COD, oil/grease, total nitrogen (as N), DO, 
TSS, and pH [12]. The operational data were also used to 
describe the treatment train and to avoid presenting the 
WWTP as a single black -box outl et, because reviewer 
comments requested clearer information on the 
wastewater-treatment process.

For the soil compartment, prima ry data were collected 
through in situ soil sampling at nine locations (Points 1 to 
9) on the WWTP pond embankments. The soil samples 
were tested using a direct shear test to obtain shear strength 
parameters (cohesion, c, and internal friction angle, φ) and 
by water content testing to determine the natural moisture 
content relevant to saturation and infiltration conditions. 
Water content determination followed the standard oven -
drying and weighing method, American Society for Testing 
and Materials (ASTM D2216) [13], while the direct shear 
test followed the principles of shear strength testing on a 
single shear plane using a direct shear apparatus (ASTM 
D3080/D3080M) [14]. Operationally, study materials 
consisted of water samples (from six sampling points) and 
soil samples (from nine embankment locations at the target 
depth). Field equipment included sampling tools and 
measurement devices corresponding to the reported 
parameters: a thermometer or temperature probe, a pH 
meter, a DO meter, BOD₅ analysis equipment (BOD bottles 
and an incubator), COD analysis equipment (reactor or 
digester and colorimetric or spectrophotometric reading as 
required by the method), oil/grease analysis equipment 
(extraction or gravimetry as required by the method), and 
TSS analysis e quipment (filtration unit, drying oven, and 
analytical balance). For soil testing, the main equipment 
included a steel sampling pipe or sampling tube, a direct 
shear apparatus, a drying oven, an analytical balance, and 
moisture cans or containers for water content testing [14]. 
The materials and referenced test methods used for the 
water and soil analyses are summarized in Table 2. 
2.3. Testing Procedures 
2.3.1. Water Quality Analysis 
Water samples were collected from six locations 
(WWTP influent, WWTP effluent, groundwater 
monitoring well, upstream point, downstream point, and 
the receiving water body, Lubuk Buaya River). During 
sampling, field observations were recorded, including 
temperature and qualitative descriptions (e.g., turbidity or 
color, visi ble sediment, and odor) to support the 
interpretation of water quality and cross-media differences. 
Evaluation was based on the parameters available in the 
laboratory results, such as BOD₅, COD, oil/grease, total 
nitrogen (as N), dissolved oxygen (DO), TSS , and pH, 
together with the field measurements (temperature and 
visual observations). The analysis was conducted across 
compartments namely 1) influent to effluent wastewater 
data were used to assess changes in quality as an indicator 
of treatment performance; 2) surface water data (upstream, 
downstream, and receiving water body) were used to 
identify potential changes in the receiving environment; 
and 3) groundwater data (community monitoring well) 
were used to examine indications of risk in the 
compartment most relevant to potential community 
exposure. Analytical procedures followed established 
standard laboratory methods for wastewater and surface 
water testing (e.g., Standard Methods for the Examination 
of Water and Wastewater) to ensure comparability ac ross 
parameters and locations [12].

2.3.2. Direct Shear Test 
Direct shear testing was performed in accordance with 
ASTM D3080/D3080M to determine cohesion (c) and 
internal friction angle (φ) from the relationship between 
normal stress and shear stress [14]. Soil samples were 
collected from nine embankment locations (Points 1 to 9) 
around the WWTP ponds and tested at a depth of 
approximately 2.0 to 2.5 m. For each sample, paired normal 
stress and shear stress (σ–τ) data were obtained under three 
applied normal loads, and these results were used to derive 
c and φ for each sampling point. The shear box area used 
in the test was 28.26 cm² . In this study, shear st rength 
parameters were not used to provide a comprehensive 
embankment stability assessment, which would require 
slope geometry, water level conditions, seepage conditions, 
and factor-of-safety analysis. Instead, c and φ are presented 
as descriptors of loca l embankment material conditions to 
support the interpretation of water movement pathways and 
potential contaminant migration around the WWTP. 
Accordingly, spatial variation in c and φ is interpreted 
primarily as evidence of embankment material 
heterogeneity and is discussed together with water quality 
results and soil moisture conditions. The direct shear 
procedure follows applicable geotechnical testing 
standards [14].

2.4. Data Analysis 
2.4.1. Water Quality Data 
Water quality analysis was conducted by presenting the 
laboratory results and performing a cross -compartment 
interpretation across three media, namely wastewater 
(influent–effluent/outlet), surface water (upstream, 
downstream, and receiving w ater body), and groundwater 
(community monitoring well). Because the reported 
influent COD value (51.4 mg/L) is lower than the influent 
BOD₅ value (10,155 mg/L), the COD -based influent –
effluent removal efficiency was excluded from the revised 
analysis pending verification against the original laboratory 
sheet. Removal efficiency was reported only for parameters 
that were internally consistent and interpretable from the 
available dataset. Regulatory compliance for wastewater 
was assessed primarily on effluent/outlet values, consistent 
with MoE Regulation No. 5/2014 [5] (Annex XLVII). 
Surface water and groundwater observations were 
interpreted as screening indicators only. No causal claim of 
environmental impact, land -application influence, or 
absence of contamination is made from the single-sampling 
dataset. For surface water, benchmarking followed GR No. 
82/2001 [15] using the applicable water class (I –IV), and 
the selected class must be stated explicitly when presenting 
single-class compliance results. 
2.4.2. Soil Data (Direct Shear Test and Water Content) 
Soil analysis was conducted by presenting direct shear 
test results and water content results for each sampling 
point. In the direct shear test, paired normal stress and shear 
stress (σ –τ) data at multiple loading levels were used to 
derive soil shear strength parameters, namely cohesion (c) 
and internal friction angle (φ), for each sample. The c and 
φ values were analyzed descriptively and comparatively 
across points to characterize spatial variation in 
embankment or pond material conditions. For the water 
content test, each sample was measured in three replicates 
(I to III) and summarized using the mean value. Water 
content procedures followed ASTM D2216 [13]. Because 
no falling -head, constant -head, or dire ct hydraulic -
conductivity test result was available, the soil analysis was 
deliberately restricted to descriptive interpretation of shear 
strength and moisture conditions, not quantitative seepage 
modelling.

2.4.3. Matrix Linking Tested Parameters to Regulatory

To ensure consistency, a compliance matrix was 
developed using GR No. 30/2024 [4] as the overarching 
framework, while numerical threshold values were 
referenced from media -specific regulations, such as MoE 
Regulation No. 5/2014 [5] for wastewater, GR No. 82/2001 
[15] for surface water, MoE Decree No. 29/2003 [16] for 
land application of wastewater, and Ministry of Health 
Regulation No. 32/2017 [17] for hygiene and sanitation 
water. In this matrix, GR 30/2024 functions as the 
governance/framework basis, wher eas the numeric limits 
are taken from the technical standards listed in Table 3. For 
groundwater, the “organic indicator” was interpreted 
according to MoH Reg. No. 32/2017 [17] as organic 
substances (as KMnO₄), rather than BOD₅. Therefore, any 
groundwater organic comparison was based on the KMnO₄ 
threshold. Table 3 was used as the reference matrix for 1) 
wastewater effluent/outlet compliance under MoE 
Regulation No. 5/2014 [5] (Annex XLVII; Group I/II); 2) 
minimum numeric requirements for land application under 
MoE Decree No. 29/2003 [16] (BOD and pH); 3) surface 
water class-based criteria under GR (PP) No. 82/2001 [15] 
(DO, BOD, COD, TSS, pH by Class I –IV); and 4) 
groundwater hygiene –sanitation benchmarks under MoH 
Reg. No. 32/2017 [17] (pH and organic subs tances as 
KMnO₄).

3.2. Soil Analysis Results 
Consistent with the cross -compartment evaluation 
framework, soil results are presented as physical context to 
support interpretation of potential water movement 
pathways (infiltration and retention) and the potential 
migration of dissolved constituents around the WWTP 
pond embankments under a land-application setting. In line 
with the study objectives and methods, the soil dataset 
includes direct shear test results used to derive shear 
strength parameters, namely cohesion (c) and internal 
friction angle (φ), and natural water content measurements 
representing embankment moisture conditions. These soil 
parameters are interpreted as contextual indicators and are 
not used to perform a comprehensive embankment stability 
analysis. 
Direct shear testing was conducted to determine soil 
shear strength parameters, namely cohesion (c) and internal 
friction angle (φ), based on paired normal stress and shear 
stress data (σ –τ) under three applied normal loads. All 
samples were collected from WWTP pond embankments 
and an inter -pond partition embankment and tested at a 
depth of 2.0 –2.5 m using a shear box area of 28.26 c m² . 
The resulting c and φ values are used to describe spatial 
variability in embankment material properties that may 
influence seepage behavior and the potential for solute 
migration. The direct shear testing setup and specimen 
preparation for all sampling points are documented in 
Figure 3, and the direct shear test results for 9 soil samples 
can be seen in Table 5.

Table 5 indicates substantial spatial variability in shear 
strength across embankment locations. Sample 4 
(Anaerobic Pond III embankment) shows the highest 
cohesion (c = 1.16 kg/cm² or 114.63 kN/m² ) with a 
moderate friction angle (φ = 17.6°). In contrast, Sample 3 
(Anaerobic Pond IV embankment) exhibits the lowest 
friction angle (φ = 7.76°) with cohesion of 0.34 kg/cm² 
(34.02 kN/m² ). This variability suggests that embankment 
materials are not homogeneous, and therefore soil 
conditions should not be generali zed from a single point. 
Several locations display high friction angles (e.g., Sample 
8, φ = 57.99°; Sample 1, φ = 51.78°) combined with 
moderate cohesion (0.51–0.82 kg/cm² ), indicating a strong 
frictional contribution to shear resistance. However, these 
values should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of 
embankment stability because hydraulic conditions (degree 
of saturation, seepage gradients, and pond water levels) and 
wetting–drying cycles can strongly affect embankment 
behavior. In this study, c and φ are used primarily as 
contextual descriptors that help interpret potential water 
movement pathways and the likelihood of solute migration 
around the WWTP ponds when considered together with 
water quality observations. Natural water content testing 
was conducted to quantify the moisture condition of 
embankment soils at each sampling point. Testing followed 
ASTM D2216, with samples collected at 2.0–2.5 m depth. 
Each sample was measured in three replicates (I –III), and 
the mean value was reported as th e representative water 
content for each location. In accordance with the study 
objectives, water content is interpreted as an indicator of 
internal embankment hydraulic conditions that may 
influence infiltration, retention, and seepage tendencies. 
Summary of natural water content for nine soil samples 
(ASTM D2216) can be seen in Table 6. 
Mean water content ranges from 27.51% to 47.34%, 
indicating relatively moist embankment conditions at 
several locations. The highest mean values occur in 
Samples 2 (47.34%) and 7 (43.09%), while the lowest 
occurs in Sample 4 (27.51%). From a water -movement 
perspective, higher water content may reflect greater 
saturation, increasing the likelihood of lateral or vertical 
seepage if hydraulic gradients exist between pond water 
and embankment soils. From a geotechnical perspective, 
increased moisture content is commonly associated with 
reduced effective stress and may be linked to reduced shear 
resistance under certain conditions, particularly for fine -
grained or poorly drained materials. Therefore, points with 
higher moisture content, especially Samples 2 and 7, can 
be considered potentially more sensitive during pond level 
rise, prolonged wet conditions, or intense rainfall events. 
This interpretation should be read jointly with Table 5 to 
avoid concluding a single indicator. 
To connect the soil results with the cross -media water 
quality findings under the study’s integrated, cross -
compartment framework, the soil parameters are 
interpreted as a site -specific physical context that may 
condition how treated or partially treated POME interacts 
with the receiving environment. Spatial variability in shear 
strength parameters (c and φ) indicates heterogeneity in 
embankment material behavior, while differences in 
natural water content re flect contrasting moisture and 
saturation states that can influence infiltration, retention, 
and preferential seepage pathways. In practical terms, 
locations showing higher water content and/or 
comparatively weaker shear resistance may represent zones 
where seepage and solute transport could be more likely if 
hydraulic gradients develop from pond water levels or 
sustained wet conditions. Accordingly, the soil dataset is 
not used to claim embankment stability, but to strengthen 
interpretation of the water results by providing a plausible 
physical explanation for why water quality responses in 
groundwater or surface water may be absent, localized, or 
episodic under a land -application setting. This linkage 
supports the study’s novelty by moving beyond outlet -
based assessment and situating water quality outcomes 
within the physical controls that govern water movement 
around the WWTP ponds. This wording was revised to 
avoid overstating soil -hydraulic relationships, because 
moisture and shear -strength data alone ca nnot confirm 
permeability, seepage rate, or contaminant migration 
without hydraulic -conductivity testing and groundwater -
gradient data.

4. Discussion 
POME is widely recognized as a high -strength 
wastewater with high and highly variable organic loading. 
For this reason, treatment performance cannot be evaluated 
adequately by relying only on "outlet numbers" without 
also considering how receiving media respond and which 
physical controls govern water and solute transport 
pathways [18]. Under a land-application setting, landscape 
heterogeneity, including soil class, topographic position, 
and embankment condition, can generate markedly 
different environmental responses even when the effluent 
source is the same [19]. Therefore, this study interprets 
three eval uation compartments, namely wastewater, 
surface water, and groundwater, together with supporting 
soil physical data (water content and shear strength) to 
examine the plausibility of water/solute movement 
pathways. 
The influent –effluent comparison indicates a clear 
contrast between the WWTP influent (Sludge Pit) and the 
WWTP effluent (Buffer Pond), but interpretation must 
begin with data -quality control. The reported influent 
BOD₅ (10,155 mg/L) exceeds the reported influent COD 
(51.4 mg/L), which is chemical ly and physically 
implausible for wastewater because COD generally 
represents a broader oxygen demand than BOD₅. Therefore, 
the revised manuscript does not use the influent COD value 
for COD removal calculations, does not infer COD 
treatment performance from the influent–effluent pair, and 
identifies verification of the original laboratory sheet as a 
mandatory step before final quantitative interpretation. 
This revision directly addresses the editor’s concern that 
the original inconsistency undermined treat ment-
performance calculations. For internally consistent 
parameters, the WWTP shows substantial reductions in 
BOD₅, oil/grease, and total N between the influent and 
effluent. However, the effluent remains non -compliant for 
BOD₅, COD, and total N under the referenced Group II 
limits, so the system should not be described as fully 
effective or feasible in a compliance sense. The appropriate 
conclusion is narrower: the existing pond -based treatment 
provides meaningful partial treatment, particularly for 
BOD₅ and oil/grease, but further polishing or operational 
upgrading is required to address COD and nitrogen 
constraints. This interpretation is consistent with literature 
showing that pond -based POME treatment can reduce 
biodegradable fractions while leaving rec alcitrant COD 
and nutrient fractions that require additional treatment 
stages [20–22]. 
Surface water measurements show low BOD₅ 
concentrations that decline toward the receiving reach, 
while COD and oil/grease remain below the laboratory 
detection limits. U nder the assumed Class II benchmark, 
the available BOD and COD values are compliant at the 
sampled locations. Nevertheless, these findings must be 
treated strictly as a single-event screening result rather than 
evidence of no environmental impact. Water -quality 
signals in oil -palm and POME -associated landscapes can 
be episodic and hydrologically driven; therefore, repeated 
dry-season, wet -season, and post -rainfall sampling is 
necessary before stronger environmental claims can be 
made [19]. 
Groundwater obser vations are also interpreted 
conservatively. BOD₅ increased from 1.52 to 2.71 mg/L 
between the paired observations, and COD was reported as 
16.5 mg/L in the after sample, but the before COD value 
was unavailable. This pattern cannot be attributed causally 
to land application or WWTP seepage because the design 
lacks a temporal baseline, a hydraulic-gradient assessment, 
and at least one upgradient or outside -influence control 
well. The groundwater data therefore function as early -
warning indicators for future monitoring, not as proof of 
contamination or proof of safety. Accordingly, future 
groundwater monitoring should pair chemical parameters 
with hydraulic-head measurements so that the direction of 
potential groundwater movement can be distinguished 
from unr elated local variability. At the treatment stage, 
persistent residual color and organic fractions may require 
an additional polishing step beyond the existing pond 
system [23]. 
Soil results show mean natural water content ranging 
from 27.51% to 47.34%, with the highest values observed 
at Samples 2 and 7, alongside substantial spatial variability 
in direct shear strength parameters. Within unsaturated soil 
mechanics, increases in water content (and the associated 
reduction in matric suction) can reduce effective stress and 
weaken shear resistance, particularly in fine -grained or 
poorly drained materials [24]. Accordingly, locations 
exhibiting higher moisture together with comparatively 
weaker shear parameters are more reasonably interpreted 
as potentially sen sitive segments during pond level rise, 
prolonged wet conditions, or intense rainfall. If hydraulic 
gradients develop (e.g., under high pond stages or sustained 
rainfall), wetter and/or mechanically weaker zones may be 
more prone to seepage expression or p referential 
percolation pathways [24]. Nevertheless, this interpretation 
remains qualitative because the study did not include 
falling-head or constant -head permeability testing; 
therefore, a direct hydraulic -conductivity test is required 
before seepage ra tes or pollutant -transport potential are 
quantified. 
Overall, the revised interpretation is that the WWTP 
provides substantial partial treatment for BOD₅ and 
oil/grease but remains non -compliant for several key 
effluent parameters, especially BOD₅, COD, an d total N. 
At the time of sampling, surface water did not show a 
pronounced organic contamination signal; however, this is 
a snapshot result that requires seasonal and event -based 
verification. The revised follow-up priorities are: 1) verify 
the influent COD value against the original laboratory sheet; 
2) repeat water-quality monitoring across seasons and after 
rainfall; 3) add at least one external control well and, 
ideally, an up-gradient/down-gradient groundwater design; 
4) target COD and nitrogen in pol ishing or upgrading

strategies; 5) conduct falling -head or constant -head 
permeability/hydraulic-conductivity testing at 
representative embankment points; and 6) inspect 
moisture-prone embankment segments after pond -level 
rise or intense rainfall. 
5. Conclusions
The feasibility evaluation indicates that PT BTN’s pond-
based WWTP provides substantial treatment benefits for 
high-strength POME, but the system is not yet aligned with 
key effluent compliance expectations for several core 
parameters. This conclusio n has been narrowed to reflect 
the editor’s concerns about data consistency, single 
sampling, no baseline data, and limited sample numbers. 
The available data support a preliminary screening 
conclusion, not a definitive environmental -impact 
conclusion. Influent conditions at the Sludge Pit showed 
high temperature and high BOD₅, oil/grease, and total 
nitrogen, confirming that untreated POME requires robust 
containment and treatment. However, the reported influent 
COD value is inconsistent with the influent B OD₅ value 
and must be verified before COD removal or overall 
treatment-performance calculations are finalized. The 
effluent showed major reductions in BOD₅ and oil/grease, 
but remained above the referenced limits for BOD₅, COD, 
and total nitrogen; therefor e, the system should be 
interpreted as partially effective but not compliant for all 
key parameters. Surface water results did not show a 
pronounced organic loading signal during the sampling 
event, and groundwater results provide only preliminary 
screening information. Because the design lacks repeated 
sampling, baseline data, and a control-well framework, no 
causal claim is made regarding environmental impact or 
absence of impact. Future work should prioritize 
laboratory-record verification, repeated seasonal and post-
rainfall monitoring, external control wells, falling -
head/constant-head permeability or hydraulic-conductivity 
testing, and targeted polishing processes for COD and 
nitrogen before stronger feasibility or impact conclusions 
are advanced.
