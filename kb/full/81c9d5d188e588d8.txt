Abstract 
Background: Sports-related ocular trauma is a substantial yet largely preventable cause of 
visual morbidity, accounting for about 10 –20% of all eye injuries and representing a leading 
cause of monocular blindness in young, predominantly male participants; in the United States 
alone some 600,000 sports eye injuries occur annually, about 13,500 causing permanent sight 
loss. 
Aim: To synthesise and critically compare current evidence on the epidemiology of sports eye 
injuries by discipline (racket, team and combat sports), their biomechanical mechanisms, t he 
types and standards of ocular protectors, and current preventive recommendations. 
Material and methods: Narrative review of peer -reviewed epidemiological studies, hospital 
and registry series, clinical case series, finite -element analyses, a scoping rev iew and 
international consensus guidance. 
Results: Risk concentrates in fast -projectile racket sports (squash, badminton, tennis, 
pickleball), popular team ball sports (soccer, basketball, baseball, handball, floorball, field 
hockey, cricket) and full -contact combat sports (boxing, mixed martial arts). Blunt closed -
globe injury dominates, producing hyphema, angle recession, secondary glaucoma, commotio 
retinae, retinal dialysis and detachment, orbital blow-out fracture and, less often, globe rupture; 
finite-element models explain the anterior- and posterior-segment stress distribution involved. 
About 90% of injuries are preventable, and polycarbonate eyewear meeting recognised 
standards, visors and face masks markedly reduce incidence and severity, whereas o rdinary 
spectacles and contact lenses give no protection. 
Conclusions: Effective prevention requires mandatory, standardised eye protection in high -
risk disciplines, rule modification, pre -participation and periodic ophthalmic screening 
(especially for fun ctionally one -eyed, highly myopic and post -surgical athletes), and 
coordinated education of athletes, coaches and parents. 
Keywords: sports-related eye injuries , ocular trauma , protective eyewear , polycarbonate, 
hyphema, retinal detachment, combat sports, racket sports, injury prevention

1. Introduction 
Vision is central to athletic performance; in sport, nearly 80% of perceptual input is visual, and 
eye health and sports medicine are therefore closely intertwined fields of considerable 
importance to athle tes [1]. The physical nature of sporting activity renders participants more 
prone to ocular injury than the general population, and ocular trauma can leave lifelong 
sequelae that require careful follow-up and management [1]. Although musculoskeletal injuries 
are by far the most common type of injury in sport, ocular trauma is an important and clinically 
distinct problem, because even a single event can permanently compromise the vision of a 
young, otherwise healthy person and alter their career and socioeconomic trajectory for the rest 
of their life [2,3]. 
The magnitude of the problem is considerable. Sports -related eye injuries are estimated to 
account for between roughly 8% and 27% of all ocular trauma, and are consistently reported 
to represent about 10 –20% of eye injuries in young people [2 –5]. In the United States, more 
than 600,000 sports - and recreation-related eye injuries are estimated to occur each year, of 
which approximately 42,000 are severe enough to require emergency-department attention and 
around 13,500 result in permanent loss of sight [2,4]. Emergency departments in the United 
States have historically treated a sports -related ocular injury roughly every 13 minutes [2,4]. 
The overwhelming majority of those injured are male, with male-to-female ratios ranging from 
about 4:1 up to 15:1 depending on the population and disciplines studied, and most are children, 
adolescents or young adults, typically clustering between about 10 and 30 years of age [4–10]. 
Approximately 80% of perceptual demands in sport are visual, and given that most sports eye 
injuries are monocular, the loss of one eye or a substantial reduction in binocular field 
represents a disproportionate handicap for a young athlete [1,11,12]. 
A recurring and clinically decisive message ac ross the literature is that the majority of these 
injuries are avoidable. It is repeatedly estimated that up to about 90% of sports -related eye 
injuries are preventable with adequate eye protection and well-designed safety procedures [2–
4,7,11,13]. Despite this, the actual use of protective eyewear remains low across most 
disciplines: in a large cohort of athletes only about 12% used any eye protection, and in racket 
sports the median proportion of injured players wearing no eyewear at the time of injury ha s 
been reported to be as high as 93% [4,5,7]. This gap between preventability and practice, 
together with the pronounced heterogeneity of injury patterns between sports, provides the 
central motivation for the present review. 
 
 
 5 
 
The epidemiology, mechanisms a nd severity of sports eye injuries differ markedly between 
disciplines, and this heterogeneity has both geographical and cultural dimensions, reflecting 
the varying popularity of individual sports across the world. Squash and soccer have dominated 
series from the United Kingdom and continental Europe, soccer in Portugal, Norway and Israel, 
basketball and baseball in the United States, floorball in Finland and Sweden, handball, soccer 
and diving in China, and badminton in much of South and Southeast Asia [2 –5,7,9,14,15]. 
Because many studies report the most frequently encountered sports as absolute numbers rather 
than as rates per unit of exposure, a sport that is simply popular in a given country will 
inevitably appear to cause the most injuries there; this is an important interpretive caveat when 
comparing series across regions [1,5]. 
The clinical and socioeconomic stakes extend well beyond the sports arena. Ocular trauma is a 
leading cause of unilateral blindness worldwide, and its consequences – emotional distress in 
the aftermath, fear of social stigma, difficulties in daily living, and the threat of further 
deterioration of the remaining vision – fall disproportionately on young people in their 
productive years, who may initially be unaware of the lifelon g impact on career and 
socioeconomic well-being [12]. Because the eye is protected against many objects by the bony 
orbit and by reflex closure yet remains vulnerable to small, fast projectiles, and because a single 
unprotected moment can suffice to cause irreversible damage, sports ophthalmology sits at the 
intersection of high preventability and high consequence – precisely the combination that 
justifies concerted preventive effort [1,12]. 
The aim of this narrative review is to bring together and critically compare the current evidence 
on ocular injuries in sport, with a deliberate emphasis on the medical dimensions of the 
problem. The review is organised around four linked questions: the epidemiology of sports eye 
injuries by discipline category (racket s ports, team sports and combat sports, together with 
selected projectile and recreational activities); the biomechanical mechanisms that translate a 
moving projectile, implement or body part into a specific pattern of ocular tissue damage; the 
types, materi als and technical standards of eye protectors and the evidence for their 
effectiveness; and the contemporary preventive recommendations that follow, including rule 
modification, screening and education. Throughout, the intention is to move beyond a simple 
catalogue of findings towards a comparative, critical synthesis that highlights both the 
consistencies and the genuine discrepancies in the evidence base.

2. Materials and Methods 
This article was prepared as a narrative review of the scientific literature on ocular injuries in 
sport and on the effectiveness of eye protection. The synthesis draws on peer -reviewed 
epidemiological studies (including large registry - and emergency-department-based analyses 
and single- and multi-centre hospital series), clinical case series and individual case reports 
illustrating characteristic or severe injuries, finite-element biomechanical simulation studies, a 
scoping review, narrative and literature reviews published in the sports -medicine and 
ophthalmology literature, and international consensus guidance, principally the International 
Olympic Committee (IOC) consensus paper on sports -related ophthalmology issues in elite 
sport [1]. 
Consistent with the approach adopted by comparable narrative reviews with elements of a 
systematic search in this field, priority was given to large epidemiological studies, systematic 
and narrative reviews, and consensus documents describing the incidence, mechanisms and 
prevention of sports-related ocular trauma [3,13,16,17]. Studies were grouped thematically into 
four domains: (i) overall epidemiology and demographics; (ii) mechanisms and 
pathophysiology, including biomechanical modelling; (iii) discipline -specific patterns, 
subdivided into racket, team and combat sports and selected projectile or recreational activities; 
and (iv) protective equipment and preventive strategies. Because the source material was 
previously published, anonymised or aggregated data, no ethical approval or informed consent 
was required for this review. 
Two methodologic al caveats apply throughout and are discussed further in the Discussion. 
First, much of the available epidemiology is retrospective and hospital - or emergency -
department-based, which tends to capture the more severe injuries and to under-represent minor 
trauma that does not present to specialist care [1,8]. Second, definitions of eye injury, injury 
severity, denominators (exposures versus participants) and follow -up duration differ 
substantially between studies, which limits direct quantitative comparison and means that most 
cross-study statements in this review are necessarily qualitative rather than pooled. 
3. Overall epidemiology and demographics 
Across settings and study designs, the demographic signature of sports -related ocular trauma 
is remarkably consistent: the typical patient is a young male. In a casualty -department survey 
from the west of Scotland, 246 sport-associated eye injuries had a mean age of about 25 years 
and a male-to-female ratio of roughly 6.5:1, with football (association football) responsible for 
 
 
 7 
 
44.7% of cases, followed by rugby, squash, badminton and skiing [7]. A five-year retrospective 
study at the largest tertiary eye hospital in Australia identified 1,793 sports -related ocular 
injuries, of which 80.4% occurred in males, with a mean age of about 29 years and almost 60% 
of patients younger than 30; soccer, Australian rules football and basketball together accounted 
for nearly half of all cases [6]. A cross -sectional study of 1,413 athletes in Tianjin, China, 
reported an eye-injury incidence of 10.7%, with handball, water polo and diving carrying the 
highest incidences and with younger age, lower family income and training for more than four 
hours per day emerging as independent risk factors [4]. 
Population-scale data reinforce this picture while adding nuance. A multi -centre Chinese 
inpatient series of 377 injured eyes found a male -to-female ratio of 15.4:1 and a mean age of 
22.5 years, with soccer (46.8%), basketball (27.1%) and badminton (16.8%) the leading causes, 
and ball strikes responsible for about three-quarters of injuries [5]. In United States high-school 
and collegiate athletes, eye -injury rates varied markedly by sport, sex and competition level: 
the highest combined rates per 100,000 athlete -exposures were in women’s bask etball, 
women’s field hockey, men’s basketball and men’s wrestling, rates were substantially higher 
at collegiate than high -school level, and injuries were significantly more frequent in 
competition than in practice [8]. Most such injuries were minor – contusions and abrasions 
predominated and only a small percentage resulted in time loss exceeding three weeks – yet 
severe, potentially blinding injuries did occur, underscoring that a low average severity does 
not eliminate the risk of catastrophic individual outcomes [8]. 
Paediatric series merit separate attention because children bear a disproportionate share of 
sports eye trauma and because injury patterns are age-dependent. In a decade-long analysis of 
pediatric sports ocular trauma from two Indianapolis tertiary centres, baseball (38.8%), 
basketball (17%) and soccer (14.9%) were the most common causes; contusion and hyphema 
together comprised about 90% of injuries; orbital fracture was the most frequent diagnosis 
requiring surgery, with baseball responsible for two-thirds of these fractures; and archery and 
golf, although rare, produced the worst presenting visual acuity [9]. Baseball - and softball -
related injuries were significantly associated with younger children (aged 3–11 years), whereas 
soccer and (A merican) football were associated with older children (12 –18 years) [9]. A 
statewide analysis of pediatric ocular trauma in California confirmed the male predominance 
(64.9%), a bimodal age distribution with peaks in early childhood and adolescence, and a 
significant seasonal pattern, and further documented health disparities, with the highest 
incidence among Black and Hispanic/Latino children [10]. 
 
 
 8 
 
The visual consequences of sports eye injuries are generally favourable on average but 
heterogeneous, and the proportion of severe outcomes is far from negligible. Contemporary 
estimates suggest that around 10 –13% of sports eye injuries result in long -term visual 
impairment [3–5,11]. In the Chinese inpatient series, presenting visual acuity was the strongest 
determinant of final visual acuity, and final low vision (≤4/200) persisted in 10.6% of eyes, 
including three eyes that were removed [5]. This dependence of prognosis on presenting acuity 
– and, by extension, on the specific sport and mechanism that determine the initial injury – is 
a recurring theme and provides a clinically actionable rationale for prevention, since preventing 
the initial insult is far more effective than treating its consequences [5,18]. 
The global burden is difficult to quantify precisely b ecause reporting is fragmented, but 
converging estimates place sports at the origin of roughly 8–27% of all eye injuries and identify 
sport as one of the leading causes of monocular blindness in young people [4,5]. In some series 
a substantial share of post-traumatic enucleations is sport-related, and bilateral ocular trauma, 
though uncommon, occurs in a minority of sports injuries [5]. These figures, together with the 
youth of the typical patient, mean that the aggregate loss measured in vision -years is 
considerable even where individual injuries are frequently minor, and they justify treating 
sports eye injury as a public-health rather than a purely clinical problem [3–5]. 
Historical casualty -department data remain informative about severity. In MacEwen’s 
Glasgow survey of 246 sport-associated eye injuries, although sport accounted for only about 
1% of all new eye -casualty attendances, 18.7% of those injured required inpatient care, 22% 
had significant anterior -segment damage (hyphema or traumatic uveitis), 6.9% had retinal 
damage, and two eyes were ultimately lost to scleral rupture and enucleation – figures that 
established sport as a source of a disproportionate number of severe, sight-threatening injuries 
relative to its share of attendances [7]. Compara ble series across decades and countries have 
repeatedly confirmed that, while the average sport eye injury is minor and treated as an 
outpatient, a substantial minority threaten sight, so that severity rather than frequency should 
drive preventive priority [2,7]. 
Temporal and seasonal patterns add further texture to the epidemiology and warn against 
interpreting raw counts uncritically. A statewide analysis of pediatric ocular trauma in 
California found that, although the absolute incidence of eye-trauma emergency visits declined 
over 2016–2021, the proportion of ocular -trauma visits relative to all emergency visits rose, 
and a clear seasonal pattern emerged, with the highest incidence in May and the lowest in 
January – a pattern attributed to school schedul es and outdoor and recreational activity [10]. 
The COVID-19 pandemic produced a distinctive natural experiment: basketball -related eye 
 
 
 9 
 
injuries in United States emergency departments fell by 47% between 2019 and 2020, a decline 
largely attributed to reduced sports participation during lockdowns, superimposed on a longer-
term downward trend that may also reflect awareness campaigns and greater use of urgent care 
[10,19]. 
The Australian tertiary -hospital series is valuable because it moves beyond frequency to 
relative risk. Using Australian rules football as the reference, multivariate analysis identified 
the greatest risk of globe rupture or penetration with martial arts (odds ratio [OR] 16.22) and 
golf (OR 10.43); of orbital blow -out fracture with skiing (OR 14.42), hockey (OR 4.39) and 
cricket (OR 2.35); of hyphema with squash (OR 4.18), badminton (OR 3.87) and cricket (OR 
2.39); and of retinal tears with foam -dart projectiles (OR 5.59) [6]. Strikingly, paintball – 
classified as high risk but the sport for w hich a face shield is mandatory in Australia – caused 
the fewest injuries (0.33% of cases), a real-world illustration of the protective value of enforced 
eyewear that mirrors the experimental evidence discussed later [6]. The most common 
mechanisms overall were projectile impact (54.4%) and incidental body contact (28.2%), and 
traumatic hyphema with anterior uveitis was the most frequent diagnosis [6]. 
The multi-centre Chinese inpatient study is instructive because it links causative sport to injury 
zone an d prognosis. Close -globe injury accounted for 95.1% of eyes and contusion for the 
overwhelming majority; open -globe injury, although only 4.9% overall, occurred more often 
in basketball (8.3%) and in injuries from physical collision (12.8%) or racket/equipment strikes 
(11.8%) than from ball strikes (2.1%), and almost all penetrating injuries were caused by 
broken spectacle lenses worn during play [5]. Injury zone tracked mechanism: badminton 
produced the most zone II (limbal –ciliary) injuries (69.8%), where as soccer and basketball 
produced more zone III (posterior-segment) injuries; hyphema was most frequent in badminton 
(85.7%) and retinal breaks in soccer and basketball [5]. Final visual acuity correlated positively 
with presenting acuity (r = 0.421), and although badminton caused poor initial vision through 
anterior-segment media opacity, its zone II injuries were relatively treatable, so badminton 
achieved the highest proportion of good final acuity, whereas the destruction associated with 
basketball and physical collision produced worse outcomes [5].

4. Mechanisms and pathophysiology of ocular trauma in sport 
The mechanisms by which sport injures the eye can usefully be divided into five broad 
categories: impact by a ball or similar projectile; impact by a racket, bat or stick; impact by an 
opponent’s body part (finger, elbow, knee, head or fist); introduction of a foreign body; and 
injury sustained during a fall or high-speed deceleration [7]. Across most series, direct ball or 
projectile impact is the single dominant mechanism, followed by accidental body contact; in 
the multi-centre Chinese inpatient data, ball strikes accounted for 74.7% of injuries, physical 
collision for 13.8% and racket or equipment strikes for 9.0% [5]. The relative frequency of 
these mechanisms varies systematically with discipline, and it is this variation that drives the 
discipline-specific injury profiles discussed in Section 5. 
The great majority of sports eye injuries are closed-globe (blunt) injuries; open-globe injuries 
are comparatively uncommon but disproportionately serious [2,5,14]. In blunt trauma, a 
projectile smaller than the orbital aperture causes rapid antero -posterior compression o f the 
globe with compensatory equatorial expansion, transmitting force to internal structures; the 
anterior segment is the region most frequently damaged, and hyphema is the most common 
 
 
 11 
 
clinical presentation [2,4]. A blunt object larger than the orbital op ening (greater than about 5 
cm in diameter) instead loads the orbital rim and walls, and may fracture the thin orbital floor 
or medial wall – a “pressure-release” mechanism that can protect the globe from rupture but 
carries a high incidence of occult intr aocular injury [2,12]. Open -globe injury is favoured by 
small, hard, high-velocity projectiles (golf balls, paintballs) and by secondary mechanisms such 
as shattered spectacle lenses, which convert a blunt insult into a penetrating one [5,14]. 
The determin ants of injury severity are well characterised and reflect the physics of the 
impacting object: its size relative to the orbital aperture, its mass, velocity and kinetic energy, 
its hardness or elasticity, and the depth to which it can enter the orbit [5]. Larger, softer, slower 
balls disperse energy across the orbital rim and face, whereas small, hard, fast objects 
concentrate energy on the globe; the area -normalised kinetic energy therefore predicts globe 
rupture better than any single variable [5]. There is more energy in a squash ball than in a 0.22 
calibre bullet by virtue of its mass, a hockey puck can travel at 90 –100 mph, and a badminton 
shuttlecock has been clocked at extreme speeds, illustrating why apparently trivial recreational 
implements can inflict blinding injury [4]. Instructively, a softer ball is not necessarily safer: 
computational and experimental work shows that a soccer ball, although slow and deformable, 
remains in contact with the eye for roughly 10 ms – some 2.5 to 10 times longer than a harder 
ball – and moulds to the orbital aperture, generating a suction effect on rebound that distorts 
the globe and stresses internal structures [5,21]. 
Finite-element analysis (FEA) has substantially advanced understanding of these mechanisms 
by quantifying the spatial and temporal distribution of stress and intraocular pressure that in -
vivo experiments cannot capture. In a soccer-ball simulation, impact generated a pressure wave 
that travelled linearly from the anterior to the posterior pole in unde r 0.1 ms, producing 
transient pockets of high and negative pressure in the vitreous adjacent to the posterior pole 
(ranging from about +39.6 to −30.9 kPa) and localising retinal stress to the posterior pole and 
vasculature; anterior-segment injuries such as hyphema and angle recession were attributed to 
direct force transmission at the point of contact, while posterior-segment lesions arose from the 
compressive and tractional forces of the propagating pressure wave [21]. Comparison of this 
linear-force mechanism with the repetitive, multidirectional angular forces of abusive head 
trauma helped explain the more localised injury profile and lower retinal -haemorrhage 
incidence of soccer -ball trauma, a distinction with medico -legal as well as clinical relevance 
[21]. 
A badminton shuttlecock simulation reproduced the clinical observation that the head of the 
shuttlecock is similar in size to the eye and therefore transfers force directly to the globe without 
 
 
 12 
 
the cushioning of the orbit; frontal impact at 80 mph pr oduced a very brief (<1 ms) spike in 
anterior-chamber pressure of about 5.7 MPa that distributed radially towards the trabecular 
meshwork (1.25 MPa), posterior lens displacement of up to 1.5 mm with zonular strain of 52%, 
and peak stresses at the limbus, ciliary body, ora serrata and optic nerve head that approached 
or exceeded the mechanical limits of these tissues [22]. These values provide a mechanistic 
basis for the characteristic badminton complications – hyphema, traumatic mydriasis, angle 
recession, secondary glaucoma, lens subluxation and retinal dialysis – and underline why 
racket sports concentrate injury in zone II of the globe [5,22]. A comparable pickleball model 
showed that the rigid, hard -plastic ball concentrates stress at the limbus and ante rior sclera at 
velocities above 30 mph, applies zonular stresses as high as 0.37 MPa (greatest at the equatorial 
zonules and on the more exposed temporal side), and produces high retinal shear at the ora 
serrata and posterior pole, consistent with clinical reports of lens subluxation, hyphema, angle 
recession and retinal tears [23]. 
Standardised terminology underpins meaningful comparison of these injuries. The 
Birmingham Eye Trauma Terminology and the Ocular Trauma Classification distinguish 
closed-globe injury (contusion and lamellar laceration, with no full-thickness wound of the eye 
wall) from open-globe injury, and within the latter separate rupture – caused by a blunt object 
through an inside-out mechanism – from penetration by a sharp object through a n outside-in 
mechanism [1,5]. Zones describe the anteroposterior location of the wound or the involved 
tissues, and the classic notion of the seven rings of trauma conceptualises the concentric 
structures at risk from blunt injury [5]. This framework matters clinically because prognosis is 
strongly zone -dependent: posterior -segment involvement (vitreous, retina, choroid, optic 
nerve) predicts poor visual outcome, whereas many anterior -segment lesions are recoverable 
[5]. 
Computational thresholds help explai n why globe rupture is uncommon in most ball sports 
despite very high transient pressures. Models of blunt eye impact suggest that rupture requires 
corneoscleral stresses exceeding roughly 23 MPa and local dynamic pressures above about 2.1 
MPa, occurring at sites of peak stress such as the corneal apex, limbus and equator, or at pre -
existing weaknesses from prior surgery or trauma [5]. In the soccer simulation, the peak 
vitreous pressure of about 2.3 MPa (some 17,000 mmHg) recorded transiently at the point of 
contact would exceed the reported dynamic rupture threshold of roughly 0.91 –0.97 MPa, yet 
globe rupture is rarely reported after soccer trauma because this pressure is sharply localised at 
the anterior segment and dissipates to below 50 kPa before propa gating through the vitreous, 
so the globe as a whole is never uniformly over-pressurised [21]. This reconciles the apparent 
 
 
 13 
 
paradox between extreme peak pressures and the rarity of rupture, and highlights that it is the 
interaction of magnitude, localisati on and object hardness – captured by area -normalised 
kinetic energy – that determines whether the globe fails [5,21]. 
The pickleball and shuttlecock simulations add mechanistic specificity to well -known clinical 
sequelae. In the pickleball model, stress concentrated at the iridocorneal angle and trabecular-
meshwork region (about 0.35 MPa), with a secondary tensile peak during the rebound phase 
as the faster -recoiling anterior structures pulled on the lagging lens and strained the zonules; 
equatorial zonules sustained the greatest stress, and the more exposed temporal side 
experienced 34% higher stress than the nasal side because of greater scleral bending, 
mechanistically explaining lens subluxation, angle recession and the trabecular residual 
stresses that may drive secondary glaucoma [23]. In the shuttlecock model, force transmitted 
through the stiff sclera reached the optic nerve head faster than through the vitreous, producing 
compressive stress of nearly 6 MPa at the lamina cribrosa and posterior optic -nerve-head 
displacement of 2.6 mm (about 10% of axial length), consistent with reported cases of optic 
nerve avulsion, while zonular strain of 52% approached the roughly 80% threshold for 
irreversible zonular damage – a margin that would be exceeded at prof essional shuttlecock 
speeds [22]. These models collectively convert clinical observations into quantitative, testable 
mechanisms and provide a rational basis for the design and evaluation of protective eyewear 
[21–23]. 
It is clinically useful to distinguis h the primary compression phase of blunt trauma from the 
secondary rebound phase, because each imparts distinct forces. During compression, the 
projectile rapidly deforms the globe, raising intraocular pressure and loading the cornea, sclera, 
iris, ciliary body and, through the propagating pressure wave, the posterior segment, potentially 
causing corneal abrasion, iridodialysis, sphincter tears, hyphema, and vitreous and retinal 
displacement [23]. During rebound, as the globe recoils and intraocular pressur e transiently 
falls below baseline, the faster recovery of the sclera and anterior structures relative to the lens 
strains the zonules and can extend tears initiated during compression, exacerbate lens 
subluxation, and increase traction on the retina, so that some of the most damaging events occur 
after the object has already begun to leave the eye [23]. This two -phase model, quantified by 
finite-element analysis, explains why maximum zonular and posterior stresses often occur 
during rebound rather than at peak compression, and why apparently minor initial injuries can 
evolve [22,23]. 
The natural protective structures of the eye also shape the injury pattern. The bony orbit absorbs 
much of the energy of larger objects, and modelling suggests that the eyelid, while it does not 
 
 
 14 
 
substantially reduce retinal stress from a large ball, distributes force over the cornea and, when 
closure is timely, may reduce the frequency of corneal abrasion relative to deeper injury [21]. 
Anatomical variation matters: a more prominent or recessed globe, as in proptosis or in certain 
refractive states, alters exposure and therefore risk, and the more exposed temporal aspect of 
the eye is preferentially injured in several models [21,23]. These observations reinforce that 
protection must be matched to the geometry of the threat, and that reliance on natural defences 
alone is insufficient in high-energy sports [21]. 
Taken together, the biomechanical evidence converges on a coherent scheme. Anterior -
segment lesions (corneal abrasion, hyphema, iridodialysis, sphincter tears, angle recession, lens 
subluxation) reflect direct compression and pressure transmission through the aqueous, 
together with the equatorial expansion that stretches the zonules and iridocorneal angle; 
posterior-segment lesions (commotio retinae, retinal dialysis and tears, macular hole, vitreous 
and retinal haemorrhage, choroidal rupture) reflect the propagation and reflection of a vitreous 
pressure wave and the tractional forces exerted at the vitreoretinal interface, particularly at sites 
of firm adhesion [5,21,22,24]. Ageing amplifies these risks: stiffening of soft tissues, 
progressive lens hardening and vitreous liquefaction reduce the eye’s capacity to absorb 
mechanical shock and increase susceptibility at the vitreo retinal interface – a consideration of 
growing importance as older adults take up dynamic sports such as pickleball [23]. 
5. Epidemiology and injury patterns by discipline 
5.1. Racket sports (squash, badminton, tennis, racquetball, pickleball) 
Racket sport s occupy a special place in the history of sports ophthalmology because the 
combination of a small, fast projectile, an implement swung in a confined space, and the close 
proximity of opponents produces a high proportion of severe, potentially blinding inj uries 
[7,14]. The close relationship between the diameter of a squash ball and the orbital aperture 
has long focused attention on squash as a cause of serious ocular trauma, and squash has 
repeatedly been identified as a leading or the leading cause of spo rts-related ocular injury in 
British series [7,14,24]. Squash injuries include retinal haemorrhage, open -globe injury, 
vitreous haemorrhage and macular lesions, and a substantial minority result in permanent visual 
loss; importantly, the odds of injury bea r no relation to player experience, so skill offers no 
protection [24]. Retinal detachment from squash-ball accidents is well documented, frequently 
associated with large retinal tears near the ora and with avulsion of the vitreous base [24]. 
 
 
 15 
 
Badminton is the paradigmatic example of a widely underestimated but dangerous racket sport, 
particularly in South and Southeast Asia. In Malaysia, badminton accounts for nearly two -
thirds of all sport-related eye trauma, and a United Kingdom series attributed 14.3% of sport-
associated eye injuries to badminton [25,26]. The shuttlecock is the dominant causative agent: 
its small, rigid base is similar in size to the eyeball and generates significant blunt force, causing 
sudden antero-posterior compression and equatorial expansion, while its sharp feathered edges 
can cause penetrating trauma [25,27]. A clinical audit of 23 eyes in a Malaysian tertiary centre 
found that shuttlecock hits caused all injuries, 96% of patients wore no protective eyewear, 
hyphema was the commone st presentation, angle recession was detected in 17 of 23 eyes as 
early as one week after injury, and commotio retinae and vitreous haemorrhage were the 
common posterior -segment findings; although visual acuity improved significantly overall, 
three eyes re tained poor final vision [25]. A more recent single -centre case series of seven 
badminton injuries spanned the full spectrum from subconjunctival haemorrhage, traumatic 
uveitis and hyphema to commotio retinae and an open -globe corneoscleral laceration from 
shattered spectacles, with the penetrating injury carrying the poorest prognosis [27]. 
Two features of badminton trauma deserve emphasis because they carry direct preventive 
implications. First, contrary to intuition, several series report that the doubles game and, more 
specifically, the smash shot from one’s own partner – struck from close range while the front 
player turns to look back – is a frequent and especially dangerous mechanism, precisely 
because the short inter -player distance leaves almost no time to react [26,27]. Two well -
documented cases of this “doubles trouble” mechanism resulted in coup–countercoup injuries 
with subluxated lens, cyclodialysis, secondary glaucoma and Berlin’s oedema, and both eyes 
ended blind (vision <3/60) despite prompt surgery [26]. Second, prescription spectacles are 
hazardous in racket sports because they can shatter on impact and convert a blunt injury into a 
penetrating one, so glass spectacles should never be worn during play and refractive correction 
should be incorporated into polycarbonate protective eyewear [26]. 
Tennis and cricket, which are often grouped with racket and bat -and-ball sports, produce a 
similar closed-globe spectrum. A three -year prospective Indian series of 76 eyes injured by 
tennis or cricket ba lls while playing cricket found that most injuries were closed -globe, with 
sphincter tears, retinal detachment, angle recession, choroidal rupture and Berlin’s oedema 
among the most frequent significant findings; posterior -segment involvement predicted poo r 
presenting and final vision, and final visual outcome depended chiefly on presenting acuity, 
time to presentation and the type of intervention [18]. Cricket specifically endangers the eye 
when the ball rises, and severe injuries including retinal detachm ent and globe rupture have 
 
 
 16 
 
been reported, prompting recommendations for face protection for batsmen facing fast bowling 
and for close fielders [28]. A narrative review of tennis and the visual system additionally 
stresses the sport’s dual character: the fa st-moving ball drives adaptive enhancement of 
dynamic visual acuity, contrast sensitivity and visuomotor coordination, yet also carries risks 
of corneal abrasion, intraocular haemorrhage and retinal detachment from ball or racket impact, 
as well as foreign bodies and cumulative visual fatigue, for which impact -resistant 
polycarbonate glasses, UV-filtering sunglasses and regular eye examinations are recommended 
[29]. 
The history of racquetball is a cautionary tale about incomplete protection. Early eye guard s 
were open frames without lenses, and injuries continued to occur because the flexible ball could 
deform through the gap between the rims, while shattering of hard resin lenses added a second 
mechanism of harm; only after clubs mandated eyewear with impac t-resistant lenses 
conforming to the ASTM F803 standard did racquetball eye injuries fall dramatically, and no 
injury has since been reported in a player wearing F803-compliant protection [24]. The largest 
badminton series, comprising 85 eyes, confirmed th e distinctive epidemiology of the sport: 
86% of injuries occurred in doubles matches and 61% were caused by the patient’s own partner 
rather than an opponent, reinforcing the specific danger of the close -range partner smash and 
the case for protective eyewear even in recreational play [24,27]. 
Tennis illustrates that even a sport with a comparatively large, soft ball produces significant 
vitreoretinal trauma. A classic series of ten eyes injured by tennis balls found vitreous 
haemorrhage in every eye, with retinal tears and dialysis, and half requiring surgery, while a 
later series of 28 retinal detachments from tennis -ball injury found that a third had more than 
one break, most breaks were infratemporal, and 10% were associated with a full -thickness 
macular hole; other reported tennis injuries include choroidal rupture and post -traumatic 
choroidal neovascularisation [24]. A review of more than 15,000 United States emergency -
department visits for tennis -related ocular injury reported open -globe injury in 1.5% , retinal 
detachment in 0.9% and posterior vitreous detachment in 0.2%, with injury most frequent in 
those under 20 years and a modest decline over two decades [24]. 
Pickleball, the fastest-growing sport in the United States, is an instructive contemporary case 
because its rapid uptake – particularly among older adults, of whom nearly 20% of players are 
aged 65 or over – is generating a new stream of ocular injuries in a population with age-related 
ocular vulnerability [23]. Reported pickleball injuries include corneal abrasion, iritis, hyphema, 
retinal tears, vitreous haemorrhage, traumatic lens subluxation and dislocation, and posterior 
vitreous detachment, mechanistically similar to other racket sports because the eye is exposed 
 
 
 17 
 
to high-energy impact at close range [23]. The rigid hard-plastic ball, which stores little elastic 
energy, transfers most of its kinetic energy directly to the eye, and the short ball-transit time at 
the non-volley line leaves little opportunity to avoid a strike [23]. Protective polycarbonate 
eyewear is very rarely worn, and expert bodies now advocate its routine use, especially for 
those at elevated risk of retinal detachment (family history, high myopia, pseudophakia, or age 
60–70 years) [23]. 
The socioeconomic dimension of pick leball injury is substantial and growing: total medical 
costs of pickleball-related injuries were estimated at between USD 250 and 500 million in 2023 
alone, encompassing emergency visits, outpatient care, surgery and hospitalisation, a burden 
that prevent ive measures such as polycarbonate glasses could materially reduce [23]. 
Documented case reports span the clinical spectrum and the demographic profile of the sport 
– a 47-year-old man with hyphema and raised intraocular pressure, a 66 -year-old man with 
posterior vitreous detachment and retinal tear, a 77 -year-old man with intraocular -lens 
dislocation, and a case series of traumatic lens subluxation – corroborating the finite-element 
prediction that the equatorial zonules and the iridocorneal angle are espe cially vulnerable in 
this sport [23]. 
5.2. Team and ball sports (soccer, basketball, baseball, handball, floorball, field hockey, 
cricket, volleyball) 
Team ball sports account for the largest absolute numbers of sports eye injuries in most 
countries, reflecting their popularity, and are characterised biomechanically by fast -moving 
balls or pucks and frequent collisions with players and equipment [3]. Epidemiological 
syntheses consistently place basketball, baseball/softball, soccer, handball, American footb all 
and hockey among the most frequently implicated team sports, with the dominant mechanism 
being ball or object impact together with accidental blows from a hand, elbow or head [3,5,8]. 
From a medical standpoint, blunt ocular trauma and hyphema predomina te, sometimes 
accompanied by raised intraocular pressure and retinal detachment requiring surgery; most 
injuries do not cause severe permanent loss of acuity, but the proportion with permanent visual 
impairment is estimated at about 10–13% [3]. 
Soccer, the most played sport in the world, is the single most common cause of sports -related 
eye injury in Europe and a leading cause elsewhere, and although classed as only moderate risk 
it accounts for a high share of complex ocular trauma with severe consequences [14,16,30]. 
Roughly 80% of soccer eye injuries occur in men under 25 and result from high-velocity blunt 
trauma from a kicked ball [30]. The apparent paradox that a large, soft ball can cause serious 
 
 
 18 
 
posterior-segment injury is resolved by the biomechanic s described above: prolonged contact 
and a suction effect distort the globe and localise a pressure wave to the posterior pole and 
superior temporal quadrant, producing retinal dialysis, giant retinal tear, macular hole and 
retinal detachment [21,30]. A pu blished case of an 18 -year-old professional soccer player 
struck in the eye by the ball illustrates how a seemingly innocuous training incident produced 
a complex injury – traumatic mydriasis, zonular damage, retinal tear, macular oedema 
progressing towards a full-thickness macular hole, and extensive commotio – that nonetheless 
resolved with emergency vitreoretinal surgery and allowed return to competitive play within 
eight weeks, demonstrating both the seriousness of soccer trauma and the value of prompt 
specialist intervention [30]. 
Basketball is the leading cause of sports eye injury in the United States, principally because of 
frequent contact with other players; the most common mechanism is player contact (a finger or 
elbow to the eye) rather than the ball, and the most common diagnosis is corneal abrasion [19]. 
A ten -year analysis of United States emergency -department visits estimated approximately 
46,800 basketball-related eye injuries, most affecting males and children, with a significant 
decline ove r the study period that was largely driven by reduced participation during the 
COVID-19 pandemic and possibly by awareness campaigns [19]. Although vitreoretinal 
injuries are relatively uncommon in basketball because of the ball’s low velocity and large size, 
retinal detachment does occur, particularly in myopic players, and there are no eye -protection 
requirements at any level of United States basketball despite the fact that most injured players 
wear none at the time of injury [19,24]. 
Baseball and softball are consistently among the leading causes of ocular trauma, especially in 
children, and are notable for a high proportion of orbital fractures and severe injuries because 
of the hardness, size and velocity of the ball; baseball was the single most commo n cause of 
injuries requiring surgery, and of orbital fractures, in a large pediatric series [9]. Field hockey 
provides one of the clearest historical demonstrations that rule changes and protective 
equipment matter: a mid-1980s report described a disturbing cluster of perforating eye injuries 
from hockey sticks after the rule penalising raising the stick above shoulder height was relaxed, 
drawing an explicit parallel with the dramatic reduction of eye injuries in Canadian ice hockey 
after the introduction of the high -sticks rule and mandatory face protection [31]. Mandatory 
protective eyewear in United States high-school field hockey was subsequently associated with 
a 52% reduction in the eye-injury rate [8]. Floorball, a fast indoor stick-and-ball sport, has been 
a particular concern in the Nordic countries – in a Finnish series it caused 45% of all sports eye 
 
 
 19 
 
injuries – which led the Finnish Floorball Federation to mandate protective eyewear in junior 
floorball [1]. 
Several discipline-specific mechanistic and policy details reinforce this pattern. Experimental 
work using artificial orbits showed that although a soccer ball penetrates the orbit less deeply 
than smaller balls, it remains in contact with the globe for far longer and exerts a suction effect 
on rebound, explaining the disproportionately high rate of serious posterior -segment injury 
from an ostensibly soft ball [21,24]. In professional basketball, the National Basketball 
Association reported 59 eye injuries in the 1 992–1993 season and 18 in 2018 –2019, none 
involving the posterior segment, reflecting the predominance of anterior contact injury; where 
retinal detachment does occur in basketball it is concentrated in myopic players, in whom the 
relatively protruding, elongated globe is more exposed [24]. In baseball, orbital floor fractures 
dominate the surgical caseload, particularly in children, and although batting helmets approved 
by the National Operating Committee on Standards for Athletic Equipment are mandatory i n 
youth leagues, eye protection itself is not required, and catcher masks have wide orbital 
openings that the American Society for Testing and Materials advises against – a regulatory 
gap for a sport the American Academy of Ophthalmology classifies as high risk [9]. 
Cricket injuries are characteristically caused by a rising ball, and the hook shot places the 
dominant-side eye at particular risk; a classic series of five severe cricket eye injuries – 
including retinal detachment and globe rupture – all invol ved a rising ball, prompting 
recommendations for face protection for batsmen facing fast bowling and for close fielders 
[28]. In a three -year Indian series, cricket injuries occurred overwhelmingly in recreational 
players using tennis or rubber balls rathe r than the hard leather ball, with batsmen most often 
affected, underscoring that even softer improvised balls cause vision-threatening closed-globe 
injury [18]. Beyond the classic ball sports, the Tianjin athlete cohort highlighted less obvious 
hazards: handball carried the highest eye -injury incidence (38.5%), followed by water polo 
(36.4%) and diving (26.7%), and in artistic gymnastics injuries arose from accidental eyelid 
poking by ribbons – a reminder that eye-injury risk is not confined to fast-ball sports [4]. 
Other team sports complete the picture. American football and ice hockey, although associated 
with frequent head impact, have comparatively low eye -injury rates where full facial or eye 
protection is standard, illustrating again the protective effect of mandated equipment; in United 
States high -school and collegiate surveillance no eye injuries were recorded in ice hockey 
during the study period, consistent with universal face protection [8]. Lacrosse provides a 
natural experiment: mandatory prot ective eyewear in women’s lacrosse reduced eye injuries 
without an increase in head or facial injuries, countering the concern that protection encourages 
 
 
 20 
 
rougher play [8]. Volleyball, handball and basketball injuries are dominated by ball or player 
contact to the anterior segment and are usually minor, but occasionally include orbital fracture 
and retinal injury, so polycarbonate goggles remain advisable for predisposed players [5,8]. 
Across team sports, competition carries a higher per-exposure risk than practice, and collegiate 
athletes have higher rates than high -school athletes, patterns that should inform where 
preventive resources and supervision are concentrated [8].

5.3. Combat sports (boxing, mixed martial arts, Muay Thai, kickboxing, martial arts) 
Combat sports create an especially high -risk environmen t for ocular trauma because they 
involve deliberate strikes to the head and face, close physical contact and, in full -contact 
formats, rules that permit powerful blows to the orbital region [15,16,35]. In these disciplines 
the head and face account for the majority of all injuries – between roughly 29.5% and 75.9% 
depending on the sport and series – with a predominance of soft -tissue damage and facial 
fractures, and the eye and periocular region constitute a substantial share of this burden 
[15,16,35]. Systematic reviews of mixed martial arts (MMA) confirm that 66.8–78% of injuries 
involve the head, frequently the orbital-eyelid region [15,16]. 
The most detailed discipline-specific data come from a retrospective case –control analysis of 
professional and amat eur combat -sports bouts in Texas, which recorded ophthalmic injury 
incidences of 9.7 per 100 bouts in boxing and 12.2 per 100 bouts in MMA; ophthalmic injuries 
represented 37.9% of all boxing injuries and 28% of all MMA injuries [35]. The most common 
ocular injuries in both sports were eyebrow and eyelid lacerations, followed by orbital and 
eyelid haematomas and corneal abrasions, with MMA showing a greater variety of injury types 
(including “eye poke”, pupil injuries and orbital haematomas) than boxing, reflecting the wider 
range of striking tools – hands, elbows, knees and feet – and the smaller, thinner gloves used 
in MMA [35]. A key clinical –competitive finding was that ocular injury was significantly 
associated with losing the bout in boxing but not in MMA, plausibly because sustained blows 
to the face that cause eye injury also impair vision, defence and reaction time [35]. 
 
 
 22 
 
A large series from the Nevada State Athletic Commission covering nearly two decades of 
professional MMA found that 73.3% of events had at least one eye injury; among 369 eye 
injuries, eyebrow and eyelid lacerations (43%), lacerations around the eye (27%) and orbital 
fractures (17%) predominated, most were right -sided, and the majority of fighters who 
sustained an eye injury lost their bout [15]. Orbital fractures accounted for a larger share of eye 
injuries in fighters who lost, and were the most common reason for a recommendation of 
ophthalmological follow-up and for mandated no-contact periods [15]. These findings, echoed 
by earlier boxing studies reporting a high prevalence of vision-threatening injuries and retinal 
tears in professional boxers, establish that combat -sports ocular trauma is frequent, clinically 
significant and probably underestimated by injury-surveillance systems that record only broad 
body regions rather than specific organs [15,35]. 
Beyond acute globe and orbital injury, combat sports raise two additional concerns. First, 
repeated head acceleration–deceleration during knockouts and knockdowns generates shearing 
forces within the retina and choroid that may promote asymptomatic peripheral retinal changes; 
boxers and other collision-sport athletes are more likely than non-collision athletes to harbour 
such lesions, which may increase the risk of severe complications after a subsequent injury 
[16,32]. Second, in most professional MMA and boxing formats no equipment directly protects 
the orbital region and standard gloves neither prevent globe trauma nor stop eye-poke incidents, 
so protection depends chiefly on rules, r efereeing and medical supervision rather than on 
personal protective equipment [16,35]. The combat -sports literature also highlights the value 
of experience from military ocular trauma, where shorter time to surgical intervention improves 
visual prognosis – a principle directly relevant to the management of severe combat-sport eye 
injuries [16]. 
Combat sports also exemplify the concept of cumulative, subclinical ocular injury. Retinal 
screening of elite athletes found asymptomatic peripheral retinal lesions in 40% of boxers, a 
prevalence comparable to rugby and far above that in non -collision athletes, attributable to 
repetitive head acceleration–deceleration and shearing at the vitreoretinal interface [32]. Even 
without overt eye trauma, combat -sport athletes exhibit vestibulo -oculomotor dysfunction – 
impaired saccades, smooth pursuit and gaze stabilisation – linked to repeated head impacts, 
and isolated reports of infectious complications such as herpes gladiatorum with ocular 
involvement highlight t he additional risks of close skin -to-skin contact [16]. Because most 
combat-sport injury surveillance records broad body regions rather than specific ophthalmic 
categories, and because prospective studies with full pre - and post -bout ophthalmic 
 
 
 23 
 
examination are lacking, the true incidence of retinal microtrauma and minor haemorrhage in 
these athletes remains unknown and is probably underestimated [16,35]. 
Two additional strands of combat -sports evidence deserve emphasis. First, the mechanical 
difference between disciplines is quantifiable: MMA fighters wear 4–6 ounce fingerless gloves 
that, in kinetic testing, produced four to five times greater peak force than traditional boxing 
gloves, with a faster load rate, helping to explain both the higher overall inju ry incidence in 
MMA (estimated at 22.9 –28.6 per 100 athlete -exposures) and its greater diversity of ocular 
injury types [15,35]. Second, older ophthalmological studies of boxers established a high 
prevalence of vision -threatening posterior -segment patholog y: one series found a 58% 
prevalence of vision-threatening injuries and retinal tears among professional boxers compared 
with 7% in controls, and an Italian study reported conjunctival, corneal, lenticular, vitreal and 
retinal problems in 40.9% of boxers versus 3.1% of controls [15]. Data from the Nevada State 
Athletic Commission indicate that about 14% of all boxing injuries involve the eye region 
[16,35]. Together these findings argue that the ophthalmic burden of combat sports is both 
mechanically explicable and clinically serious, and that experience from military ocular trauma 
– where reduced time to surgical repair improves visual outcome and lowers the risk of 
enucleation – should inform the organisation of ringside and post-bout ophthalmic care [16,35]. 
5.4. Projectile, recreational and other sports (paintball, airsoft, golf, skiing, motocross, 
rugby) 
Paintball is among the most dangerous recreational activities for the eye. The projectile is 
small, relatively massive and travels at 80 –130 m/s or more , producing a sharply localised 
impact that transfers most of its energy to the globe and frequently causes severe injury [33,36]. 
Reported injuries include hyphema, iridodialysis, traumatic cataract, lens subluxation, scleral 
rupture, retinal detachment and secondary (often delayed) glaucoma, with a high rate of surgery 
and of permanent visual loss [33,36]. A recurring and preventable pattern is that many injuries 
occur when players remove eye protection prematurely – after they believe the game has ended, 
or because of fogging – while others continue shooting; in one series all victims had used eye 
protection during play but were injured after removing it [33]. Traumatic glaucoma following 
paintball injury may not manifest until years after the initial trauma, underscoring the need for 
prolonged follow-up [36]. Crucially, no ocular injuries have been reported in players wearing 
eye protection compliant with the relevant American Society for Testing and Materials 
(ASTM) standard, and the consistent recommend ation is that ASTM -approved protectors be 
worn at all times while any player remains on the field [24,33]. 
 
 
 24 
 
Golf produces infrequent but characteristically devastating eye injuries. A scoping review of 
golf-related ocular injury found high rates of enucleat ion or evisceration (about one -third of 
reported cases) and of blindness in at least one eye (about 38%), with adults typically injured 
by golf balls and children by golf clubs, often away from the course and without supervision 
[34]. These findings suppor t constant adult supervision of children using golf equipment, 
education in course etiquette and safety, and consideration of protective eyewear, particularly 
for beginners [34]. Skiing and snowboarding, by contrast, cause facial and craniofacial trauma 
more often than isolated globe injury; a decade-long analysis of skiing-related facial trauma in 
adults found that concussions predominated, that nasal and cervical -spine fractures were the 
most common fractures, and that males sustained the majority of inju ries, while also drawing 
attention to ultraviolet (snow -blindness) risk and to the limited evidence that helmets protect 
the face [37]. 
Rugby illustrates the danger of intentional or reckless injury in contact sports. A report of giant 
retinal tears resulting from deliberate eye gouging during a rugby tackle demonstrates that the 
eye is particularly vulnerable to punching or gouging, and that such foul play can cause sight -
threatening retinal detachment; the authors emphasise that any athlete complaining of persistent 
floaters after a blow to the eye or head should be examined by an ophthalmologist within 24 
hours [38]. Older series similarly identified combat sports, motocross and “modern” health -
club and adventure sports (squash, capoeira, paintball, motocross, new forms of soccer, bungee 
jumping) as emerging causes of severe ocular trauma, with a consistent finding that most 
injuries could have been prevented by adequate, correctly used eye protection [2]. 
The literature on emerging and mixed activities re inforces the theme that no dynamic sport is 
inherently eye-safe. A ten -year series of ocular injuries from modern health -club, adventure 
and war -game sports found that health -club activities collectively formed the largest share 
(41.6%), squash alone accounting for 29.2%, followed by paintball, motocross and new forms 
of soccer; three -quarters of these injuries were severe, and no patient was wearing adequate 
protection at the time of impact [2]. Instructive individual mechanisms in that series included 
paintball players struck after removing eye protection, motocross riders in whom a dislodged 
stone broke a non-impact-resistant lens that was driven into the eye, and retinal haemorrhage 
from the abrupt rise in intravascular pressure during bungee jumping – each pointing to a 
distinct, addressable failure of protection or regulation [2]. Airsoft and paintball together 
illustrate the projectile-sport paradigm, in which full-face protection worn continuously is the 
decisive preventive measure [2,33]. 
 
 
 25 
 
Discipline-specific detail sharpens these conclusions. In golf, historical injuries from the 
caustic liquid centres of older golf balls have been superseded by blunt and rupture injuries 
from modern balls and clubs; adults are typically struck by balls during play, children by clubs 
away from the course, and about 9% of injuries occur in spectators, so safety measures must 
extend to bystanders and to driving -range design [34]. In skiing, the paradox that helmet use 
has risen sharply yet facial and eye prote ction remains rare is compounded by risk 
compensation – helmeted, and especially occasional -helmet-using, skiers take greater risks – 
and by the high frequency of cervical -spine fractures, which co -occur with facial trauma and 
carry life -threatening potent ial [37]. In paintball, detailed case series document that severe 
injuries frequently follow removal of eye protection once players believe the game has ended, 
and that non -standard protectors fail when the ball enters beneath or displaces the mask; 
traumatic glaucoma may present years later, so both strict, continuous use of ASTM-compliant 
protection and prolonged follow-up are essential [33,36]. 
6. Clinically important ocular sequelae and their comparative frequency 
The clinical spectrum of sports eye injury spans the entire globe and orbit, and the same lesions 
recur across disciplines but with markedly different frequencies, reflecting the underlying 
mechanism. This section compares the principal sequelae and their determinants. 
Hyphema – blood in the an terior chamber – is the single most common clinically significant 
presentation of blunt ocular trauma across virtually all ball and racket sports, and is reported in 
a high proportion of racket -sport injuries in particular (up to about 85 –96% in badminton 
series) [5,25]. It results from shearing of iris and ciliary vessels during compression and 
equatorial expansion, and is important not only in itself but as a marker of more severe 
underlying injury and as a cause of secondary complications, including rais ed intraocular 
pressure and, over the longer term, angle -recession glaucoma [5,18,25]. Angle recession, 
detectable on gonioscopy, is frequently associated with hyphema and can lead to traumatic 
glaucoma years or decades later through progressive trabecular dysfunction; badminton audits 
detected angle recession in the majority of injured eyes, and traumatic angle -recession 
glaucoma developed in a subset over months to years [22,25]. Secondary glaucoma may also 
arise after hyphema, paintball injury and blunt trauma generally, and can require prolonged 
medical or surgical management, with visual acuity frequently failing to return to baseline 
[31,36]. 
Commotio retinae (Berlin’s oedema) is a common finding after blunt sports trauma, 
representing transient photoreceptor outer-segment disruption manifesting as retinal whitening; 
 
 
 26 
 
it is usually self -limiting when peripheral but is associated with poor visual outcome when it 
involves the macula [18,24]. Retinal dialysis (disinsertion at the ora serrata), giant retinal tears 
and rhegmatogenous retinal detachment form the most vision -threatening group of posterior -
segment injuries and are associated particularly with soccer, squash, tennis, basketball and 
combat sports; they arise from equatorial expansion and vitreoreti nal traction and, when they 
involve the macula, frequently leave permanent visual deficit despite successful reattachment 
[7,18,24,38]. Traumatic macular hole is comparatively uncommon and, unlike idiopathic 
macular hole, frequently closes spontaneously, so a period of observation may be appropriate 
in isolated cases, whereas complex trauma warrants earlier surgery [24,30]. 
Retinal detachment (RD) deserves particular emphasis as a severe, potentially blinding 
emergency whose risk is elevated in athletes. It s incidence in the general population is 
estimated at 5 –20 cases per 100,000 person -years, and risk factors of specific relevance to 
athletes include myopia, lattice degeneration, prior ocular surgery (especially cataract surgery 
and vitrectomy), a family history of RD and contralateral RD [11]. Collision sports increase 
the likelihood of retinal injury and of peripheral retinal lesions in elite athletes; blunt trauma 
from sports balls can cause equatorial expansion and vitreoretinal traction culminating in 
retinal tears or detachment [11]. A retrospective screening study of 88 professional athletes 
found that peripheral retinal lesions were significantly more prevalent in collision-sport athletes 
– 40.5% in rugby, 40.0% in boxing and 12.5% in judo – than in non-collision athletes (6.1%), 
with participation in collision sport associated with an approximately twelve -fold increased 
adjusted odds of peripheral retinal lesions [32]. Many of these lesions were asymptomatic and 
detectable only on careful fundus exa mination; ultra -widefield imaging showed high 
specificity but relatively low sensitivity (about 45%) compared with dilated fundus 
examination, so it cannot replace a thorough dilated examination for screening in high -risk 
athletes [32]. 
Orbital blow-out fracture is a distinctive sports injury caused by a sudden explosive rise in 
intra-orbital pressure, most often from high-velocity ball impact, that fractures the thin orbital 
floor or medial wall while the rim remains intact [1,12]. In a series of pure orbi tal blow-out 
fractures, 37% were sustained during sport – soccer most commonly – and parts of an 
opponent’s body were responsible for 70% of the sporting cases; although visual -acuity 
recovery was usually complete, permanent loss of binocular visual field with diplopia on 
certain gaze directions was almost universal, a handicap of particular importance to young 
athletes with high dynamic -vision demands [12]. The association between a fracture of the 
orbital rim itself and globe rupture, and the recognised dissociation between orbital and ocular 
 
 
 27 
 
injury (an intact orbit does not guarantee an intact globe, and vice versa), together mandate full 
ophthalmic examination of every athlete with periorbital trauma [1,12]. Open -globe injury, 
though comparatively rare i n sport, is the most serious outcome and is associated especially 
with small hard projectiles (golf, paintball), with shattered spectacles, and with intentional 
trauma; it carries a poor visual prognosis and frequently requires multiple procedures, including 
evisceration or enucleation [14,33,34]. 
Choroidal rupture and vitreous haemorrhage warrant separate mention. Choroidal rupture, a 
break in the choroid, Bruch’s membrane and retinal pigment epithelium, is a recognised 
outcome of blunt sports trauma with relatively low incidence because of the higher tensile 
strength of choroidal tissue, but it threatens central vision when it involves the macula and can 
be complicated by later choroidal neovascularisation [21,24]. Vitreous haemorrhage is 
common after posterior-segment injury – reported in roughly a third of soccer injuries – and, 
by obscuring the fundus, can delay the diagnosis of underlying retinal tears or detachment, so 
ultrasonography and close follow -up are important [21,24]. Open -globe injury remains the 
worst outcome: in golf, more than half of injuries are open -globe and about a third end in 
enucleation or evisceration, and in paintball a quarter or more of injured eyes reach no light 
perception, outcomes that starkly quantify the cost of unprotecte d exposure to small, high -
energy projectiles [33,34]. 
Several further sequelae complete the clinical picture. Traumatic optic neuropathy, an acute 
injury of the optic nerve after blunt trauma (often to the forehead), may present with severe 
vision loss – light perception or worse in up to 60% of cases – with an initially normal -
appearing disc and later optic atrophy, and must be considered after high -energy head and 
orbital impacts [2]. Iridodialysis, sphincter tears, traumatic mydriasis and cyclodialysis c lefts 
reflect direct anterior -segment disruption and may cause glare, photophobia and hypotony, 
while ciliary-body injury can produce prolonged hypotony and choroidal effusion [26]. Angle-
recession glaucoma is a characteristically delayed complication: in badminton and other blunt-
trauma cohorts, recession was detected in a high proportion of eyes within weeks, elevated 
intraocular pressure developed in a subset over the following months, and an estimated 10% of 
eyes progress to angle -recession glaucoma ove r about ten years, so lifelong intermittent 
monitoring after significant blunt trauma is warranted [22,25]. Commotio retinae reflects 
photoreceptor outer -segment disruption and light scatter rather than true oedema on 
histopathology, which explains both its transient nature and the poor prognosis when the fovea 
is involved [24]. 
 
 
 28 
 
Retinal detachment also carries a distinctive functional cost that is directly relevant to athletes: 
it is associated with longer periods of disability than retinal tears or less se vere ocular injuries 
and is more likely to limit return to sport, so counselling athletes on realistic recovery timelines 
and the probability of resuming participation is an important part of management [11]. Because 
most rhegmatogenous detachments are idi opathic but traumatic detachment is frequently 
preventable, and because early diagnosis of retinal tears and regular ophthalmic review can 
pre-empt progression to detachment, the case for structured screening and protection in 
predisposed athletes is strengthened [11]. Prompt recognition is critical: any athlete reporting 
new floaters, flashes, a curtain or shadow across the visual field, or sudden reduction of vision 
after ocular or head trauma must be referred urgently, because the interval to treatment 
influences the visual prognosis of both retinal detachment and severe globe injury [1,11,38]. 
The acute management of these injuries follows from their mechanism and severity. Hyphema 
requires rest, head elevation and avoidance of exertion, with monitoring for raised intraocular 
pressure and for secondary haemorrhage, which can occur in a minority of eyes within a week 
of injury and worsens the prognosis; larger hyphemas and those with angle recession carry a 
long-term glaucoma risk that mandates follow -up [ 18]. A suspected open globe is an 
emergency in which the priority is to prevent extrusion of intraocular contents: the eye should 
be shielded rigidly rather than padded, pressure and manipulation avoided, antiemetics given 
to prevent Valsalva, broad -spectrum antibiotics and tetanus prophylaxis considered, and the 
patient transferred urgently to an eye centre [2]. The sports -medicine physician’s role is to 
distinguish minor injuries from those requiring immediate referral – sudden loss of vision or 
visual field, a peaked or irregular pupil, an absent red reflex, marked hyphema, foreign -body 
sensation with a possible penetrating body, diplopia or restricted motility, and severe pain – 
and to initiate protective first aid without delay [1,2]. 
7. Eye protection: types, materials and technical standards 
The single most effective countermeasure against sports eye injury is appropriate, correctly 
used eye protection, and the evidence that it works is both strong and consistent [1,4,13,20,24]. 
Effective protectors di ssipate impact force over a larger area, shifting load from the eye and 
face to the skull without causing intracranial injury [4]. The material of choice is polycarbonate, 
a highly impact-resistant plastic that is roughly eight times stronger than comparable materials, 
capable of absorbing ultraviolet light, and available in both prescription and non -prescription 
forms; ordinary eyeglass lenses have only a small fraction of the impact resistance of 
polycarbonate of comparable thickness [4,20]. A newer impac t-resistant material, Trivex, 
 
 
 29 
 
offers similar protection [2]. Finite-element modelling of paediatric soccer injury indicates that 
polycarbonate protective eyewear can reduce retinal strain by roughly 47 –69%, significantly 
decreasing the severity of eye deformation on impact [11]. 
Protection takes several forms matched to the demands of each sport: sports goggles or 
spectacles with polycarbonate lenses; faceguards or visors attached to helmets; and full -face 
masks or shields [1,20]. Recommended configurations include polycarbonate sports goggles 
for basketball, soccer, tennis and racket sports; a batter’s helmet with a polycarbonate or wire 
face guard plus polycarbonate goggles for baseball and softball fielders; full face masks for 
ice-hockey goalkeepers and helmets with full-face protection for field players; polycarbonate 
eye shields attached to a wire face mask for American football; full-face protection for lacrosse, 
paintball and airsoft; and high-impact, ultraviolet-filtering eyewear or goggles for skiing [20]. 
A central and repeatedly emphasised point is that street wear offers no protection and may be 
harmful: participants using ordinary corrective glasses or sunwear are at greater risk of severe 
injury than those using no eyewear at all, because glass or standard plastic lenses can shatter, 
and hard or gas -permeable contact lenses may break on impact; contact lenses provide no 
protective benefit and are not appropriate protective equipment [4,20]. 
Technical standards are what distinguish genuine protection from a false sense of security, and 
their heterogeneity between regions and sports is itself a barrier to prevention [1]. Certification 
by recognised bodies indicates that a protector has been tested to a defined standard by an 
independent laboratory; the ASTM has established performance standards for eyewear in 
several sports (for example ASTM F803 for racket sports and, historically, sport -specific 
standards for baseball, basketball, women’s lacrosse, field hockey and skiing), and the 
European Committ ee for Standardization publishes analogous standards [20,24]. The 
importance of standard-compliant equipment is demonstrated most clearly in racquetball and 
paintball: injuries continued to occur with early “open” eye guards that lacked lenses, because 
the ball could deform through the gap, whereas no injuries have been reported in players 
wearing ASTM F803 -compliant eyewear with impact -resistant lenses [24,33]. 
Ophthalmologists and optometrists, together with local bodies and professional associations, 
are well placed to formulate and disseminate guidance on choosing the correct protector for a 
given activity, since low-quality or poorly fitting eyewear, or a helmet without a visor, can give 
a false sense of security while providing inadequate protection [13]. 
Two refinements of protective -eyewear science deserve mention. First, an impact -resistant 
alternative to polycarbonate, Trivex, offers comparable protection with improved optical 
quality, widening the options for prescription protective lenses [14]. Second, protection against 
 
 
 30 
 
impact and protection against ultraviolet radiation can be combined but must be separately 
verified: the eye-sun protection factor (E-SPF) integrates ultraviolet transmission through the 
lens with ultraviolet reflected from its bac k surface, and predictive protection factors model 
real-world performance under a defined exposure geometry, showing that a small, flat or ill -
fitting frame can perform poorly even with an intrinsically good lens, whereas goggles or large 
close-fitting fra mes give near -complete protection [17]. These principles argue for dual -
purpose sports eyewear – impact-resistant, certified to the relevant ASTM or equivalent 
standard, and providing certified ultraviolet filtration up to 400 nm with adequate frame 
coverage – particularly for outdoor and high-reflectance sports [1,17]. 
8. Prevention: rules, screening, education and current recommendations 
Prevention of sports eye injury is best conceived as a multi -level strategy that integrates 
protective equipment, rule and environment modification, systematic screening and 
coordinated education, consistent with established injury -prevention frameworks [1,3]. 
Because approximately 90% of injuries are preventable and comprehensive programmes 
combining eye protection, rule change and education can reduce the incidence and severity of 
injuries by as much as 70 –100%, the case for a proactive, organised approach is compelling 
[3,11,13]. 
The strongest evidence base concerns mandatory protective-eyewear policies. The introduction 
of mandatory eye protection has repeatedly produced steep, durable falls in injury rates: eye 
injuries in the National Hockey League fell to essentially zero after visors were mandated for 
new players; the World Squash Federation requires appropriate eyew ear for juniors in its 
championship competitions, after which no significant ocular injuries were reported; 
mandatory protective eyewear in United States high-school field hockey more than halved the 
eye-injury rate; and the Finnish Floorball Federation ma de protective eyewear mandatory in 
junior floorball after floorball was found to cause 45% of sports eye injuries in a national series 
[1,5,8]. The consistent conclusion is that the effectiveness of protection depends heavily on 
compliance, and that mandat ory, standardised policies in high -risk disciplines are justified 
[1,3]. 
Rule modification and modification of the playing environment form a complementary pillar. 
Historical experience in ice hockey (the high -sticks rule and mandatory face protection) and 
the cautionary counter -example of relaxed high -stick rules in field hockey demonstrate that 
rules governing dangerous play directly affect eye -injury rates [31]. In team sports, stricter 
enforcement against elbowing, late tackles and dangerous play, and age-appropriate restriction 
 
 
 31 
 
of the most hazardous techniques, can reduce the number of events leading to eye injury, 
analogous to concussion -prevention strategies; in combat sports, sanctions for eye pokes, 
glove-design modification and prompt stoppage when eye injury is suspected are rational 
measures [3,16,35]. Because a substantial minority of the most severe injuries result from 
intentional foul play – punching, gouging or high-sticking – firmer control of deliberate injury 
is itself a preventive intervention, and sportsmen should be aware that the sports venue confers 
no immunity from the law [12,38]. 
Screening and medical surveillance constitute a third pillar. The IOC consensus recommends 
periodic ophthalmic examination in high -risk athletes, including assessment of visual acuity 
and ocular structures, with baseline documentation and follow-up after injury, and stresses that 
the eyes are among the most exposed yet least protected organs in elite sport [1,16]. Pre -
participation ophthalmological examination is especially important in children and adolescents, 
the group most at risk of injury and of its permanent consequences, and standardised screening 
should include questions about previous eye injury, monocular status, corrective lenses, 
protective equipment and participation in high -risk sport [3]. In collision -sport athletes, 
dedicated retinal screening for asymptomatic peripheral lesions is valuable, and dilated fundus 
examination remains the reference standard because ultra -widefield imaging, although fast, 
non-invasive and cost-effective for initial screening, has limited sensitivity for subtle peripheral 
lesions [32]. Certain groups warrant particular vigilance and, in some cases, mandatory 
protection or activity restriction: functionally one-eyed athletes, highly myopic athletes, those 
with peripheral retinal degeneration or a history of retinal detachment, and those who have 
undergone cataract, refractive or retinal surgery [1,11,20]. For patients with peripheral lattice 
degeneration or a h istory of retinal detachment, abrupt head movements can trigger 
detachment, so contact and high -acceleration sports (including weightlifting and roller -
coasters) are generally discouraged, whereas cycling and swimming are considered safe [11]. 
Education an d behavioural change are the fourth pillar and are essential to convert the 
availability of protection into its actual use. Studies of knowledge, attitudes and practices 
consistently show poor adoption of ocular protection even among those aware of the ris k, and 
interventions targeting athletes, coaches, parents and organisations improve awareness and 
uptake [13]. A pragmatic study in India reduced eye -injury incidents by almost 70% through 
education directed at those at risk, and “enhanced education” packa ges combining individual 
and group sessions have improved compliance in occupational settings [11,13]. Educational 
reinforcement, exemplified by the World Sight Day theme “Love your eyes”, and the 
involvement of ophthalmologists and optometrists in formula ting job - or sport -specific 
 
 
 32 
 
guidance, are recognised routes to the behavioural change that ultimately determines whether 
preventable injuries are in fact prevented [13]. Because risk -taking behaviour – the “winning 
at all costs” attitude – itself contributes to injury, education that raises awareness of the long -
term consequences of eye injury and that shapes pro -safety attitudes is an integral component 
of prevention rather than an optional adjunct [3].

On-field and acute management, and systematic surveillance, are further components of a 
mature preventive system. The IOC consensus provides practical guidance for the sports -
medicine physician: assess visual acuity, pupillary responses and the red reflex; recognise the 
signs of orbital fracture, open -globe injury and orbital compartment syndrome; avoid any 
pressure on a suspected rup tured globe and any ophthalmic ultrasound in that setting; protect 
the eye with a rigid transparent shield rather than a pad; and refer urgently when red -flag 
features are present [1]. The recommended field bag includes a penlight with a cobalt -blue 
filter, a direct ophthalmoscope, fluorescein strips, a transparent hard -shell shield and 
appropriate lubricating, anaesthetic and antibiotic drops, and a smartphone can serve for 
illumination and for photo and video documentation that conveys more information to the 
receiving ophthalmologist than a written description [1]. At the organisational level, systematic 
recording of eye injuries within sports -injury registries – ideally using standardised, organ -
specific categories consistent with consensus reporting sta ndards – is necessary to assess the 
effectiveness of interventions and to identify emerging risks, a need felt most acutely in combat 
sports, where current surveillance under-records ophthalmic trauma [3,16]. 
Concrete examples demonstrate that rule change works. The ban on body -checking in youth 
ice hockey significantly reduced concussion rates, and by analogy measures such as shorter 
round durations, limits on absorbed strikes and prompt stoppage when eye injury is suspected 
have been proposed for combat s ports; reduced full -contact sparring in favour of technical 
training, and emphasis on blocking and evasive technique, are associated with fewer head 
injuries and, plausibly, fewer orbital injuries [16]. In field and ice hockey the historical 
sequence – rising injuries after relaxation of the high -stick rule, followed by sharp falls after 
mandatory face protection – provides perhaps the clearest evidence that governance of 
dangerous play and equipment directly determines eye -injury rates [31]. These preceden ts 
support international harmonisation of rules and of protective standards across analogous 
sports [3,16]. 
8.1. Special populations and individualised protection 
Certain athletes warrant an individualised approach in which protection is mandatory and, 
occasionally, participation in specific sports is discouraged. Functionally one -eyed athletes – 
those with corrected acuity of 20/40 or worse in one eye – and athletes who are pseudophakic 
or have undergone refractive, cataract or retinal surgery, or who have suffered previous ocular 
trauma, should wear polycarbonate protection for any sport carrying eye-injury risk and should 
avoid disciplines in which adequate protection is unavailable, such as boxing and certain 
 
 
 34 
 
martial arts [1,2,20]. High myopia and periph eral retinal degeneration warrant particular 
caution because they predispose to retinal detachment; heavy occupational or athletic lifting 
and rapid head acceleration (as in weightlifting and on roller-coasters) have been implicated in 
retinal detachment a mong myopes, and patients with lattice degeneration or previous 
detachment are generally advised to avoid contact and high -acceleration sports, whereas 
cycling and swimming are considered safe and beneficial [11]. Children and adolescents merit 
special emphasis because they sustain a disproportionate share of injuries and because early 
education and habit formation are decisive; decisions about eye protection should prioritise 
individual ocular history and retinal risk factors rather than the nominal risk c ategory of the 
sport [9,11]. 
9. Non-traumatic ocular considerations in sport 
Although mechanical trauma dominates the sports -ophthalmology literature, several non -
traumatic considerations are relevant to a complete account of the athlete’s eye and to the 
design of protective eyewear. Ultraviolet radiation (UVR) is an under -recognised hazard in 
outdoor and high -reflectance sports; acute high -dose exposure causes photokeratitis (snow 
blindness), while chronic cumulative exposure contributes to pinguecula, pte rygium and 
cortical cataract, with a less direct and more heterogeneous association with age -related 
macular degeneration [1,17]. UVR effects are best understood as tissue-specific responses to a 
shared exposure, modified by wavelength, exposure geometry a nd environment; altitude, and 
reflection from snow, water and sand amplify ocular dose, making skiing, sailing, surfing, 
cycling and endurance running high -exposure activities [1,17]. Effective ocular 
photoprotection requires both adequate spectral filtering (certified blocking of UVA and UVB 
up to 400 nm) and geometric protection (frame and lens shape that limit peripheral, oblique 
and reflected radiation); importantly, lens tint and polarisation do not indicate ultraviolet 
protection, and a clear certified lens may protect better than a dark, non-certified sunglass lens 
[17]. These principles are directly relevant to the design of dual -purpose protective eyewear 
that guards against both impact and ultraviolet exposure [1,17]. 
The tissue -specific character of ultraviolet ocular disease follows from exposure geometry. 
Peripheral light focusing concentrates irradiance about 22 -fold at the nasal limbus and about 
8-fold at the nasal lens cortex, which explains why pinguecula and pterygium arise typically at 
the nasal conjunctival–limbal region and why cortical cataract often begins in the nasal lens 
cortex [17]. Acute high -dose exposure causes photokeratitis (snow blindness), a painful, 
usually bilateral keratoconjunctivitis that appears several hours after expos ure and resolves 
 
 
 35 
 
within one to three days, whereas chronic cumulative exposure drives pinguecula, pterygium 
and cortical cataract over years, with a more heterogeneous and indirect association with age -
related macular degeneration mediated partly by short-wavelength visible light [17]. Dosimetry 
from outdoor competition confirms that athletes accumulate substantial exposure – in one study 
of school rowers the median personal ultraviolet dose in a single race exceeded the 
recommended daily threshold in most participants – so preventive counselling and certified, 
well-fitting protective eyewear should begin early, particularly in children whose clearer lenses 
transmit more ultraviolet and short-wavelength light to deeper ocular structures [17]. 
Contact lenses warrant specific comment because they are widely used by athletes for refractive 
correction but must not be mistaken for protection. Contact lenses provide no protective benefit 
against trauma and are not appropriate protective equipment; moreover, lens we ar carries its 
own spectrum of complications relevant to active individuals, including contact -lens-induced 
dry eye and discomfort, giant papillary conjunctivitis, superior limbic keratoconjunctivitis, 
allergic or toxic conjunctivitis, superficial punctate keratitis, corneal abrasion, hypoxic corneal 
oedema, and, most seriously, microbial keratitis – the risk of which is increased by poor 
hygiene, prolonged wear and, notably, swimming in contact lenses [20]. Athletes who require 
refractive correction and wi sh to participate without spectacles or lenses may consider 
refractive surgery, but this is itself a risk factor for ocular-surface disease and dry eye through 
corneal nerve transection, which can affect up to about 30% of patients and occasionally causes 
persistent, severe symptoms [1]. Finally, the broader environmental context of sport – wind, 
dust, dry or air -conditioned indoor environments and, in electronic sports, prolonged screen 
use – contributes to dry-eye disease and digital eye strain, which, wh ile not sight-threatening, 
can measurably impair comfort, quality of life and performance [1]. 
Finally, the athlete’s eye is not only an organ at risk but also a determinant of performance, and 
this dual role has preventive relevance. Because vision supplies nearly 80% of perceptual input 
in sport, uncorrected refractive error, subclinical retinal or optic -nerve pathology and post -
concussion visual dysfunction can degrade performance and may predispose to further injury, 
which is one rationale for comprehensive pre-participation visual assessment [1]. Sports vision 
– dynamic visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, depth perception, peripheral awareness and 
visuomotor coordination – can be trained and adapts with practice, as illustrated in tennis, and 
correcting deficits at the base of the so-called sports-vision pyramid supports both performance 
and safety [1,29]. Behavioural and psychological factors also modulate risk: sport psychology 
situates injury prevention within the broader physical -culture sciences, a nd personality and 
risk-taking dispositions influence the propensity to engage in hazardous play and to adopt or 
 
 
 36 
 
reject protective equipment, so that education aimed at attitudes and at winning -at-all-costs 
behaviour is a legitimate component of an ocular-injury-prevention strategy [3,39,40]. 
10. Discussion 
Viewed as a whole, the evidence assembled here supports several robust and mutually 
reinforcing conclusions, while also exposing genuine discrepancies and limitations that temper 
any simple synthesis. The first robust conclusion is epidemiological: sports eye injuries are 
common, disproportionately affect young males, cluster in a predictable set of high -risk 
disciplines, and, although usually of low average severity, carry a small but import ant risk of 
permanent, life-altering visual loss [2–5,8]. The second is mechanistic: the pattern of injury in 
any given sport is largely determined by the physics of the impacting object and the anatomy 
it loads, so that fast small projectiles and close -range implements concentrate high -energy, 
potentially penetrating trauma on the globe, whereas larger objects load the orbit and may 
fracture it while sparing or occultly injuring the eye [5,12,21 –23]. The third, and most 
consequential for practice, is preve ntive: roughly 90% of these injuries are preventable, 
polycarbonate protection meeting recognised standards is highly effective, and mandatory -
eyewear policies have repeatedly produced large, durable reductions in injury where they have 
been implemented [1,13,24,33]. 
The comparative dimension of this review reveals both consistency and instructive discrepancy 
between disciplines. There is striking cross -study consistency in the demographic profile, in 
the dominance of blunt closed -globe injury, in the prima cy of hyphema as a presenting sign, 
and in the effectiveness of standard -compliant polycarbonate protection [4,5,24,25,33]. The 
discrepancies are equally informative. The dominant mechanism differs systematically – ball 
impact in soccer and squash, player contact in basketball, deliberate striking and eye pokes in 
combat sports, and premature removal of protection in paintball – and each discrepancy points 
to a different preventive lever [19,33,35]. The injury zone differs too: racket sports and small 
projectiles concentrate damage in zone II (limbus, ciliary body, angle, zonules) and thus in 
hyphema, angle recession and lens subluxation, whereas large slow balls localise a posterior 
pressure wave and thus retinal dialysis and detachment, a distinction now g iven quantitative 
substance by finite-element modelling [5,21,22]. Even the apparently paradoxical observation 
that a soft soccer ball can blind, or that badminton – an ostensibly gentle recreational game – 
is a leading cause of monocular blindness in part s of Asia, is resolved once mechanism rather 
than intuition guides interpretation [5,21,25]. 
 
 
 37 
 
Several limitations of the underlying evidence base must be acknowledged and constrain the 
strength of any recommendation. First, most epidemiological data are retrospective and derive 
from emergency departments, hospitals or specialist eye centres, which over -represent severe 
injuries and under -count minor trauma managed elsewhere or not at all; true population 
incidence is therefore poorly defined [1,8]. Second, there is substantial heterogeneity in injury 
definitions, severity grading, denominators and follow -up, which precludes valid quantitative 
pooling and means that many cross-study comparisons in this review are necessarily qualitative 
[3,5]. Third, because m ost series report absolute case numbers rather than exposure -adjusted 
rates, the apparent ranking of dangerous sports is confounded by local popularity, so a sport 
that merely has many participants will spuriously appear especially hazardous [1,5]. Fourth, 
the finite-element studies, although powerful, rely on simplifying assumptions and on material 
properties derived from ex -vivo tissue that may not fully reproduce in -vivo behaviour, and 
most do not yet incorporate ageing or protective eyewear [21 –23]. Fifth, protective-eyewear 
use is frequently undocumented in clinical records, which hampers direct estimation of its real-
world effectiveness and argues for routine recording of eyewear status in all sports eye -injury 
encounters [6]. Finally, combat -sport sur veillance in particular tends to record broad body 
regions rather than specific ophthalmic categories, almost certainly underestimating the true 
burden of ocular trauma and precluding reliable estimation of retinal microtrauma; 
standardised, organ -specific surveillance (for example in line with consensus reporting 
standards) is a clear priority [15,16,35]. 
These limitations notwithstanding, the practical implications are clear and actionable. Effective 
prevention requires the integration of medical and orga nisational interventions: mandatory, 
standardised polycarbonate eye protection in high -risk disciplines; rule and environment 
modification to limit dangerous play; pre-participation and periodic ophthalmic screening, with 
particular attention to functional ly one-eyed, highly myopic and post -surgical athletes and to 
asymptomatic peripheral retinal lesions in collision-sport athletes; unified acute-care protocols 
with clear criteria for red-flag symptoms, removal from play and prompt specialist referral; and 
sustained education of athletes, coaches and parents to change attitudes and improve 
compliance [1,3,11,13,16]. International harmonisation of guidelines, systematic injury 
surveillance within sports -injury registries, and interdisciplinary collaboration b etween 
ophthalmology, sports medicine, biomechanics, epidemiology and health education would 
materially strengthen both the evidence base and its translation into practice [3,16]. Future 
research should prioritise prospective, exposure-adjusted studies; incorporation of ageing and 
of protective eyewear into biomechanical models to quantify protection and inform equipment 
 
 
 38 
 
standards; and prospective ophthalmic studies in combat sports, including routine retinal 
assessment before and after competition and long-term follow-up of cumulative eye and head 
trauma [16,23,36]. 
A final synthesising observation is that the epidemiological and biomechanical strands of 
evidence, though methodologically very different, tell a consistent story. Epidemiology 
identifies which sports and mechanisms injure the eye and how severely; biomechanics 
explains why, translating the size, speed, hardness and geometry of each impacting object into 
a predictable pattern of anterior - or posterior-segment damage; and the protective -equipment 
literature closes the loop by showing that interrupting the transfer of energy to the globe 
prevents the injury [5,21,22,33]. This convergence is reassuring, because it means that 
recommendations rest not on any single fragile dataset but on mutually rein forcing lines of 
evidence, and it is the strongest available justification for the mandatory, standardised, enforced 
protection advocated throughout this review [1,3,13]. 
Several priorities for future research follow directly from these gaps. Prospective, exposure-
adjusted cohort studies with long -term follow-up are needed to define true incidence and the 
natural history of injuries such as angle recession and peripheral retinal lesions; biomechanical 
models should incorporate ageing and, crucially, protect ive eyewear so that the magnitude of 
protection can be quantified and used to inform equipment standards; and combat sports in 
particular require prospective studies with full ophthalmic examination before and after 
competition to capture cumulative and su bclinical trauma [16,22,23]. Equally important is 
implementation research: identifying which combinations of mandate, education and 
equipment design most effectively raise and sustain protective -eyewear use, and how best to 
harmonise standards internationally, would translate the well-established efficacy of protection 
into population-level effectiveness [3,13,16]. 
A recurring and sobering theme is the gap between the strong evidence for protection and its 
weak uptake. Studies of knowledge, attitudes and pr actices show that even athletes who are 
aware of the risk frequently do not use protection, whether because of concerns about comfort, 
appearance or performance, a false sense of security from ordinary spectacles or from helmets 
without visors, or a sporting culture that normalises risk [13]. This behavioural gap means that 
technical solutions alone are insufficient; durable reductions in injury have been achieved 
chiefly where protection is mandatory and enforced, as in ice hockey, women’s lacrosse, high-
school field hockey and junior floorball and squash [1,8,13]. The implication for policy is clear: 
voluntary recommendation is necessary but not sufficient, and in high -risk disciplines 
mandatory, standardised, enforced protection – supported by sustained e ducation that targets

attitudes as well as knowledge – is the intervention most likely to convert preventability into 
prevention [3,13]. 
11. Conclusions 
Ocular injuries in sport are frequent, potentially severe and, above all, largely preventable. 
They affect predominantly young males, cluster in fast -projectile racket sports, popular team 
ball sports and full -contact combat sports, and produce a characteristic spectrum of blunt 
closed-globe injury – hyphema, angle recession and secondary glaucoma, commotio retinae, 
retinal dialysis and detachment, orbital blow-out fracture and, less often, globe rupture – whose 
distribution across the eye is now well explained by the biomechanics of each sport. Although 
average severity is modest, a small but clinically imp ortant minority of injuries cause 
permanent, often monocular, visual loss in people at the start of their productive lives. 
The central and consistent message of the literature is that approximately nine in ten of these 
injuries could be prevented. Polycarbonate eyewear conforming to recognised standards, sport-
specific visors and full-face protection markedly reduce both the incidence and the severity of 
injury, whereas ordinary spectacles and contact lenses offer no protection and may aggravate 
harm. Effective prevention therefore demands the integration of medical and organisational 
measures: mandatory, standardised eye protection in high -risk disciplines; rule modification 
and firmer control of dangerous play; pre-participation and periodic ophthalmic screening, with 
individualised protection for functionally one-eyed, highly myopic and post-surgical athletes; 
unified acute -care and referral protocols; systematic, organ -specific injury surveillance; and 
sustained education of athletes, coaches and parents . The combination of medical and 
organisational analysis should form the basis for designing effective eye -injury prevention 
programmes in sport, so that participation in physical activity, with all its health benefits, need 
not be paid for in avoidable blindness.
