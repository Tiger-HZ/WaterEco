 Micropollutants in the effluent meet the European environmental quality standards

Abstract 1 
In this work the occurrence and fate of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), phthalic acid 2 
esters (PAEs), mono and diethoxylate alkylphenols (AP 1-2EOs) and alkylphenols (APs) have been 3 
investigated during a two -weeks period in a facility treating mixed textile -domestic wastewater 4 
(Prato, Italy). The wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) consists of primary sedimentation, activated 5 
sludge biological oxidation, secondary sedimentation, clariflocculation and ozonation. The sludge is 6 
treated withi n the facility by thickening, dewatering and final incineration , thus providing the 7 
almost quantitative removal of the adsorbed micropollutants . Naphthalene (50%), di(2 -ethylhexyl) 8 
phthalate (74%) and branched 4 -nonylphenols (59%) were the individual main representative 9 
compounds of each class in the influent wastewater, which showed c oncentration ranges of 5.6-66, 10 
85-290 and 21-133 µg/L for PAHs, PAEs and APs+AP 1-2EOs, respectively. The WWTP efficiently 11 
removed PAHs, PAEs and APs+AP1-2EOs, providing efflue nt concentrations of 0.075-0.16 ng/L 12 
0.38-9.9 µg/L and 0.53 -1.4 µg/L . All targeted priority and priority -hazardous micropollutants 13 
showed effluent concentrations in line with the European environmental quality standards (EQS) , 14 
even though for di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate and benzo(a)pyrene after correction for the dilution 15 
factor of the recipient. The WWTP performance were evaluated by mass balance, verifying its 16 
accuracy by monitoring Pb and Cd as conservative species. The biological treatment sections 17 
provided mass losses of 85.5%, 74.5% and 56.8% for APs+AP1-2EOs, PAEs and PAHs , 18 
highlighting efficient biotransformation performances of the activated sludge process. Howev er, for 19 
the more volatile PAHs (e.g. naphthalene), a significant contribution of stripping cannot be 20 
excluded. A remarkable mass loss was also determined in the ozonation stage for PAEs (72.9%) 21 
and especially PAHs (97.0%), whereas a lower efficiency was ob served for APs+AP1-2EOs 22 
(41.3%). The whole plant allowed for obtaining an almost quantitative removal (96.7 -98.4%) for all 23 
targeted compounds. 24 
Keywords: organic micropollutants; activated sludge; ozonation; environmental quality standard; 25 
biotransformation; sludge sorption 26

1 Introduction 27 
Wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) have an important role in the con trol of environmental 28 
pollution, especially towards organic micropollutants, but may represent at the same time a point -29 
source of contaminants in watercou rses. Accordingly, the accurate monitoring of organic 30 
micropollutants within the various treatment stages of WWTPs is required in order to obtain an 31 
effluent with the lowest possible contamination , through the optimization of the treatment 32 
conditions. This aspect is particularly relevant since organic compounds may undergo different 33 
processes in WWTPs (i.e. degradation, partition between liquid and solid phases, aerosolization and 34 
evaporation). The fate of organic compounds during wastewater treatment m ay be carefully 35 
investigated by the mass -balance approach, which consists in the simultaneous evaluation of mass 36 
flows of target contaminants in the various WWTP treatment stages . This approach allows for 37 
obtaining a number of relevant information on organ ic compounds in wastewater, such as (i) mass 38 
loading, (ii) partitioning behaviour, (iii) degradability, (iv) overall persistence under real -world 39 
conditions, as well as (v) mass loading to surface water via effluent discharge (Heidler and Halden, 40 
2008). This approach has been adopted for evaluating the fate of different organic pollutants, 41 
including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) (Manoli and Samara, 1999 ; Qiao et al., 2014 ; 42 
Sun et al., 2018; Yao et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2018), phthalic acid esters (PAEs) (Clara et al., 2010; 43 
Gao et al., 2014 ; Martínez-Alcalá et al., 2017 ; Roslev et al., 2007 ; Tan et al., 2007 ), as well as 44 
ethoxylate alkylphenols (AP nEOs) and alkylphenols (APs) (Loyo-Rosales et al., 2007 ; McAdam et 45 
al., 2010 ; Tan et al., 2007 ). These micropollutant classes have been frequently detected in 46 
wastewater and their occurrence at μg/L levels has been associated with various toxicological 47 
effects, such as endocrine disruption in marine organisms, neurotoxicity and alterations at the 48 
ecosystem level (Sánchez-Avila et al., 2009). 49 
PAHs are ubiquitous pollut ants formed mainly by incomplete combustion processes (Deblonde et 50 
al., 2011; Peng et al., 2015 ). Natural processes such as forest fires or v olcanic eruptions contribute 51 
to PAH emissions, as well. Being ubiquitous, PAHs have been often detected in several wastewater 52 
3 
 
treatment plants (Blanchard et al., 2004). In humans, the metabolization of PAHs with four or more 53 
rings leads to products able to interact with enzymatic systems, such as cytochrome P450, inducing 54 
the development of tumours (Apostoli P. Cassano F., 1997 ). For these reasons most PAHs have 55 
been list ed as priority (naphthalene and fluoranthene) or priority hazardous substances 56 
(benzo(a)pyrene, benzo(b)fluoranthene, benzo(k)fluoranthene, benzo(g,h,i)perylene, indeno (1,2,3 -57 
c,d)pyrene and anthracene) (Directive 2013/39/EU). 58 
PAEs are widely used in vario us industrial and household applications such as adhesives, paints, 59 
inks and rubbers. However, a bout 90% of PAEs are used as plasticizers for polymers, mainly 60 
polyvinyl chloride and other resins, because of the stability and fluidity of high molecular weig ht 61 
congeners (Bergé et al., 2014 ; Julinová and Slavík, 2012 ). Since p hthalates are not chemically 62 
bound to the polymeric matrix, they can enter the environment by losses during manufacturing 63 
processes and by leaching and/or evaporation. Accordingly, also PAEs can be considered 64 
ubiquitous pollutants and may reach WWTPs throu gh both domestic and industrial wastewater, as 65 
well as urban runoff and atmospheric depositions (Clara et al., 2010 ; Dargnat et al., 2009 ). 66 
Although these compounds are excreted from the organism quite rapidly, they may accumulate in 67 
fatty tissues and biological fluids (Del Bubba et al., 2018 ), thus negatively affecting hormonal and 68 
reproduction systems due to their estrogenic activity (Julinová and Slavík, 2012 ).The most widely 69 
used PAE is the di-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (DEHP) which has been listed as a priority substance in 70 
the Directive 2013/39/EU. 71 
APnEOs with a branched alkyl chain of 8 -9 carbon atoms are non-ionic surfactants largely used as 72 
detergents, emulsifiers, wetting and solubilizing agents (Salgueiro-González et al., 2017 ). Their 73 
main degradation metabolites are AP nEOs oligomers with 1 -2 EO units (AP1-2EO) and APs, the 74 
latter being the final stage of the ethoxylate chain breakdown (Sacco et al., 2006 ). Furthermore, 75 
branched octylphenols (OPs) and nonylpheno ls (NPs) are highly used as such, for plastic 76 
manufacture as well as for production of textiles and pesticide formulations (Sánchez-Avila et al., 77 
2009). These metabolites are known to be more toxic and more estrogenic than the parent 78 
4 
 
substances. In particular, branched OPs and NPs can mimic natural hormones by interact ing with 79 
the estrogenic receptors causing serious damages to the endocrine system (Ying et al., 2002 ). 80 
Therefore, they are included in the group of endocrine disrupting chemicals and their occurrence in 81 
the water cycle is considered a relevant environmental issue. The potential adverse effects of APs 82 
and APnEOs to humans and other organisms led to the inclusion of 4 -tert-octylphenol (4-t-OP) and 83 
4-nonylphenol (4-NP, i.e a mixture of more than two hundred isomers) in the list of chemicals of 84 
concern in European waters as priority and priority hazardous substances, respectively (European 85 
Parliament and Cou ncil of the European Union, 2008 ). Moreover, the Directive 2013/39/EU set 86 
annual environmental quality standards (EQS) in water for 4-t-OP and 4-NP (European Parliament 87 
and Council of the European Union, 2013). 88 
Based on the previous considerations, the aim of this work was to give an accurate picture of the 89 
concentrations of PAHs, PAEs, 4-t-OP1-2EOs and 4-NP1-2EOs, as well as 4-t-OP and 4-NPs, both 90 
within wastewater and sludge treatment lines of the main WWTP of the industrial textile district in 91 
the city of Prato (Italy) , which is one of the most important in Europe . This facility consists of a 92 
complex wastewater treatment train (i.e. preliminary treatments, primary settling, oxic biological 93 
treatment, secondary sedimentation, clariflocculation and ozonation), together with a sludge section 94 
including thickening, dewatering and a multiple-hearth waste-to-energy plant for sludge 95 
incineration. As far as we know, such a complex configuration has not been previously considered 96 
for ma ss balance of organic micropollutants. The WWTP object of this research was previously 97 
investigated for the presence of the aforementioned organic compound classes (Lepri et al., 1997 ). 98 
However, in the previous work, only the wastewater treatment line was considered, which at that 99 
time was devoid of the ozonation phase. The evaluation of the WWTP output in the receiving water 100 
body in relation to the environmental quali ty standards set by the European Directives was also 101 
given. Furthermore, the calculation of the mass balance of these analytes was performed in various 102 
treatment sections of the WWTP, as well as in the whole plant . Accordingly, the aforementioned 103 
analytes were determined in wastewater and sludge within all treatment stages, as well as in ashes 104 
5 
 
produced during sludge incineration. The heavy metals Cd and Pb, which are listed as priority 105 
substances by the Directive 2013/39/EU , were also included in the study and monitored in the 106 
WWTP matrixes mentioned above , as species not subjected to degradation processes, as well as to 107 
any significant evaporation/aerosolization phenomena, in order to check the correctness of the mass 108 
balance calculation. In this regard, it should be highlighted that in only one study the analysis of 109 
conservative species was performed in order to assess the reliability of mass balance calculation of 110 
organic micropollutants in WWTPs (Clara et al., 2010). 111

3 Results and Discussion 237 
Concentrations of the investigated micropollutants (i.e. PAHs, PAEs, AP 1-2EOs and APs) were 238 
monitored in the sampling points reported in Fig. 1, which involved both the water and the sludge 239 
treatment lines. 240 
3.1 Overall removal performances of the WWTP 241 
3.1.1 PAHs 242 
In this study 16 different PAHs have been investigated and their relative pe rcentages in the WWTP 243 
influent and effluent are shown in Fig. 2A. 244 
Among investigated PAHs, Nap resulted by far the most abundant compound in inlet samples, 245 
accounting for about 50% of the whole PAH concentration. This finding might be due to the fact 246 
that this PAH is the only one used for the production of dyes and moth -killer products, which are 247 
widely applied in textile industry (Azpíroz et al., 2008 ; Rogers, 1996). Interestingly, Nap was the 248 
prevalent PAH in the effluent wastewater, as well . The pre dominance of Nap in the WWTP outlet 249 
could be explained by its lower tendency to be partitioned into sludge. Furthermore, it should be 250 
remarked that an increased resistance to degradation by ozone has been reported for PAHs, with 251 
decreasing the ring number (Butkovic et al., 1983 ; Kulik et al., 2006 ; Trapido et al., 1995 ). In this 252 
regard, it should also be noted that a prevalence of Nap in effluent wastewater is a finding 253 
highlighted also in other studies regarding either the same WWT P herein investigated (Lepri et al., 254 
1997) or other facilities (Mezzanotte et al., 2016; Vogelsang et al., 2006). 255 
11 
 
Inlet concentrations (water and particulate matter) of total PAHs ranged from 5.6 to 66 µg/L (see 256 
Table 2), which were in quite good agreement with data obtained about twenty years ago in the 257 
influent of the same WWTP (Lepri et al., 1997 ). The lowest concentrations were determined in 258 
samples 4 and 7, which represented public holidays . However, even considering only the working 259 
days, a high concentration variability was observed, probably due to the fact that PAHs are 260 
ubiquitous pollutants deriving from a wide range of sources , including any incomplete combustion 261 
process of organic substances (Peng et al., 2015). 262 
As illustrated by data reported in Table 2, t he largest decreases of PAH concentrations were 263 
observed after the biological treatment and secondary sedimentation (sampling point C) and 264 
following ozonation (sampling point E) , as well. It is particular ly remarkable that this latter 265 
treatment provided removal efficiencies in respect to the previous stage, mostly included between 266 
94% and 98%, with the only exception of sample 7, which showed a quite lower performance 267 
(82%). As a result, in all the investigated samples the effluent wastewater showed to tal PAH 268 
concentrations ≤ 22 ng/L. 269 
In agreement with data reported in a previous study (Lepri et al., 1997), a strong enrichment of total 270 
PAHs was generally observed in the particulate phase of WWTP inlet (sampling point A) and 271 
effluents from primary and secondary sedimentation (sampling points B and C), where mg/kg to 272 
tens of mg/kg were determined, depending on the sampling day considered . The enrichment tended 273 
to decrease going from the inlet to the secondary settling. PAH concentrations separately 274 
determined in the aqueous and particulate phase s were conversely not available in samples taken at 275 
the clariflocculation outlet (sampling point D) and in the WWTP effluent (sampling point E). In 276 
fact, in these sampling stations, the concentrations of suspended solids were always lower than 25 277 
mg/L and the extraction was therefore performed on the whole sample (see paragraph S1.5.1 of the 278 
Supplementary Material section). 279 
Concentrations of priority hazardous PAHs found in the influent ranged from 0.6 to 18 µg/L and 280 
accounted on average for about 17% of total PAHs, highlighting their significant occurrence in the 281 
12 
 
inlet of the sewage facility under study. The whole treatment reduced the concentrati ons of these 282 
PAHs at tens of ng/L, which is an overall remarkable result. 283

3.2 Mass balance 476 
Table 5 shows the mass of heavy-metals and organic compound classes herein investigated, passing 477 
through the various sampling points of Fig. 1. Table 6 illustrates the mass balances (MB) deriving 478 
from equations 4 -9, together with the percentage imbalances (Δ), obtained as the percentage ratio 479 
between the mass balance and the total positive contributions to a given treatment section. Hence, 480 
positive values of MB and Δ represent net removal within each WWTP section and whole facility. 481 
A correct approach to the evaluation of the results of a mass balance calculation for a given 482 
substance, must take into account its physicochemical properties, which determine its fate during 483 
the various stages of wastewater and sludge treatment. Among the m, it should be considered the 484 
tendency of the substance to undergo transfer from the liquid phase to the atmosphere or to be 485 
degraded by the activated sludge biomass. Furthermore, it is required the achievement of a dynamic 486 
equilibrium between all the pos itive and negative contributions to the mass balance itself. In this 487 
20 
 
regard, it should be remarked that, even within the same depuration section, such an equilibrium is 488 
difficult to be obtained because of the many processes and treatments included in each section. 489 
Another limitation in the evaluation of the mass balance of P and PH substances derives from their 490 
low concentrations (i.e. ppt-ppb). Hence, even small measuring inaccuracies, which are intrinsic in 491 
the determination of so low concentrations, may have a significant impact on the correctness of the 492 
mass balance calculations, due to the error propagation related to the multiplying factors of 493 
volumetric flow rate s of WWTP and coagulant/flocculant reagents, as well as ash amounts 494 
produced by the incinerator (see equations 1-3). 495 
As regards the contribution of the addition of coagulant/flocculant reagents to the mass balance, the 496 
analysis performed on these reagents did not reveal concentrations above method detection limits 497 
with the only exception of Cd and Pb in FeCl 3, which is dosed at the R1 dosage point (see Fig. 1). 498 
More in detail, for these metals, concentrations of 15.1±3.2 and 92.4 ±3.9 mg/L were determined 499 
based on five replicated analyses in the FeCl3 reagent. 500
