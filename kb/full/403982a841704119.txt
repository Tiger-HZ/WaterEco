One of the main obstacles to resolving the conflict between an increasing population of cormorants, Phalacrocorax carbo sinensis , and recreational and commercial fishing, is the limited evidence of the effect of the birds’ predation on fish stocks. Tagging and releasing fish with PIT-tags followed by intensive scanning of cormorant colonies has proven a viable method to estimate the impact of cormorants on local fish populations. In 2022 and 2024, a total of 2783 cod ( Gadus morhua ), 11 – 38 cm and 2644 flounder ( Platichthys flesus ), 12 – 30 cm, were caught in pound nets and tagged with 14- or 23-mm PIT-tags, in an open coastal area of the Western Baltic. Subsequent scanning events in local cormorant colonies and roosting sites revealed that a high proportion of the PIT-tags had been deposited in the colonies. The period from tagging till the birds had left the colony was 2–7 months. After correcting for the chance of detecting an eaten tag, determined by two feeding trials, the predation proportion for the different size classes of cod and flounder was calculated. Thus, on average between 52% and 88% of cod were eaten by great cormorants, depending on cod size. For flounder, the average predation proportion ranged between 14% and 84% for the least and most vulnerable size classes, respectively. The results show that cormorant predation is size selective, especially on flounder, and that predation on the most vulnerable sizes of both cod and flounder was very high. The high predation by cormorants measured in two years in an open coastal area indicates a potential mortality bottleneck in the life cycles of cod and flounder in the Western Baltic areas with the current, dense cormorant population. Further similar studies combined with population modelling are needed to establish cause-effect relationship between predation and population size.
