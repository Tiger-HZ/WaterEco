Abstract
In this study, the agronomic response of radish and the leaching behavior of nutrients, salts 
and trace elements were investigated under fertigation with synthetic waters reproducing 
treated domestic wastewater, industrial effluent and landfill leachate. The water composi -
tions were derived from a systematic review of 75 wastewater-fertigation studies and were 
evaluated in two nutrient-poor sandy soils through batch tests and a 40-day laboratory pot 
experiment. Mixed-effects models identified significant Soil × Water × Time interactions 
for 13 of the 14 drainage-water variables. Nitrate, sodium, chloride and potassium showed 
high mobility, whereas trace-element concentrations in drainage water remained low. For 
biomass, Soil and Water had significant main effects on all variables, whereas no Soil × 
Water interaction was detected. The nutrient-containing waters increased radish biomass 
relative to the deionized-water control, with the highest total dry biomass recorded under 
industrial-effluent fertigation in the Siliceous Riparian Sandy Entisol. In contrast, separate 
analyses of leaf elemental concentrations detected significant Soil × Water interactions for 
cadmium, copper, magnesium, manganese, lead and zinc. Foliar sodium reached 25.7 g 
kg⁻¹ dry matter under landfill-leachate fertigation in the Calcareous Sandy Calcisol, in -
dicating a potential crop-quality concern. Although the synthetic waters enhanced short-
term biomass production, they also promoted substantial leaching of nutrients and salts. 
Therefore, their agronomic use in sandy soil requires careful control of water composition, 
application rate and drainage, together with validation using real wastewater in longer-
term and field-scale experiments.

1 Introduction
The growing global demand for freshwater has been confronted with an increasingly limited 
availability of this resource. Projections indicate that by 2030, demand could far exceed the 
total available volume under sustained population growth, urban expansion, and increasing 
pressure on production systems (Ricart and Rico 2019). This water stress has been aggra -
vated by the degradation of surface water as well as groundwater quality, which increases 
the vulnerability of agricultural systems that depend on good-quality water resources to 
maintain stable and safe yields (Sidhu et al. 2019). Competition among the agricultural, 
industrial, and domestic sectors for access to water resources has become a central challenge 
for sustainable water management and has driven the search for innovative strategies for 
saving, recycling, and reuse (Jeong et al. 2020). In this scenario, the reuse of treated waste-
water has been considered a key strategy to relieve pressure on conventional sources and 
ensure crop irrigation. In particular, the use of treated domestic wastewater offers an addi -
tional and relatively stable source of irrigation water, less dependent on seasonal droughts 
and closely associated with urban flows (Gava et al. 2023).
In this context, the problem is exacerbated in sandy soil, considered particularly fragile 
for agricultural production due to its low water retention capacity, limited nutrient reserves, 
and reduced organic matter content (Alessandrino et al. 2021; de Holanda et al. 2025). The 
usage of treated wastewater as fertigation emerges as a particularly useful tool because it 
makes it possible to take advantage of dissolved nutrients present in effluents from waste -
water treatment plants (WWTPs) and reduce dependence on external mineral fertilizers, 
if water quality remains within safe ranges for soil and crops (Muscarella et al. 2024). 
However, fertigation with treated wastewater poses sanitary, environmental, and regulatory 
challenges. These effluents may contain high concentrations of nutrients, metals, organic 
matter, and other compounds capable of triggering environmental issues (Mainardis et al. 
2022; Chauhan and Kumar 2020). Therefore, their agricultural use requires both effective 
treatments and strict compliance with specific regulatory frameworks, including European 
directives on water quality and groundwater protection, complemented by the guidelines of 
the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations for wastewater reuse in 
agriculture (Radini et al. 2023; FAO 1992). From environmental, policy and economic per-
spectives, fertigation with treated effluents supports circular economy strategies by enhanc-
ing resource efficiency, promoting nutrient recycling and reducing dependence on mineral 
fertilisers, particularly under rising fertiliser costs. However, when application rates are not 
aligned with crop demand and soil retention capacity, drainage waters may transport mobile 
nutrients and salts beyond the root zone, increasing the risk of groundwater contamina -
tion (Azad et al. 2020). Therefore, the current literature advocates a holistic approach to 
assessing nutrient dynamics in soil, plant uptake, and corresponding drainage water qual -
ity (Albdaiwi et al. 2022; Romero-Trigueros et al. 2021). Even though the number of case 
studies is increasing, there is currently insufficient experimental evidence from controlled 
conditions to establish a clear connection among irrigation water quality, crop response, 
drainage composition, and soil nutrient dynamics. However, the abatement of major inor -
ganic contaminants and trace elements (TEs) to meet FAO guidelines for irrigation water 
is often unfeasible. Therefore, alternative agronomic practices must be adopted to mitigate 
the impacts of using waters with concentrations exceeding the suggested limits (Obijianya 
et al. 2025).

Despite the increasing number of studies on wastewater fertigation, the marked 
variability in wastewater composition, treatment level and experimental conditions limits 
direct comparison among individual case studies. This study uses the evidence synthesized 
by Osorio et al. ( 2025) from 75 wastewater-fertigation studies to define standardized 
experimental water profiles and establish a consistent basis for evaluating the effects of 
different wastewater compositions. Category-specific median compositions were used to 
construct synthetic waters representing domestic wastewater, industrial effluent, and landfill 
leachate. This evidence-based approach reduced the influence of extreme concentrations 
and site-specific or temporal variability, allowing the effects of three contrasting water 
compositions to be compared under controlled conditions across the soil-plant-drainage 
system. Unlike studies based on a single wastewater source, this design provides a 
reproducible framework for examining how literature-derived wastewater profiles interact 
with nutrient-poor sandy soils.
Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the short-term response of the soil-plant-drain-
age system to fertigation with synthetic solutions reproducing treated domestic wastewater 
(DW), industrial effluent (IE), and landfill leachate (L), including compositions exceed -
ing selected FAO irrigation-water quality guidelines (FAO 1992). Batch tests and a 40-day 
laboratory pot experiment with radish were conducted using two nutrient-poor sandy soils, 
a Siliceous Riparian Sandy Entisol (SRSE) and a Calcareous Sandy Calcisol (CSC). The 
study jointly evaluated nutrient and salt mobility, plant biomass, elemental accumulation in 
leaves and roots, post-experimental soil chemistry, and the temporal composition of drain -
age water under controlled laboratory conditions.
2 Materials and Methods
2.1 Soil and Fertigation Water Preparation
Two soils with different textures and chemical properties were selected to represent differ -
ent pedological conditions (see Table 1): a Siliceous Riparian Sandy Entisol (SRSE) and 
a Calcareous Sandy Calcisol (CSC). Each soil was oven-dried at 80 °C for 24 h, gently 
disaggregated, and sieved through a 2-mm sieve to remove coarse fragments and plant 
debris. The homogenized material was stored in sealed polyethylene containers until use. 
The synthetic fertigation waters were formulated from the database compiled by Osorio et 
al. (2025), which included 75 wastewater-fertigation studies. Reported ion concentrations 
were standardized and grouped by water source, including domestic wastewater (28 stud -
ies), industrial effluents (21) and leachates (8). The source datasets were heterogeneous: the 
industrial-effluent category included different origins and treatment levels, whereas leachate 
compositions showed high dispersion and lacked consistent treatment reporting. Category-

extreme observations. Indeed, when a dataset is skewed or contains extreme outliers, the 
median is preferable to the mean because it reliably represents the usual value without being 
influenced by extremely high or low numbers (Filzmoser et al. 2009). For example, the 
mean SO₄²⁻ and Na⁺ concentrations reported for leachates (658.0 and 573.3 mg L⁻¹, respec-
tively) were substantially higher than the corresponding medians (307.2 and 345.0 mg L⁻¹). 
The resulting profiles represent literature-derived central compositions, not typical Euro -
pean values or worst-case scenarios. Target concentrations were converted to molar units 
and reproduced using analytical-grade salts, as detailed in Table 1s. Deionized water was 
used as a rainwater proxy (B), and no real wastewater was used.
2.2 Pot Tests
Pot experiments were conducted in a controlled laboratory to evaluate the response of 
Raphanus sativus L . (radish) to fertigation with different water types. Polypropylene pots 
measuring 25 cm in height and 9.9 cm in internal diameter were used following Kang and 
Wan (2005), who recommended a minimum depth of 20 to 25 cm to provide sufficient space 
for root system development. The experiment followed a 2 × 4 factorial designs compris -
ing two soil types, SRSE and CSC, and four fertigation water treatments, B, DW, IE and 
L. Two independent pots were established for each soil-water combination, resulting in 16 
experimental units. Five seeds were initially sown in each pot to ensure successful plant 
establishment. After seedling emergence, the plants were thinned to one individual per pot. 
Therefore, each pot containing one plant was considered an independent experimental unit 
and biological replicate. Laboratory temperature was maintained at 20 °C throughout the 
experiment, while relative humidity was maintained at 60%. Fertigation was conducted for 
approximately 40 days under laboratory conditions. Irrigation requirements were estimated 
using the FAO CROPWAT model based on reference evapotranspiration and crop param -
eters, including the crop coefficient (Kc) and root depth (Allen et al. 1998). Irrigation was 
scheduled using a soil water balance approach to maintain soil moisture near field capac -
ity, with irrigation triggered at 60% of critical depletion and assuming a 70% application 
efficiency. Fertigation was applied in 13 events between days 1 and 34, at intervals of 2 to 4 
days depending on the crop developmental stage. The volume applied per event ranged from 
68.26 to 205.60 mL per pot, resulting in a cumulative irrigation volume of 1744.84 mL per 
pot. The complete irrigation schedule is provided in Table 2s of the SI.
After each fertigation cycle, drainage water was collected, filtered through 0.45 μm 
membranes, and analyzed for pH and EC using a multiparameter probe (HI98194, Hanna 
Instruments, USA). Samples were stored in HDPE vials at 4 °C until analysis of DOC, 
major ions, and TEs. At the end of the tests, soil profiles in each pot were carefully sepa -
rated into two depth intervals (0–10 and 10–20 cm) to characterize the vertical distribution 
of major ions and TEs (See section 3s in SI). The plants were harvested and separated into 
aboveground biomass, corresponding to the leaves, and belowground biomass. The entire 
belowground fraction was collected, weighed and processed as root biomass because the 
hypocotyl was not separated or quantified as an independent plant fraction. Fresh biomass 
was therefore determined separately for leaves and roots before further processing. Leaf 
material was cut with sterile scalpels, damaged tissue was removed, and the fresh biomass 
was recorded. Samples were placed in pre-weighed paper bags and then dried at 65 °C in a

The root systems were gently washed with running water to remove adhering soil par -

in pre-weighed paper bags, dried at 65 °C until a constant mass was reached, and reweighed. 
The dried material was ground in the same way.
2.3 Analytical Methods
DOC in water was analysed measuring the ultraviolet absorbance at 254 nm (Weishaar et 
al. 2003) using an UV/VIS ONDA TOUCH UV-21 spectrophotometer. Major ions were 
analysed with an Inuvion Thermo Fisher Scientific chromatography system equipped with 
an isocratic dual pump with an IonPac AS14A 4 × 250 mm column, the pre-column AG14A 
4 × 50 mm, and an ADRS 600 (4 mm) self-suppressor for anions and an IonPac CS12A 
4 × 250 mm column, the pre-column CS12A 4 × 50 mm thermostatted at 35 °C, and a CDRS 
600 (4 mm) self-suppressor for cations. The AS-DV Thermo Fisher Scientific autosampler 
was employed to run the analysis, while quality control (QC) samples were run every 20 
samples. TEs were analysed with an ICP-OES (PerkinElmer, USA) to quantify their con -
centration in water samples after acidification with ultrapure 1 M nitric acid and filtering on 
0.45 μm. Element concentrations were additionally determined by ICP-OES using a second 
subsample analysed with a hydride generation system to minimize spectral interferences. 
All the equipment was correctly acid washed, and method blanks were carried out. For 
all plant samples, dry matter content was determined gravimetrically prior to microwave-
assisted digestion (HNO₃:H₂O₂, 10:1) and subsequent determination of total element con -
centrations by ICP-OES (iCAP PRO XP Duo, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Analytical quality 
control included reagent blanks, duplicate samples and certified reference plant materials 
from the interlaboratory calibration programme (WEPAL-ISE).
2.4 Statistical Analysis
Statistical analyses were selected according to the structure of each dataset. For endpoint 
variables measured once per experimental unit, analysis of variance was used. Specifically, 
two-way ANOV A was performed to evaluate the main and interactive effects of soil type, 
SRSE and CSC, and fertigation water treatment, B, DW, IE and L, on plant biomass, leaf 
elemental composition and post-experimental soil chemistry. The pot was considered the 
experimental unit, with two independent pots per Soil × Water combination. Biomass data 
were log10-transformed before analysis to reduce right-skewness and stabilize variance 
across treatments. When significant differences were detected, Tukey’s HSD test was used 
for pairwise comparisons. Because separate ANOV As were conducted for multiple response 
variables, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the 
false discovery rate, separately for the Soil, Water and Soil × Water terms.
Root elemental composition was evaluated descriptively because only one analytical 
result was available for each Soil × Water combination, preventing a valid inferential analy-
sis. Drainage-water variables were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, with Soil,

Water, Time and their interactions included as fixed effects and pot identity included as a 
random effect to account for repeated measurements. The significance of the fixed effects, 
including the Soil × Water × Time interaction, was evaluated using Wald chi-square tests. 
P-values for the drainage-water models were adjusted across the 14 response variables using 
the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Because only two independent pots were available per 
Soil × Water combination, statistical inference was considered exploratory and restricted to 
the experimental conditions. This limited replication reduced the strength of the approach, 
particularly for detecting interactions. Repeated drainage-water measurements improved 
temporal resolution but did not increase biological replication; therefore, p-values were 
interpreted alongside effect direction, magnitude and consistency, using p < 0.05 as the 
threshold for statistical significance. All analyses were performed using Python 3.14.6, pan-
das 3.0.5, SciPy 1.18.0 and statsmodels 0.14.6.
3 Results and Discussion
3.1 Water Leaching Dynamics

nificant. The temporal trajectories of most drainage-water parameters varied according 
to the specific Soil × Water combination, supporting their interpretation at the treatment-
combination level rather than through the separate main effects of Soil and Water. Given 
the limited biological replication, these interactions should be interpreted as evidence of 
different temporal patterns within the present experiment rather than as precise estimates

NO₃⁻ concentrations closely followed the composition of the fertigation waters and 
the observed drainage patterns, suggesting that input concentration and water-driven 
transport were important under the experimental conditions. Across both soils, NO₃⁻ 
exhibited a near-conservative behaviour over most of the test period. In CSC pots under 
IE and L, NO₃⁻ rapidly exceeded the 50 mg L⁻¹ threshold. However, NO₃⁻ concentrations 
in the leachates declined markedly toward the end of the test, indicating that most of the 
readily mobile nitrate had likely been leached earlier in the experiment. This decline 
could also be associated with increasing plant N demand during the final growth phase 
(Solangi et al. 2024), although plant N uptake was not directly quantified. Furthermore, 
the lower concentrations observed in SRSE may reflect soil-specific differences in pore-
water residence time and microbial N transformations (Gu and Riley 2010). However, 
pore-water residence time, denitrification rates and microbial activity were not measured; 
therefore, the contribution of these processes cannot be determined. Nevertheless, these 
patterns are consistent with the high mobility of NO₃⁻ generally reported in coarse-
textured soils with low sorption capacity, but do not demonstrate a specific transport or 
microbial mechanism (Chen et al. 2024). Overall, NO₃⁻ leaching emerged as the dominant 
environmental risk, with clear implications for groundwater quality and eutrophication 
potential (Sanchezperez et al. 2003).
Rubio-Asensio and Intrigliolo ( 2024) similarly found lower NO₃⁻ concentrations in the 
root zone when fertigation frequency was increased, despite identical weekly water and 
fertilizer inputs, highlighting the importance of synchronizing water application with crop N 
demand to limit nitrate leaching. Conversely, NH₄⁺ exhibited a clear soil-dependent behav-
iour. In CSC, fertigation produced early NH₄⁺ peaks above the 0.5 mg L⁻¹ threshold. In con-
trast, NH₄⁺ peaks in SRSE were modest and displayed a periodic pattern. The higher NH₄⁺ 
concentrations in CSC leachates may reflect reduced retention under high ionic-strength 
conditions, as indicated by the EC trends in Figs. 2s and 3s of the SI. Competition with 
Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ for exchange sites can increase NH₄⁺ mobility (Jellali et al. 2010), while the 
low organic carbon content of SRSE and CSC may further limit sorptive and microbially 
mediated retention. This interpretation is consistent with Garbowski ( 2024), who observed 
greater nutrient retention and lower N losses after incorporating biologically active algal 
biomass into sandy soil. Nevertheless, microbial processes were not directly quantified in 
the present study. These processes should therefore be regarded as possible explanations 
rather than demonstrated causes of the observed soil-specific patterns.
The PO₄³⁻ behaviour was consistent with the batch tests (see Section 4s and Fig. 1s in the 
SI), showing a marked decrease relative to the fertigation inputs, which is consistent with 
retention by reactive soil surfaces. However, under irrigation with IE, apparent increases 
in PO₄³⁻ concentrations were observed during the later sampling dates. This trend could 
reflect a progressive reduction in retention efficiency under high P loading, potentially 
involving saturation of high-affinity sorption sites rather than complete removal from 
porewater (Van Der Salm et al. 2016). However, sorption-site occupancy and mineral 
precipitation were not measured, so the mechanism responsible for this pattern cannot be 
confirmed. Moreover, the non-significant Soil × Water × Time interaction indicates that

this temporal response was not sufficiently consistent across the experimental units. Na⁺ 
concentrations in the leachates were high and closely reflected the Na⁺ inputs, particularly 
in CSC under L fertigation. This conservative behavior highlights a potential long-term 
concern, as prolonged fertigation with Na-rich waters may increase Na ⁺ loading to 
groundwater and, if sustained over time, could pose a risk to soil structural stability (Gao 
et al. 2021). Cl⁻ showed conservative behavior throughout the test, with leachates closely 
reflecting fertigation inputs (Zhao et al. 2025). However, Cl⁻ concentrations increased 
gradually, which could be associated with concentration effects arising from plant water 
uptake and the observed decline in soil moisture during the later developmental stages 
(Alessandrino et al. 2024; Figs. 2s and 3s in the SI). Actual evapotranspiration and pore-
water residence time were not directly measured, so the contribution of evapoconcentration 
cannot be quantified. Hydraulic loading should be adjusted to both soil moisture conditions 
and the hydraulic capacity of the receiving soil. He et al. ( 2020) showed that regulating 
wastewater application according to soil-water conditions reduced the risk of hydraulic 
overloading, emphasizing the need to align fertigation rates with crop demand and soil 
drainage capacity. However, porosity, saturated hydraulic conductivity and soil drainage 
capacity were not measured in the present experiment. Consequently, the observed 
drainage patterns cannot be used to demonstrate hydraulic overloading or differences 
in hydraulic functioning between the two soils. In addition, the potential effects of Na⁺ 
cannot be assessed from salinity alone, as soil structural responses depend on the relative 
abundance of monovalent and divalent cations. Xie et al. ( 2024) found that decreasing the 
sodium adsorption ratio and increasing Ca²⁺ availability mitigated aggregate deterioration 
under brackish-water irrigation. A similar mechanism may underlie the structural risks 
associated with sustained Na⁺ loading in the studied soils. Nevertheless, this mechanism 
could not be verified because aggregate stability, porosity and hydraulic conductivity 
were not measured. Therefore, no treatment-induced change in soil structure or hydraulic 
functioning can be inferred from the present results.
The temporal variation of K⁺ concentrations in drainage water, including the late-stage 
increase observed in CSC, could have resulted from a combination of solution-phase trans-
port and short-term ionic interactions. Classical cation exchange may have been limited 
by the low CEC and coarse texture of both soils. The parallel trends of K⁺ and Mg²⁺ may 
thus reflect transient competitive interactions within the soil solution rather than exten -
sive exchange on mineral surfaces (Baki et al. 2025). In CSC, the late-stage increase in K⁺ 
concentrations could reflect changes in ionic equilibria or transport conditions rather than 
simple unidirectional sorption. Moreover, irrigation with high-ionic-strength waters rich in 
Na⁺ may enhance cation mobilization, as Na⁺ can compete for exchange sites and promote 
the displacement of previously retained cations (Hasan and Abu-Awwad 2025). However, 
exchange-site occupancy and cation-exchange dynamics were not measured, so these pro -
cesses remain possible explanations. The temporal profiles of DOC differed among fertiga-
tion treatments, with the highest mean concentration recorded under L-CSC on day 42 at 
12.94 mg L⁻¹, but no consistent difference was observed between the two soils. However, 
DOC was consistently lower than the corresponding inputs, which is consistent with DOC 
retention or transformation within the soil system, as already observed in the batch tests. 
Physicochemical processes such as cation bridging and adsorption onto reactive mineral 
surfaces could have contributed to DOC removal (Xu and Tsang 2024). However, microbial 
activity and DOC biodegradation were not quantified; therefore, the relative contributions

of physicochemical retention and biological transformation cannot be determined. More -
over, the similarity between DOC and PO₄³⁻ trends suggests coupled removal processes, 
potentially involving co-precipitation or joint retention mechanisms (Gerke 2010). This 
interpretation remains hypothetical because these processes were not measured directly. 
In longer-term systems, biological accumulation may modify the hydraulic and treatment 
performance of sandy media. Wu et al. ( 2021) showed that biomass accumulation in inter -
mittent sand filters contributed to contaminant removal while simultaneously modifying 
permeability through bioclogging. Although microbial biomass and hydraulic changes were 
not directly measured in the present short-term experiment, bioclogging should be con -
sidered a hypothesis for future longer-term investigation rather than an explanation of the 
drainage patterns observed here.
TE concentrations in leachates remained consistently low throughout the test (Figs. 1 and 
2). All measured concentrations of Fe, Cr, Cu and Zn were far below thresholds relevant to 
groundwater protection, remaining well under WHO health-based guideline values for total 
Cr (0.05 mg L⁻¹) and Cu (2.0 mg L⁻¹), and below aesthetic limits for Fe (0.3 mg L⁻¹) and 
Zn (3 mg L⁻¹) (WHO 2017). Even for Cu and Zn, which were directly supplied with the 
fertigation waters, leachate concentrations remained well within safe limits, while Fe and 
Cr variations were minor and linked to soil-derived fractions or isolated redox fluctuations. 
Although the Soil × Water × Time interactions were significant for all four TEs, this statisti-
cal significance reflected differences in their temporal trajectories rather than concentrations 
exceeding environmental thresholds. Recent field evidence also indicates that TE transfer 
under wastewater irrigation is strongly influenced by soil properties and plant-specific accu-
mulation, rather than by wastewater concentrations alone (Ibrahim and Hammad 2024). The 
low concentrations measured in drainage water, therefore, indicate limited short-term leach-
ing of the evaluated TEs under the tested conditions. This finding is consistent with previous 
studies reporting strong immobilization of Cu and Zn in wastewater-irrigated soils, where 
pH, carbonates and organic matter promote their retention in low-mobility forms (Kovač et 
al. 2022; Abd-Elwahed 2018). However, the present data do not allow the relative contribu-
tions of soil retention and plant uptake to be quantified. Under the low metal loads tested, 
TEs did not represent a significant short-term environmental concern. Instead, the main 
risks to groundwater quality were associated with salinity and nutrient loads, consistent with 
observations from similar systems (Qadir et al. 2006). Post-experimental soil chemistry was 
primarily influenced by fertigation water composition. After Benjamini-Hochberg adjust -
ment, a significant water effect was detected for K⁺ [F(3,8) = 8.34, adjusted p = 0.033], Na⁺ 
[F(3,8) = 18.78, adjusted p = 0.018] and Cl⁻ [F(3,8) = 8.93, adjusted p = 0.033]. No significant 
effects were detected for NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻, PO₄³⁻, Fe, Cr or Cu, and the Soil × Water interaction 
was not significant for any of the evaluated variables. Zn was excluded from inferential 
analysis because all post-experimental soil concentrations were below the analytical quanti-
fication limit and were therefore recorded as zero, resulting in no variability among samples. 
Complete ANOV A results are provided in Table 4s of the SI. The drainage-water and post-
experimental soil results indicate different pathways for the applied solutes. Highly mobile 
constituents were mainly reflected in their temporal losses through drainage, while K⁺, Na⁺ 
and Cl⁻ also showed a detectable treatment effect in the soil at the end of the experiment. 
Therefore, the absence of a significant final soil effect for the other analytes does not nec -
essarily indicate limited transport, as part of the applied load may have already been lost

3.2.1 Biomass Response
Radish biomass differed markedly among fertigation waters and between the two soils 
(Table 3). The nutrient-containing waters promoted greater leaf and root development 
than the blank treatment, although the magnitude of the response varied among biomass 
components.
Two-way ANOV A showed significant effects of Soil [F(1,8) = 6.05–73.74, adjusted 
p ≤ 0.039] and Water [F(3,8) = 11.66–54.63, adjusted p ≤ 0.003] on all biomass variables. In 
contrast, the Soil × Water interaction was not statistically significant [F(3,8) = 0.47–2.20, 
adjusted p = 0.453–0.708]. Interaction effects were specific to each response variable and 
statistical model. Therefore, the non-significant Soil × Water interaction for biomass does 
not conflict with the significant Soil × Water interactions detected for some leaf elemental 
concentrations or with the significant Soil × Water × Time interactions observed for several 
drainage variables. These results indicate that treatment-dependent responses were detected 
for certain chemical variables, but not for biomass under the present experimental design. 
Given the limited biological replication, this result indicates only that no Soil × Water inter-
action was detected under the present experimental design and should not be interpreted as 
evidence that biomass responses to fertigation water were equivalent between the two soils.

SRSE, particularly for healthy leaf fresh mass, clean root fresh mass and total dry biomass.

was detected under the present design. Given the limited biological replication, this result

Accordingly, the foliar values reported here describe treatment-induced changes in elemen-

which would require integrating concentration with the biomass of each plant fraction.
Foliar Na accumulation increased markedly under L and, to a lesser extent, under IE and 
DW, reaching 25.7 g kg⁻¹ dry matter under L-CSC, equivalent to approximately 2.6% of dry 
matter (Table 4). This pattern was consistent with the elevated Na concentrations measured 
in drainage water (Figs. 1 and 2), reflecting substantial transfer of Na from the fertigation 
solutions to aboveground tissues. Accordingly, crop assessment under wastewater fertiga -
tion should not be restricted to potentially toxic elements, since substantial Na accumula -
tion may also alter the chemical and organoleptic quality of edible tissues. Saline irrigation 
has been shown to modify soluble solids, sugars, proteins and vitamin C concentrations 
in radish (Zhu et al. 2024), while NaCl accumulation in edible leaves has been associated 
with a perceptible salty taste in sensory evaluations (Amoruso et al. 2022). However, sen-
sory attributes were not evaluated in the present study, and the marketable hypocotyl was 
not analysed separately. Therefore, the observed foliar Na enrichment identifies a potential 
organoleptic and crop-quality concern rather than direct evidence of altered taste, consumer 
acceptability or commercial quality. Moreover, although plants retained part of the applied 
Na, repeated application of Na-rich waters may promote salinity- and sodicity-related 
deterioration of soil structure and hydraulic functioning, particularly in soils with limited 
buffering capacity (Kramer et al. 2022; Adeyemo et al. 2022). This represents a potential 
longer-term risk identified from previous studies and was not directly evaluated in the pres-
ent experiment because aggregate stability, porosity and saturated hydraulic conductivity 
were not measured. Future studies should therefore combine tissue-specific Na measure -
ments, sensory evaluations and direct monitoring of soil physical and hydraulic properties.
Most micronutrients remained within the ranges generally reported for vegetable 
tissues, although Zn enrichment was observed under IE-CSC without evidence of 
hyperaccumulation (Kabata-Pendias 2010). The Soil × Water interactions identified for 
several trace elements reflected differences in accumulation patterns rather than uniformly 
high concentrations. Cd and Pb were below or close to their analytical quantification limits 
in most treatments, while Ni remained at relatively low concentrations despite significant 
Soil and Water main effects. Therefore, the statistical responses of these elements did 
not correspond to substantial accumulation in leaf tissues. The pronounced interaction 
observed for Cu was driven principally by the high concentration measured in B-CSC 
and the lower concentrations recorded under treatments that produced greater biomass. 
Because this pattern did not correspond directly to the Cu supplied by the fertigation 
waters, soil-derived Cu and biomass dilution probably contributed more strongly than 
irrigation inputs alone. Zn also showed a soil-dependent response, with marked enrichment 
under IE-CSC but no comparable increase in SRSE. In contrast, Fe varied numerically 
among treatments but was not significantly affected by either experimental factor.

3.3 Limitations and Future Research
The use of standardized synthetic solutions enabled direct comparison of fertigation-water 
composition and soil effects, but interpretation is limited by the short experiment, two nutri-
ent-poor sandy soils, one crop species, and low replication (two pots per Soil × Water combi-
nation). Consequently, statistical strength was limited, especially for interaction effects, and 
significant or non-significant results should be considered preliminary evidence rather than 
definitive or broadly generalizable conclusions. Repeated drainage measurements improved 
temporal characterization but did not increase biological replication. The synthetic solutions 
also did not reproduce the variability, microbiological components, or organic complex -
ity of real wastewater. Root elemental composition was based on one analytical result per 
treatment combination and was interpreted descriptively. Further experiments with greater 
biological replication are needed to confirm these patterns and estimate treatment effects 
and Soil × Water interactions more precisely.
Bulk density was measured before the experiment, whereas porosity and saturated 
hydraulic conductivity were not determined. Consequently, possible changes in soil struc -
ture and hydraulic behaviour could not be directly assessed. Microbial biomass, microbial 
activity, N-transformation rates and pore-water residence time were also not quantified. 
Accordingly, the hydraulic, sorptive and microbial processes proposed in the discussion 
should be regarded as hypotheses consistent with the observed patterns and previous lit -
erature rather than as mechanisms demonstrated by the experiment of this study. Drainage 
volumes were also not recorded for every pot and sampling event, preventing the calculation 
of cumulative solute loads and complete nutrient mass balances. The leaching assessment 
was therefore based on concentration patterns and temporal trends. Further research should 
examine these responses using real wastewater, longer cultivation periods, greater bio -
logical replication and field-scale experiments involving a wider range of soils and crops. 
Future experimental designs should include systematic measurement of drainage volumes, 
repeated assessment of soil physical and hydraulic properties, and replicated elemental anal-
yses of roots and edible organs. For Na-rich waters, tissue-specific Na distribution should be 
evaluated together with sensory and market-quality attributes to determine whether tissue 
accumulation affects the quality and acceptability of the harvested product. These measure-
ments would allow short-term concentration responses to be distinguished from cumulative 
agronomic and environmental effects.

4 Conclusions
The use of category-specific median compositions synthesized from 75 wastewater-ferti -
gation studies enabled the preparation of chemically defined and reproducible synthetic 
waters. These solutions captured the central nutrient and salinity characteristics reported for 
domestic wastewater, industrial effluent and landfill leachate while reducing the influence 
of extreme observations and avoiding dependence on a single site-specific effluent. The pro-
files therefore represent standardized, literature-derived compositions rather than universal 
wastewater characteristics, typical European values or conservative worst-case scenarios. 
Their use provided a consistent experimental basis for comparing water-composition effects 
across the soil-plant-drainage system in two nutrient-poor sandy soils with extremely low 
buffering capacity.
The ionic composition of the fertigation waters was rapidly transmitted to the drainage, 
particularly in SRSE. Nitrate and, to a lesser extent, ammonium and phosphate frequently 
exceeded groundwater quality thresholds, especially under IE and L. Sodium, chloride and 
potassium also showed high mobility, while trace-element concentrations in drainage water 
remained comparatively low. Significant Soil × Water × Time interactions for most drainage 
variables confirmed that solute transport depended on the specific treatment combination 
and sampling time. Post-experimental soil chemistry retained significant Water effects for 
K+, Na+ and Cl−, indicating that these elements were partly retained in the soil while also 
being transported through drainage.
Agronomically, nutrient-rich waters improved radish growth relative to the control, 
resulting in greater aboveground and belowground biomass. Soil and Water significantly 
affected all biomass variables, whereas their interaction was not significant. The highest 
total dry biomass was recorded under IE-SRSE. Foliar K, Ca and Mg generally remained 
within the sufficiency ranges reported for radish, while P remained near the lower suffi -
ciency range. Root elemental composition showed numerical increases in several macro -
nutrients under IE and L, confirming nutrient delivery to the soil-root interface, although 
the absence of biological replication prevented statistical confirmation of greater nutrient 
uptake. Na accumulation was pronounced in leaves and numerically high in roots under L 
and IE, particularly in CSC. Foliar Na reached 25.7 g kg⁻¹ dry matter under L-CSC, identi-
fying a potential crop-quality concern.
These results indicate potential agronomic and environmental risks when nutrient and 
salt inputs exceed crop demand and soil retention capacity. Although the synthetic waters 
acted as effective short-term nutrient sources, the observed biomass gains were accompa -
nied by substantial nutrient and salt leaching, particularly under IE and L. Longer-term 
experiments using real wastewater, systematic drainage-volume measurements, replicated 
root analyses and field validation are therefore required before wastewater fertigation can 
be recommended for nutrient-poor sandy soils.
