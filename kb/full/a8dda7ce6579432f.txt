1 Introduction
This article centers on how a set of informational obligations arising under EU 
ambient air quality protection law are implemented, putatively, via visual tools at 
the municipal level in one of Poland’s regions, namely Lesser Poland (Małopolska). 
Poland, a Member State of the EU since 2004, is a unitary state characterized by a 
well-developed three-tier self-government system. The country is divided into six-
teen regions (precisely referred to as voivodeships), including Lesser Poland, which 
are further subdivided into counties and, at the lowest level, municipalities–public 
authorities with which residents interact most frequently.
Why focus on Lesser Poland? Lesser Poland constitutes a particularly relevant case 
due to its long-standing and severe air pollution challenges, especially with regard 
to ambient air pollution in the period following Poland’s political and economic 
transformation after 1989. These challenges have been driven by a combination of 
unfavorable geographical conditions, deeply rooted household heating practices, and, 
above all, the dominant role of coal in the energy sector, coupled with the widespread 
use of outdated and inefficient heating installations [15].
At the same time, Lesser Poland has emerged as a pioneer of regulatory innovation 
at the regional level. The regional authority—the Lesser Poland Regional Assembly 
(Sejmik Województwa Małopolskiego), acting as a regional legislative body—adopted 
two landmark anti-smog resolutions that constitute binding local law [9]. The first 
resolution, applicable exclusively to the city of Kraków, the capital of the region, was

as extensive informational and educational activities, reflecting a broader regulatory 
strategy that combined legally binding measures with communicative and behavioral 
instruments aimed at fostering social acceptance and ensuring effective compliance. 
These regulatory interventions, together with complementary instruments—includ-
ing, for example, EU-funded initiatives such as projects implemented under the LIFE 
Program, as well as Polish national programs offering financial support for the ther-
mal modernization of households—have contributed to a measurable improvement 
in air quality (e.g [11, 13]). Nevertheless, despite these advances, pollution levels in 
the region remain among the highest in the European Union.
Against this backdrop, a distinctive air quality-related infosphere has emerged in 
Lesser Poland in recent years, characterized also by the increasing presence of visual 
elements in the public sphere, such as boards displaying air quality information 
through color-coded indices and emoticons. This development has been driven by 
the need to fulfil obligations originating at the European Union level, subsequently 
specified in national legislation and implemented at the regional level. Within this 
evolving infosphere, municipal websites constitute an important component, as resi-
dents regularly access them in search of information necessary for everyday activi-
ties. Alongside the two anti-smog resolutions, the Air Protection Program (Program 
Ochrony Powietrza) constitutes a key regional legal instrument in this field. First 
adopted in 2008 and currently in force in its 2023 version, the Program (which is 
required under EU law whenever air quality standards are exceeded) imposes, inter 
alia, an obligation on municipalities to publish specified air quality information on 
their official websites. Accordingly, the present article deliberately limits its scope to 
municipal websites, excluding both other communication channels and other tiers of 
territorial self-government.
The article investigates whether, and in what ways, selected obligations concern-
ing the publication of information on ambient air quality on official municipal web-
sites in the Lesser Poland region are fulfilled through the use of visual tools. It further 
examines, where such visuals are present, whether they convey information in a 
clear, accessible, and internally consistent manner.
More generally, the use of visual communication is often justified on the grounds 
that it enables faster information processing, imposes lower cognitive demands on 
recipients, reaches broader audiences more effectively, and aligns more closely with 
contemporary social and communicative practices (e.g [19, 27]). As information 
relating to air quality and associated health warnings is, in many respects, particularly 
well-suited to visual modes of communication, including diagrams, color-coding 
systems, and charts, the use of visual materials has long been the subject of schol-
arly inquiry within the environmental sciences. In particular, diverse modes of data 
visualization—especially those relating to air pollution–have received sustained aca-

municipal authorities may choose either to invest additional effort in presenting data 
in a clear and user-friendly manner (also by visual tools) or to limit their actions to 
the formal fulfillment of legal requirements.
The study pursues three primary objectives.
First, the article seeks to identify whether–and, if so, which–visual elements related 
to air quality communication are present on municipal websites, focusing exclu-
sively on information required under binding legal regulations.
Second, it examines the placement and contextualization of these visual elements 
within the structure of municipal websites, as well as their general characteristics in 
light of applicable legal requirements.
Third, the article provides an assessment of these visual elements by identifying 
potential shortcomings, analyzing their internal coherence, and evaluating the extent 
to which they effectively fulfil the relevant legal obligations.
More broadly, the aim is to capture how a specific fragment of EU law is concret-
ized at the local level through visual tools, and how it operates in practice, within the 
defined scope, at the municipal level. In this sense, the analysis also contributes to 
the wider discussion on the conditions necessary for effective visual communication 
in the legal field.
Visual elements of communication (“visuals”) are understood here in a broad 
sense, as elements distinct from written text. Although written communication also 
relies on visual perception, in non-pictographic writing systems meanings tend to be 
more fixed and tightly controlled, and their interpretation typically requires sustained 
formal education. By contrast, visual communication conveys meaning through dif-
ferent mechanisms and produces a distinct mode of interpretation - often faster, more 
holistic, and less cognitively demanding. Typical elements of visual communication 
include images, color, tables, diagrams, charts, emoticons, and maps. The public 
communication of public authorities, both historically and in contemporary contexts, 
is typically characterized by the integration of textual and non-textual elements, with 
textual modes of communication generally retaining primacy.
Methodologically, the study combines quantitative and qualitative content analy-
sis of all municipal websites in the Lesser Poland region. The quantitative component 
focuses on the overall characteristics of visual air quality-related messages, analyzing 
all municipal websites using predefined analytical categories. The qualitative compo-
nent, in turn, is based on a close and contextual reading of selected webpages, aimed 
at identifying the communicative context of visual messaging, as well as potential 
problems in its presentation and interpretation. Prior to the empirical analysis, the

focusing on the types, placement, and potential shortcomings of visual elements used 
in air quality communication, as well as the extent to which legal requirements are 
fulfilled. The article concludes with a summary of the findings.
2 Two Perspectives Integrated and Developed in the Present 
Research
This research focus emerges at the intersection of two evolving scholarly 
perspectives.
The first is the body of scholarship commonly described as law and visuality or 
visual jurisprudence [22], or, in an alternative conceptualization, visual law [4]. 
Within this framework, scholarly inquiry seeks to move beyond the traditional 
dominance of verbal and textual forms of legal communication and to recognize 
images and other visual elements as powerful and distinct modes of legal commu-
nication- or even legal argumentation–operating according to logics different from 
those of written text. This line of research addresses, inter alia, modes of visual 
communication in law and about law; the relationships between visual and textual 
forms in legal contexts; classifications and typologies of visual messages relevant 
to law; assessments of the effectiveness of such messages in fulfilling the functions 
of law and shaping its social operation; as well as the inherent limitations of visual 
forms of legal communication.
From the perspective of visual law, it is particularly important—when examining 
developments in air pollution communication in Lesser Poland–to recognize that cer-
tain informational obligations imposed on public authorities under EU law are, and 
may be, fulfilled through visual tools that go beyond traditional text-based communi-
cation. The latter typically consists of purely textual announcements concerning, for 
example, exceedances of air quality standards, including numerical data and informa-
tion about the affected areas. By contrast, visual tools encompass maps, infographics, 
graphs, emoticons, and other graphical means. Moreover, air quality indices—widely 
used worldwide and also required under EU law–are typically presented in graphic 
form, most often through color gradients designed to convey information rapidly and 
intuitively, in accordance with established visual conventions. A distinct category is 
formed by visual messages–broadly understood as infographics–developed by public 
authorities to inform the public about air pollution-related issues, such as regula-

tory measures adopted to protect air quality, as well as relevant prohibitions and 
standards. In all these cases, it is essential that such visual elements are, or may be, 
created and disseminated by public authorities–here, those operating in the Lesser 
Poland region–in the performance of legally defined tasks or obligations. Visual 
communication may, in some cases, constitute the primary form of a legal message, 
directly fulfilling a specific duty (for example, where the obligation to inform about 
air quality is implemented through a map). In other cases, visual communication 
functions as a supplementary element, supporting primary textual communication 
(for instance, when provisions of local legal acts concerning bans on specific fuels or 
combustion practices are presented by visual tools).
The second research strand, which remains less extensively developed, originates 
in studies of EU law focusing on the conditions of multilevel governance and on 
processes of EU law implementation. Its objective is to capture EU law in action–not 
only how it is implemented through formal legal instruments, but also how it operates 
at the local level (see [24, 25]). This approach challenges a long-standing compli-
ance-oriented perspective in European legal studies, which has traditionally centered 
on whether national or subnational measures formally conform to EU requirements. 
In particular, scholarly attention has often focused on transposition, understood pri-
marily as the translation of directives’ objectives into national legal measures (see 
[26]). By contrast, the approach adopted here is oriented towards analyzing the rela-
tionships between different levels of governance and examining how EU law is “real-
ized” or “materialized” in specific policy fields. To date, EU air pollution measures 
have been only partially addressed within the multilevel governance framework [18]. 
Within the narrower scope of this study, particular attention is paid to how the provi-
sions of EU directives concerning informational obligations are translated into con-
crete bundles of obligations assigned to various actors within a single region (Lesser 
Poland), and subsequently into specific actions undertaken at the municipal level—
especially the preparation and publication of materials on municipal websites. Adopt-
ing such a perspective makes it possible to capture the differentiation and variability 
that emerge at the local level in the process of implementing EU law.
3 Legal Context
The obligation to publish certain information on municipal websites, which lies at 
the core of this article, must be viewed against a broader legal background. For many 
years, regional and national actions in the field of ambient air quality management 
in the European Union were anchored primarily in Directive 2008/50/EC on ambi-
ent air quality and cleaner air for Europe. Together with related EU instruments, 
this directive constituted the central regulatory framework guiding Member States’ 
responsibilities in air quality governance. Recently replaced by the recast Directive 
(EU) 2024/2881, it established the conceptual and operational foundations of EU air 
quality regulation. Although the new 2024 directive—scheduled to be transposed 
into national legal orders by 11 December 2026—refines and strengthens several 
aspects of the framework, particularly with regard to enforcement mechanisms, the 
underlying regulatory philosophy remains largely unchanged. For the purposes of

this analysis, the informational obligations embedded in both directives are of par-
ticular importance.
3.1 Informational Requirements Under Directive 2008/50/EC and Evolving 
Requirements Under Directive (EU) 2024/2881
The preamble of the 2008 Directive emphasized continuous public communication 
regarding air quality, including exceedances of alert thresholds and “the risks of 
exposure”. It explicitly required that “up-to-date information on concentrations of all 
regulated pollutants in ambient air should be readily available to the public”, high-
lighting accessibility not only in terms of source availability but also in terms of clar-
ity, legibility, and ease of interpretation. Article 1(4) further identified the provision 
of such information to the public as one of the Directive’s central objectives. Article 
26 elaborated these obligations, requiring Member States to ensure that the public is 
“informed, adequately and in good time” about (1) ambient air quality; (2) air qual-
ity plans and programs; (3) postponements or exemptions granted to Member States. 
Crucially, the Directive required that information be disseminated free of charge and 
via “easily accessible media, including the Internet or any other appropriate means of 
telecommunication”. This left open the choice of communication channels, allowing 
for flexibility and technological adaptation.
Annex XVI provided detailed specifications, underlining the expectation that 
information be “routinely made available to the public”, thereby introducing the 
principle of continuity of communication. It also set out substantive requirements, 
such as reporting exceedances of limit values, target values, alert thresholds, infor-
mation thresholds, and long-term objectives. Additionally, it mandated that pollutant 
concentration data–at least for sulphur dioxide, nitrogen dioxide, particulate matter 
(PM₁₀), ozone, and carbon monoxide–be updated daily, and, where feasible, hourly. 
The Annex also imposed more informational duties, including explanations of likely 
health impacts, identification of populations at risk, recommended protective behav-
iors, descriptions of expected symptoms, and guidance on where additional informa-
tion may be obtained. Member States were further required to provide information 
on preventive actions aimed at reducing pollution or exposure, including descriptions 
of main source sectors and recommended mitigation measures. While the Directive 
prescribed what information had to be made public, it remained ambiguous on how 
this should be organized. It did not specify whether a single unified system should 
exist or whether multiple systems operating at different administrative levels would 
suffice. This regulatory ambiguity created space for varied local practices–includ-
ing the adoption of visual communication tools such as colors, maps, diagrams, and 
infographics–particularly in the digital environment, which the Directive implicitly 
privileged as the most effective dissemination channel.
The 2024 Directive retains these core informational obligations but significantly 
strengthens and expands them. As in its predecessor, the preamble highlights the 
importance of public information, but it introduces a new and prominent emphasis 
on “sensitive population and vulnerable groups” (Recital 20), reflecting contempo-
rary public-health and environmental-justice concerns. Recital 44 underscores that 
information must be made available through digital and, where relevant, non-digital

communication channels, thereby formally mandating digital dissemination as a 
core element of compliance. Article 2(4) introduces another important innovation: a 
requirement that air quality information be comparable across the Union. This shift 
towards standardization aligns with broader EU efforts to harmonize environmental 
monitoring systems and reduce disparities arising from uneven data collection or 
communication practices.
Article 15 significantly accelerates the temporal expectations for communica-
tion: in cases of exceedances of alert or information thresholds, the public must be 
informed “within the shortest possible timeframe and, as far as possible, within a few 
hours”, using multiple media and communication channels to ensure broad accessi-
bility. Next, Article 22 expands the scope of required public information, mandating 
publication of data on air quality status, the locations of sampling points, postpone-
ment decisions, air quality plans and roadmaps, short-term action plans, and summary 
assessments of exceedances. Importantly, the Directive now requires Member States 
to “establish and make available through a public source, in an easily understand-
able manner, an air quality index”. This reflects the growing centrality of graphical 
and color-based indices as tools of intuitive public communication. It also obliges 
them to disseminate information on symptoms associated with pollution peaks and 
recommendations for exposure-reduction behaviors, and encourages displaying such 
information in locations frequented by vulnerable populations, including healthcare 
facilities.
A major conceptual innovation is introduced in Article 22(5), which requires that 
information be provided “in a coherent and easily understandable manner”. This 
marks a shift from merely requiring the availability of data to mandating the intelligi-
bility and communicative coherence of the entire system. It signals an EU-level rec-
ognition of risks related to inconsistent, misleading, or overly technical presentation 
of air quality information. Annex X further specifies the necessary components of 
public information systems: hourly measurements, comparisons to WHO guideline 
values where possible, detailed descriptions of exceedances, health-impact explana-
tions, recommended precautions, and information on relevant preventive actions. The 
Annex thus provides not only substantive requirements but also a pathway toward 
uniformity and clarity in visual and textual communication - key elements of the 
emerging visual infosphere.
3.2 Implementation of EU-Level Informational Obligations in the Polish Legal 
System
At the national level, the informational duties required under EU air quality law are 
implemented primarily through the Environmental Protection Law (Prawo ochrony 
środowiska) of 27 April 2001. The Act provides a domestic legal architecture that 
translates EU-level obligations—originally those arising under Directive 2008/50/
EC and now under Directive (EU) 2024/2881, probably with further updates–into 
specific institutional responsibilities and communicative requirements.
With regard to informing the public about the current state of ambient air quality, 
Article 92b(1) of Environmental Protection Law establishes a centralized informa-
tional obligation at the national level. This provision requires the Chief Inspector

information on air quality publicly available on an official website, including, inter

to information on ambient air by creating a single, official point of reference (see

information should be presented, in what format, or with what degree of detail or 
intelligibility for non-professional audiences. Consequently, the provision primar-
ily fulfils the function of formal accessibility of information, while to a much lesser 
extent addressing its effective communicability. It is only at the level of local practice, 
closer to residents, that information on air quality is multiplied, processed, and visu-
alized by municipalities, including on municipal websites, as will be shown below–
often in the absence of binding guidelines concerning communication standards.
In parallel, the Environmental Protection Law designates the Regional Crisis 
Management Centers (Wojewódzkie Centra Zarządzania Kryzysowego) as the prin-
cipal bodies responsible for disseminating certain information to both the public and 
relevant institutions. Pursuant to Article 93(1), these centers are required to “imme-
diately notify the public and the entities referred to in Article 92(2)(1), in a manner 
customarily used in the given area, of the risk of exceeding or the actual exceedance 
of alarm, information, permissible, or target levels of pollutants in ambient air”. The 
statute further specifies in detail the mandatory content of such notifications. These 
statutory requirements closely mirror the detailed informational standards set out in 
Annex XVI to Directive 2008/50/EC and Annex X to Directive 2024/2881, thereby 
demonstrating a clear continuity between EU-level norms and their implementation 
in national law. However, the significance of this framework ultimately depends on 
how the role performed by the Regional Crisis Management Centers is echoed and 
reinforced at the local–mainly municipal-level.
3.3 Regional Implementation: Air Protection Programs
While the Environmental Protection Law ensures the formal realization of certain 
informational duties, the more concrete and tangible operationalization of these obli-
gations from the residents’ perspective takes place primarily at the regional level. 
This occurs through Air Protection Programs (see [1]), which are legally binding 
instruments adopted for each region pursuant to a statutory mandate.
The current Air Protection Program for the Lesser Poland region—the 2023 update 
corresponding to the period analyzed in this study–illustrates how EU air quality 
obligations have been integrated into regional governance structures. Importantly, 
Air Protection Program for the Lesser Poland imposes direct and legally binding 
duties on municipalities, including those related with regional-based anti-smog reso-
lutions. Municipalities are required to conduct at least three informational campaigns 
per year concerning the requirements arising from anti-smog resolutions and the 
availability of financial support mechanisms for replacing obsolete heating systems. 
In addition, each municipality must carry out at least one informational-educational

targeted communication, delivering relevant information at least twice per year to 
every address where a solid-fuel installation is operated.

air quality communication is the Air Protection Program’s explicit requirement con-

mandates that, on the official municipal website, in a clearly visible location on the 
homepage, the following information must be provided: current air quality informa-

Crucially, neither the Air Protection Program nor higher-ranking legal acts specify 
the form, scope, or level of detail of the required air quality information. It remains 
unclear, for example, whether municipalities are expected to present an aggregated 
air quality index or to provide concentrations of individual pollutants, and, if the lat-
ter, which pollutants should be included. The legal framework likewise fails to clar-
ify whether information should be communicated through maps, numerical tables, 
iconographic indicators, or other visual elements. Equally ambiguous is the source of 
data that municipalities are expected to rely upon. Potential sources include official 
state monitoring stations operated by the Chief Inspector of Environmental Protec-
tion—which are spatially limited and do not cover all municipalities—as well as 
sensors acquired under regional and national programs and installations operated by 
commercial providers and financed directly from municipal budgets. The absence 
of legal guidance in this respect may result in substantial heterogeneity of practices 
across municipalities.
Despite the absence of formal standardization, certain implicit obligations can 
nevertheless be derived. First, information must be immediately visible, without 
requiring multi-step navigation. The requirement that information be placed “in a 
clearly visible location on the homepage” suggests that air quality data should be 
embedded directly on the main page, rather than merely accessible via a hyperlink 
to a subpage. In case of the obligation to publish a link to Ekointerwencja, the Air 
Protection Program does not specify its exact placement, size, or visual prominence, 
nor does it clarify whether both mandatory informational elements should be pre-
sented together or separately. Second, although municipalities retain discretion as

4 Analysis of the Municipalities’ Websites
There are 182 municipalities in the Lesser Poland region, including 14 urban munici-
palities, 50 urban-rural municipalities, and 118 rural municipalities. In addition, 
three cities with county (powiat) status—which combine the competences of both 
municipalities and counties—are included in the research sample and, for the sake 
of consistency, are hereinafter also referred to as municipalities. Consequently, the 
analysis covers a total of 179 local government units, as six websites could not be 
identified and therefore were not analyzed. These entities differ significantly in terms 
of territorial size, population, and fiscal capacity, as well as with regard to air quality 
conditions and the scope and intensity of measures undertaken by municipal authori-
ties in the field of air quality protection. This differentiation is shaped, inter alia, by 
local political configurations, including political divisions and affiliations that influ-
ence the composition of municipal authorities and, in turn, the level of determination 
in pursuit the anti-smog policies. Structural factors also play a key role, including 
the availability of infrastructure (e.g., access to gas networks) as well as patterns of 
behavior and habits among residents.
The study examines the official websites of 179 municipalities (excluding social 
media profiles), as accessible on 4 April 2026. The research process began with an 
exploratory review of the websites, followed by the development of analytical cat-
egories (e.g., the presence of obligatory elements on websites, the number of clicks 
required to access air quality information, the sources of data used, the scope of infor-
mation presented, and the types of visuals employed), which were then applied in a 
quantitative content analysis of the websites. This was later supplemented by qualita-
tive analysis aimed at capturing the broader contextual determinants of the observed 
communication practices related to informational obligations.

 Municipalities consequently rely predominantly on tools and materials prepared 
by the regional self-government through the LIFE project. While this reliance 
generates a certain degree of uniformity in the resources used, the manner in 
which these materials are integrated into municipal websites often fails to ensure 
overall visual or communicative coherence. This is so because municipalities 
decide which information is displayed within the provided widgets, leading to 
highly divergent implementations and inconsistent communicative outcomes. 
Moreover, alongside the visual materials made available through the LIFE proj-
ect, municipalities sometimes incorporate additional ready-made components 
(widgets) presenting air quality data sourced from other providers (e.g. Airly). 
This layering of heterogeneous visual and informational elements further con-
tributes to the fragmented and cacophonous character of this visual environmen-
tal communication.

4.3 Visual Elements Used
As already indicated, municipalities predominantly rely on ready-made systems for 
presenting information on air quality. Municipal websites generally do not display 
1 3

data directly sourced from the national monitoring stations operated by the Chief 
Inspector for Environmental Protection, a situation partly attributable to the limited 
number and spatial distribution of these stations. Instead, municipalities draw on sev-
eral alternative data sources. Municipalities make use of data generated by sensors 
purchased and managed by the Lesser Poland regional government–most of them 
financed under the LIFE program—and installed within municipal territories. The 
regional authorities provide municipalities with several standardized banner formats 
that display this air quality data. Moreover, many municipalities rely on data from 
monitoring stations supplied by commercial providers, among which Airly clearly 
predominates; this data is often accompanied by links to Airly maps (as well as, in 
some cases, to other commercial systems as Syngeos). These providers also make 
banners and widgets that can by to some extent customized by the municipalities.
In principle, a higher number of sensors and more diverse data sources could 
provide more comprehensive and context sensitive information, better reflecting the 
geographical distribution of pollution levels within a given municipality. However, 
the simultaneous use of displays and banners associated with different measurement 
and data-presentation systems poses significant challenges. Visual representations 
vary considerably across data providers, particularly with regard to color schemes, 
scales, and predefined air quality indices, which may differ in their meaning and 
interpretation.
Crucially, as noted, the visual elements displayed on municipal websites are 
generally not designed or customized by the municipalities themselves, with only 
marginal exceptions. Instead, they result from decisions to adopt existing banners, 
widgets, or maps developed by external providers. As a consequence, both the form 
and the content of visual communication concerning air quality are largely shaped 
by the technical and visual standards imposed by these providers, rather than by 
locally defined communication strategies tailored to municipal needs or audiences. 
However, local authorities can adjust the provided tools to a greater or lesser extent, 
either to strengthen their effectiveness and enhance their impact on reducing ambient 
air pollution, or merely to meet legal requirements without a deeper commitment to 
combating smog.
The elements in the list of observed visuals on municipal websites discussed below 
should not be treated as discrete, as they are typically integrated within a single tool 
or interface, or users are redirected from one element to another.
Colors
Color constitutes a key instrument of visual communication in the analyzed field. 
Across all examined systems, color gradients are used to convey information about 
air quality, typically ranging from light green to dark brown or even purple. This 
general scheme, with some differences, is applied consistently regardless of whether 
the system is provided by the Lesser Poland regional authorities, by commercial 
providers such as Airly, or by other solutions such as the so-called “bar indicators”. 
Similar approaches can also be observed in air quality indices and visualizations 
developed in other entities or countries, albeit with certain variations [20].
Within this color logic, green intuitively signals good air quality, while darker 
colors communicate a deterioration of conditions. The use of color is deliberately 
designed to render complex pollutant data quickly and intuitively understandable.

4.4 Main Issues
The practice outlined above of communicating legally required information through 
visual tools gives rise to a number of problems (these issues go beyond the previously 
discussed strategies of minimalism and the avoidance of developing bespoke visual 
solutions).
The main problem—apart from the occasional, though relatively rare, omission of 
mandatory information (see Table 1)—stems from the absence of any standardiza-
tion regarding both the form and content of air pollution information presented on 
municipal websites. There exists a plethora of differences and locally specific prac-
tices, often shaped at the county level, concerning the content and way of presenta-
tion of such information. Even a minimal level of standardization would significantly 
enhance the uniformity, usability, and user-friendliness of air pollution data. Such 
standards should ensure that information is easy to locate, comprehend, and interpret. 
Equally crucial is the provision of guidance and explanatory elements clarifying what 
is being displayed and how it should be understood. The educational function of 
air quality communication calls for a more contextualized presentation of otherwise 
standardized data, enabling users to grasp both its meaning and implications. While 
there is considerable flexibility regarding the sources of air quality data, clear stan-
dards are needed to determine which information should be displayed directly in the 
main homepage widget and which may be placed on secondary pages. Moreover, the 
widespread practice of relying on ready-to-use widgets supplied by various external 
entities should be tempered by the introduction of unifying standards, ensuring con-
sistency, reliability, and coherence across municipal websites.
The next problem concerns the lack of clarity resulting from the simultaneous pre-
sentation of data derived from multiple sources, which may communicate different,

5 Conclusion
Policy-laden choices are evident in decisions concerning whether, what, and how air 
quality data are presented on municipal websites. This is particularly important given 
that such websites are frequently visited and constitute a key space in which public 
Fig. 13 Air quality banner of the municipality of Chrzanów
 
1 3
M. Stępień
authorities can communicate issues directly affecting residents’ everyday lives. The 
importance of public authority websites cannot be overstated, as they significantly 
influence access to information and, consequently, access to certain fundamental 
rights.
In general, the visual infrastructure observable on the websites of municipalities 
in Lesser Poland is developed by actors other than the municipalities themselves. 
Municipalities typically embed externally prepared banners and widgets and select 
which data are displayed within them. They retain control over the placement, vis-
ibility, and relationship of these elements to other information concerning air quality 
and broader environmental issues. In this sense, municipalities possess a degree of 
navigational and compositional discretion.
Without advancing a strong or accusatory thesis, the overall impression emerging 
from the analysis is that municipalities rarely approach air quality communication in 
a strategic and holistic manner. Information is frequently presented without sufficient 
contextualization and largely without explanatory elements. As a result, the use of 
visual tools appears secondary and instrumental in nature: municipalities tend to rely 
on ready-made solutions rather than developing tailored visual narratives that would 
enhance comprehension and engagement within a given local community.
Among visual tools, color plays the most prominent communicative role. Emoti-
cons are frequently used and often serve as the primary interpretative cue for users. 
Diagrams and charts are also embedded on municipal websites, but accessing them 
commonly requires additional user actions. Maps–which offer significant communi-
cative potential–are relatively widespread; however, interacting with them typically 
requires users to navigate further into the website and to possess the motivation to 
do so.
Once again, the absence of locally developed visual elements, such as original 
infographics, is telling. It suggests a tendency to prioritize minimum compliance 
with legal obligations rather than treating air quality communication as a substantive 
policy goal. This reflects a minimalist approach to fulfilling legal information duties, 
oriented primarily toward formal compliance rather than effective communication. 
Overall, visual communication in this area appears fragmented, at times cacopho-
nous, and weakly substantiated. As suggested above, this situation may result either 
from a lack of visual literacy at the municipal level or from limited institutional inter-
est in this mode of communication-or from a combination of both.
As a consequence, the potential of visual communication remains largely unex-
plored. At the same time, the informational infrastructure provided by the Lesser 
Poland authorities (as well as by commercial providers) offers a solid starting point 
for developing locally sensitive–yet to some extent standardized—strategies for 
communicating air quality information. The EU LIFE project, and more broadly EU 
air pollution policies, provide incentives to pursue such improvements. There are 
now resources available–accumulated experience and observable differences among 
municipalities—that could, in the future, transform the communication of air-related 
information. The emergence of a specific infosphere focused on this issue is particu-
larly notable and holds significant potential for further development.
