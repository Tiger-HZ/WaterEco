Electronic structure tailoring of COFs photocatalysts via triazine moieties 
for efficient H 2O 2 generation and water decontamination 
Yan Lin1, Ziyan Du1, Xin Wu2, Ziyi Chen1, Yanxia Yang1, Xin Peng1, Chunping Yang3,4* and 
Shenglian Luo3,5* 
ABSTRACT Developing efficient photocatalysts for hydro-
gen peroxide (H 2O 2) synthesis is vital for sustainable chem-
istry, yet optimizing the electronic structure of triazine-based 
covalent organic frameworks (COFs) through precise spatial 
engineering remains a challenge. In this work, we constructed 
four model COFs to systematically decode how the spatial 
arrangement and incorporation level of triazine moieties 
regulate the electronic structures and H 2O 2 production effi-
ciency. Combined experimental and theoretical analyses re-
vealed that FB-AT achieved an optimal donor-acceptor 
architecture via rational spatial arrangement of triazine and 
benzene moieties. This configuration established an in-
tramolecular potential gradient, which not only promoted 
charge separation by suppressing the exciton binding energy 
but also enriched the electron density at triazine sites. These 
electron-rich active centers significantly facilitated the oxygen 
reduction reaction by lowering the thermodynamic energy 
barrier for *
OOH intermediate formation. Consequently, FB- 
AT exhibited a remarkable H 2O 2 production rate of 
11055 μmol g−1 h−1 in pure water, along with a superior solar- 
to-chemical conversion efficiency of 1.16%. Additionally, FB- 
AT enabled complete degradation of phenol, tetracycline, and 
rhodamine B within 5–15 min of visible light irradiation. This 
work provides crucial guidance for the molecular-level design 
of high-performance COF photocatalysts. 
Keywords: covalent organic frameworks, electronic structure, 
oxygen reduction reaction, H 2O 2 photosynthesis, water 
decontamination 
INTRODUCTION 
Hydrogen peroxide (H 2O 2) is a versatile, environmentally 
friendly chemical critical for wastewater treatment, fuel cells, 
and medical disinfection [1–5]. It is conventionally produced 
through the energy-intensive anthraquinone process, which 
requires high temperature and pressure [6,7]. In contrast, pho-
tocatalytic H 2O 2 synthesis using solar energy to drive the reac-
tion of water and oxygen with photocatalysts has emerged as a 
promising, green, and sustainable alternative [8–11]
. The design 
of high-efficiency photocatalysts is crucial for this technology. 
Widely used materials such as TiO 2 and g-C 3N 4 suffer from low 
activity or require sacrificial agents [12–15]. Covalent organic 
frameworks (COFs), as crystalline porous materials featuring 
designable structures and high specific surface areas, present a 
promising platform, enabling precise regulation of electronic 
and structural properties to boost photocatalytic performance 
[16–21]. However, their efficiency for H 2O 2 production is pri-
marily limited by severe recombination of photogenerated 
charges [22]
. 
To address this, researchers have modified COF electronic 
structures by introducing functional groups such as benzene, 
thiophene, triazine, heptazine, and sulfone [23–27]. Among 
them, electron-deficient triazine moieties can modulate the 
electron cloud distribution and promote the separation of 
photogenerated charge carriers [28]. For instance, Hao et al. [29] 
designed a two-dimensional sp2 carbon-conjugated triazine COF 
with an optimized band structure and enhanced charge trans-
port properties. Similarly, Tong et al
. [30] synthesized triazine- 
based COFs in which the N-heterocyclic microenvironment 
regulated electron affinity and carrier dynamics, thereby boost-
ing photocatalytic efficiency. However, the rational spatial 
arrangement and optimal density of triazine moieties have 
received insufficient attention, and systematic studies on their 
specific effects in the COF photocatalytic H 2O 2 generation are 
still lacking [31]. 
Additionally, constructing donor-acceptor (D-A) configura-
tions in COFs can effectively tune molecular orbital energy levels 
and enhance charge separation, thereby boosting photocatalytic 
activity [32–34]
. For example, Chen et al. [35] developed D-A 
COFs that reduced over 99% of Cr(VI) within 75 min under 
visible light. Gu et al. [36] synthesized triazine-based D-A COFs, 
achieving 99% conversion and 98% selectivity in a model reac-
tion. Despite these advances, key questions regarding the rela-

Herein, four imine-linked COFs (denoted as FB-AT, FT-AT, 
FT-AB, and FB-AB) were synthesized via a green and mild acetic 
acid-catalyzed route (
Fig. 1 and Fig. S1). By precisely regulating 
the spatial arrangement and content of triazine moieties, we 
optimized their electronic structures for enhanced photocatalytic 
H 2O 2 production. FB-AT demonstrated superior performance, 
achieving a production rate of 11055 μmol g−1 h−1 in water and 
air without sacrificial agents, with a solar-to-chemical conver-
sion (SCC) efficiency of 1.16%, along with excellent pollutant 
degradation capability. This comparative analysis systematically 
elucidated the regulatory mechanism of triazine moieties, pro-
viding new insights for designing high-performance COF pho-
tocatalysts. 
EXPERIMENTAL SECTION 
Chemicals and reagents 
All chemicals and reagents were analytical grade or better and 
used without further purification. 1,3,5-Tris-(4-aminophenyl) 
benzene (AB), 4,4′,4′′-(1,3,5-triazine-2,4,6-triyl)trianiline (AT), 
5′-(4-formylphenyl)-[1,1′:3′,1′′-terphenyl]-4,4′′-dicarbaldehyde 
(FB) and 4,4′,4′′-(1,3,5-triazine-2,4,6-triyl) tribenzaldehyde (FT) 
were purchased from Jilin Chinese Academy of Sciences-Yan-
shen Technology Co., Ltd., China. Hydrogen peroxide, acetic 
acid (HAc), acetonitrile, silver nitrate, phenol, tetracycline (TC), 
rhodamine B (RhB), benzyl alcohol (BA) and tert
-butyl alcohol 
(TBA) were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., 
Ltd., China. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) was obtained from 
Hefei Bomei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China. 
Preparation of FB-AT 
This work improves the method for synthesizing imine-linked 
COFs using HAc in aqueous solution at room temperature [37]. 
FB (124.9 mg, 0.32 mmol) was weighed and added to a 10 mL 
brown glass bottle. Subsequently, 2 mL of HAc (8.75 M) was 
introduced into the bottle, and the mixture was stirred for 
30 min to pre-activate the aldehyde monomer. Separately, AT 
(85.1 mg, 0.24 mmol) was weighed into a 10 mL centrifuge tube, 
followed by the addition of 2 mL of acetonitrile. The suspension 
was sonicated until homogeneous dispersion was achieved. The 
dispersed AT solution was then quickly injected into the pre- 
activated FB solution, leading to an immediate color change to 
bright yellow. The resulting mixture was vortexed for 10 s, 
transferred to a 10 mL centrifuge tube, and allowed to stand at 
room temperature for 3 days. The yellow precipitate that formed

Photocatalytic H 2O 2 production 
Catalyst (10 mg) and pure water (20 mL) were mixed and dis-
persed by ultrasonication. The dispersion was placed in dark 
with aeration treatment in O 2 atmosphere for 30 min to achieve 
adsorption saturation equilibrium. In photocatalytic reactions, a 
300 W Xe lamp with a 420 nm cutoff filter was employed as the 
light source. The reaction system was cooled by circulating water 
to keep it at room temperature. After sampling every 15 min, 
solid-liquid separation procedure was carried out with a filter of 
0.22 μm. The filtrate was collected, and the concentration of 
photogenerated H 2O 2 was determined by iodometry. Specifi-
cally, potassium iodide (KI) solution (1 mL, 0.4 M) and potas-
sium hydrogen phthalate (C 8H 5KO 4) solution (1 mL, 0.1 M) 
were added to a diluted solution (3 mL) and kept for 1 h. Under 
acidic conditions, H 2O 2 can react with I− to generate I 3 −, which 
exhibits strong absorption at approximately 350 nm. Thus, the 
absorbance at 350 nm by using ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) 
spectroscopy can measure the amount of I 3 −, which can further 
determine the amount of H 2O 2 produced in each sample by the 
standard curve shown in Fig. S25. 
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
Materials synthesis and characterization 
The crystal structures of the four COFs were characterized by 
powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD). The experimental patterns of 
FB-AT, FT-AT, FT-AB, and FB-AB showed characteristic peaks 
at 2θ = 4.00°, 4.09°, 4.00°, and 3.88°, respectively, which matched 
well with the simulated patterns based on the AA stacking 
model. Pawley refinements confirmed a good fit between the 
experimental and simulated data (Figs S2–S5) [38]. For instance, 
FB-AT yielded refinement factors of R wp = 6.60% and R p = 
4.46%. Refinement of the unit cell parameters revealed a = 
25.78 Å, b = 25.78 Å, c = 7.2 Å, α = β = 90°, γ = 120° for FB-AT, 
a = 23.87 Å, b = 24.88 Å, c = 3.47 Å, α = β = 90°, γ = 123.05° for 
FT-AT, a = 25.83 Å, b = 25.83 Å, c = 7.2 Å, α = β = 90°, γ = 120° 
for FT-AB, and a = 26.48 Å, b = 26.42 Å, c = 3.5 Å, α = β = 90°, γ 
= 120.86° for FB-AB, respectively. The formation of imine lin-
kages was verified by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spec-
troscopy (Fig. S6). The appearance of characteristic C=N 
stretching bands at 1584, 1586, 1579, and 1593 cm−1 for FB-AT, 
FT-AT, FT-AB, and FB-AB, respectively, coincided with the 
disappearance of precursor peaks, specifically the aldehyde C=O 
stretch (1691 and 1706 cm−1) and the N–H stretches of AB (3350 
and 3429 cm−1) and AT (3321 and 3464 cm−1). This indicated 
the successful occurrence of the Schiff base condensation. Fur-
thermore, the chemical structure of FB-AT was corroborated 
by 13C solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (13C NMR) 
spectroscopy (Fig. 2j–k). The spectrum displayed characteristic 
signals at approximately 153 and 160 ppm, assigned to C–N and 
C=N species, respectively, providing additional evidence for the 
formation of imine linkages. The elemental composition and 
chemical states of FB-AT were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron 
spectroscopy (XPS). The survey spectrum (Fig. S7) confirmed 
the presence of C, N, and O. In the high-resolution C 1s spec-
trum, peaks at 284 and 288 eV were assigned to C–C/C=C and 
C=N bonds, respectively. For the N 1s spectrum, the strong peak 
at 399 eV was attributed to triazine N, the peak around 400 eV 
corresponded to C=N, and the peak at 402 eV was assigned to 
–NH 2 (Fig. S8). Collectively, the PXRD, FT-IR, 13C NMR, and 
XPS results demonstrated the successful synthesis and structural 
integrity of the four COF catalysts. As shown in Fig. 2a–e, the 
morphologies of the four COFs were characterized using scan-
ning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM). FB-AT exhibited a needle-clustered rod-like 
morphology, which contributed to an increased specific surface 
area and enhanced oxygen exposure, thereby improving its 
photocatalytic performance by exposing more active sites. In 
contrast, the other three COFs displayed a reticular morphology. 
Furthermore, HRTEM images of FB-AT revealed long-range 
ordered channels. The measured pore size was approximately 
1.69 nm, which was in good agreement with the theoretically 
simulated value. Additionally, TEM-energy dispersive X-ray 
spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping confirmed the uniform 
distribution of C and N elements throughout the FB-AT sample 
(
Fig. 2f–h) [39]. 
The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface areas and pore 
structures of the COFs were determined by N 2 adsorption-des-
orption measurements at 77 K (
Fig. 2i and Figs S9–S11). The 
raw data were fitted using a nonlocal density functional theory 
(NLDFT) model, giving BET surface areas of 526.93, 308.57, 
25.62, and 36.94 m2 g−1 for FB-AT, FT-AT, FT-AB, and FB-AB, 
respectively. Among these, FB-AT possessed the largest specific 
surface area, which provided more reactive sites and facilitated 
charge transfer and mass transport during the photocatalytic 
process [40,41]. The pore size distribution of FB-AT showed a 
peak at approximately 1.68 nm (Fig. 2i), which was consistent 
with the above TEM observations. To further investigate the 
stability of the catalysts, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was 
conducted. The results indicated that FB-AT and FT-AT 
exhibited better thermal stability compared to FT-AB and FB- 
AB, suggesting their potential suitability for broader application 
scenarios (Fig. S12). 
Photoelectric property 
To investigate the optical properties of the materials, the light 
absorption characteristics of FB-AT, FT-AT, FT-AB, and FB-AB 
were characterized using UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy 
(UV-vis DRS). The results showed that all four materials 
exhibited excellent visible-light absorption performance 
(Fig. 3a). By converting the UV-vis DRS data into Tauc plots 
(Fig. S13), the optical band gaps (E g) of the four COFs were 
estimated to be 2.44, 2.49, 2.40, and 2.26 eV, respectively. Sub-
sequently, the flat-band potentials (
E fb) of the four COFs were 
determined via Mott-Schottky (MS) electrochemical measure-
ments (Fig. S14). The measured E fb values were −0.74, −0.69, 
−0.73, and −0.68 V vs. Ag/AgCl, respectively. The positive 
slopes of the MS plots indicated that all these COFs were n-type 
semiconductors. Accordingly, the conduction bands (CB) 
energies of the four COFs were determined to be −0.54, −0.49, 
−0.53, and −0.48 V vs. normal hydrogen electrode (NHE), 
respectively [42]. Their respective valence bands (VB), calculated 
by combining the CB potentials with the E g values, were 1.90, 
2.00, 1.87, and 1.78 V vs. NHE. Only photogenerated charge 
carriers with sufficient redox potential were thermodynamically

capable of driving surface redox reactions [43]. As shown in the 
energy band diagram (Fig. 3b), all four COFs were thermo-
dynamically favorable for the two-step one-electron oxygen 
reduction reaction (ORR) and the one-step two-electron water 
oxidation reaction (WOR) [44,45]
. 
FB-AT exhibited the weakest PL intensity (Fig. S15), indicat-
ing a higher charge separation efficiency. Consistently, it also 
demonstrated the strongest photocurrent response (
Fig. 3c), 
suggesting the most efficient generation of photogenerated 
charge carriers [46]. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy 
(EIS) Nyquist plots revealed that FB-AT possessed the smallest 
semicircular radius among the samples (Fig. 3d), corresponding 
to the lowest interfacial charge transfer resistance and the most 
favorable charge transfer kinetics [47,48]. Time-resolved pho-
toluminescence (TR-PL) decay profiles were fitted to obtain the 
average carrier lifetimes (
τ ave), which followed the order: FB-AT 
(1.01 ns) > FT-AT (0.58 ns) > FT-AB (0.57 ns) > FB-AB 
(0.56 ns) (Fig. 3e and Table S1). The prolonged carrier lifetime 
of FB-AT suggested suppressed charge recombination. Fur-
thermore, temperature-dependent PL spectra showed a mono-
tonic increase in emission intensity upon cooling (
Fig. 3f and 
Figs S16–S18). The corresponding exciton binding energies (E b), 
derived from fitting the Arrhenius equation [49], were deter-
mined to be 74.6, 76.8, 82.6, and 101.0 meV for FB-AT, FT-AT, 
FT-AB, and FB-AB, respectively. The lowest E b value of FB-AT 
among the series indicated that its excitons dissociated most 
readily into free charges, which was consistent with its superior 
photocatalytic performance. 
Based on femtosecond time-resolved transient absorption (fs- 
TA) spectroscopy characterization, the photogenerated carrier 
dynamics of the four COFs were further investigated. The fs-TA 
spectrum of FB-AT exhibited a strong and persistent absorption 
signal in the 500–650 nm range (orange-yellow region) (Fig. 3g, 
Figs S19, S21 and S23), which was attributed to excited-state 
absorption by photogenerated electrons [50]. The decay curves 
of the four COFs were fitted using a double-exponential model 
(Fig. 3h, Figs S20, S22 and S24), revealing two distinct relaxation 
pathways of photogenerated charge. The short-lived component 
(τ 1) was associated with electron trapping, while the long-lived 
component (τ 2) was related to electron-hole recombination 
[50,51]. Notably, FB-AT possessed the longest τ 2 (706.39 ps) 
among the COFs, indicating an extended carrier lifetime, which 
was consistent with the TR-PL results (Table S2).

Mechanism of photocatalytic H 2O 2 production 
Control experiments were performed to explore the pathway of 
photocatalytic H 2O 2 production. As shown in Fig. 4c, replacing 
air with O 2 did not significantly enhance the H 2O 2 yield, whereas 
only a trace amount of H 2O 2 was detected under an Ar atmo-
sphere. These results confirmed that O 2 was indispensable and 
air provided sufficient O 2 for the reaction, which proceeded 
predominantly via ORR. We conducted quenching experiments 
using SOD (·O 2 − scavenger), BA (h+ scavenger), silver nitrate 
(AgNO 3, e− scavenger) and TBA (·OH scavenger). As shown in 
Fig. 4d, H 2O 2 production was enhanced by 10% BA, but sig-
nificantly inhibited by 1 mM AgNO 3 and 300 U mL−1 SOD. The 
10% TBA showed no inhibitory effect. The results indicated that 
electrons participated in the ORR, ·O 2 − was the key intermediate, 
and ·OH was not generated in the WOR. 
Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy was employed 
using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) as a spin trap 
to detect ·O 2 − and ·OH, thereby further clarifying the photo-
synthetic mechanism of H 2O 2 production by COFs. As shown in 
Fig. 4g, no signal peaks appeared under dark conditions, while 
distinct DMPO-·O 2 − characteristic peaks were observed after 
visible light irradiation, and the peak intensity increased sig-
nificantly with prolonged irradiation time. These results con-
firmed that ·O 2 − was continuously generated during the 
photocatalytic process and that light irradiation was essential for 
its formation. The ·OH was detected for FB-AT under light 
irradiation (Fig. S33). However, the VB potential of FB-AT was 
insufficiently positive to reach the oxidation potential of ·OH/ 
H 2O 2 (2.73 V vs. NHE). This thermodynamic limitation hin-
dered hole-mediated water oxidation and the subsequent gen-
eration of ·OH. Therefore, we hypothesized that the excess ·O 2 − 
radicals in FB-AT could react with H+ to form ·OH [53,54]. 
Subsequently, in-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier trans-
form (
in-situ DRIFT) spectroscopy was used to monitor reaction 
intermediates. The in-situ DRIFT spectrum of FB-AT exhibited 
three peaks at 869, 1157, and 1342 cm−1, which were assigned to

Theoretical calculations 
To gain deeper insights into how incorporating triazine moieties 
influenced the ORR mechanism at the atomic scale, we con-
ducted DFT calculations. As shown in Fig. 5
b, electrostatic 
potential (ESP) analysis revealed that FB-AT exhibited a con-
centrated negative potential in the triazine region, originating 
from its well-defined D-A structure and gradient potential dis-
tribution. This unique electronic structure thereby promoted 
charge separation and transfer more effectively than the other 
three COFs. Molecular orbital analysis disclosed unique features 
of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest 
unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) in these COFs (
Fig. 5a 
and Fig. S36). Unlike the others, FB-AT possessed an LUMO 
with higher density localized on the central triazine moieties 
compared to its own HOMO. Moreover, the LUMO exhibited a 
uniform distribution, facilitating enhanced electron delocaliza-
tion within the framework and thereby stabilizing it. This sta-
bilization was beneficial for the efficient transfer and utilization 
of photogenerated electrons [56]
. 
In addition, the adsorption configurations of O 2 at various 
sites on FB-AT were first optimized (Fig. 5c and Fig. S37), 
identifying the carbon atom in the imine bond (site 5) as the 
preferred active site for O 2 adsorption. Gibbs free energy (ΔG) 
diagrams for the ORR pathways were then calculated for all 
COFs (Fig. 5d). The protonation of adsorbed O 2 to form *OOH 
emerged as a rate-determining step, for which FB-AT showed 
the most favorable ΔG (−0.54 eV). This suggested that FB-AT 
has the lowest kinetic barrier for this critical step, rationalizing 
its superior activity. Moreover, FB-AT also had the smallest ΔG 
for the desorption of *H 2O 2, implying efficient release of the

Triazine moieties structure-activity relationship 
The combined experimental and theoretical results revealed that 
the spatial arrangement and content of the triazine moieties, 
along with their synergistic interaction with benzene moieties, 
were the pivotal factors regulating the electronic structure of 
COFs, which ultimately governed their photocatalytic H 2O 2 
production performance. 
(1) FB-AB, which lacked triazine moieties, exhibited the 
lowest H 2O 2 yield. This poor performance was attributed to the 
absence of electronic defect sites, a non-uniform electron cloud 
distribution, rapid recombination of photogenerated carriers 
(with a carrier lifetime of merely 0.56 ns), and a high exciton 
binding energy (101.0 meV). 
(2) FT-AT and FT-AB, which incorporated triazine moieties 
but in suboptimal spatial configurations, exhibited only mod-
erate electron-accepting capability. The insufficient synergy 
between the triazine and benzene units resulted in weak LUMO 
electron delocalization and a high energy barrier for *
OOH 
formation, consequently leading to inferior photocatalytic 
activity and charge separation efficiency compared to FB-AT. 
(3) In contrast, FB-AT, featuring an optimal D-A configura-
tion composed of triazine and benzene units, exhibited a gra-
dient potential distribution. This led to enhanced LUMO 
electron density on triazine sites, a reduced exciton binding 
energy of 74.6 meV, and an extended carrier lifetime of 1.01 ns. 
Electrostatic potential analysis confirmed that the negative 
potential was primarily localized in the triazine region, which 
promoted more uniform electron distribution, delocalization, 
and stabilization. Furthermore, this configuration significantly 
lowered the Δ
G for the formation of *OOH, the rate-deter-
mining step in the ORR, to a favorable value of −
0.54 eV. 
Therefore, FB-AT exhibited the most outstanding photocatalytic 
activity. 
CONCLUSIONS 
In summary, we synthesized four functionalized COFs and 
revealed that the spatial arrangement and incorporation level of 
triazine moieties modulated the electronic structure, thereby 
enabling efficient solar energy utilization and enhancing pho-
tocatalytic H 2O 2 production. The results demonstrated that FB- 
AT, with an optimal D-A configuration consisting of one tria-
zine moiety and one benzene moiety, exhibited superior pho-
tocatalytic performance compared to the other three COFs. 
These two moieties synergistically regulated the electronic 
structure of FB-AT, effectively reducing the exciton binding 
energy and prolonging the lifetime of photogenerated charge, 
allowing photogenerated carriers to be more easily generated 
and smoothly migrated. Moreover, this electronic regulation 
effectively lowered the energy barrier for the formation of 
*
OOH, the rate-determining step in the 2e− ORR, thus pro-
moting H 2O 2 production. Without any sacrificial agents, the 
H 2O 2 production rate in pure water under air reached up to 
11055 μmol g−1 h−1, with an SCC efficiency of 1.16%, surpassing 
that of the majority of reported COFs. FB-AT also demonstrated 
excellent photocatalytic degradation activity toward a wide range 
of pollutants, including dyes, phenolic compounds, and anti-
biotics, achieving complete removal within 5–15 min of visible 
light irradiation. Overall, this work provides fundamental 
insights into structure-performance relationships for designing 
high-performance COF photocatalysts for H 2O 2 synthesis and 
water decontamination.
