Livestock products arrive as meat, dairy, eggs, leather, and processed foods, but their watersheds begin earlier. Irrigated forage, grain and oilseed rotations, fertilizer, seed systems, pasture, manure storage, processing, refrigeration, transport, labor, and discarded food form an ecological infrastructure that is often distant from consumption. This paper names that separation agricultural metabolic distance: the spatial, informational, contractual, ecological, and temporal distance between a finished product and the land, water, organisms, labor, and wastes through which it was produced. The paper advances a central hypothesis: livestock-system burdens tend to increase, persist, or become harder to govern when agricultural metabolic distance and absolute animal-product throughput increase together; durable reductions require lowering both. Efficiency or traceability alone may change impact intensity or visibility without reducing basin depletion, nutrient load, land conversion, or total emissions. The hypothesis predicts stronger system-level outcomes when basin limits, diversified production, accountable supply chains, regional transition infrastructure, food-waste prevention, and dietary demand change operate as a coordinated portfolio. Evidence is traced across Great Salt Lake, the U.S. Corn Belt, arid Southwestern agriculture, and the Amazon-Cerrado frontier. A nine-year Iowa field experiment found that diversified rotations maintained or increased corn and soybean yields and similar returns while reducing manufactured nitrogen, herbicide, fossil-energy use, and freshwater toxicity, although labor demand increased. Prairie-strip research found that placing native perennials on 10% of row-crop catchments reduced sediment movement by 95%, phosphorus loss by 90%, and total nitrogen loss by 85% under the studied conditions. The demand-side evidence is also directional but heterogeneous. A study linking 55,504 observed U.K. diets to 570 life-cycle assessments estimated standardized vegan diets at 25.1% of high-meat diets for greenhouse-gas emissions and land use, 46.4% for water use, 27.0% for eutrophication, and 34.3% for potential biodiversity impact. Low-meat diets were at least 30% below high-meat diets for most greenhouse-gas, land, and eutrophication indicators. These estimates do not establish identical outcomes in every geography, nor do they substitute for nutritional, labor, welfare, or distributional analysis.
