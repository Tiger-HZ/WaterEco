Abstract
Karst basins have permeable surface-subsurface connections that can accelerate nitrogen transport. The Lipu River Basin 
in southern Guilin is affected by intensive agriculture and industrial activity, but its nitrate sources and transformations 
remain poorly resolved. We integrated dual nitrate isotopes (δ 15N-NO3⁻ and δ 18O-NO3⁻), hydrochemical data, and a 
Bayesian SIAR mixing model. River-water TN was significantly higher in winter (7.09 ± 6.59 mg/L) than in summer 
(2.95 ± 1.48 mg/L). River-water NO 3⁻-N was lower in summer than in winter and lower than groundwater only during 
summer; the winter river-groundwater difference was not significant. The highest individual groundwater NO 3⁻-N values 
occurred near the industrial park, but the land-use group difference was not significant (Kruskal-Wallis, p = 0.48). The 
SIAR model identified manure and sewage as the largest modeled summer nitrate source, contributing 49% at the basin 
scale. These results identify elevated winter river loads and groundwater nitrate hotspots near tributaries and industrial 
zones as management priorities.

Pollution-control strategies developed for porous-media-

karst environments (Guo et al. 2019). When industrial and 
agricultural nitrogen inputs interact with karst hydrology,

poorly characterised. Discrete conduit flow and rapid sur -
face-subsurface exchange challenge lumped and semi-dis -
tributed watershed models such as SWAT and HEC-HMS. 
These models represent the subsurface as a continuous 
porous medium and may not capture heterogeneous flow 
paths or nitrogen transport reliably (Iriawan H et al. 2021). 
For source apportionment, dual NO 3⁻ isotopes are increas -
ingly combined with Bayesian mixing models, the IsoSource 
mass-balance approach, or statistical receptor models such 
as PMF and APCS-MLR. Most applications have focused 
on non-karst settings. Few studies have quantified source 
proportions and their uncertainty in coupled karst river-
groundwater systems, where conduits create heterogeneous 
links between surface water and groundwater. We therefore 
investigated the Lipu River Basin, where nitrate concentra -
tions are elevated but source contributions remain unquanti-
fied. We combined dual nitrate isotopes with hydrochemical 
data to trace sources and assess transformation pathways in 
this karst river-groundwater system.
Dissolved inorganic nitrogen (DIN) in natural waters 
occurs mainly as NO3
−, NO2
− and NH4
+. Owing to its high 
mobility, NO3
− is typically the dominant DIN species and 
a sensitive indicator of pollutant transport in karst systems 
(Zuo et al. 2023). In addition, high NO 3
− levels are asso -
ciated with methemoglobinemia in humans and aquatic 
ecosystem degradation (Yan et al. 2023). NO 3
− pollution 
sources in karst basins include point and river sources. 
Point sources include mainly industrial parks and landfills. 
In addition, NO 3
− concentrations and sources are affected 
by various nitrogen transformations. For example, higher 
NH4⁺ concentrations can promote nitrification (Ma et al. 
2021). They may also reduce net NO3⁻ removal by assimila-
tion, thereby promoting nitrate accumulation in karst basins. 
Fertilizer application commonly dominates nitrate inputs in 
agricultural areas (Wang et al. 2024), whereas point sources 
are often more important in urbanized areas (Lai et al. 2023). 
However, manure, sewage, and soil nitrogen can also domi-
nate groundwater NO3
− inputs in agricultural areas Integrat-
ing multiple tracers with source-apportionment models can 
quantify these contributions and distinguish natural from 
anthropogenic controls.
NO3
− sources largely reflect basin nitrogen sources (Iqbal 
et al. 2022; Schaefer et al. 2016). Linking land use types to 
cations and anions has been a traditional approach to identi-
fying NO3
− sources and nitrogen cycling processes (Biddau 
et al. 2023). The NO 3
− stable isotope method (δ 15N-NO3
− 
and δ 18O-NO3
−) provides meaningful insights into NO 3
− 
sources and nitrogen conversion processes because different 
NO3
− sources exhibit unique isotopic characteristics (Xue et 
al. 2009). The NO3
− stable isotope method requires minimal 
auxiliary information, offers high accuracy and operational 
simplicity, and directly identifies NO 3
− sources (Yang et 
al. 2024). In general, manure and sewage have relatively 
high δ15N-NO3⁻ values, whereas atmospheric deposition has 
high δ18O-NO3⁻ values. Mineral fertilizer and soil nitrogen 
usually have lower δ 15N-NO3⁻ values, which helps con -
strain nitrate sources in aquatic systems (Wu et al. 2022). 
However, isotope signatures can overlap between fertilizer 
and soil nitrogen and between soil nitrogen and manure or 
sewage. Source mixing and isotope fractionation during 
biogeochemical transformations further limit source appor-
tionment (Lin et al. 2019). Consequently, NO3
− isotopes are 
often combined with complementary tracers. In this study, 
auxiliary tracers were used to distinguish mixing from deni-
trification within the analytical scope, while the potential 
value of additional tracers and integrated approaches is 
acknowledged in the Conclusion (Grechanik et al. 2024; Li 
et al. 2023).
This study integrated a Bayesian isotope mixing model 
with NO 3⁻ stable isotope analysis. Additional covariates 
(hydrochemical parameters and land use types) enhanced 
source apportionment accuracy compared with traditional 
mixing models. The hydrological status of the southwest -
ern karst basin is complex, with intense agricultural and 
human activities. Groundwater, rainfall, and river water 
are key sources of river recharge. Towns and farmlands are 
scattered around karst basin (Chang et al. 2024). There -
fore, karst basins are typical areas of mixed land use (Yi 
et al. 2015). Frequent seasonal groundwater-river water 
exchange results in severe groundwater nitrogen pol -
lution, with average nitrogen and NO 3
− concentrations 
reaching 24.35 and 15.15 mg/L (Boumaiza et al. 2022), 
respectively, posing a major threat to lake water quality 
and safety. In coupled karst river-groundwater systems it 
remains unclear (i) how the dominant NO 3
− sources dif -
fer between river water and groundwater, (ii) whether 
nitrification or denitrification controls the NO 3
− pool, and 
(iii) what the proportional source contributions-with their 
uncertainty-actually are.
To address these research gaps, we investigated inor -
ganic nitrogen pollution in river water and groundwater 
in the Lipu River Basin within Lipu County, considering 
domestic sewage, industrial sewage, and other pollution 
sources. By integrating NO 3
− stable isotope (δ 15N and 
δ18O) analysis with water chemistry data, meteorological 
parameters, environmental factors, and the Bayesian iso -
tope mixing (SIAR, Stable Isotope Analysis in R) model, 
the objectives of this study are to: (1) clarify the distribu -
tion characteristics of DIN (NO 3⁻-N + NO2⁻-N + NH4⁺-N) 
species under the influence of various discharge types in a 
typical karst area; (2) investigate the spatiotemporal distri -
bution of nitrogen-containing pollutants in river water and 
groundwater and their correlations with comprehensive 
hydrochemical indicators; and (3) identify the sources of

Materials and methods
Description of the study area
The study area lies in Lipu City, southern Guilin, in north -
eastern Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region. It extends 
into western Pingle County and reaches the western banks of 
the Lijiang and Guijiang rivers. The basin spans 110°12′6″-
110°40′57″ E and 24°20′54″-24°39′27″ N. The region has 
distinct wet and dry seasons, with long, hot, rainy summers 
and generally mild winters. The long-term mean temperature 
is 18.9 °C, and mean annual precipitation is 1,441.5 mm, 
concentrated from April to August. The submerged aquifer 
in the area is 0.5–3.5 m deep. The lithology of the area is 
composed mainly of clastic, carbonate, igneous, and loose 
rocks. The Quaternary Holocene Q4 is located on the upper 
terrace, with pore aquifers and partial pressure. The northern 
part of the basin overlies the Lower Carboniferous stages of 
the Datangdian and Yanguandian C1, which contain fissure 
cave water with a small flow of springs (Zhang et al. 2020).
Watershed monitoring
Two sampling campaigns were conducted in 2022, one dur-
ing the wet season (summer) and one during the dry sea -
son (winter; Fig. 1). The summer campaign included 27 
river sites and 12 groundwater sites. The winter campaign 
included 24 river sites and 14 groundwater sites, yielding 
51 river-water and 26 groundwater samples in total. Main -
stream sites were distributed from upstream Tianjingling to 
the downstream Guijiang confluence. Tributary sites cov -
ered the Pulu, Dumo, Datang, Xinping, Limu, and Maling 
rivers. Groundwater sites followed the inferred flow direc -
tion and represented three aquifer types: Quaternary porous 
aquifers, Carboniferous fissure-cave aquifers, and bedrock-
fissure aquifers. Table S1 lists all sampling sites, coordi -
nates, water-body types, and remarks. Text S1 describes the 
hydrogeological setting.
Sample collection
Sampling sites covered the basin’s recharge, runoff, and dis-
charge zones and were classified by dominant surrounding 
land use and discharge type (industrial, sanitary, or other). 
These categories were based on land use rather than direct 
measurements of flow or pollutant load at individual outlets. 
One local effluent sample (LTE) was collected at the location 
listed in Table S1. The summer isotope dataset comprised 
39 mixture samples (27 river-water and 12 groundwater 
samples) plus LTE. LTE was used only to assess whether 
local effluent fell within the literature-defined manure-and-
sewage prior; it was not entered as a mixture sample or as 
a separate modeled source. Before collection, brown glass 
bottles were rinsed three times with ultrapure water and 
three times with sample water, then sealed with aluminum 
foil and capped. Duplicate samples represented at least 10% 
of sites in each sampling batch. Groundwater was collected 
from phreatic wells and karst springs that tap the shallow 
aquifer. The water table was 0.5–3.5 m below ground sur -
face. Wells were purged until pH, electrical conductivity, 
and temperature stabilized, after which samples were col -
lected 0.5–1.0 m below the water table.

Sample preparation and analysis
Detection of inorganic carbon and nitrogen compounds
Immediately after the samples were transported to the 
laboratory, NH4
+-N, NO 3
−-N, NO 2
−-N, and inorganic car -
bon (DIC, including CO 3
2− and HCO 3
−) were measured. 
The remaining samples were filtered using a 0.45-µm filter 
membrane (Tianjin Jinteng, China) and stored in the dark at 
0–4 °C. DIN concentrations were measured spectrophoto -
metrically with a JH-TD401 multiparameter water-quality 
analyzer. DIC was measured using total-alkalinity test kits 
(10–200 mg/L, Lu Heng Bio, China).
Hydrochemical detection and data analysis
The anion and cation indicators to be tested included K +, 
Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, SO4
2−, and Cl−. The samples to be tested 
were filtered using a 0.22-µm filter membrane and stored 
away from light at 4 °C. Cationic concentrations were 
determined using an inductively coupled plasma emission 
spectrometer (Optima 8000, USA) in accordance with the 
method specified in the National Environmental Protection 
Standard (HJ 776–2015, China). Anions were detected using 
ion chromatography (DIONEX ICS-2100, USA). Anions 
were determined using the method specified in the National 
Environmental Protection Standard (HJ 84-2016, China). 
Because the data were non-normally distributed, non -
parametric tests were used throughout. The Friedman test 
compared nitrogen species measured in the same samples. 
Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for 
two-group and multigroup comparisons, respectively, and 
Spearman’s rank correlation assessed associations between 
solutes. Significance was set at p < 0.05. A summary of the 
key tests is given in Table S5.
Sampling and analysis of dual nitrate isotopes 
(δ15N-NO3
- and δ18O-NO3
- )
Sample collection for NO3
− isotopes
River water samples were collected onsite using PET sam -
ple bottles. First, they were filtered through a 0.45 μm filter 
membrane to remove various suspended solids and micro -
organisms. Subsequently, the water samples were stored at 
temperatures below 4 °C. Dual NO 3⁻ isotopes were deter -
mined by the bacterial denitrifier method, in which sample 
NO3⁻ is converted to N 2O by denitrifying bacteria and the 
N2O analysed by isotope-ratio mass spectrometry. Results 
were calibrated against the international reference materi -
als USGS32, USGS34 and USGS35 (with IAEA-NO 3 as a 
check); the analytical precision (1σ) was better than ± 0.3‰ 
for δ15N and ± 0.5‰ for δ18O. Text S3 summarizes method 
detection limits and the precision of the hydrochemical mea-
surements. Samples were filtered through 0.22-µm cartridge 
filters, and nitrate concentrations were measured before the 
samples were frozen and stored. An aliquot of the denitrify-
ing bacterial culture was plated and incubated at 30 °C for 3 
days. A single colony was then transferred to 6 mL of TSB 
medium and incubated for 8 h at 32 °C and 180 rpm. The 
remaining strain was stored at 4 °C.
Sample pre-treatment and isotope determination
A 0.2-mL aliquot of culture was added to 10 mL of tryptic 
soy broth (TSB) and incubated under the same conditions for 
16 h. Next, 10 mL of this culture was transferred to 238 mL of 
TSB and incubated for 48 h. A colorimetric test was performed 
after culture expansion. The next day, 0.2 mL of 10 mol/L 
NaOH was injected to terminate the reaction and absorb CO2. 
The generated gas was then analyzed instrumentally.
Hydrogen and oxygen isotopes were measured using a 
liquid water isotope laser spectrometer. Stable carbon iso -
tope (δ13C) analyses were performed by the Karst Geologi -
cal Resources and Environment Supervision and Testing 
Center, Institute of Karst Geology, Chinese Academy of 
Geological Sciences, Guilin.

Results and discussion
Dissolved inorganic nitrogen concentrations
DIN was detected in all river-water samples. River-water 
TN ranged from 0.24 to 16.11 mg/L, with a mean of 
5.02 ± 4.04 mg/L. This mean was lower than the ground -
water mean of 6.11 ± 5.01 mg/L, but the difference was 
not significant ( p > 0.05). Nitrate was the dominant 
DIN species in river water, averaging 4.27 ± 3.55 mg/L. 
Mean NH 4⁺-N and NO 2⁻-N were 0.68 ± 0.42 and 
0.06 ± 0.04 mg/L, respectively. The same order occurred 
in groundwater, with means of 5.75 ± 4.82, 0.36 ± 0.28, 
and 0.07 ± 0.05 mg/L, respectively. Nine of the 76 samples 
(12%) exceeded the WHO/China drinking-water guideline 
for NO 3
−-N (10 mg/L, equivalent to 50 mg/L as NO 3
−); 
exceedances were more frequent in groundwater (19%) 
than in river water (8%) and were concentrated in ground -
water near the industrial park and in winter river water at 
food/grain-processing tributaries (Table S2). The NH 4
+-N 
concentration level is significantly ( p < 0.05) higher than 
that in large rivers with good water quality, such as the 
middle reaches of the Yangtze River (0.24 mg/L) and the 
Pearl River (0.33 mg/L) (Zhang et al. 2011; Xue et al. 
2009), and the water quality is roughly the same as that 
of the main stream of the Yellow River, China, which is 
greatly affected by local industrial and agricultural activi -
ties. Compared with karst river basins abroad, the Lipu 
River Basin is much better (p < 0.05) than the Silver Spring 
in Florida in USA (1.55 mg/L) and the Coesle River Basin 
in France (2.34 mg/L). In summary, the water quality dem-
onstrates a higher contamination risk compared to major 
rivers both in China and internationally, reflecting clear 
evidence of human impact.

and groundwater samples from the Lipu River Basin. In 
Fig. 2a, the nitrate nitrogen in the river water during sum -

that the aquifer buffered seasonal variation, whereas river

anthropogenic inputs. This pattern is consistent with rapid 
conduit flow. Regional faults and folds may provide prefer-
ential groundwater pathways (Hu 2024).

Nitrogen content in river water
During the observation period, river-water TN averaged 
4.95 ± 4.36 mg/L. Using the WHO and Chinese nitrate 
guideline, 8% of river sites exceeded the threshold. Mean 
nitrogen concentrations were relatively high in the Puling, 
Dumo, Datang, and Limu tributaries. Concentrations in the 
lower Maling River were 2.2 mg/L higher than upstream 
values, possibly reflecting inputs from the nearby grain- 
and food-processing industrial park. The concentrations of 
various forms of nitrogen in the mainstream are NH 4
+-N 
(0.55 ± 0.38 mg/L), NO3

NH4

reach and higher in the upper and lower reaches. The middle 
reach passes through urban Lipu, whereas site DT2 receives 
inputs from several industrial sectors, including metal, oil, 
rice, and flour processing. Strong karst development in the 
tributaries leads to low DO (< 2 mg/L), and an anoxic envi-
ronment severely inhibits nitrification (Diab et al. 1993), 
resulting in the accumulation of NH 4
+-N in the tributaries. 
Mean TN was higher in the tributaries (5.11 mg/L) than in 
the mainstream (2.64 mg/L). This difference was driven 
mainly by elevated NH4⁺-N at a few tributary sites, whereas 
the NO 3⁻-N difference was not statistically significant 
(Mann-Whitney, p = 0.11; Table S5)., and the flow rates in 
tributaries were mostly over 3.5 m 3/s, which is more than 
30% of the flow rate of the main stream. The pollution load 
is relatively high, indicating that tributaries such as the Pulu 
and Dumo Rivers are the core sources of N pollution in 
the basin (Fakouri et al. 2022). The river water monitoring 
points were mainly driven by OT and SW.
Points L5, DM1, and DM2 were affected by nearby 
karst skylights, with higher DO (5 mg/L) and NO 3
−-N 
concentrations of 3.05, 4.16, and 2.93 mg/L, respectively. 
Near PL2, L6, and DT2, there were large karst springs 
with NO 3

respectively, all higher than the average NO 3

conclusion of groundwater recharge to the river water. 
Considering the regional monitoring points were pure car -
bonate rock hydrogeological conditions, the retention time 
of pollutants around the large springs and skylights was 
potentially shortened (Meng et al. 2021) and the natural 
degradation rate was relatively low.
Nitrate-nitrogen content in groundwater
Groundwater TN averaged 5.743 mg/L, and 18.83% of 
groundwater sites exceeded the WHO and Chinese nitrate 
guideline. TN at sites U10 and U11, near the metalworking 
industrial park, was two to three times the regional mean 
(Fig. 5c, d). The area near the industrial-wastewater out -
let was therefore a nitrate hotspot and may have contrib -

upstream agricultural area has become a significant source 
of groundwater NO 3
−-N pollution, and the fundamental 
driving factor is extensive application of organic fertil -

nitrification process. The concentrations of NO 3
−-N in the 
U1–U3 region were 1.65–3.57 mg/L higher than the aver -

Lipu taro in the region. Organic nitrogen is converted into 
NH4

NO3

sive or uncomposted, the rate of nitrate formation exceeds

resulting in accumulation of NO 3

affected by nearby karst skylights, with NO3

karst springs near U10, U11, and U12, with NO 3

monitoring data near the karst spring showed no significant 
differences from the other points in the basin; however, the 
monitoring points near the karst skylight showed signifi -
cant differences from those of the other points ( P < 0.05). 
Sites U10-U12 sampled bedrock-fissure groundwater with 
limited precipitation recharge, short subsurface flow paths, 
and low discharge (Yi et al. 2023). Spring flow ranged from 
0.014 to 0.325 L/s. Mean NO 3⁻-N across U10-U12 was 
approximately 10 mg/L, possibly reflecting inputs from the 
nearby new-materials industrial park.

Seasonal variation
In summer, mean TN was lower in river water 
(2.96 ± 2.36 mg/L) than in groundwater (6.74 ± 5.36 mg/L). 
In winter, the pattern reversed: river-water TN averaged 
7.12 ± 6.65 mg/L, compared with 5.77 ± 4.86 mg/L in 
groundwater (Figs. 5 and 6). River-water NO3
−-N, NH4
+-N, 
and NO2
−-N were higher from October to March than from 
July to September, likely because summer rainfall and run -
off diluted river concentrations. During the winter, there is 
less precipitation and water flow slows down, and NO 3
−-N 
accumulation in the basin leads to higher concentration. 
Sewage outlets within the basin also affect the distribution 
of nitrogen; however, the dilution and accumulation effects 
caused by seasonal changes are more significant. Notably, 
there was a significant difference in NO 2
−-N between the 
wet and dry seasons, with concentrations in the summer 
being approximately three times those in the winter. The 
unique hydrological and water quality conditions in the 
study area during the summer (high load input, turbidity, 
and anoxic microenvironment) disrupted nitrogen transfor -
mation (Zhao et al. 2020) and inhibited the nitrite consump-
tion pathway (Fig. 5b). In groundwater, the high value areas 
of the DIN are often concentrated at the IW monitoring point 
(Fig. 5cd), which reflects both the continuous influence of 
industrial pollution sources and the fissure-pipe type flow 
characteristics of karst basins. Once pollutants enter a karst 
aquifer through infiltration, they migrate to relatively con -
nected channels, forming relatively stable high-value areas.
Between summer and winter, the contribution of NO3⁻-N 
to total DIN in river water declined from 88.07% (winter) 
to 77.56% (summer). In summer, dilution by high rainfall 
reduces absolute concentrations while the dominant sig -
nal shifts towards non-point (agricultural) sources, par -
tially masking point-source contributions. In winter, lower 
discharge concentrates the point-source signal; elevated 
NO3⁻-N at DT1, LM2 and L13, combined with elevated Cl⁻ 
and EC, indicates direct industrial and sanitary inputs.

Hydrochemical and isotopic constraints on NO3
- 
sources and transformation
Two independent diagnostics are used here to identify the 
processes controlling NO 3⁻ rather than its sources: the 
nitrogen enrichment slope (NES, Eq. 1), which relates log 
C(NO3⁻) to δ¹⁵N and δ¹⁸O, and the conservative tracers Cl⁻ 
and EC, whose covariation with NO₃⁻ distinguishes source 
mixing from in-situ removal. Together they constrain the 
extent of denitrification and provide the process framework 
within which the source apportionment in Section “ SIAR-
based nitrate source apportionment” is interpreted.
Nitrogen enrichment slope: log C(NO3
-) versus δ15N and δ18O
At the SW and IW outlets the NES was positive, log 
C(NO3
−) increasing with δ 15N (Fig. 6a, b). This is the sig -
nature expected where manure- and sewage-derived NO 3
− 
accumulates along the flow path, because these sources carry 
high δ15N, generally + 10 to + 25‰ (Serna et al. 2010). At the 
OT outlets the slope was negative. The most probable con -
trol is the low δ15N of synthetic fertilisers (urea, ammonium 
salts and nitrate fertilisers), which is close to the atmospheric 
value of 0‰ and generally ranges from − 6 to + 6‰: where 
fertiliser is applied in excess of plant uptake and soil fixa -
tion, runoff and leaching deliver high NO 3
− concentrations 
at low δ15N (Šturm et al. 2011). The contrast in the sign of 
the NES between SW/IW and OT therefore separates the two 
dominant source families independently of, and prior to, the 
mixing model. The fitted relationships and their coefficients 
of determination are shown in Fig. 6.
The regressions against δ18O were likewise positive at SW 
and IW (Fig. 6c, d). Because nitrification incorporates oxygen 
from ambient water, NO3
− produced in situ inherits a δ 18O 
close to that of the local water, so a positive log C(NO 3
−)-
δ18O relationship indicates that NO3
− at these outlets is largely 
nitrification-derived while the contributing sources (domes-
tic, industrial and soil mineralisation) retain distinguishable 
δ18O signatures (Buchwald et al. 2012). The weaker relation-
ship at OT is consistent with a larger, isotopically distinct 
fertiliser contribution. Critically, neither regression shows 
the joint enrichment of δ15N and δ18O with falling NO3
− that 
would indicate denitrification; that possibility is evaluated 
quantitatively in Section “Extent of denitrification”.

Extent of denitrification
The δ18O-NO3
− values of all samples fall within the range 
produced by nitrification, confirming that nitrification, 
rather than direct input of oxidised nitrate, establishes the 
NO3
− pool in both compartments. Where denitrification does 
occur, nitrogen and oxygen isotopes fractionate according to 
a Rayleigh model, so δ15N and δ18O both increase as NO 3
− 
decreases, with an enrichment ratio ε N/εO of about 1.3–2.1. 
Most river-water samples plotted to the left of the εN/εO = 2.1 
line and showed no coupled increase in δ15N and δ18O, that 
is, no resolvable denitrification. In groundwater, δ 15N and 
δ18O were inversely related to NO 3
−-N with an enrichment 
ratio of about 1.38, which does indicate denitrification (Plata 
et al. 2025); the δ values nevertheless remained low and the 
samples were only suboxic (DO < 2 mg/L), so the extent 
of removal was limited. This bound is consistent with the 
loss of the NO 3
−-Cl− relationship in groundwater (Section 
“Conservative tracers (Cl − and EC): mixing versus in-situ 
removal”) and means that the proportions derived in Section 
“SIAR-based nitrate source apportionment” can be treated as 
near-conservative mixtures, with the caveat that the ground-
water M&S contribution may be marginally underestimated.

Conclusion
This study combined dual nitrate isotopes, hydrochem -
istry, and SIAR modeling to evaluate nitrate sources and 
transformations in a coupled karst river-groundwater sys -
tem. Groundwater TN exceeded river-water TN in summer, 
whereas river-water TN was higher in winter. Ground -
water near the metalworking and new-materials park and 
tributaries near the grain and food-processing park con -
tained localized NO 3⁻-N hotspots. In Fig. 7, the strongest 
NO3⁻-EC relationship occurred at SW outlets (R² = 0.593), 
followed by IW outlets (R² = 0.464); the NO 3⁻-Cl⁻ rela -
tionship was also strongest for SW (R² = 0.483). Mea -
sured δ18O-NO3⁻ values fell within the nitrification-derived 
range, and an εN/εO slope of approximately 1.38 suggested 
limited groundwater denitrification. The three-source SIAR 
model identified M&S as the largest modeled summer 
source, contributing 49% at the basin scale. Because nitrate 
isotopes were measured only in summer, these source pro -
portions should not be interpreted as seasonal estimates. 
Management should prioritize manure and sewage control 
in karst-sensitive zones, localized industrial groundwater 
hotspots, and winter monitoring of tributaries affected by 
food-processing activities.
