Abstract
Gaps in communication between inpatient and outpatient psychiatric providers can lead to poor care
continuity, negatively impacting patient outcomes such as increased readmission rates and worsened
mental health symptoms. This quality improvement project aimed to address these communication gaps by
implementing a structured discharge summary communication process between inpatient psychiatric
services at a large community-based academic medical center and outpatient psychiatric providers at a local
clinic. A pre- and post-intervention survey design was used to assess provider perceptions regarding the
timeliness, completeness, and usefulness of discharge summaries. Between January and March 2025, 210
discharge summaries were faxed from the inpatient unit to outpatient facilities. Providers at the outpatient
clinic reported improvements in the timeliness of discharge summaries, with 60% expressing satisfaction
with the speed of delivery. However, significant challenges remained, including insufficient medication
detail and low confidence in managing post-discharge medication regimens. Despite these challenges, 100%
of respondents agreed that receiving discharge summaries in advance improved patient care. The results
suggest that structured discharge communication can improve continuity of care and provider confidence,
but further improvements are needed in the completeness of medication details. Standardizing discharge
summaries and refining the communication process could enhance patient safety, reduce readmission rates,
and improve medication management post-discharge. Future efforts should focus on improving discharge
documentation, addressing gaps in medication information, and extending this process to other psychiatric
units and community-based healthcare settings.

Introduction
Potential gaps in communication between inpatient and outpatient providers are especially concerning
given the prevalence of serious psychiatric disorders; approximately one in five U.S. adults live with a
mental illness 
[1]
, highlighting the widespread impact of poor care continuity on both individual patients
and the broader healthcare system. Inpatient psychiatric hospitalization represents a critical juncture in the
care of individuals with mental health conditions, yet it is often followed by significant challenges in
transitioning patients to outpatient care. In a review of psychiatric inpatient records for 189 Medicaid
recipients, Smith et al. 
[2]
 found that only 44% to 62% of patients attended an outpatient mental health
appointment within seven days of discharge, depending on whether they had been engaged in outpatient
care before admission. Research shows that poor continuity of care during this transition contributes to
negative outcomes, such as higher rates of readmission, medication errors, and worsened mental health
symptoms. Additionally, in a general psychiatric outpatient population rather than a specifically post-
discharge cohort, non-attendance at outpatient appointments has been associated with a greater burden of
disease on patient-reported and subjective outcomes, including poorer social functioning and increased
symptom severity 
[3]
. Smith et al. also found, in that same review of medical records from two inpatient
psychiatric hospitals, that communication between inpatient staff and outpatient providers was associated
with increased odds of attending a timely outpatient follow-up appointment after discharge, particularly
among patients who had not received outpatient care in the 30 days before admission 
[2]
.
Currently, communication of discharge information between inpatient psychiatric providers at Lakeland
Regional Health (LRH) and outpatient psychiatric providers at Peace River Center (PRC), both located in
Florida, USA, is limited to prescribed discharge medications. Patients are mostly advised to follow up with
outpatient psychiatry within three to five days of discharge. Physician-written discharge summaries, which
include crucial information about the hospital course, such as medication changes, administration dates for
long-acting injectables, and relevant treatment updates, are not routinely faxed upon discharge. This lack of
timely communication before outpatient follow-up appointments and difficulty in the outpatient clinic
identifying patient contact information have been identified as a significant barrier to effective outpatient

The gap in communication remains a significant unaddressed issue, with outpatient providers at PRC

can lead to exacerbations of symptoms and unnecessary readmissions. This lack of coordination can have

patient outcomes and reducing the burden of psychiatric readmissions.
This quality improvement project aims to address this critical gap in care coordination by implementing a
structured discharge summary communication process. The primary purpose is to improve the timeliness
and completeness of discharge information shared between community-based inpatient psychiatric services
and outpatient psychiatric providers. The discharge summaries in this intervention were completed by
providers on the day of patient discharge, compared to the institutional baseline delay of up to 24 hours. By
enhancing the structural content of the summaries and the workflow, this project seeks to ensure that
outpatient providers have the necessary discharge information to enhance provider perception and optimize
communication processes.
Materials And Methods
Project design
This quality improvement project employed a pre- and post-intervention survey design adapted from real-
world provider feedback to evaluate whether a structured discharge summary communication process could
improve care coordination between inpatient psychiatric patients at a large community-based academic
medical center in Central Florida, USA, and outpatient psychiatric providers at a single, local outpatient
facility within the community. The primary aim was to enhance continuity of care by improving the
timeliness and completeness of discharge information shared after inpatient psychiatric hospitalization.
This quality improvement study was exempt from formal IRB review. 
Setting
The project was conducted at the inpatient psychiatric unit of a large community-based academic medical
center and at a single, community-based outpatient psychiatric clinic located in Central Florida, USA. The
inpatient unit provides acute stabilization to a broad psychiatric patient population, while the outpatient
facility delivers follow-up care with a focus on uninsured or underinsured individuals.
Data collection
Two investigator-developed 10-question surveys (see Appendix A) were distributed using the SurveyMonkey
internet platform (SurveyMonkey Inc., San Mateo, CA, USA). Each survey question consisted of 5-point Likert
scale multiple-choice responses. These surveys assessed provider perceptions regarding notification of
patient hospitalizations, the effectiveness of current communication workflow, confidence in medication
knowledge after discharge, and perceived barriers to continuity of care. Surveys were disseminated via email
with coordination from the outpatient provider manager and were completed electronically. The pre-
intervention survey was distributed on January 13, 2025, and final responses were received on January 23,
2025. Eleven of 14 outpatient providers responded, and responses were submitted anonymously. Due to
limited participation in the initial survey, the post-intervention survey required respondents to identify
themselves by name to ensure completion tracking. The post-intervention survey was distributed on March
12, 2025, with final responses received by July 14, 2025. A total of five outpatient providers completed the
post-intervention survey.
Intervention
Between January 21, 2025, and March 7, 2025, the senior inpatient behavioral health program manager faxed
discharge summaries from all adult patients to the designated outpatient clinic on the business day
following a patient’s discharge. All adult patients were eligible regardless of psychiatric diagnoses, without
specific diagnostic exclusion or inclusion criteria. The referral site was determined by patient insurance

updated medication lists, recent long-acting injectable administration dates, and clinical details necessary
for outpatient treatment planning.
Evaluation of measures
Provider perceptions regarding timeliness, accuracy, and usefulness of discharge summaries were compared
before and after the intervention. Outcomes of interest included provider satisfaction with communication
workflow, provider confidence in managing medication regimens after hospitalization, and perceived
impact on continuity of patient care. Due to the anonymity of pre-intervention responses, individual
respondents could not be matched between survey phases. Therefore, analysis is limited to descriptive
statistics, including frequency counts and percentage distributions of responses from the two independent
groups.
Results
Intervention reach and results
During the intervention period from January 21, 2025, through March 7, 2025, the inpatient behavioral
health unit faxed 210 discharge summaries to outpatient psychiatric facilities. Of these transmissions, 202
(96%) were faxed to the partner clinic for insurance-directed follow-up referrals, while only eight (4%)
discharge summaries were sent to the hospital’s internal network outpatient psychiatry offices. Because so
few patients were eligible for follow-up at the internal outpatient clinics, these providers did not
meaningfully engage with the intervention, limiting post-intervention evaluation to partner providers. A
total of 11 outpatient providers completed the pre-intervention survey, while five partner providers
completed the post-intervention survey. Because pre-intervention surveys were anonymous and
respondents could not be matched to post-intervention responses, analysis was restricted to descriptive
statistics comparing grouped response trends.

Before the intervention, a significant portion of respondents expressed concerns about the communication
process between inpatient and outpatient providers. A majority of providers (8/11, 73%) reported that they
were always or usually notified about patient hospitalizations. However, confidence in the current handoff
process was low, with 8/11 respondents (73%) indicating they were either not confident or neutral about the
current system. The effectiveness of the existing communication methods, such as discharge summaries, was
rated as 'somewhat effective' by 6/11 respondents (55%), with a substantial portion also deeming them
'somewhat ineffective' (Table

Post-intervention provider perceptions
After the intervention, PRC providers reported mixed results across communication and care management.
Two of five respondents (40%) were satisfied with the timeliness of receiving discharge summaries, one
(20%) was neutral, and two (40%) were dissatisfied or very dissatisfied. Confidence in medication knowledge
remained a challenge, with 3/5 respondents (60%) reporting that they were not so confident or not confident
at all in their understanding of medication changes post-discharge (Table 
2
).
Despite these concerns, all five respondents (5/5, 100%) agreed or strongly agreed that receiving discharge
summaries before follow-up appointments improved their ability to manage patient care. The completeness
of discharge summaries, however, remained a concern, with only 2/5 providers (40%) reporting that
summaries were usually complete and accurate, 2/5 (40%) reporting that they were sometimes complete, and
1/5 (20%) reporting that they were never complete. Separately, 4/5 providers (80%) rated the detail provided
on medication adjustments as insufficient or not at all sufficient. When asked about the impact on
medication errors, 3/5 providers (60%) agreed or strongly agreed that the intervention reduced the risk of
errors. Communication with inpatient providers showed little change, with only 2/5 respondents (40%)
reporting that communication had somewhat improved and 3/5 (60%) reporting no change at all. Access to
discharge summaries was rated easy by 2/5 respondents (40%), neutral by 1/5 (20%), and difficult or very
difficult by 2/5 (40%), though this still left some providers struggling with timely access. Finally, when asked
if they would recommend continuing the faxing process, 3/5 respondents (60%) were likely or very likely to
endorse continuing the intervention, while 1/5 (20%) was neutral and 1/5 (20%) was very unlikely to do so
(see Table

Summary
The intervention increased the timely transfer of discharge summaries to outpatient providers, and the five
PRC providers who completed the post-intervention survey perceived it as beneficial to patient care.
Providers agreed that receiving discharge information in advance improves their ability to manage clinical
needs; however, limitations in medication detail and low confidence in medication knowledge remained
significant and largely unchanged challenges, with 4/5 respondents (80%) rating the medication-adjustment
detail as insufficient and 3/5 (60%) remaining not confident in their knowledge of post-discharge
medication changes. These findings underscore the importance of refining discharge documentation and
strengthening communication workflows to sustain and expand improvements in continuity of care.
Discussion
This quality improvement project sought to improve the timeliness and completeness of discharge
information shared between inpatient psychiatric providers at LRH and outpatient psychiatric providers at
PRC to enhance continuity of care. The findings suggest that the implementation of a structured discharge
summary communication process was associated with improved provider-reported perceptions of care
coordination, although most respondents reported no change in day-to-day communication with the
inpatient team, which is consistent with research showing the importance of timely and accurate discharge
summaries in preventing readmissions and medication errors. Previous studies have highlighted the critical
role of communication between inpatient and outpatient providers, particularly for patients with psychiatric
conditions. Improved relational continuity of care for patients with serious mental illness could help reduce
premature deaths and suicides, decrease emergency department visits, and enhance overall quality of life
[7]
.
The pre- and post-intervention survey results from this project indicated that providers at PRC experienced
some improvements in their ability to manage care when discharge summaries were received before follow-
up appointments. As seen in the post-intervention survey, all five respondents (100%) agreed that receiving
discharge summaries in advance improved patient care, although this unanimity rests on a denominator of
only five providers. These findings align with those of Smith et al. 
[2]
, who demonstrated that better
communication between inpatient and outpatient providers increased the likelihood of patients attending
outpatient follow-up appointments after discharge. Their study also highlighted that communication with
outpatient providers may be especially important for patients who were not engaged in outpatient care prior
to admission 
[2]
. This improvement could be attributed to the enhanced communication process established
by the intervention, which facilitated better-informed care decisions for patients post-discharge.
Despite the positive trends, the results also revealed persistent challenges in the areas of medication detail
and provider confidence in managing post-discharge medication regimens. Most PRC providers (4/5, 80%)

(60%) were not confident in their medication knowledge after receiving the summaries. Taken together,
these two findings represent the most important unresolved problem identified by this project: the
intervention improved the transmission of discharge summaries without demonstrably improving the
adequacy of their medication content. This lack of confidence in medication adjustments is particularly
concerning given the heightened risks associated with psychiatric medication management following
discharge. This leads to further consideration of creating standardization amongst the physician-written
summaries to ensure completeness 
[8]
.
A study by Stockdale et al. 
[9]
 found that organizational barriers, such as a lack of financial resources, a lack
of integrated information technology, and organizational cultures not conducive to change, hinder
communication between inpatient and outpatient mental health clinicians. Other work has demonstrated
that the content quality of discharge summaries improves following a structured curriculum and formal
teaching for the clinicians who write them 
[10]
. Standardized, template-driven discharge summaries
embedded in the electronic health record have likewise been implemented at the institutional level, with
improved content completeness and greater satisfaction among the providers receiving them 
[11]
. Thus,
further work is needed to ensure that discharge summaries include comprehensive and actionable
information on medication adjustments, especially for patients who may require complex pharmacological
interventions 
[12,13]
.
Additionally, the intervention improved the timeliness with which discharge summaries reached PRC,
although only 2/5 providers (40%) reported being satisfied with the speed of delivery, reinforcing the notion
that timely communication is essential for successful patient transitions from inpatient to outpatient care.
However, further efforts should be made to standardize and refine the discharge documentation. In this
study, although discharge summaries were transmitted promptly, gaps in the details regarding medication
adjustments may still pose a risk to patient safety and reduce the effectiveness of the intervention, since
unintended medication discrepancies at discharge are a well-described source of post-discharge adverse
drug events 
[12,13]
. It is essential to ensure that all relevant clinical information is conveyed in a clear,
structured format to empower outpatient providers to make informed treatment decisions.
Limitations
There are several limitations to this quality improvement project that should be considered when
interpreting the results. First, patient insurance status significantly influenced outpatient referral patterns,
resulting in the exclusion of LRH outpatient psychiatric providers from the post-intervention survey
analysis due to insufficient patient volume. Discharged patients were referred for follow-up based on
insurance coverage, and because LRH outpatient psychiatry accepts only specific insurance plans, the vast
majority of referrals were directed to PRC instead. As a result, most LRH outpatient providers did not directly
experience the communication intervention and therefore could not meaningfully evaluate its impact.
Additionally, the pre-intervention survey was conducted anonymously, preventing the ability to separate
LRH provider responses from PRC provider responses and limiting the internal validity of the baseline data.
Anonymity also precluded respondent-level comparison of pre- and post-intervention responses, reducing
the ability to assess whether changes in satisfaction or confidence occurred at the individual level. The
change of anonymity may have impacted response patterns and provider satisfaction with the intervention.
Several challenges existed with survey design and provider engagement. The surveys were not piloted, nor
were content validity or reliability assessed. The survey questions were varied in structure rather than
standardized using validated scaling measures, restricting the ability to conduct inferential statistical
analysis. Due to these limitations, results were analyzed using descriptive statistics only. Provider
participation also impacted the robustness of findings. Only 11 of 14 eligible providers completed the pre-
intervention survey. Follow-up participation also initially remained low, with only two of five PRC providers
completing the post-intervention survey within the first month. Multiple reminders were required to secure
responses, raising the possibility that survey fatigue or frustration may have contributed to negatively
skewed feedback. The post-intervention denominator of five providers also means that each respondent
represents 20% of the sample, so a change in a single response produces a large apparent shift in the
reported percentages and may lead to response bias.
Lastly, resource limitations also affected survey quality. The free version of the SurveyMonkey platform
restricted surveys to 10 multiple-choice questions, eliminating the opportunity to include open-ended
questions that could have provided richer qualitative insights into communication challenges or suggestions
for workflow improvement. Also, the study evaluated provider perceptions only; objective clinical outcomes
such as documented medication-related errors, readmission rates, or follow-up adherence were not
measured. The relatively short intervention period further limited the ability to assess sustained
improvements in communication processes or patient safety outcomes. Several survey items also combined
qualitatively different Likert categories, such as 'satisfied or neutral' and 'sometimes or usually complete,'
which limits the interpretability of those results and may overstate the proportion of favorable responses.
These limitations collectively reduce generalizability and constrain the strength of conclusions that can be
drawn regarding intervention effectiveness. Future efforts should incorporate validated measurement tools,

improved participant engagement strategies, and objective outcome data over a longer time frame to better
assess the impact of communication enhancements on continuity of psychiatric care.
Conclusions
This quality improvement project suggests that implementing a structured discharge summary
communication process between inpatient and outpatient psychiatric providers can improve provider-
perceived care coordination. Perceived communication with the inpatient team did not improve for most
respondents, and no objective clinical outcomes were measured, so the effect on patient care remains
unknown. Providers at PRC reported benefits from receiving discharge summaries in advance, particularly
for continuity of care and clinical decision-making. Confidence in post-discharge medication changes did
not improve, however, and most post-intervention respondents continued to rate the medication-
adjustment detail in discharge summaries as insufficient. The intervention therefore improved the
transmission of discharge summaries without demonstrably improving their medication content. However,
challenges remain regarding the completeness of medication information, which must be addressed to fully
realize the potential benefits of improved discharge communication. Future efforts should focus on refining
discharge documentation, ensuring that detailed medication changes are included, and exploring additional
methods to enhance provider confidence in managing post-discharge care. Expanding this process to other
psychiatric units and healthcare settings, while addressing the gaps identified in this study, could detail a
methodology that can be applied to broader systems.
