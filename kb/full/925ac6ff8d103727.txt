EXPANSION OF THE GROUNDS 
FOR LOSS OF UKRAINIAN CITIZENSHIP 
IN THE SPHERE OF NATIONAL SECURITY: 
THE QUALITY OF LAW AND THE LIMITS 
OF STATE DISCRETION
DOI: https://doi.org/10.61345/1339-7915.2026.2 .29
Annotation. This article examines the expansion of the statutory grounds for loss of Ukrainian 
citizenship introduced by Law of Ukraine No. 4502-IX and Presidential Decree No. 110/2026, 
adopted in response to the security challenges posed by the armed aggression of the Russian 
Federation against Ukraine. Drawing on the doctrine of the three-part test for permissible 
restrictions of human rights (legality, legitimate aim, and necessity/proportionality) as developed 
in international human rights law and the case law of the European Court of Human Rights, the 
article evaluates whether the newly introduced mechanisms for the termination of citizenship 
meet the requirements of legal certainty and non-arbitrariness. Particular attention is paid to 
the ambiguity of the terms “aggressor state” and “occupying state,” the excessively broad 
formulation of “participation in armed aggression,” the relationship between citizenship loss 
and the principle of non bis in idem, and the absence of adequate procedural safeguards, 
including the right to be heard, in the administrative procedure established for the termination 
of citizenship. The article concludes that while the expansion of these grounds pursues the 
legitimate aim of protecting national security and state sovereignty, several provisions of Article 
19 of the Law of Ukraine “On Citizenship of Ukraine” fail to ensure an adequate level of legal 
certainty, and it recommends that the terminology of the law be clarified and that appropriate 
procedural guarantees be introduced.
Key words: citizenship, loss of citizenship, national security, rule of law, legal certainty, 
proportionality, three-part test, European Court of Human Rights, right to be heard, non bis in 
idem.
1. Problem statement. 
Contemporary security challenges, above all the armed aggression of the Russian Federation against 
Ukraine, have brought to the fore the question of how the institution of citizenship relates to the 
interests of national security. The full-scale war has given rise to various forms of cooperation by 
individual citizens with the aggressor state, manifested in collaborationist activity, participation 
in the armed formations of the enemy, and similar conduct. Under these conditions, the legislator 
increasingly resorts to the instruments of loss or deprivation of citizenship as one of the means of 
responding to such threats. At the same time, the expansion of the grounds for loss of citizenship 
raises the question of whether such legislative changes comply with the principles of the rule of 
law and international standards for restricting human rights. The institution of citizenship is directly 
linked to the exercise of fundamental rights and freedoms, and its termination must therefore satisfy 
the requirements of legality, legal certainty, proportionality, and due process of law. In this context, 
it is particularly important to assess the amendments to Ukraine’s citizenship legislation adopted 
in 2025, which substantially expanded the list of grounds for loss of citizenship in connection with 
cooperation with the aggressor state or participation in the activities of occupation authorities. 
Given Ukraine’s international legal obligations and its course toward European integration, it is 
necessary to determine to what extent the newly introduced mechanisms comply with the criteria 
for the permissible restriction of human rights formulated in international law and in the case law of 
the European Court of Human Rights, and whether they ensure a proper balance between the needs 
of national security and the guarantees of human rights protection.

2. Analysis of recent research and publications. 
Certain aspects of the problem of deprivation and loss of citizenship have been examined by 
Ukrainian and foreign scholars, including V. Lipkan, Ya.  Polianska, N. Hrytsenko, I. Sofinska, 
O. Sadovska, Rainer Bauböck, Audrey Macklin, Matthew  J. Gibney, Laura van Waas, Joseph Carens, 
Christian Joppke, Rogers Brubaker, Ayelet Shachar, Peter  J. Spiro, Patti Tamara Lenard, and others. The 
scholarly discussion on the loss (deprivation) of citizenship generally centers on several key issues: 
the limits of state sovereignty in the sphere of citizenship, the balance between national security 
and human rights, and the transformation of the institution of citizenship under the influence of 
contemporary security challenges. At the same time, the problem of expanding the grounds for 
loss of citizenship in connection with cooperation with the aggressor state remains insufficiently 
researched in domestic legal scholarship, which calls for further scientific study.
3. The article aims to analyze the novelties of Ukrainian legislation concerning the expansion of 
the grounds for loss of citizenship, to assess their compliance with international standards, and to 
identify the principal legal problems arising from their application under current conditions.
4. Presentation of the research material. 
The adoption of Law of Ukraine No. 4502-IX “On Amendments to Certain Laws of Ukraine Concerning 
the Exercise of the Right to Acquire and Retain Ukrainian Citizenship, ” which entered into force on 
16 January 2026, marked a new stage in the development of the legal regulation of the institution 
of citizenship. The purpose of the law was to update citizenship legislation in view of the need to 
ensure the national security and national interests of Ukraine [1].
One of the key novelties of this legislative act was the revision of Article 19, “Grounds for Loss of 
Citizenship of Ukraine, ” of the Law of Ukraine “On Citizenship of Ukraine” (hereinafter, the Law), which 
expanded the list of legal facts that may give rise to the termination of a person’s legal bond with 
the state. Some of these grounds are not new to Ukrainian legislation: the voluntary acquisition of 
the citizenship of another state and voluntary entry into the military service of a foreign state had 
already been recognized as grounds for loss of citizenship. The new version of the Law narrowed 
their application, limiting it to states having the status of an aggressor state or an occupying state. 
By contrast, the provisions on loss of citizenship in connection with the commission of certain crimes 
against the foundations of national security, as well as participation in armed aggression or in an 
occupation administration, are fundamentally new to Ukrainian legislation [2].
These legislative novelties raise a number of scholarly and practical questions concerning the limits 
of restricting the right to citizenship, the balance between the public interests of the state and the 
guarantees of human rights, and the compliance of such mechanisms with international standards. 
In this connection, a comprehensive scholarly analysis of the new grounds for loss of citizenship and 
the limits of their application under conditions of ongoing armed aggression acquires particular 
importance.
Attitudes toward the institution of deprivation of citizenship in contemporary scholarship are far 
from uniform. Representatives of the liberal school of international law and citizenship theory 
generally emphasize the impermissibility of using deprivation of citizenship as an instrument of 
state policy. In particular, A. Macklin and R. Bauböck stress the undesirability of depriving a person 
of citizenship, even as a response to threats to national security, since deprivation effectively 
undermines the stability of a citizen’s legal status and contradicts the fundamental character of 
citizenship as an institution of political membership [4, p. 172; 5, p. 47]. Another group of researchers, 
by contrast, draws attention to the growing role of national security considerations in contemporary 
citizenship policy and admits the possibility of depriving a person of citizenship in exceptional 
cases connected with terrorism or serious crimes against the state [6, p. 173; 7, p. 728; 8, p. 150]. In 
this context, the position of Matthew J. Gibney deserves attention: he argues that the institution 
of deprivation of citizenship is not incompatible with the liberal European conception of human 
rights, provided that its application is grounded in clear legal norms that limit state discretion and

ensure adequate guarantees for the protection of individual rights [9, p. 652]. Accordingly, what 
matters is not the mere possibility of depriving a person of citizenship, but compliance with the legal 
standards governing its application. A similar approach is reflected in the position of Fionnuala Ní 
Aoláin, UN Special Rapporteur on the promotion and protection of human rights while countering 
terrorism, who notes that international law does not prohibit deprivation of citizenship as such, but 
only its arbitrary application. From this perspective, such deprivation is permissible provided that 
it complies with domestic and international law, pursues a legitimate aim, respects the principle 
of proportionality, and is accompanied by adequate procedural guarantees [10, pp. 9–10]. These 
requirements correspond to the well-established concept, in both international and national law, of 
the three-part test, according to which state interference with human rights is permissible only if it 
is prescribed by law, pursues a legitimate aim, and is necessary and proportionate in a democratic 
society.
The question thus arises as to what extent the mechanisms for the termination of citizenship 
introduced by Law No. 4502-IX and the Decree of 11 February 2026 satisfy the requirements of the 
three-part test.
The legality criterion. The legality criterion requires that interference with a person’s legal status 
be based on clear, foreseeable, and unambiguous legal norms that limit the discretion of public 
authorities. This follows from the principle of legal certainty, according to which a law must be 
formulated with sufficient precision to enable a person to foresee the legal consequences of their 
conduct, and the discretion of the authorities must not amount to unlimited power that creates a risk 
of arbitrary interference with human rights [11, pp. 29–30].
In assessing the extent to which the introduced amendments comply with the legality criterion, 
several problematic aspects deserve attention.
The vagueness of the concepts of “aggressor state” and “occupying state. ” These concepts lack a 
clear legal definition. Article 19 of the Law merely states that the relevant status is to be established 
by the Verkhovna Rada of Ukraine, without specifying the form of the legal act or the procedure for 
its adoption. Nor does the Presidential Decree governing the procedure for citizenship proceedings 
provide such detail [13]. The term “aggressor state” is used in certain legislative acts (the Law of 
Ukraine “On Cinematography, ” the Law of Ukraine “On Media, ” and the Resolution of the National 
Bank of Ukraine “On Approval of the Regulation on Bank Licensing”) in different meanings, and only 
within the scope of each respective act. The term “occupying state” is likewise used in the Law of 
Ukraine “On Ensuring the Rights and Freedoms of Citizens and the Legal Regime on the Temporarily 
Occupied Territory of Ukraine” without any elaboration of its content. This points to a gap in the legal 
regulation caused by the absence of a basic law that would comprehensively define the procedure for 
granting such status, the form of the relevant act, and its legal consequences, which in practice risks 
divergent formulations being applied by courts and public authorities. The absence of a normative 
definition of the term “aggressor state” has likewise been noted by government representatives [14].
The use of Resolution of the Verkhovna Rada of Ukraine No. 129-VIII, which recognized the 
Russian Federation as an aggressor state [13, 14], as the normative basis for applying the relevant 
consequences, is also debatable: a parliamentary resolution is traditionally regarded as an act of 
a political-legal character that may lack the attributes of a normative act, and the question of the 
proper legal basis for such a decision therefore remains open.
The lack of clear criteria for determining that a state is engaged in armed conflict with Ukraine or 
is contributing to aggression. Paragraph 4 of Article 19 of the Law provides for loss of citizenship for 
“participation in armed aggression against Ukraine on the part of a state … that is engaged in armed 
conflict with Ukraine or has contributed (is contributing) to the commission of armed aggression 
against Ukraine and/or participation in the occupation administration of the Russian Federation on 
the temporarily occupied territory of Ukraine. ” The categories “a state engaged in armed conflict with 
Ukraine” and “a state that has contributed (is contributing) to the commission of armed aggression” 
lack a universal normative definition, and the Law does not establish a procedure for their official 
determination. Although the concept of “armed aggression” is enshrined in Article 1 of the Law of 
Ukraine “On Defense of Ukraine, ” there is no mechanism for determining whether a particular state 
falls within these categories: in practice, this task is entrusted to law enforcement, intelligence, and 
counterintelligence bodies, which, under paragraph 113 of the Decree of 11 February 2026, establish 
the facts of such cooperation.

In the absence of clear statutory criteria, the limits of application of this norm will largely be determined 
by administrative practice, creating a risk of excessive executive discretion. As the Venice Commission 
has observed, “it would be contrary to the rule of law for the legal discretion granted to the executive to 
be expressed in terms of an unfettered power” [11, p. 31]. Similar concerns regarding the vagueness of 
the grounds for deprivation of citizenship have been expressed by members of a UN expert group [15].
The excessive breadth of the concept of “participation in armed aggression. ” The preceding 
paragraphs of Article 19 of the Law already contain specific grounds for loss of citizenship, in 
particular contractual military service or conviction for crimes against the foundations of national 
security under Articles 111, 111-1, and 111-2 of the Criminal Code of Ukraine (paragraph 3 of Article 
19). The legislator therefore evidently intended to capture other forms of “participation, ” but the 
absence of further specification makes it impossible to define the content of this concept with 
precision. As a result, the formula “participation in armed aggression” may cover a wide range of 
conduct, from direct participation in hostilities to informational, organizational, or material support, 
part of which is already covered by other norms and is established, depending on the case, either by 
a court or by law enforcement bodies. This leaves considerable room for an expansive interpretation 
of the norm. As the European Court of Human Rights has emphasized, a law must be formulated 
with sufficient precision to enable a person to foresee the consequences of their conduct (see, e.g., 
Sunday Times v. United Kingdom, 1979), so that the use of several evaluative categories without 
normative specification creates a risk of the expansive interpretation of this ground and calls into 
question its compliance with the principle of legal certainty.
The principle of non bis in idem. Particular attention should be paid to the ground for loss of 
citizenship under paragraph 3 of Article 19 of the Law, which is linked to the entry into force of a 
court’s judgment of conviction. Since the Criminal Code of Ukraine does not provide for deprivation 
of citizenship as a form of criminal punishment, scholars have expressed the view that applying this 
measure after conviction may create a situation of a factual double response by the state to the same 
offense [17, p. 17]. Analyzing the provisions of the draft law, a coalition of human rights organizations 
concluded that “in effect, this establishes an additional punishment for a crime already provided for 
under the Criminal Code of Ukraine” [16].
At the same time, these conclusions are not beyond dispute. Under current legislation, termination of 
citizenship is applied within the framework of a separate administrative procedure of an independent 
public-law character, and is not an automatic consequence of a conviction. In Ghoumid and Others 
v. France, the ECtHR found it permissible to deprive of citizenship persons convicted of terrorist 
offenses, emphasizing that the measure in question was applied within a separate procedure, was not 
automatic in character, and, by its legal nature, did not constitute a criminal sanction, and therefore 
did not in itself indicate a violation of the principle of non bis in idem [18]. Accordingly, provided 
that the criteria established by the ECtHR are observed, the Ukrainian state may lawfully rely on this 
ground for the termination of citizenship.
Procedural safeguards and the right to be heard. One of the key requirements of the legality 
criterion is that normative acts must comply with human rights guarantees, among which an 
important place is occupied by the right of a person to state their position (the right to be heard).
International law and legal doctrine have developed the approach that procedures for the termination 
or deprivation of citizenship must be accompanied by adequate procedural safeguards, in particular 
notification of the person of the initiation of the procedure and affording them the opportunity to be 
heard [19]. The absence of such safeguards creates a risk of arbitrary state interference with a person’s 
status as a member of the political community [17, p. 8; 20, p. 95]. Accordingly, a proper procedure 
must include, at a minimum, notification of the person, an opportunity to provide explanations, and 
access to an effective mechanism for appealing the decision.
Under the Decree of 11 February 2026, the procedure for loss of citizenship on the grounds set out 
in paragraphs 3–5 of Article 19 of the Law has a multi-stage administrative character: authorized 
state bodies establish the existence of the relevant circumstances, after which the materials are 
transmitted to the territorial bodies of the State Migration Service, which verify the information and 
prepare a submission to the Commission on Citizenship Affairs under the President of Ukraine. The 
final decision is taken by the President by way of decree. This procedure indicates the predominantly 
administrative character of the process and the limited participation of the individual concerned, 
which manifests itself in three respects.

First, the current procedure does not provide for mandatory notification of the person at the stage 
of establishing the grounds for loss of citizenship, which is at odds with the position of UN experts, 
according to which the state must notify a person in advance of its intention to deprive them of 
citizenship so that they may defend their rights [15, p. 12]. Second, the procedure does not provide 
for the mandatory taking of explanations or other evidence from the individual: the decision is 
formed on the basis of materials collected by state bodies, without any guaranteed opportunity for 
the individual to participate in their assessment, which is at odds with the right to present arguments 
in one’s own defense before a decision is taken [15, p. 12]. Third, the final decision is taken by the 
President on the basis of the Commission’s submission, and judicial review is in practice available 
only after the decision has been taken, which diminishes the effectiveness of procedural safeguards 
at the stage when the decision itself is being formed.
In our view, the use of an administrative procedure without the involvement of the person concerned 
can be justified only where objective obstacles to their participation exist, in particular where the 
person is outside territory controlled by the state and cannot be summoned to provide explanations. 
A similar approach can be found in Australian law, where the administrative procedure applies only 
“where a person has committed the relevant conduct outside Australia, or where they have left 
Australia before they can be brought to trial” [12, p. 547]. By contrast, paragraph 130 of the Decree of 
11 February 2026 extends the administrative procedure under paragraphs 3 and 5 of Article 19 of the 
Law to citizens who are present on territory controlled by the Ukrainian authorities as well, without 
providing for any mandatory mechanism for involving the person even where there is a genuine 
possibility of obtaining explanations from them, which raises legitimate doubts as to the compliance 
of this approach with the rule of law and with the right to be heard. In this context, attention should 
be drawn to Al-Nashif and Others v. Bulgaria, in which the ECtHR held that “[e]ven where national 
security is at stake, the concepts of lawfulness and the rule of law in a democratic society require 
that measures affecting fundamental human rights must be subject to some form of adversarial 
proceedings before an independent body competent to review the reasons for the decision and 
relevant evidence, ” and that such a body “must be able to react in cases where invoking that concept 
has no reasonable basis in the facts or reveals an interpretation of ‘national security’ that is unlawful 
or contrary to common sense and arbitrary” [16].
5. Conclusions. 
The expansion of the grounds for loss of Ukrainian citizenship introduced by the legislative 
amendments of 2025–2026 generally reflects the trend toward strengthening the security dimension 
of state policy that is characteristic of many contemporary states. Under conditions of ongoing 
armed aggression, this transformation of the normative framework may be regarded as a legitimate 
instrument for protecting sovereignty and national security. At the same time, the application of this 
institution must take place within the bounds of the constitutional guarantees of human rights and 
must comply with international standards. The revised version of Article 19 of the Law of Ukraine 
“On Citizenship of Ukraine” contains a number of provisions that fail to ensure an adequate level 
of legal certainty – one of the key elements of the rule of law. In addition, a significant problem 
with the existing mechanism is the lack of procedural safeguards in the relevant proceedings, in 
particular the inability of the citizen to provide their own explanations or evidence before the final 
act is adopted. In light of this, it appears necessary to give more precise legislative definition to the 
terminology that is of key importance for the termination of citizenship, in particular to establish 
normatively the forms of participation in armed aggression. It is likewise important to introduce 
adequate procedural safeguards, including mandatory notification of the person concerned of the 
initiation of the relevant proceedings and their right to present their position on the merits of the 
case before a final decision is taken.
