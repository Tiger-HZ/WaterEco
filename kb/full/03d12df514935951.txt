ABSTRACT 
This study presents a comprehensive hydro -meteorological analysis of the 
Lake Naivasha Basin, a critical freshwater ecosystem in Kenya's Rift 
Valley, to evaluate the temporal and spatial variability of precipitation, 
temperature, evaporation, and lake water levels over multiple decades. The 
analysis integrates primary and secondary datasets obtained from the Kenya 
Meteorological Department, the Water Resources Authority, and satellite 
observations. Rigorous data quality control procedures were applied, 
including interpolation -based gap filling for missing precipitation and 
temperature records, outlier detection using visual inspection and Z -score 
statistics, and homogeneity assessment through the Standard Normal 
Homogeneity Test (SNHT) and Pettit's test. Spa tial datasets were 
standardised to the WGS 84 UTM Zone 36S coordinate reference system to 
ensure consistency in spatial analyses. Time-series analyses were conducted 
using monthly and annual aggregations to examine seasonal and interannual 
hydro-climatic variability. The results reveal pronounced seasonal and long-
term fluctuations in rainfall and temperature, indicating the influence of 
climate variability and ongoing climate change. A strong association was 
observed between precipitation variability and l ake-level dynamics, with 
hydrological responses further intensified by anthropogenic pressures such 
as irrigation expansion and land -use change. These findings enhance 
understanding of the hydro -meteorological processes governing the Lake 
Naivasha Basin and provide valuable evidence to support sustainable water 
resources management, climate change adaptation, and informed regional 
planning.

INTRODUCTION 
The Lake Naivasha Basin is one of Kenya’s 
important freshwater systems and is located within 
the eastern arm of the Great Rift Valley. The basin 
supports a wide range of ecological, economic, 
and social activities, including irrigated 
agriculture, horticul tural production, tourism, 
domestic water supply, fisheries, biodiversity 
conservation, and other livelihood activities. Lake 
Naivasha and its associated rivers and wetlands 
therefore constitute an important water -resource 
system whose sustainability is cl osely linked to 
both human well -being and ecosystem 
functioning. The basin is also recognised as an 
important ecological system, with its wetlands 
providing habitat for diverse aquatic and terrestrial 
species while supporting economically significant 
activities around the lake. Consequently, changes 
in the availability and quality of wat er within the 
basin have implications that extend beyond 
hydrological processes to include food production, 
livelihoods, ecosystem conservation, and regional 
economic development. 
The hydrological functioning of the Lake 
Naivasha Basin is strongly influenced by hydro -
meteorological conditions, particularly 
precipitation, temperature, evaporation, river 
inflows, and changes in lake levels. These 
variables determine the quantity, timi ng, and 
spatial distribution of water entering, being stored 
within, and leaving the basin. Variability in 
rainfall and river discharge can consequently 
produce substantial changes in lake storage and 
water availability. A long -term assessment by 
Odongo et al. (2015), covering approximately five 
decades of observations, demonstrated 
considerable hydro -climatological variability 
within the basin and identified a significant 
decline in observed lake volume. Importantly, the 
study found that observed changes in precipitation 
alone could not adequately explain the decline in 
lake volume and minimum river flows, suggesting 
that human -induced changes in land cover, 
infrastructure, groundwater interactions, and 
water abstraction may also contribute to changes 
in the basin’s hydrological regime. 
The importance of hydro -meteorological 
variability is further demonstrated by research 
examining extreme streamflow conditions in the 
basin. Kyambia and Mutua (2014) analysed 
streamflow records from 1959 to 2008 and 
reported significant changes in several indicators 
of extreme flows. Such changes have implications 
for water-resource planning because variations in 
the frequency and magnitude of high and low 
flows can affect wat er supply, agricultural 
production, ecosystem requirements, flood risk, 
and the reliability of water -resource 
infrastructure. These findings demonstrate that 
understanding both average conditions and 
extremes is essential for evaluating the changing 
characteristics of the basin’s water resources. 
In addition to climate and hydrological variability, 
the Lake Naivasha Basin has undergone 
substantial land -use and land -cover changes 
associated with agricultural expansion and 
intensification. These changes are particularly 
important because they can alt er 
evapotranspiration, runoff generation, 
groundwater recharge, and the overall basin water 
balance. Odongo et al. (2019) demonstrated that 
transitions between grassland and cropland 
influenced evapotranspiration in the Lake 
Naivasha Basin, with newly cult ivated areas

Water abstraction constitutes another important 
pressure on the basin’s water resources. Lake 
Naivasha supports intensive agricultural and 
industrial activities, while groundwater is also 
utilised to meet increasing water demands. 
Yihdego et al. (2016) demonstrated the 
importance of groundwater –surface-water

have important consequences for lake levels and

levels should be understood as the outcome of 
interactions among climatic inputs, surface -water

abstraction, land -use change, and other human

rainfall variability alone. 
These interacting pressures create significant 
challenges for sustainable water -resources 
management in the basin. Water demand from 
agriculture, domestic users, industry, tourism, and 
ecological systems must be accommodated within 
a freshwater system char acterised by substantial 
temporal variability in water availability. During 
periods of low rainfall and reduced river inflows, 
competition among water users may intensify, 
increasing pressure on water -allocation systems 
and potentially creating conflicts among user s. 
Conversely, periods of exceptionally high rainfall 
and river discharge may increase the risks of 
flooding, erosion, sediment transport, and damage 
to water infrastructure. Effective management 
therefore requires an understanding of both water 
availability and the factors that control its 
temporal and spatial variability. 
The relationship between hydro -meteorological 
variability and water governance is particularly 
important in the Lake Naivasha Basin. Water 
governance encompasses the institutions, policies, 
regulations, allocation mechanisms, monitoring 
systems, and stakeholder processes through which 
water resources are managed and shared. Ndungu 
et al. (2014) identified knowledge gaps and 
institutional challenges associated with integrated 
water resources management (IWRM) in the Lake 
Naivasha Basin and emphasised the importance of 
integrating scientific information with stakeholder 
knowledge to support sustainable 
governance. This suggests that effective 
governance cannot be separated from reliable 
information concerning the basin’s hydrological 
and climatic conditions. 
A key challenge is that water -resource 
management systems may be influenced by 
assumptions based on historical patterns of water 
availability. However, increasing hydro -
meteorological variability and uncertainty can 
make such assumptions less reliable. Cha nges in 
rainfall seasonality, temperature, evaporation, 
streamflow, and lake levels may alter both water 
supply and demand. Higher temperatures, for 
example, can increase evaporative losses and 
water requirements for agriculture, while changes 
in rainfall intensity and seasonality can modify 
runoff and river inflows. Consequently, water -
resource planning based exclusively on historical 
averages may not adequately account for 
emerging variability and changing hydrological 
conditions. 
The available evidence indicates that the Lake 
Naivasha Basin requires an integrated assessment 
of climatic, hydrological, land -use, and 
anthropogenic processes. Odongo et al. (2015) 
found that precipitation changes alone could not 
account for the observed long-term decline in lake 
volume, while Odongo et al. (2019) demonstrated 
the influence of land -use transitions on 
evapotranspiration. Similarly, Yihdego et al. 
(2016) highlighted the importance of groundwater 
and water abstraction in the basin’s water 
balance. Collectively, these studies demonstrate 
that the basin’s water resources are controlled by 
a complex interaction of natural and human 
factors. How ever, there remains a need for 
integrated analysis that explicitly connects 
observed hydro-meteorological changes with their

Against this background, the present study 
examines historical and recent hydro -
meteorological trends and variability in the Lake 
Naivasha Basin and assesses their implications for 
water-resource governance and environmental 
planning. The study focuses on precipitation, 
temperature, evaporation, river inflows, and lake-
level fluctuations to establish the temporal and 
spatial characteristics of the basin’s hydro -
meteorological system. It further investigates the 
relationships among these variables and considers 
how their variability influences water availability 
and the sustainability of water resources. 
The study is guided by the recognition that hydro-
meteorological processes and water governance 
are closely interconnected. Changes in the 
quantity and timing of water availability can 
influence water allocation, competing water uses, 
ecosystem requirement s, monitoring, 
conservation, and environmental planning. 
Conversely, human activities and management 
interventions can modify hydrological processes 
and affect the water balance of the basin. The 
study therefore seeks to move beyond a 
description of climatic and hydrological trends by 
examining their implications for the management 
and governance of a water system subject to 
increasing environmental and socio -economic 
pressures. 
METHODS 
The Study Area 
Lake Naivasha Basin is located in the central part 
of the Kenyan Rift Valley, approximately 100 
kilometres northwest of Nairobi. The basin lies 
between latitudes 0°45′S and 1°15′S and 
longitudes 36°00′E and 36°45′E, covering an 
estimated area of about 3,400 km². Lake Naivasha 
itself is a freshwater lake situated at an altitude of 
approximately 1,884 meters above sea level, 
making it one of the few freshwater bodies in the 
eastern Rift Valley. The physical and 
Hydrometeorological features in the study area 
include the basin, which is characterised by a 
complex hydrological system that includes several 
perennial and seasonal rivers, the most significant 
being the Malewa, Gilgil, and Karati Rivers, all of 
which contribute over 90% of the lake’s surface 
inflow. The Lake is endorheic, with no visible 
surface outlet, and water loss occurs primarily 
through evaporation and groundwater seepage, 
which plays a significant role in the region’s 
hydrogeology. One hydroclimatological 
assessment estimated the lake area at 
approximately 169 km² and the basin area at 
about 3,500 km² , while more recent satellite -
based assessments report a mean lake area of 
approximately 129 km² for 1984 –2020, 
demonstrating the substantial temporal variability 
in the lake’s extent (Odongo et al., 2015; Midekisa 
et al., 2021).

Climate 
The basin lies in the Kenyan Rift Valley, with 
altitudes ranging from about 1 890 m (at the lake 
surface) up to ~3 990 m at some ridges of the 
catchment, with a total basin area of about ~3 500 
km², with the lake covering around ~169 km². 
Rainfall within the basin is normally influenced by 
relief, such as the wetter slopes of Aberdare 
Mountains versus drier lowlands, and the basin 
lies partially in a rain- shadow. The climate in the 
Lake Naivasha Basin is classified as semi -arid to 
sub-humid, with bimodal r ainfall patterns, 
typically occurring from March to May (long 
rains) and October to December (short rains). 
Annual rainfall in the basin ranges significantly: 
lowland areas receive about ~610 mm per year, 
while wetter slopes get up to ~1 525 mm per year. 
This varies significantly across the basin, ranging 
from about 600 mm in the lowlands to over 1,200 
mm in the highland catchments. It is significant to 
note that rainfall is bimodal: a “long-rains” season 
roughly March–June (MAMJ) and a “short-rains” 
season roughly October –December (OND). The 
climate classification is therefore semi-arid to dry 
in parts: The lake site itself experiences an average 
maximum temperature of ~26.5 °C, a mean 
minimumof ~ 16.4 °C; average rainfall of ~600 
mm, and the Mean annual temperature ranges 
from 15°C to 25°C, with higher elevations 
experiencing cooler conditions. Potential 
evaporation (or evapotranspiration) in the basin is 
very high relative to rainfall: values of ~1 400 mm 
and even up to ~1 525 mm in dry months, making 
the water balance quite “tight”: the rainfall covers 
not only runoff but large evaporation losses; this 
causes a serious challenge of high evaporation and 
relief‐induced heterogeneity and spatial and 
temporal variability in water input. Additionally, 
high evaporation and relatively modest rainfall in 
lowland zones mean the lake and its inflows are 
sensitive to both rainfall variability and catchment 
changes, including land‐use and abstraction.

in Naivasha town. The lake and its surrounding 
wetlands also support a diverse range of flora and

Socio-Economic Importance 
Lake Naivasha is a critical resource for local 
livelihoods, providing water for domestic, 
agricultural, industrial, and ecological needs. The 
horticulture and floriculture industries, which rely 
heavily on irrigation from the lake and its 
tributaries, are among the most significant 
economic activities in the area, contributing 
substantially to Kenya’s export earnings. 
Additionally, fishing and tourism are key 
livelihood sources for local communities. 
DATA COLLECTION AND PROCESSING 
The study employed a mixed -methods approach 
combining long -term hydro -meteorological and 
hydrological datasets with primary field 
investigations and qualitative information on 
water resources governance. To avoid ambiguity 
in the temporal scope of the stud y, the datasets 
were organised into distinct analytical periods 
according to their availability and purpose. 
The long-term hydro -meteorological and 
hydrological analysis covered the period 1957 –
2022, providing a sufficiently long record for 
assessing trends, variability, and changes in the 
basin’s water balance. The 2002–2010 period was 
used only where datasets with consistent and 
comparable observations were available and 
where the analysis requ ired a common period 
across variables or stations; it was not treated as a 
separate overall study period. The 2017–2019 
period refers specifically to primary field 
investigations and stakeholder 
consultations undertaken to obtain information on 
water use, institutional arrangements, governance 
practices, and stakeholder perceptions. Where 
satellite-derived land use/land cover data were 
used, the analysis covered selected years within 
the period for which suitable imagery was 
available rather than implying c ontinuous annual 
observations. 
Approximately ten field visits and stakeholder 
consultation sessions were undertaken between 
2017 and 2019 across the Lake Naivasha Basin. 
The field component was designed to complement 
the long -term quantitative analysis by providing

Secondary Hydro-Meteorological and 
Hydrological Data 
Secondary datasets were obtained from 
authoritative institutions and established climate 
and remote -sensing databases. The principal 
hydro-meteorological variables comprised 
precipitation, minimum and maximum air 
temperature, evaporation, reference 
evapotranspiration, river discharge, and lake level. 
Additional spatial datasets, including land 
use/land cover, soil characteristics, topography, 
and drainage networks, were incorporated to 
support interpretation of catchment processes and 
hydrological responses. 
Precipitation records were obtained from fourteen 
meteorological stations located within and around 
the Lake Naivasha Basin from the Kenya 
Meteorological Department (KMD). The 
available station records were used to characterise 
spatial and temporal rainfall variability and to 
identify long -term trends. Where station records 
contained gaps or did not provide sufficient spatial 
coverage, satellite-derived precipitation products, 
particularly the Climate Hazards Group InfraRed 
Precipitation with Station data 
(CHIRPS) and Tropical Rainfall Measuring 
Mission (TRMM) datasets, were used as 
supplementary sources. These products were not 
treated as direct replacements for station 
observations without evaluation; rather, their 
suitability was assessed by comparison with 
available ground observations before they were 
used to supplement incomplete records. 
Daily minimum and maximum air temperature 
records were obtained from KMD meteorological 
stations. ERA5 reanalysis data were used to 
supplement spatial and temporal gaps in the 
station records and to provide spatially consistent 
temperature information where observations were 
incomplete or unavailable. The temperature 
datasets were subsequently aggregated to monthly 
and annual scales for trend and variability 
analysis. 
Evaporation observations were obtained from 
available hydrological monitoring stations. Where 
direct evaporation observations were unavailable 
or incomplete, atmospheric evaporative demand 
was represented through reference 
evapotranspiration (ETo) estimated using the 
FAO Penman –Monteith method. The distinction 
between observed evaporation and estimated 
reference evapotranspiration was maintained 
throughout the analysis because the two variables 
represent different components of the 
hydrological system. ETo was used primarily to 
characterise atmospheric evaporative demand, 
whereas lake and surface-water evaporation were 
considered in the water-balance assessment using 
the most appropriate available observations or 
estimates. 
Daily and monthly river-discharge records for the 
principal surface -water inflows to Lake 
Naivasha—the Malewa, Gilgil, and Karati 
rivers—were obtained from the Water Resources 
Authority (WRA). These rivers were selected 
because they represent the principal monitored 
surface-water inflows to the lake. The discharge 
records were used to quantify seasonal and 
interannual variability in inflows and to 
investigate the relationship between rainfall 
variability, catchment response, and changes in 
lake level. 
Historical Lake Naivasha water -level records 
were obtained from the Kenya Marine and 
Fisheries Research Institute (KMFRI) and the 
WRA. The lake -level records were used as an 
indicator of changes in lake storage and were 
analysed in relation to precipitation, river inflows, 
evaporation, and water abstraction. Where 
necessary, lake-level observations were converted 
into changes in storage using an appropriate lake 
area–elevation relationship rather than assuming

Primary Data and Governance Information 
Primary information was collected during field 
investigations and stakeholder consultations 
conducted between 2017 and 2019. The field 
component did not constitute a replacement for the 
long-term hydro-meteorological record; rather, it 
provided contemporary contextual information for 
interpreting the quantitative findings and 
assessing their implications for water resources 
governance. 
Information on water -resource utilisation was 
obtained from the WRA and other relevant 
institutions. The information included locations of 
water abstraction points, water permits, authorised 
abstraction volumes, reported abstraction levels 
where available, sectoral water demands, and the 
principal categories of water use. These data were 
used to characterise the spatial and sectoral 
distribution of water demand and to identify areas 
where changes in water availability could 
potentially create or intensify competition among 
users. 
Qualitative information on water governance was 
collected through stakeholder consultations, field 
observations, institutional records, and relevant 
policy and planning documents. Stakeholders 
were selected purposively based on their 
institutional responsi bilities, resource use, or 
involvement in water management within the 
basin. The consultations focused on institutional 
roles and responsibilities, water -allocation 
procedures, monitoring and enforcement, 
stakeholder participation, conflict and competition 
over water, environmental protection, and 
perceived changes in water availability. Particular 
attention was given to how stakeholders perceived 
the effects of rainfall variability, changing river 
flows, lake-level fluctuations, droughts, and other 
hydro-meteorological changes on water 
management decisions. 
The governance component therefore examined 
both formal governance arrangements, including 
policies, institutions, regulations, permits, and 
allocation procedures, and practical governance 
responses, including stakeholder participation, 
monitoring, enforce ment, conflict management, 
and adaptation to changing water availability. 
Data Preprocessing and Quality Control 
Prior to analysis, all hydro -meteorological, 
hydrological, and geospatial datasets underwent 
systematic quality -control and preprocessing 
procedures. These included screening for missing 
observations, duplicate records, transcription 
errors, inconsistencie s, extreme values, and 
changes in measurement practices. The 
procedures were applied consistently across 
datasets to ensure that observed trends reflected 
environmental changes rather than errors or 
artificial discontinuities in the records. 
Short-duration gaps in precipitation and 
temperature records were filled using 
interpolation only where the length of the missing 
period and the characteristics of the surrounding 
observations justified such treatment. Linear 
interpolation was used for sho rt, isolated gaps, 
while longer gaps were supplemented using 
observations from neighbouring stations or 
validated gridded datasets. Interpolation was not 
applied indiscriminately to long periods of 
missing data because excessive infilling can 
artificially reduce the observed variability of a 
time series. 
The quality of the datasets was evaluated using 
time-series plots, station -to-station comparisons, 
scatterplots, and statistical screening. Suspected

Temporal homogeneity was assessed using 
the Standard Normal Homogeneity Test 
(SNHT) and Pettitt's change-point test. These tests 
were used to identify possible discontinuities 
associated with station relocation, changes in 
instrumentation, modifications in observation 
practices, or other non-climatic influences. Where 
statistically significant breaks were identified, the 
affected records were examined in relation to 
station metadata and neighbouring observations 
before deciding whether adjustment or exclusion 
was appropriate. 
The geospatial datasets were standardised using 
the WGS 84 / UTM Zone 36S coordinate 
reference system. Raster datasets were resampled 
to a common spatial resolution appropriate to the 
analysis, while vector datasets were checked for 
geometric and topological inconsistencies. Daily 
observations we re aggregated into monthly and 
annual series for analyses requiring longer -term 
temporal scales. Seasonal aggregation was also 
undertaken to distinguish the main rainfall seasons 
and corresponding hydrological responses. 
Hydro-Meteorological and Hydrological 
Analysis 
The hydro -meteorological analysis focused on 
identifying the magnitude, direction, and 
significance of changes in the principal climatic 
and hydrological variables. Descriptive statistics, 
including means, standard deviations, coefficients 
of variation, minimum and maximum values, and 
seasonal distributions, were used to characterise 
temporal and spatial variability. 
Long-term trends in precipitation, temperature, 
evaporation, river discharge, and lake levels were 
assessed using appropriate parametric and non -
parametric statistical techniques. Where the 
assumptions of parametric methods were not 
satisfied, non -parametric approaches were 
preferred because hydro -climatic datasets 
frequently exhibit skewness, serial correlation, 
and extreme observations. Trend magnitude was 
estimated using the Sen's slope estimator, while 
statistical significance was evaluated using 
the Mann–Kendall test, with appropriate 
consideration of serial dependence where 
necessary. Change -point analysis was used to 
identify periods during which substantial shifts in 
hydro-meteorological behaviour may have 
occurred. 
Relationships among precipitation, temperature, 
evaporation, river discharge, and lake level were 
evaluated using correlation and regression 
analyses. These analyses were used to determine 
whether observed changes in lake levels and river 
inflows could be statistically associated with 
changes in climatic variables. The interpretation 
of these relationships considered the influence of 
water abstraction, land -use change, and other 
anthropogenic factors so that statistical 
associations were not automatically interpreted as 
direct causal relationships. 
Water Balance and Groundwater Estimation 
The Lake Naivasha water balance was formulated 
to quantify the principal components controlling 
changes in lake storage. The general water -
balance equation was expressed as: 
ΔS = P + Qin + GWin − E − Qout − GWout − W 
− ε 
Where ΔS represents the change in lake 
storage; P is direct precipitation on the lake 
surface; Qin represents surface -water inflows 
from the principal rivers; GWin represents 
groundwater inflow; E represents evaporation 
from the lake surface; Qout represents surface -
water outflow; GWout represents groundwater 
outflow; W represents anthropogenic water 
abstraction; and ε represents the residual or error 
term.

Limitations in Water Balance and 
Groundwater–Lake Interactions 
Although the basin water balance incorporated 
major hydrological components including 
precipitation, evapotranspiration, surface inflows, 
and streamflow, constraints remain regarding the 
quantification of groundwater –lake exchanges 
due to limited long-term groundwater monitoring 
data within the Lake Naivasha Basin. 
Consequently, groundwater inflow and outflow 
processes were inferred indirectly from observed 
lake level variations, hydrological responses, and 
findings from previous basin -scale 
hydrogeological studies. The analysis therefore 
constrains the plausible direction and relative 
magnitude of groundwater –lake interactions 
rather than providing direct volumetric estimates. 
Existing studies within the basin indicate that 
Lake Naivasha is hydraulically connected to 
surrounding aquifer systems, with groundwater 
contributing to lake recharge during wetter periods 
and potential seepage losses occurring during dry 
conditions. Howe ver, uncertainties associated 
with subsurface heterogeneity, abstraction rates, 
and limited piezometric observations restrict 
precise quantification of these exchanges. 
Accordingly, interpretations of groundwater 
contributions in this study are presented a s 
indicative basin -scale estimates intended to 
support understanding of hydrological variability 
and water resource governance, rather than as 
definitive groundwater flux measurements.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS 
The principal period adopted for the long -term 
hydro-meteorological and hydrological trend 
analysis was 1957–2022, providing an extended 
record for examining temporal variability, trends, 
and changes in the hydro -climatic characteristics 
of the Lake Naivasha Basin. The use of a long 
observation period is particularly important in 
hydro-climatic studies because it all ows short -
term interannual variability to be distinguished 
from persistent long -term trends and potential 
shifts in the hydrological regime. Previous 
research on the Lake Naivasha Basin has similarly 
demonstrated the value of long-term records, with 
Odongo et al. (2015) analysing approximately five 
decades of precipitation, river -flow, and lake -
volume data for the period 1960 –2010 to 
investigate hydro -climatological trends, 
variability, and changes in the basin water 
balance. Their findings demonstrated substantial 
variability in hydro -climatic conditions and a 
significant long -term decline in lake volume, 
while also indicating that climatic variability alone 
could not explain all observed hydrological 
changes. 
Because the individual datasets used in this study 
differed in their periods of record, availability, and 
continuity, specific sub -periods were analysed 
where appropriate. In particular, the period 2002–
2010 was used only for analyses requiring a 
common temporal window among variables or 
datasets with shorter overlapping records. This 
shorter period therefore represents a dataset-
specific analytical window rather than a 
replacement for the principal 1957 –2022 study 
period. Such differentiation is important because 
hydro-meteorological variables and hydrological 
observations frequently have different lengths and 
levels of temporal continuity, requiring commo n-
period analyses when relationships among 
variables are examined. 
The period 2017–2019 was reserved for primary 
field investigations, stakeholder consultations, 
and collection of contemporary information 
concerning water use, governance arrangements, 
institutional practices, and stakeholder 
perceptions. These field -based data complement 
the long-term hydro -meteorological analysis by 
providing information on contemporary water -
resource management conditions and stakeholder 
experiences. Accordingly, the different periods 
reported in the study should be understood as 
serving distinct analytical purposes: 1957–2022 
represents the principal long -term hydro -
meteorological and hydrological assessment 
period; 2002 –2010 represents selected common -
period analyses dictated by data availability; and 
2017–2019 represents the period of field 
investigations and stakeholder engagement. 
This distinction is consistent with previous 
research in the basin, where different datasets and 
analytical periods have been used to investigate 
specific components of the water balance. For 
example, Odongo et al. (2019) examined land-use 
and land-cover effects on evapotranspiration using 
MODIS and meteorological/reanalysis data for 
2003–2012, demonstrating that the appropriate 
analytical period may vary according to the 
research variable and data source. The authors 
found that conversion from grassland to cropland 
increased evapotranspiration by up to 
approximately 12%, highlighting the importance 
of considering both climate and land-use changes 
when interpreting long -term changes in basin 
water resources.

Temperature Trends 
Temperature analysis undertaken in this study 
indicates a persistent warming tendency within 
the Lake Naivasha Basin. Mean annual 
temperature increased at an estimated rate of 
approximately 0.02°C yr⁻¹ (0.2°C 
decade⁻¹) during the most recent three decades 
examined. The observed increase indicates a 
progressive warming of the basin and is consistent 
with broader evidence of rising temperatures 
across Kenya and East Africa. National -scale 
analyses have reported statistica lly significant 
increases in mean, maximum, and minimum 
temperatures over Kenya, although the magnitude 
and statistical significance of trends vary spatially 
and seasonally (Ayugi et al., 2019). More recent 
climate assessments by the Kenya Meteorological 
Department likewise indicate a persistent national 
warming tendency, with warming becoming 
particularly pronounced after the early 2000s 
(Kenya Meteorological Department [KMD], 
2026). 
The results further indicate that maximum 
temperatures have increased more rapidly 
than minimum temperatures , suggesting that 
daytime thermal conditions may be changing more 
strongly than nighttime conditions. Such changes 
are hydrologically important because increasing 
air temperatures can influence evaporative 
demand, crop water requirements, soil -moisture 
losses, and lake -water losses. However, 
temperature effects on evaporation should not be 
interpreted independently of other climatic 
controls, including humidity, wind speed, net 
radiation, and precipitation. Indeed, research in 
the Lake Naivasha Basin has de monstrated that 
changes in evapotranspiration reflect interactions 
among climatic conditions and land -use/land-
cover transitions rather than temperature alone 
(Odongo et al., 2019). 
The observed warming trend therefore has 
important implications for the basin's water 
balance. Higher temperatures may increase 
atmospheric evaporative demand and potentially 
intensify water requirements for irrigated 
agriculture and other water -dependent activities. 
In a basin already experiencing substantial 
pressure from agricultural intensification and 
water abstraction, increasing thermal conditions 
may contribute to additional stress on available 
water resources. Odongo et al. (2015) showed that 
changes in lake volume and river flows in the 
basin cannot be explained solely by precipitation 
variability, emphasising the need to consider 
multiple interacting climatic and anthropogenic 
drivers. Similarly, Odongo et al. (2019) 
demonstrated that land -use transitions, 
particularly expansion of cultivated land, can 
substantially modify evapotranspiration and 
consequently influence basin water availability. 
The temperature findings should therefore be 
interpreted as part of a broader hydro-
meteorological system in which warming 
interacts with precipitation variability, 
evaporation, river inflows, land-use change, water 
abstraction, and lake -level dynamics. The 
observed warming tendency provides an 
important indication of changing climatic 
conditions, but its imp lications for water 
availability depend on how temperature changes 
interact with other components of the basin water 
balance. Consequently, the temperature results 
provide an important basis for assessing future 
water demand, evaporation losses, agricultur al 
water requirements, ecosystem water needs, and 
the capacity of existing water -governance 
arrangements to respond to changing hydro -
meteorological conditions.

SPI/SSI and Extreme Event Analysis 
Meteorological and hydrological extremes were 
assessed using the Standardised Precipitation 
Index (SPI) and Standardised Streamflow Index 
(SSI). To capture both short -term and long -term 
hydroclimatic variability, SPI and SSI were 
computed at accumulation periods of 1 -, 3 -, 6 -, 
and 12 -month timescales. The 1 –3-month 
windows were used to assess short-term moisture 
deficits and seasonal anomalies, while the 6 –12-
month windows were applied to evaluate 
prolonged drought and hydrological persistence 
relevant to water resource governance in the Lake 
Naivasha Basin. 
Extreme drought and wet events were classified 
using standard SPI/SSI threshold categories. 
Drought conditions were identified when SPI or 
SSI values fell below −1.0, while flood/wet 
conditions were identified when index values 
exceeded +1.0. Event severit y was interpreted as 
follows: 
• Mild drought/wet event: ±1.0 to ±1.49 
• Moderate event: ±1.5 to ±1.99 
• Severe to extreme event: ≥ ±2.0 
A drought or flood event was considered to begin 
when SPI/SSI crossed the respective threshold (≤ 
−1.0 or ≥ +1.0) and ended when values returned 
above or below the threshold. These thresholds 
enabled consistent identification, comparison, and 
interpretation of hydroclimatic extremes across 
the 2002–2010 analysis period. 
Streamflow and Lake Level Variability 
River discharge data from major inflows (Malewa 
and Gilgil Rivers) indicate a general decline in 
streamflow, particularly in minimum flows, while 
mean and maximum flows show weak or no 
consistent trends. The Standardised Streamflow 
Index (SSI) highlights notable periods of 
hydrological drought during the 1990s an d early 
2000s, coinciding with low lake levels. Lake 
Naivasha exhibits substantial interannual 
fluctuations, largely driven by variable inflows 
and evaporation. Despite occasional rises due to 
extreme rainfall events, the long -term trend 
indicates declining lake levels, posing risks to 
aquatic ecosystems and water availability. 
Evapotranspiration and Water Balance 
Rising temperatures and extended dry periods 
have led to an estimated 5 –8% increase in 
evapotranspiration (ET) over the study period. 
This has exacerbated water losses from the lake 
and catchment, amplifying the effects of reduced 
rainfall and streamflow. Water balance analyses 
suggest that declining inflows combined with 
increasing ET are primary drivers of observed lake 
level reductions. While groundwater inflows and 
outflows remain less understood, they likely play 
a critical role in buffering the system during dry 
spells. 
Impact of Land Use Changes 
Remote sensing and land cover analyses reveal 
extensive anthropogenic alterations in the basin 
over the past two decades. Significant conversion 
of natural forests and wetlands to agriculture and 
urban areas has occurred, particularly in upper 
catchments. Deforestation likely increases surface 
runoff and sediment loads, reducing water quality 
and altering river hydrology. Agricultural 
expansion, notably floriculture near the lake, has 
intensified water abstraction, further stressing the 
lake’s water balance. Land cover changes directly 
influence runoff, infiltration, and 
evapotranspiration, underscoring the need for 
integrated catchment management. Protecting 
wetlands, riparian zones, and upland areas is 
critical to maintaining hydrological resilience. 
Ecosystem services, including fish and bird 
habitats and tourism, depend on stable water levels 
and quality. Governance strategies must 
emphasise sustainable water use, pollution 
control, and flexible management approaches to 
buffer against both drought and flood risks. 
Scenario planning for drier or wetter futures is 
essential, as even small shifts in effective rainfall 
(0.4–2%) can significantly alter lake levels.

Lake Naivasha Water Levels Analysis 
The Lake Naivasha Basin exhibits a relatively 
tight water balance, characterised by high 
evaporation relative to rainfall. Consequently, the 
lake, tributaries, and groundwater resources are 
highly sensitive to fluctuations in rainfall timing 
and magnitude, as well as changes in evaporation. 
Declines in lake volume or drops in minimum 
river flows reduce the system’s buffering capacity 
during dry seasons, compromising the reliability 
of water supply for irrigation, domestic use, and 
ecosystem services. 
Between approximately 2002 and 2010, Lake 
Naivasha experienced substantial shrinkage, with 
water levels dropping at an estimated rate of ~10 
cm per year. This trend heightened the risk of 
water stress during dry periods. More recently, 
rising lake levels s uggest temporarily improved 
water availability; however, the underlying drivers 
whether increased rainfall, catchment changes, or 
shifts in groundwater dynamics remain uncertain, 
making reliance on this apparent improvement 
potentially risky. 
Seasonal rainfall variability, particularly during 
the long rains (March –June) and short rains 
(October–December), directly affects dry -season 
flows. Observations indicate that some tributaries, 
such as the Karati River, occasionally dry up 
during dry periods. Conversely, elevated lake 
levels increase the risk of flooding and shoreline 
inundation, particularly given the lake’s shallow 
depth and large surface area fluctuations. Most 
documented publications highlight frequent 
flooding events that have displaced local 
populations. These hydrological extremes, such as 
low flows and droughts to high water levels and 
floods increases the significant challenges for 
water management infrastructure, ecosystem 
resilience, and livelihoods dependent on the lake, 
including fisheries, tourism, and irrigation. 
Effective management must therefore account for 
both climatic variability and human -induced 
changes to ensure sustainable and resilient water 
resource use in the basin.

Implications and Future Outlook 
The Lake Naivasha Basin is characterised by a 
bimodal rainfall regime, moderate annual 
precipitation, high evaporative demand, and 
pronounced spatial climatic variability driven by 
topography. Long -term hydro -meteorological 
observations indicate substantia l temporal 
variability in rainfall, river discharge, and lake 
water levels. Although Lake Naivasha 
experienced prolonged declines in water levels 
over several decades, a period of recovery has 
been observed since approximately 2010. These 
fluctuations refl ect the combined influence of 
climatic variability and anthropogenic drivers, 
including land -use change, increasing water 
abstraction, catchment degradation, and changing 
hydrological connectivity. 
The study demonstrates that climate variability, 
particularly changes in rainfall distribution, 
increasing temperatures, and enhanced 
evapotranspiration, is intensifying pressure on the 
basin's water resources. These climatic stresses are 
compounded by rapid agricultural expansion, 
urbanisation, and growing water demand, 
resulting in reduced streamflows , altered 
groundwater recharge, and increasing competition 
among domestic, agricultural, industrial, and 
environmental water users. Consequently, the 
basin's hydrological regime is increasingly shaped 
by the interaction between climate and human 
activities rather than by climatic factors alone. 
These findings underscore the need for an 
integrated and adaptive water governance 
framework that recognises the interdependence of 
climate, hydrology, ecosystems, and socio -
economic development. Sustainable manageme nt 
of the Lake Naivasha Basin will require 
coordinated policies that integrate climate 
adaptation with land -use planning, water 
allocation, ecosystem conservation, and 
catchment restoration.

Priority policy actions should include 
strengthening hydro -meteorological monitoring 
networks and data management systems; 
integrating land-use planning with water resources 
governance; regulating surface and groundwater 
abstractions based on adaptive allocation rules that 
respond to climatic variability; protecting 
groundwater recharge areas, riparian corridors, 
and wetland buffer zones; restoring degraded 
catchments to enhance hydrological resilience; 
and improving water -use efficiency across all 
sectors. In addition, scenario -based planning for 
both drought and flood risks should be 
incorporated into basin management to enhance 
preparedness under future climate uncertainty. 
Overall, the study highlights that sustainable water 
security in the Lake Naivasha Basin will depend 
on transitioning from reactive resource 
management to an evidence -based, climate -
resilient, and ecosystem -centred governance 
approach capable of addressing the combined 
impacts of climate variability and increasing 
anthropogenic pressures. Such an integrated 
framework is essential for safeguarding the basin's 
ecological integrity while sustaining the 
livelihoods and economic activities that depend on 
its water resources.
