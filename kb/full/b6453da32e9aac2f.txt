Lisa Yang Center for Conservation Bioacoustics, Cornell University, Ithaca, New York, USA<sup>2</sup>Cornell Lab of Ornithology, Cornell University, Ithaca, New York, USA<sup>3</sup>Great Basin Bird Observatory, Reno, Nevada, USA<sup>4</sup>U.S. Forest Service, Southwest Forest Science Complex, Flagstaff, Arizona USA<sup>5</sup>U.S. Forest Service, Natural Resources, Vacaville, California, USA<br><br>Abstract:Species-habitat relationships for social, wide-ranging species may be overlooked or misidentified because group dynamics and large-scale movements can obscure habitat selection, especially when measured with presence-absence data. Metrics that capture temporal patterns in group presence may be necessary for effective conservation planning of such species. However, the high-resolution data needed to assess the frequency, duration, and timing of species presence (here: activity level) are challenging to collect and rarely incorporate group size. We evaluated how passive acoustic monitoring can be used to assess species activity level and vocal activity rate (an expected proxy for group size) to inform conservation decision-making of terrestrial social, mobile species. We illustrate this approach for two species of conservation concern: a bird, the Pinyon Jay (<i>Gymnorhinus cyanocephalus</i>), and a mammal, the African forest elephant (<i>Loxodonta cyclotis</i>). Our primary objective was to use acoustic data to understand if and how species-specific metrics of activity level varied with management-relevant habitat characteristics. However, a challenge of using acoustic data to assess activity level is identifying if a change in acoustic activity is due to a change in group size or individual vocal activity rate. We therefore tested if human-observed group size varied with species vocal activity rate. We found activity level was strongly associated with management-relevant habitat features for both species and was suggestive of behaviors that were driving temporal patterns in resource use. Pinyon Jay activity level identified possible foraging areas in open woodlands with frequent long-duration visits. African forest elephant activity level suggested rivers may be perceived as an attractive but risky resource during the dry season. Vocal activity rate was positively associated with both human-observed group size and activity level for jays only, suggesting sites with greater activity are not consistently used by larger groups.
