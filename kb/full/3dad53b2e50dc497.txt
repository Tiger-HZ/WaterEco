Abstract
Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) affects all age groups, including children and 
adolescents. However, middle-aged and older individuals are the most vulnerable, 
and T2DM has been a major contributor to the global disease burden for decades. 
While previous research has focused on clinical outcomes, such as cardiovascular 
diseases and complications, less attention has been paid to broader impacts, in-
cluding health-related quality of life (HRQOL). This study investigated whether 
T2DM predicts HRQOL among middle-aged and older Australians. We used data 
from waves 9, 13, 17, and 21 of the Household, Income and Labour Dynamics in 
Australia (HILDA) Survey. The analysis included 27,564 person-year observations 
from 11,042 individuals. Fixed-effects regression models were employed to exam-
ine the association between T2DM and HRQOL. T2DM was negatively associated 
with HRQOL, particularly with the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and the 
SF-6D health-state utility value. Specifically, T2DM was associated with lower PCS 
scores (β = −1.01, 95% CI: −1.62 to −0.41) and lower SF-6D utility values (β = 
−0.0080, 95% CI: −0.0154 to −0.0007), whereas no significant association was 
observed with the Mental Component Summary (MCS). The negative associations 
with PCS and SF-6D were most evident among men and adults aged 60 years and 
older, with additional subgroup analyses indicating a greater physical HRQOL bur-
den among participants with insufficient physical activity, disability, smoking, alco-
hol consumption, and selected income groups. T2DM was associated with poorer 
physical HRQOL and lower health-state utility over the 13-year follow-up, with 
the greatest burden observed among men, older adults, and several behavioural and 
health-related subgroups. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating 
HRQOL assessment into routine diabetes care and support targeted prevention and 
management strategies that promote healthy lifestyles, including regular physical 
activity, smoking cessation, and moderation of alcohol consumption. The estimated

Introduction
Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) remains a significant public health problem, with 
well-established disease and economic burdens worldwide (Internaltional Diabetes 
Federation, 2021; Islam et al., 2023; Ong et al., 2023; Safiri et al., 2022). The preva-
lence of T2DM has been rapidly increasing in all age groups and throughout the 
world (Ong et al., 2023). The condition is projected to affect more than 500 million 
people between 2020 and 2030 (Ong et al., 2023). This complex metabolic disorder 
contributed to a significant disease burden, resulting in 1.47 million deaths, 66.3 mil-
lion disability-adjusted life years (Safiri et al., 2022), and an annual healthcare cost 
exceeding US$966 billion globally (Internaltional Diabetes Federation, 2021). 
T2DM also impacts a substantial portion of the Australian population, affecting 
approximately 1.5 million individuals and incurring an estimated economic cost of 
AU$3.4 billion in 2020-21 (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2025; Australian Institute 
of Health and Welfare, 2023, 2024; Islam et al., 2023).
Beyond its direct health and economic consequences, T2DM significantly impairs 
the health-related quality of life (HRQOL) of individuals. Existing evidence suggests 
that individuals with T2DM experience a decline in HRQOL. This deterioration is 
attributable to the multifaceted nature of the condition, encompassing its chronic 
trajectory, the presence of comorbidities, and the potential for associated disabili-
ties (Doubova et al., 2013; Mwadulo et al., 2023). HRQOL is a multidimensional 
concept that encompasses various aspects of an individual’s physical, mental, and 
social well-being (Kaplan & Hays, 2022; Wilson & Cleary, 1995). HRQOL serves 
as a fundamental tool for assessing population health status and is increasingly used 
as an outcome measure to evaluate adverse health outcomes associated with diabetes 
(Alzahrani et al., 2023; Wilson & Cleary, 1995). The relationship between chronic 
conditions and HRQOL is well described by Wilson and Cleary conceptual model 
(Alzahrani et al., 2023; Wilson & Cleary, 1995), which posits that biological function 
influences symptom status, functional status, general health perceptions, and ulti-
mately overall HRQOL. The model further recognises that these relationships are 
shaped by individual characteristics (e.g., age and health behaviours) and environ-
mental characteristics (e.g., social support and healthcare environments). Within this 
framework, T2DM and its associated complications may impair HRQOL through 
their effects on symptoms, functional limitations, and individuals’ perceptions of 
health. Multiple observational studies have consistently found that T2DM has a nega-
tive impact on HRQOL among adults (Baumert et al., 2021; Homady et al., 2023; 
Khalifa et al., 2020; Mokhtari et al., 2019; Mwadulo et al., 2023; Saleh et al., 2015; 
Shamshirgaran et al., 2020; Zang et al., 2021).

Moreover, the economic burden of diabetes has been shown to have a consider-
able detrimental impact on the quality of life of patients in the middle and later stages 
of life (Leng et al., 2021). In Australia, mirroring the global trend, T2DM presents 
a substantial challenge to HRQOL, particularly within the aging population (Feng 
& Astell-Burt, 2017; Tapp et al., 2010). However, the existing evidence regarding 
this impact has been limited and inconclusive. These discrepancies in evidence may 
be attributed to several factors, including small sample sizes, shorter study dura-
tions, a focus on specific subpopulations, controlling for established confounders, 
and the use of diverse methodologies for assessing HRQOL. For example, some 
studies relied on a single question to assess HRQOL and collected data at only two 
time points, potentially limiting the depth and accuracy of their findings (Feng & 
Astell-Burt, 2017).
While several studies have examined the relationship between T2DM and HRQOL 
among adult populations, evidence from prospective cohort studies has been limited, 
and the results have been inconclusive (Bayliss et al., 2004; Grandy et al. 2012; 
Maatouk et al., 2012; Marrero et al., 2014a, b; Shamshirgaran et al., 2020). Notably, 
these prospective studies primarily focused on the general population and overlooked 
middle-aged and older adults, a population at high risk for T2DM-related complica-
tions. For example, a study conducted in the USA included a broad range of ages, 
making it difficult to differentiate whether the HRQOL changes were based on the 
presence and/or absence of diabetes mellitus in older adults (Grandy et al., 2012). 
The two other studies did not focus on the population level and excluded individu -
als without diabetes (Bayliss et al., 2004; Maatouk et al., 2012). This suggests the 
need to examine the health burden of T2DM in the most at-risk population, while 
controlling for a wide range of potential confounders, including physical activity, a 
well-established risk factor for T2DM (Kyu et al., 2016).
Furthermore, a few studies have examined HRQOL using health state utility val-
ues, which is an essential health outcome for conducting comprehensive economic 
evaluations of interventions for T2DM (Clarke et al., 2002; Keng et al., 2022; Mar-
rero et al., 2014a, b; Mok et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024). The value obtained from 
these studies for health state utility varies due to differences in sample size and the 
tools used to measure health state utility values. Research on the long-term decrement 
in HRQOL associated with T2DM in Australia remains scarce, particularly among 
aging populations. Most Australian studies have not included SF-6D utility values, 
limiting their applicability for economic evaluations. Given the aging demographic 
and rising prevalence of T2DM (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, 2023), 
there is a critical need for a longitudinal study examining the long-term decrements in 
HRQOL associated with T2DM among middle-aged and older adults. To address this 
evidence gap, we aimed to investigate the relationship between T2DM and HRQOL. 
Additionally, we aimed to estimate the disutility associated with T2DM using the 
Short-Form Six-Dimension (SF-6D) health utility index as a preference-based mea-
sure. These findings will provide essential insight into public health policy, early 
detection strategies, and the management of diabetes. Our findings may also serve as 
a crucial foundation for future economic evaluations of different interventions aimed 
at improving the HRQOL of people with T2DM.

Subject, Materials and Methods
Data source and sample selection
We utilised data from the Household Income and Labour Dynamics Australia 
(HILDA) Survey, a nationally representative longitudinal survey of the Australian 
population that commenced in 2001. Since then, the survey has collected a range of 
information on key aspects of life, including wealth, labour market outcomes, house-
hold and family relationships, fertility, health, and education, each year. A multistage 
sampling approach was employed to select an initial sample of households to be 
included in the survey. Individuals aged 15 years or older residing in each household 
were included in the sample. The survey tracks the lives of over 17,000 Australian 
adults annually. More comprehensive information about the HILDA Survey has been 
published elsewhere (Watson & Wooden, 2021). We analysed data from four waves, 
Waves 9, 13, 17, and 21, spanning 13 years. These waves were selected because 
they included updated information on chronic diseases, including T2DM. Data from 
participants were excluded if there were missing observations for either the exposure 
variable (T2DM) or the outcome variable (HRQOL) in any of the studied waves. 
The analytic sample was drawn from Waves 9, 13, 17, and 21 of the HILDA survey, 
comprising 32,482 individuals and 86,807 person-wave observations. We excluded 
individuals aged under 15 years or those who did not complete the self-completion 
questionnaire (9,556 individuals; 28,423 observations). The sample was further 
restricted to respondents aged over 45 years, excluding 11,609 individuals (29,036 
observations). This study restricted to individuals aged over 45 years, as the risk 
and prevalence of type 2 diabetes increase substantially from midlife onwards. This 
age threshold is commonly used in epidemiological research and screening recom -
mendations to identify populations at higher risk (Australian Institute of Health and 
Welfare, 2023, 2026). Restricting the sample to this age group also improves compa-
rability by focusing on individuals with a more homogeneous risk profile and reduces 
potential bias arising from the low prevalence of T2DM in younger populations. 
Observations with missing information on HRQOL measures (PCS, MCS, SF-6D) 
or diabetes status were also excluded (275 individuals; 1,784 observations). The final 
analytic sample consisted of 11,042 individuals contributing 27,564 observations. All 
analyses were conducted at the person-wave level. The final analytic sample included 
27,564 yearly observations from 11,042 unique individuals. Details on the sample 
selection are illustrated in Appendix Table A1. More details on the missing data can 
be found in Appendix Table A2.
Outcome Measures
The outcome variable of our study is HRQOL. In the HILDA Survey, HRQOL was 
assessed using the validated and well-established Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) 
(Alzahrani et al., 2023; Australian Breau of Statistics, 1995; Butterworth & Cro-
sier, 2004; Norman et al., 2013; Ware Jr, 2000). This survey comprises 36 questions 
intended to evaluate multiple aspects of physical and mental health. The responses 
are converted into scores ranging from 0 to 100, where higher scores represent better

health status in each dimension (Brazier et al., 1992; Keramat et al., 2023), and it is 
the most widely used generic profile measure of HRQOL. The SF-36 consists of eight 
dimensions: physical functioning (PF), role physical (RP), role emotional (RE), men-
tal health (MH), social functioning (SF), vitality (VT), bodily pain (BP), and general 
health (GH) (Ware Jr, 2000). These dimensions are combined into two scores: the 
Physical Component Summary (PCS) and the Mental Component Summary (MCS). 
Both the PCS and MCS are standardised using linear z-score transformations, with 
a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10. The theoretical range for PCS scores is 
from 4.54 to 76.09, while the range for MCS scores is from − 1.21 to 76.19 (Keramat 
et al., 2023). Higher scores indicate better health status (Australian Breau of Statis-
tics, 1995; Perales et al., 2014; Ware Jr, 2000).
We also examined the health utility index using the SF-6D utility index, a widely 
recognised method for estimating utilities for profile measures. The SF-6D utility 
is a generic preference-based instrument used to measure an individual’s HRQOL 
(Mukuria et al., 2025). It is derived from the SF-36 and utilises information from 
six of its subscales: physical functioning, role limitations caused by physical health 
problems, role limitations caused by emotional problems, social functioning, vitality, 
and bodily pain (Baxter et al., 2015). The SF-6D scores range from 0.29 to 1, with 
1 indicating perfect health and 0.29 referring to the worst possible health (Baxter 
et al., 2015; Kaplan & Hays, 2022; Keramat et al., 2023; Stephen J Walters & John 
E Brazier, 2005a, b). We used two SF-6D in this study. The SF-6D utility index 
is suitable for economic evaluations, designed to generate utility values from the 
SF-36 (Brazier et al., 2002; Norman et al., 2013). SF-6D utility scores were derived 
from the SF-36 using the standard SF-6D scoring algorithm based on UK general 
population preferences (Brazier et al., 2002). This approach is commonly applied in 
analyses using HILDA data and ensures comparability with existing studies. As the 
sensitive analysis, we also used the Australian preference weights developed by (Le 
et al., 2024; Norman et al., 2014; Summerfield & Wilkins, 2025) HILDA provides the 
Australian-weighted utility as separate positive and negative components (_ghsf6ap 
and _ghsf6an); following HILDA conventions, the Australian-weighted score was 
calculated as positive minus negatives. The Australian value set permits negative 
values for health states valued as worse than death. SF-6D is a preference-based mea-
sure anchored on a scale from 0 (death) to 1 (full health), allowing its use in health 
economic evaluation.
Exposure Variable
Self-reported data were used to measure the main exposure variable. The primary 
focus of our study is T2DM. The survey respondents were asked, “Have you ever 
been told by a doctor or nurse that you have been diagnosed with a serious illness or 
medical condition?” The participants responded “Yes” or “No”. Then, they selected 
the condition they have from a list of 11 options shown on the showcard: hyperten-
sion, heart disease, Type 1 diabetes, Type 2 diabetes, chronic bronchitis/emphysema, 
cancer, asthma, arthritis/osteoporosis, anxiety/depression, other mental health condi-
tions, and circulatory disease.

Potential Confounders
Potential confounding factors were selected based on the availability of the vari-
ables in the HILDA Survey and previous literature (Meisters et al., 2024; Teli et 
al., 2023). These factors include age [45–59 years (middle-aged), and 60 years and 
above (older)], gender (male vs. female), race (Indigenous vs. Non-Indigenous), edu-
cation (Year 12 and below, certificate, and university degrees), labour force status 
(employed, unemployed/not in the labour force), remoteness (major city vs. regional/
remote area), smoking status (never smoked, ex-smoker, and current smoker), alco-
hol consumption (never drunk, ex-drinker, and current drinker), body mass index 
(underweight, healthy weight, overweight, and obese), physical activity (less than 
recommended level vs. recommended level), disability (yes or No) and chronic con-
ditions (no chronic condition, single chronic condition, and multimorbidity). Partici-
pants in the HILDA Survey were asked whether they had ever been diagnosed with 
a serious illness or medication condition from the 11 lists that were shown to them 
(hypertension, heart disease, type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, chronic bronchitis/
emphysema, cancer, asthma, arthritis/osteoporosis, anxiety/depression, other mental 
health conditions, and circulatory disease). We classify the chronic condition as in 
prior research (Keramat et al., 2023). We equivalized annual household income using 
the OECD-modified equivalent scale and then categorised it into quantiles (poorest 
[quintile 1] to richest [quintile 5]). Additional information on potential confounders 
is provided in Appendix Table 2.
Statistical Analysis
The data were analysed using descriptive statistics and longitudinal regression mod-
els. Descriptive statistics were used to characterise the analytic sample, including 
frequency (percentage) for categorical variables and mean (standard deviation) for 
continuous variables. To compare the baseline characteristic, chi-square test was 
used for categorical variables and independent samples t-tests was used for continu-
ous variables.
We hypothesised that having T2DM would be negatively associated with the 
HRQOL of middle-aged and older Australians. To examine this hypothesis, we 
deployed the fixed regression models. The authors’ selection of a fixed-effects GLS 
regression model is suitable for analysing continuous outcomes. This method effi-
ciently controls individual-specific characteristics that remain constant over time, 
minimising the potential bias caused by time-invariant confounding factors and con-
trolled unobserved characteristics. This enhances the credibility of causal inference 
regarding the effects of T2DM on HRQOL. A Hausman test was performed to deter-
mine the most suitable model; however, the test results were not reported. The test 
statistic, which adheres to an asymptotic χ2 distribution, rejected the null hypothesis, 
suggesting that the fixed-effects regression model was favoured over the random-
effects GLS model (Brüderl & Ludwig, 2015). This model helps identify within-
person differences in the association between T2DM and HRQOL. The fitted model 
presented here assumes the following structure:

Three separate regression models were fitted for the component summaries of the 
SF-36 (PCS and MCS) and the SF-6D utility value to examine within-person differ-

founders. We also checked the multicollinearity among the exposure variables. The 
results showed that the variance inflation factors (VIF) were at an acceptable level 
(results not included here). Furthermore, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were con-
ducted to assess heterogeneity and the robustness of the primary findings. Separate 
fixed-effects models were estimated by sex, age group, physical activity, disability 
status, income quintile, smoking status and alcohol consumption to examine poten-
tial variation in the association between T2DM and HRQOL. Model robustness was 
further evaluated by re-estimating the primary models using random-effects GLS 
regression and comparing the direction and magnitude of the estimates with those 
from the fixed-effects models. The fixed-effects models were also re-estimated after 
imputing missing observation data, and the results were compared with the complete-
case estimates. The proportion of missing data was low (< 5%), with further details 
provided in Appendix Table A2. The model reported adjusted regression coefficients 
(βs) with 95% CIs and considered statistical significance at P < 0.05. All analyses 
were performed by STATA 17 version (Stata SE 17, College Station, TX: Stata Corp 
LLC, USA).
Results
Descriptive Analyses
Table 1 presents the characteristics of the final analytic sample, comprising 11,042 
individuals and 27,564 person-year observations. The pooled data revealed that 
half of the study participants (50%) were aged 60 years or older, and they were pre-
dominantly female (53%). The majority were married (65%) and resided in major 
cities (63%). Employment rates were substantial, at 52%, and 26% of the popula-
tion held university degrees. Notably, a high proportion of participants were cur-
rent drinkers (81%), and two-thirds (66%) did not meet the recommended physical 
activity guidelines. Furthermore, a substantial proportion of the samples were clas-
sified as obese (28%), with 41% experiencing disability and 31% living with mul-
timorbidity. The results also showed that 9% of the study participants had T2DM 
(pooled data).

At baseline (Wave 9), participants with T2DM had significantly lower HRQOL 
across PCS, MCS, and SF-6D utility values than participants without T2DM. Par-
ticipants with T2DM were more likely to be in lower income groups and differed 
by education and labour force status. They also had a higher prevalence of obesity, 
disability, and multimorbidity, and differed in behavioural characteristics including 
smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity (Appendix Table A3).
Table 2 provides a summary of the mean HRQOL scores at baseline, final wave, 
and pooled data for the analytical sample. The pooled data analysis revealed that the 
mean PCS score was 45.46, the mean MCS score was 49.55, and the mean SF-6D 
utility value was 0.74.
Appendix Figure A1 shows the trends in mean PCS and MCS scores across the 
study waves. Mean PCS scores gradually declined from 45.84 in 2009 to 45.24 in 
2017, before increasing slightly to 45.48 in 2021. Despite this recovery, the 2021 
mean PCS score remained below the baseline level observed in 2009. In contrast, 
mean MCS scores remained stable between 2009 (50.15) and 2013 (50.14), then 
declined to 49.69 in 2017 and further to 48.45 in 2021. Appendix Figure A2 presents 
the mean SF-6D utility values across the study waves. The mean SF-6D utility value 
remained relatively stable between 2009 (0.744) and 2013 (0.741), followed by a 
gradual decline to 0.737 in 2017 and 0.731 in 2021. Appendix Figure A3 shows the 
prevalence of T2DM across the study waves. The prevalence increased progressively 
from 8.4% in 2009 to 9.6% in 2021.

pronounced decline in PCS compared to those without T2DM (Schunk et al., 2017). 
Moreover, a recent longitudinal study of older adults in New Zealand found that 
diabetes mellitus is negatively associated with PCS in older adults with diabetes com-
pared to those without (Shamshirgaran et al., 2020). Furthermore, our findings are 
corroborated by several cross-sectional and longitudinal studies conducted in vari-
ous countries, including Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Bangladesh 
(Homady et al., 2023; Khalifa et al., 2020; Mokhtari et al., 2019; Saleh et al., 2015). 
Despite consistent findings, some prior studies used a different survey instrument 
than the SF-36 to measure HRQOL [21, 23, 24], warranting caution when comparing 
current results with those of previous studies. The chronic nature of T2DM and its 
complications affects the physical aspects of HRQOL. The adverse effects of T2DM 
on physical aspects underscore the importance of holistic approaches to diabetes care 
that encompass both medical and psychosocial aspects (Maatouk et al., 2012).
Additionally, a negative association was observed between T2DM and the SF-6D 
utility value, even after adjusting for socio-demographic and health-related factors, 
which with previous studies (Keng et al., 2022; Marrero et al., 2014a, b; Mok et 
al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024). This decrease demonstrates a notable disutility associ-
ated with T2DM, capturing the overall physical, psychological, and social challenges 
faced by people living with T2DM. Even small reductions in utility can lead to sig -
nificant losses in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) across populations (Mukuria 
et al., 2025; Mulhern & Meadows, 2014). For example, a systematic review and 
meta-analysis found that T2DM-related complications are associated with a decline 
in HRQOL, as measured by the SF-6D and EQ-5D. These studies also emphasise 
that the choice of measurement instrument significantly impacts the reported health 
utility values.
Furthermore, our study demonstrates that T2DM is associated with a greater 
reduction in HRQOL among males and older adults. This finding aligns with pre-
vious research, which also indicated that the overall burden attributed to T2DM 
was higher in these groups (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2025; Australian Insti-
tute of Health and Welfare, 2024; Safiri et al., 2022; Shamshirgaran et al., 2020). 
Although the estimated effect sizes were modest, this is expected when using sum-
mary HRQOL measures. The observed reduction in SF-6D among males and aged 
older adults were below commonly reported minimal clinically important difference 
thresholds (S. J. Walters & J. E. Brazier, 2005a, b), indicating a modest effect at the 
individual level. Therefore, while statistically significant, the clinical magnitude of 
this association should be interpreted with caution. However, given the increasing 
prevalence of diabetes, even small average reductions may have population-level 
implications. Older patients often develop more complications and comorbidities 
over time, which may explain the greater reduction in HRQOL observed in this age 
group (Clarke et al., 2002; Shamshirgaran et al., 2020). The observed difference 
between males and females may be attributed to variations in disease-to-health-seek-
ing behaviours, coping strategies, and the presence of diabetes-related complications 
(Schunk et al., 2015).

We did not find any evidence of the relationship between T2DM and MCS score. 
This finding is consistent with a prior study (Marrero et al., 2014a, b). However, two 
previous studies in Germany and New Zealand found a negative association between 
T2DM and MCS (Schunk et al., 2017; Shamshirgaran et al., 2020). These conflicting

counselling, psychosocial support, self-care behaviours, and education services may 
be present among people with T2DM. This may help reduce the negative emotional 
impact of T2DM (Marrero et al., 2014a, b). In contrast, PCS and SF-6D are more 
sensitive to the physical burden of diabetes.
Implications for Policy and Practice
Our study demonstrated a negative association between T2DM and HRQOL, as 
measured by PCS and SF-6D utility values. This finding suggests that T2DM has a 
detrimental effect on physical and overall health. These findings support consider-
ing HRQOL alongside conventional clinical indicators because glycaemic measures 
alone may not fully capture the physical and overall health burden experienced by 
people with T2DM (McDool et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024).
The subgroup analyses identified negative associations between T2DM and PCS 
and SF-6D among males and adults aged 60 years and older. Lower PCS was also 
observed among participants undertaking less than the recommended level of physi-
cal activity, participants with and without disability, those in the third income quin-
tile, former and current smokers, and current alcohol consumers. Lower SF-6D utility 
was evident among participants with disability, current smokers and current alcohol 
consumers. These findings suggest that HRQOL and functional limitations should be 
considered when assessing older adults, people with disability and individuals report-
ing insufficient physical activity or other potentially modifiable health behaviours. 
The findings support diabetes policies and clinical practices that consider physical 
functioning and health utility alongside conventional clinical indicators.
Public health policy should promote preventive care and early intervention for 
individuals affected by T2DM. This could involve adopting a healthy lifestyle, such 
as improving diets (increasing consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and 
fibre, and reducing consumption of highly processed foods) and increasing physi-
cal activity, increasing access to health care, and encouraging regular screening for 
patients with diabetes (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, 2023). In clini-
cal practice, routine diabetes reviews could incorporate brief assessments of physi-
cal HRQOL, functional limitations and physical activity. Individuals experiencing 
reduced physical functioning could then receive safe, individualised support that 
considers age, comorbidities, capacity and barriers to participation.

Furthermore, this study utilises up-to-date data that reflects the realities follow-
ing the COVID-19 pandemic, ensuring its relevance and applicability in the current 
context. The SF-6D is a preference-based measure that has demonstrated validity for 
assessing health utility among people with T2DM (Mukuria et al., 2025). Its inclusion 
can complement clinical outcomes by quantifying patients’ preference-based health 
status. Differences in SF-6D valuation may, however, affect estimated utility differ-
ences and subsequent economic evaluations (McDool et al., 2021). The lower SF-6D 
values identified in this study represent health-utility decrements rather than direct 
economic losses. SF-6D utilities can be combined with survival or duration informa-
tion to estimate QALYs and inform cost–utility analysis (Mukuria et al., 2025; Wang 
& Poder, 2023). Consistent with the Australian setting, we used Australian popula-
tion preference weights as separate analysis. A negative T2DM coefficient therefore 
represents a health-utility decrement, or disutility. These utility estimates can inform 
future QALY-based cost–utility analyses. Nevertheless, this study did not estimate 
QALYs, healthcare costs, intervention effects or cost-effectiveness. The estimates 
therefore provide inputs for future economic evaluations rather than evidence that a 
particular intervention provides economic value.
Additionally, our study revealed the disutility associated with T2DM using the 
SF-6D utility index, a preference-based measure of health (Mukuria et al., 2025). 
This estimate of disutility offers valuable insights for future economic evaluations, 
particularly cost-utility analyses, of interventions to enhance the HRQOL of indi-
viduals with T2DM. Our disutility estimates can be incorporated into cost-utility 
analyses of interventions for T2DM (e.g., lifestyle modification, health education 
programmes). This reinforces the evidence base for directing resources towards inter-
ventions that enhance both clinical outcomes and quality of life. The previous study 
demonstrates that SF-6D utilities for chronic illnesses are reliable and valuable for 
economic evaluation (Song et al., 2022). The lower utility values among individu -
als with T2DM may indicate that, despite existing preventive services (screening, 
lifestyle advice, glycaemic control), the net effect on HRQOL remains substantial 
(Mulhern & Meadows, 2014). This suggests that current preventive care might not be 
entirely effective, or that improvements are needed in how it is delivered, including 
behavioural support, doctor-patient interactions, and the content of services. In other 
words, the disutility may reflect both the burden of disease and potential shortcom-
ings in service delivery or patient involvement. Focusing only on glycaemic metrics 
without considering behavioural, psychosocial, and lifestyle factors can lead to a 
significant loss of quality of life, which remains unaddressed (McDool et al., 2021; 
Wang et al., 2024). Our findings can be used to calculate the quality-adjusted life years 
(QALYs) lost due to T2DM, which is essential for evaluating the economic viability 
of health interventions aimed at reducing T2DM risk, such as lifestyle modifications 
(e.g., improved diet and exercise) and health education programs. By quantifying the 
decline in HRQOL associated with T2DM, our findings help policymakers compare 
the value of interventions, such as improved diet, lifestyle programmes, or education-
based support, using a standard metric (e.g., QALYs) (Wang & Poder, 2023). This 
provides a foundation for informed decisions about investments in primary care, pre-
ventive health, and the management of chronic diseases. Additionally, since delivery 
and behavioural factors can influence utility values, health services aimed at reducing

communication between healthcare professionals and patients, accessibility, follow-

Moreover, disutility values indicate that interventions should go beyond medi-

absolute utility estimates should be interpreted with caution in the Australian context.
Strengths, Limitations, and Avenues for Future Research
Our study has several strengths. Firstly, our study utilised several waves of data from 
a nationally representative sample to explore longitudinal relationships between 
T2DM and HRQOL using a large, population-based dataset. Using a large sample 
size enhances statistical power, leading to more precise estimates of differences in 
HRQOL between patients with T2DM and the general population and providing 
essential policy implications. Secondly, a wide range of covariates, including poten-
tial confounding chronic conditions and physical activity, were accounted for in our 
analysis. Thirdly, we performed age- and gender-stratified analyses to test whether 
the effect of T2DM on HRQOL varies by age and gender, which is indispensable for 
effective target intervention. Fourthly, our study findings may inform public health 
policies and clinical practices by providing robust evidence on the importance of 
targeted interventions for patients with T2DM to improve their HRQOL. Finally, we 
quantify the disutility associated with T2DM using the SF-6D utility index, which 
provides valuable information for future economic healthcare interventions aimed at 
improving HRQOL of people with T2DM.
Likewise, our study has several limitations. First, participants in the HILDA Sur-
vey provided self-report information, including diagnosed T2DM, which might lead 
to reporting biases. Second, given the observational nature of the data, causal infer-
ence could not be established. Third, this study uses UK-based SF-6D preference 
weights rather than Australian-specific tariffs. While this approach is widely used 
and facilitates comparability, it may influence absolute utility estimates. However, 
relative differences and trajectories are unlikely to be substantially affected. Despite 
these limitations, our study makes a significant contribution to understanding the 
decrement in HRQOL associated with T2DM and found the disutility value asso-
ciated with T2DM, which is vital for future economic evaluations. Our findings 
suggest possible avenues for further research. For example, future studies may inves-
tigate the causal relationship between T2DM and HRQOL among middle-aged and 
older adults. Future studies may also evaluate the cost-effectiveness of interventions, 
such as exercise programs or physical therapy, designed to improve the HRQOL of

people with T2DM. Additionally, our study offers new avenues for exploring the 
mechanisms behind mental resilience in the face of physical decline among people 
with T2DM. Understanding these factors could lead to more effective interven-
tions to enhance the HRQOL of people with T2DM. Future research should exam-
ine dynamic changes in HRQoL following T2DM diagnosis to determine whether 
declines emerge immediately or accumulate over time. It would also be valuable to 
assess whether the adverse impact is greater among socioeconomically disadvan -
taged groups or among those with poorer health-related behaviours, such as physi-
cal inactivity, smoking, and obesity. Future economic evaluations should combine 
longitudinal utility estimates with intervention costs, healthcare utilisation, survival 
and duration of health benefits to calculate QALYs and cost-effectiveness directly. 
Moreover, future studies should examine how disutility among T2DM changes lon-
gitudinally and how it is modified by specific interventions (behavioural, lifestyle, 
service-delivery) (Wang & Poder, 2023).
Conclusion
Using a large population-based longitudinal dataset spanning four survey waves 
(2009–2021), this study found that T2DM was associated with poorer physical 
health-related quality of life and lower health-state utility among Australian adults. 
Overall, T2DM was associated with lower PCS scores and SF-6D utility values, 
whereas no significant association was observed with the MCS after adjustment for 
confounders. Subgroup analyses demonstrated that these negative associations var-
ied across population groups. The negative associations with PCS and SF-6D were 
most evident among men and adults aged 60 years and older. Lower PCS was also 
observed among participants undertaking less than the recommended level of phys-
ical activity, both participants with and without disability, individuals in the third 
income quintile, former and current smokers, and current alcohol consumers. Lower 
SF-6D utility values were additionally observed among participants with disability, 
current smokers, and current alcohol consumers. Collectively, these findings suggest 
that T2DM disproportionately affects physical functioning and health utility across 
several demographic, behavioural, and health-related population subgroups.
These findings reinforce the importance of preventing T2DM and addressing its 
broader consequences for physical functioning and health utility. Comprehensive dia-
betes prevention and management should incorporate routine assessment of HRQOL 
alongside clinical outcomes and support healthy lifestyles through regular physical 
activity, smoking cessation, moderation of alcohol consumption, and a improved 
diet. Greater attention to population subgroups experiencing a higher HRQOL bur-
den may facilitate more targeted and equitable diabetes care. The estimated SF-6D 
utility decrements provide T2DM-specific health utility estimates for future quality-
adjusted life-year (QALY)-based economic evaluations of diabetes prevention and 
management strategies. Future research should determine whether targeted lifestyle 
and clinical interventions can improve HRQOL, reduce healthcare utilisation and 
costs, and provide sustained clinical and economic benefits among these higher-risk 
population groups.
