Abstract14
Green stormwater infrastructure (GSI) is widely regarded as an important nature-15
based solution to combat the combined effects of urbanization and climate change,16
yet its efficacy varies substantially across locations. GSI has not been evaluated17
at broader scales due to complexity in representing local hydrology in larger scale18
models. Using an urban-resolving Earth system model, we assess the suitability19
of rain gardens, a form of ground-based GSI, in runoff reduction in urban areas20
across the contiguous United States. We find strong influence of background cli-21
mates on the runoff reduction potential across cities. Our results demonstrate22
effective runoff reduction in colder and wetter climates from widespread urban23
greening via installation of rain gardens compared with arid climates, possi-24
bly enhancing resilience and livability in certain cities. Most runoff reduction25
was due to enhanced urban evapotranspiration rather than increased infiltration.26
However, not all urban areas are optimal for GSI implementation, likely due to27
influence of background climate.28

Introduction29
Climate change affects urban environments as built infrastructure continues to age and30
worldwide urban populations increase1. Urban flooding and runoff have become promi-31
nent issues for many cities and regions, arising from a combination of urban growth,32
development in floodplains, altered precipitation patterns, and increases in impervi-33
ous surfaces2. In addition, cities face urban heat island effects and more temperature34
extremes3, 4, 5, 6. Urban infrastructures in particular are at risk from hydroclimatic35
extremes associated with climate change if they are stressed by age and demand, or36
are located near coastlines, storm tracks, rivers, and arid areas of vegetation7. Coupled37
together, these factors stress the urban landscape and create livability challenges.38
Climate models consistently project that frequency, duration, and severity of39
hydroclimatic extremes will increase over this century under climate change 8, 9. There40
is thus a pressing need to advance understanding of how climate adaptation strate-41
gies can mitigate the negative impacts from climate change coupled with urban42
development4, 10. Many modeling studies use mesoscale weather forecasting models,43
such as the Weather Research Forecasting Model (WRF) 11, to investigate adaptation44
strategies such as green roofs, cool roofs, and urban reforestation for heat adapta-45
tion12, 13, 14, 15, 16, often with a focus on cooling benefits. However, mesocale modeling46
has a high computational demand, with most studies focusing on events in single47
cities12. Utilizing global climate models (GCMs) to model urban adaptation strategies48
at a subgrid level reduces computational costs and promotes study of multiple urban49
areas on a global scale 3, 17. Prior studies have used GCMs like the Community Earth50
System Model (CESM) to study the impacts of urban adaptation strategies such as51
green roofs, white roofs, reflective pavement, and street vegetation 3, 18, 19, 20. However,52
the hydroclimatic effects of in-ground vegetated green stormwater infrastructure have53
rarely been assessed across scales, primarily due to a lack of representation of the54
complex hydrologic processes associated with those systems.55

Green stormwater infrastructure (GSI) has been commonly proposed as one56
promising tactic to combat some of the effects of urbanization and climate change,57
such as greater urban flooding and decreased water quality, and increase resilience and58
livability21, 22. GSI is unique in that it has potential to provide both hydrologic and59
thermodynamic benefits 23, 24, 3. In-ground vegetated GSI systems provide benefits due60
to their mimicry of and connection to the natural hydrologic cycle24, including vegeta-61
tion and hydrologic complexity, though these systems are not often well modeled across62
different locations. Ground-based GSI can capture rain where it falls, decreasing the63
amount of surface runoff, helping alleviate urban flooding issues, treating stormwater,64
and providing cooling through evapotranspiration, among other functions23, 24, 3. How-65
ever, different types of GSI are not always suitable solutions due to prevailing weather66
patterns, soil type, and other factors25. Additionally, while GSI is a promising tactic, it67
also has environmental justice ramifications depending on how it is implemented 26, 27.68
Typical studies determining GSI effectiveness and suitability are location-specific69
or completed on a small scale 21, 28. For this work, we consider local scale to refer to70
parcels to city scale, and regional scale to refer to scales larger than a single city.71
Local work is extensive and focuses on runoff reduction, evapotranspiration, infiltra-72
tion, and by nature is context specific 29, 30, 21. While upscaling work can be done to73
go from site-specific to city-level, it typically requires intimate knowledge of the sewer74
systems and other hydraulic infrastructure in place 29. As such, local scale studies are75
better suited to evaluating site-specific performance than to generating broadly trans-76
ferable findings. Regional-scale assessments, while less useful for individual site-specific77
performance prediction, do reveal general suitability patterns within different climate78
forcing contexts and allow for comparative analyses between locations. For this rea-79
son, it serves as a compelling complement to local scale work, providing an initial80
screening of potential implementation locations as well as identifying key processes81
and factors that should receive more attention in local studies. By identifying where82

Additionally, despite its climate change adaptation potential, there remain bar-93

Methodology117
Community Land Model Urban118
In this study, we utilized the Community Earth System Model (CESM) 36. CESM is119
a fully-coupled, global-scale climate model hosted by the National Center for Atmo-120
spheric Research (NCAR) and researchers in the Earth system science community 37.121
CESM contains seven components that can communicate through a coupler: the atmo-122
sphere, land, land ice, ocean, sea ice, river, and wave model 37, 36. Currently, CESM123
is one of the few GCMs with an urban land parameterization as part of its land124
model38, 5, providing a key motivation for its use in this study. The Community Ter-125
restrial Systems Model (CTSM), the land component of CESM (formerly known as126
the Community Land Model (CLM)), focuses mainly on how terrestrial ecosystems127
and their various chemical, biological, and physical processes can affect and be affected128
by climate. Grid cells in CTSM are made up of five land units: vegetated, glacier,129
crop, lake, and urban. CTSM has been evaluated widely against both in situ and130
remote sensing observations around the globe 39, 40, 41, 42. The urban parameterization131
scheme embedded within CTSM (CLMU), simulates “local urban climate as an oper-132
ational component of a global climate model” 43. CLMU models the energy, radiation,133

Each grid cell in CTSM is composed of different percentages of each land unit. The137
model computes outputs for each land unit and its allocated area, and for some output138
variables, will calculate an area-weighted average over the whole grid cell area. CLMU139
includes an embedded global urban surface dataset developed for global climate mod-140

and properties for every grid cell containing an “urban” subgrid land unit. Informa-142
tion provided includes radiative, thermal, and morphological characteristics of urban143
areas38.144
The urban scheme in CLMU is based on an “urban canyon” conceptual structure,145
comprised of wall (sunlit and shaded), canyon floor (pervious and impervious), and146
roof facets 17. A schematic representation of CLMU and its role within the broader147
gridcell is displayed in Figure 1. Importantly, Figure 1 illustrates that CLMU only148
makes up only a portion of the overall gridcell. Adequate biophysical and hydrolog-149
ical processes are captured over these facets 17, 43. For example, the pervious floor of150
the canyon represents surfaces able to infiltrate water. Liquid water can pond on roofs151
and canyon floors, supporting evaporation. Water in excess of the maximum ponding152
depth or snowmelt water on both roofs and floors runs off. Pervious canyon floor evap-153

the wetness of the entire soil column (to mimic vegetation roots) and water is removed155

phological, and hydrological characteristics of these facets for each grid cell in CTSM is157
determined by satellite-derived data45. For the purposes of our simulations, values for158

examine hydrologic performance at a specific location. While such studies are useful175
from a hydrologic and runoff management perspective, they are limited in scope and176
are unable to provide information on broader suitability trends across locations and177
climates. While we do sacrifice some hydrologic precision by utilizing a GCM, we are178

Validation of Rain Garden Hydrology247
The CLMU itself has been evaluated extensively against remote sensing and in248
situ observations on urban temperature and surface energy balance across the249
globe28, 39, 40, 41, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59. Previous work has validated CESM’s capability to250
robustly represent the hydrologic cycle and specifically total runoff by gridcell 42.251
Because urban hydrologic data are scarce, urban runoff has not been directly validated.252
However, since the water balance and cycling are closely tracked in each landunit in253
CESM, including CLMU, Gray et al.’s evaluation, even in the absence of measured254
urban runoff data at a global scale, can provide confidence and indicative information255
in CESM’s urban hydrologic modeling 42.256
To ensure the robustness of our simulation results, we further validated our new257
rain garden parameterization against published reports. There is a robust and growing258
body of literature on rain garden pilot installations and experiments in the United259

meaning that water availability is the main limiting factor in ecological processes85, 86,486
which explains why there is little runoff reduction in arid areas. Temperate and cold487

to their stronger seasonality. Cold regions in CONUS experience four strong seasons491
with wide temperature ranges, moreso than temperate climates 87. In these seasons,492

Discussion606
For many municipalities and urban areas, GSI has been proposed as a promising cli-607
mate adaptation strategy due to potential flood mitigation benefits. From a hydrologic608
perspective, GSI implementation can reduce surface water runoff, due largely to infil-609
tration and evapotranspiration, and potentially alleviate urban flooding, if extensively610
implemented89, 90. Vegetated GSI also can provide cooler “microclimate” areas due611
to evapotranspiration, affecting urban meteorology 91. An increase in vegetative cover612
offers more possibilities for carbon sequestration, helps improve air quality, and also613
provides a habitat for biodiversity 92. Because GSI offers a variety of benefits across614
sectors, it can be seen as a potentially appealing option for many municipalities with615
aging infrastructure. However, the results from this study illustrate that not all cities616
are suitable candidates for rain garden implementation from a hydrologic perspective.617
Based on this work, urban areas in the Midwest, Northeast, and those bordering the618
Gulf of Mexico would be more suitable areas to implement rain gardens for runoff619
reduction, whereas urban areas across most of the American West and Southeast show620
little to no runoff reduction across the 10-year study period. Some areas in the Pacific621
Northwest demonstrate increases in runoff in our simulations, likely primarily due to622
a decrease in evapotranspiration and corresponding increase in subsurface runoff.623
A significant portion of the runoff reduction delivered by our parameterization624
was due to enhanced urban evapotranspiration rather than just increased infiltra-625
tion. While most GSI is designed to reduce and capture runoff through infiltration,626
experimental studies have also demonstrated the importance of evapotranspiration in627
GSI water balances75, 76. This combination of infiltration and evapotranspiration pro-628
vides credence to our parameterization’s implicit vegetation representation and ability629
to mimic vegetative processes. Our results show that in a region without increased630
evapotranspiration (namely urban areas in Washington and Oregon) there was more631
subsurface runoff occurring, indicating that more water was present in the soil with632

Conclusion790
Many cities understandably have concerns about implementing GSI systems into their791
municipal infrastructure. There is high uncertainty for many urban planners surround-792
ing GSI suitability for their specific location, particularly since GSI systems are much793
more variable in performance than their grey stormwater infrastructure counterparts.794
Additionally, for GSI systems to maintain functionality, they need to be properly main-795
tained and upkept (i.e., cleared of debris that can cause clogging, etc.) 34. While our796
results provide information about the potential suitability of widespread rain gardens797
across many locations, the runoff reduction functionality of the systems does not con-798
sider the holistic socio-political context that could be useful for decision makers. Our799
work serves as a large-scale suitability assessment, identifying important processes and800
factors that should receive more attention in local scale studies, but lacks the ability801
to incorporate site-specific nuances. Other work has noted that GSI implementation802
can have environmental justice implications, particularly since access to green space803
is often inherently political and does have economic impacts as well 26, 27. Prior work804
advises that when making decisions about GSI implementation, urban planners and805
policymakers need to consider the impacts of not just the functionality of GSI systems,806
but how the location of GSI will impact the community and the different populations807
within the community26, 27. Prior work also advises that community engagement with808
GSI planning and maintenance processes is vital, especially to ensure longevity and809
enhance resilience for the impacted communities 106, 107. Thus, while our results pro-810
vide insight into suitability of rain gardens across the contiguous United States, they811
only capture one aspect of GSI implementation and must be evaluated in context.812
Despite these limitations, our rain garden parameterization successfully bridges813
microscale vegetation processes to a macroscale climate model. While some artifacts of814
the model are reflected in our results and some uncertainty exists in how we represent815

Northeast, as well as in states bordering the Gulf of Mexico.831
Through this work, we leveraged modeled data detailing potential hydrologic832
impacts of ground-based, vegetated GSI systems to advance knowledge on rain garden833
suitability for diverse areas and contexts. This research builds on previous studies com-834
pleted on the mesoscale and local levels, and provides data on rain garden suitability835
trends on a larger scale. Such information could have important policy implications836
for U.S. municipal leaders weighing GSI investments, such as rain gardens, with837
