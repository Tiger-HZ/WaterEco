A randomized placebo-controlled study evaluating the 
efficacy of a multi-nutrient supplement on lower urinary 
tract health in male cats with feline idiopathic cystitis
Wan-Jing Chen · Kai-Wen Huang
Received: 23 April 2026 / Accepted: 7 September 2026
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract
Feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC) is the most common diagnosis within the spectrum of feline lower urinary tract 
disease (FLUTD), accounting for approximately 60–65% of cases presenting with lower urinary tract signs 
(LUTS), particularly in male cats with narrow urethras. FIC is now recognized as a systemic stress-sensitive 
disorder characterized by neuroendocrine dysregulation and urothelial barrier dysfunction, rather than a purely 
local bladder disease. Multimodal environmental modification (MEMO) is the cornerstone of therapy, but 
recurrence rates remain high, and many cats experience repeated episodes despite environmental optimiza -
tion. This prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled trial evaluated the efficacy of a novel multi-nutrient 
supplement (HT-3) containing cranberry extract and marigold extract, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), inulin, 
spirulina, and L-lysine as an adjunct to standardized MEMO in neutered male cats with recurrent FIC. We 
hypothesized that HT-3 would significantly reduce LUTS severity, prolong the disease-free interval (DFI), 
reduce the recurrence rate, and favorably modulate urinary biomarkers and behavior compared with MEMO 
plus placebo. Thirty neutered male cats with documented recurrent FIC were enrolled in a 6-month random -
ized placebo-controlled exploratory pilot study. All cats received a strictly standardized MEMO protocol and 
were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either HT-3 or an isocaloric placebo. The primary endpoints were 
cumulative LUTS scores, recurrence rate, and DFI at 6 months. The secondary endpoints included urinaly -
sis parameters, urinary cortisol: creatinine ratio (UCCR), urinary glycosaminoglycan: creatinine ratio (GAG: 
Cr), and behavioral scores. Short-term (2-week), intermediate (3-month), and long-term (6-month) responses 
were analyzed. The baseline characteristics were comparable between the groups. Both groups showed early 
improvement at 2 weeks following MEMO implementation; however, the HT-3 group demonstrated a signifi -
cantly greater reduction in LUTS scores by week 4 (− 63.8% vs. −39.3%, p = 0.01) and maintained lower scores 
through month 6 (1.2 ± 0.6 vs. 2.8 ± 1.0, p = 0.002). The recurrence rate over 6 months was 13.3% (2/15) in 
the HT-3 group versus 40.0% (6/15) in the placebo group ( p = 0.05). Mean DFI was longer in the HT-3 group 
(162 ± 15 vs. 110 ± 35 days, p = 0.01), and Kaplan–Meier analysis showed a significantly higher recurrence-free 
probability over 6 months (log-rank p = 0.03). The HT-3 group showed greater reductions in UCCR (− 45% 
vs. −20%, p = 0.05) and a significant increase in urinary GAG: Cr (+ 25% vs. no change, p = 0.02). Behavioral 
scores showed greater reductions in fear-related and hiding behaviors, together with greater improvements in

Introduction
 Feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC), the most common diagnosis of feline lower urinary tract disease (FLUTD), is a 
major cause of lower urinary tract signs (LUTS) in cats and is increasingly recognized as a multifactorial, stress-
sensitive disorder involving interactions among the bladder, neuroendocrine responses, environment, and care -
giver-related factors1. Cats with FIC exhibit exaggerated systemic stress responses to environmental challenges, 
which can be quantified using behavioral measures and stress biomarkers such as the urinary cortisol: creatinine 
ratio (UCCR)2. Epidemiological studies indicate that without effective long-term therapy, historical cohorts of 
cats with a history of FLUTD exhibit remarkably high relapse rates, with approximately 30% to 50% of affected 
individuals experiencing recurrent episodes within 6 to 12 months3.
Multimodal environmental modification (MEMO) is widely recommended as a core management strategy, but 
recurrence remains common, and additional supportive approaches are often used in clinical practice4.
Beyond its medical implications, FIC substantially impacts both feline welfare and the caregiver’s quality 
of life. Periuria is consistently cited as one of the leading non-medical reasons for relinquishing cats to shelters 
and for euthanasia, underscoring the profound emotional and practical burden that chronic LUTS imposes on 
caregivers. Recurrent emergency visits for urethral obstruction, the need for hospitalization and catheterization, 
and the constant fear of recurrence contribute to caregiver stress, financial strain, and, in some cases, “caregiver 
fatigue” or anticipatory grief 2,3,5,6. From the cat’s perspective, repeated flares are associated with chronic pain, 
increased hiding, and sickness behaviors, indicating a marked deterioration in the overall quality of life2,4. These 
bidirectional welfare effects highlight the need for treatment strategies that not only reduce LUTS and prevent 
obstruction but also address pain, stress, and owner–cat relationship breakdown in a holistic manner3.
However, spontaneous remission and relapse, owner compliance limitations, and the multifactorial nature 
of FIC limit the efficacy of MEMO alone, and a substantial proportion of cats continue to experience recurrent 
episodes despite appropriate environmental interventions. Therefore, nutritional and nutraceutical strategies have 
attracted increasing interest. Trials of oral glucosamine and chondroitin aimed at supporting the GAG layer have 
produced mixed results, with several randomized studies failing to show a clear reduction in recurrence compared 
with placebo, possibly due to limited bioavailability and the inability to address systemic stress or inflammation7. 
In contrast, previous dietary intervention studies in FIC may have influenced not only stress-related pathways but 
also local inflammatory and urinary tract mechanisms through nutrient composition, including altered sodium 
content and supplementation with fish oils, vitamin E, DHA, and lysine8.
HT-3 (Harvest Tree Valor Pets, Singapore), which contains cranberry and marigold extracts, docosahexaenoic 
acid (DHA), inulin, spirulina, and L-lysine, was developed to provide a multi-target approach. Cranberry and 
marigold extracts supply A-type proanthocyanidins that inhibit bacterial adhesion and may reduce oxidative dam-
age to the urothelium9. DHA, an omega-3 fatty acid, is metabolized into pro-resolving mediators, such as resolvins 
and protectins, which actively terminate inflammation rather than merely suppressing it10. L-lysine, widely used 
in feline herpesvirus management, may contribute to immune modulation and reduction of stress-associated viral 
reactivation, which is hypothesized to exacerbate FIC in some cats 11. Inulin is a well-known prebiotic that con -
sists of long-chain β-fructans. Its potential role in FIC is linked to the modification of the “gut-bladder/gut-kidney

endpoints over 6 months.
Materials and methods
Study design and ethical approval
This was a 6-month, prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial conducted at the 
National Taiwan University Hospital and a collaborating feline practice. The study protocol was reviewed and 
approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) prior to commencement (No. 20260022), 
and written informed consent was obtained from all owners before enrollment. Adverse events (AEs) were graded 
according to the Veterinary Cooperative Oncology Group–Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events 
version 2 (VCOG-CTCAE v2) 14. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and 
regulations. Household social environment was documented at baseline. All enrolled cats were from single-cat 
households; therefore, intercat aggression related to multicat living was not a variable in this cohort. All cats were 
maintained on the same pre-existing diet with mixed food.
Study population
Neutered male domestic cats aged 2–8 years with a documented history of at least two FIC episodes in the pre -
ceding 12 months based on veterinary records were eligible. To ensure baseline diagnostic and chronological uni-
formity for subsequent disease-free interval tracking, all subjects were required to be in clinical remission at the 
time of study entry, defined as an absence of active LUTS for a minimum quiescent window of 21 days following 
the complete resolution of their last historical FIC episode. The diagnosis of FIC requires compatible clinical 
signs (dysuria, pollakiuria, hematuria, and periuria) and exclusion of other causes by physical examination, uri -
nalysis, urine culture, and abdominal ultrasound examination. Cats with radiographically or ultrasonographically 
confirmed uroliths requiring specific therapy, bacterial urinary tract infection, neoplasia, chronic kidney disease 
(serum creatinine level > 180 µmol/L) or urethral obstruction were excluded. The baseline study diagnosis was 
confirmed using a standardized evaluation including physical examination, urinalysis, urine culture, and imaging. 
We acknowledge that the diagnostic workup applied during historical episodes may not have been completely 
identical at every prior episode.
Thirty cats met the criteria for inclusion in this study (Table 1). To reduce confounding, only neutered males were 
included, acknowledging that this limits generalizability but reflects the group at the highest risk of obstruction.
MEMO and interventions
The cats were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either HT-3 (treatment group) or isocaloric placebo powder 
(control group) using a computer-generated block randomization schedule prepared by an independent investiga-
tor not involved in enrollment or outcome assessment. HT-3 was supplied by Harvest Tree Valor Pets (Singapore) 
and was provided as a palatable powder formulation for the present study. Treatment allocation was concealed 
in sequentially numbered identical sachets. Owners, clinicians, and laboratory assessors remained blinded until 
database lock. All cats, regardless of group, underwent a standardized MEMO protocol at baseline, including the 
provision of multiple litter boxes (number of cats + 1) in quiet locations, multiple feeding and resting stations,

vertical spaces and hiding places, daily interactive play sessions, synthetic feline facial pheromone diffusers, and 
promotion of water intake via wet diets and water fountains 4. After enrollment and baseline evaluation, all cats 
began the assigned study treatment and the standardized MEMO protocol at the same baseline timepoint.
HT-3 was supplied as a palatable powder. Each sachet contained 150 mg cranberry extract, 100 mg marigold 
extract, 50 mg DHA, 250 mg L-lysine, 70 mg inulin, and 100 mg spirulina. The placebo was a matched isocaloric 
powder identical in appearance, texture, and administration schedule. The owners mixed one sachet daily into the 
cat’s main meal with a small amount of canned food. Adherence was monitored using sachet counts and owner 
logs, with ≥ 90% consumption considered acceptable compliance.
Clinical, laboratory and behavioral outcome measures
A multi-dimensional assessment strategy was employed to capture short- and long-term treatment effects. Behav-
ior was assessed monthly using an owner-completed 4-domain score including hiding, social interaction, play 
activity, and fear-related behavior. Each domain was rated from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating greater 
impairment. Owners were trained at baseline using standardized examples.
Lower urinary tract signs (LUTS)
The owners were trained to complete a daily diary recording using a numerical rating scale adapted from vali -
dated feline lower urinary tract sign scoring protocols15 as follow:
 ● The frequency of urination attempts per 24 h (pollakiuria).
 ● Presence and intensity of dysuria/stranguria (straining, vocalization, and prolonged posture).
 ● Macroscopic hematuria was also noted.
 ● Occurrence of periuria (urination outside the litter box).
Each domain was scored from 0 (absent) to 3 (severe), yielding a daily cumulative LUTS score of 0–12. To evalu-
ate clinical progression over time without compounding data artifact, baseline values represented a single-day 
assessment prior to treatment. The scores reported for subsequent milestones (Week 2, Week 4, Month 3, and 
Month 6) were calculated as the mean daily cumulative LUTS score across the 7 consecutive days preceding that 
specific follow-up evaluation node. Clinical efficacy responders for short-term evaluation were defined a priori 
as subjects achieving at least a 50% reduction in the cumulative LUTS score compared to baseline by Week 
4. For intermediate (3 months) and long-term (6 months) efficacy assessments, evaluations were based on the 
maintenance of absolute score reductions and continuous monitoring of recurrence rates, rather than the categori-
cal 50% threshold. A recurrent episode was defined as a LUTS score ≥ 2 lasting > 24 h after an initial period of 
near-remission (LUTS ≤ 1 for ≥ 7 days). A composite LUTS score was used to summarize global clinical burden 
over time; however, each component (pollakiuria, dysuria/stranguria, hematuria, and periuria) was also recorded 
individually to preserve clinical interpretability.

Urinalysis and urinary biomarkers
Urine samples were collected using nonabsorbent hydrophobic litter at baseline and monthly rechecks. Standard 
urinalysis included urine specific gravity (USG), pH, sediment examination (crystals per high-power field), and 
dipstick assessment for blood and protein levels. Urinalysis was performed on freshly collected urine samples. 
Urine specific gravity was measured using a refractometer (ATAGO Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), urine pH was mea-
sured using calibrated pH dipstick (MACHEREY-NAGEL GmbH & Co. KG, Düren, Germany), and sediment 
examination was performed by light microscopy after centrifugation. Crystalluria was recorded as present or 
absent and, when applicable, semi-quantitatively described.
The urinary cortisol: creatinine ratio (UCCR) was measured by cobas c analyzer and e immunoassay analyzer 
(Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland). From morning urine samples collected by owners at home to minimize stress, 
serving as a noninvasive index of systemic stress load2. Urinary GAG: Cr ratio was measured using a dimethyl-
methylene blue (DMMB) assay16,17.
Statistical analysis
Because no prior randomized data were available for this formulation in cats with recurrent FIC, this study was 
designed as a preliminary pilot trial, and no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. Statistical 
analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics (version 26.0). Continuous variables were assessed for normal -
ity using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group comparisons, including changes from baseline in LUTS score, 
UCCR, and GAG: Cr, were performed using the independent-samples t test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appro -
priate. Categorical variables, including recurrence rates and the proportion of cats with residual crystalluria, were 
compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Unless otherwise stated, p values were 
two-sided and represented between-group comparisons, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
The primary endpoints were cumulative LUTS scores, recurrence rate, and DFI at 6 months. The secondary 
endpoints included urinalysis parameters, UCCR, GAG: Cr. The time to first recurrence (DFI) was analyzed 
using Kaplan–Meier survival curves with log-rank testing; cats without recurrence were censored at day 180.
Results
Clinical responses
All 30 randomized cats completed the 6-month follow-up and were included in the final analysis. Both groups 
showed a reduction in LUTS scores within the first 2 weeks after study initiation. By day 14, mean LUTS 
scores decreased from 5.8 ± 1.2 to 3.9 ± 1.0 in the HT-3 group and from 5.6 ± 1.4 to 4.1 ± 1.1 in the placebo group 
(between-group p = 0.69), indicating comparable early responses (Table 2).
By week 4, however, a greater separation between groups emerged: mean LUTS scores were 2.1 ± 0.8 in the 
HT-3 group versus 3.4 ± 1.1 in the placebo group (p = 0.01). Short-term efficacy, defined a priori as at least a 50% 
reduction in LUTS score by week 4, was achieved in 80% (12/15) of the HT-3 cats compared with 47% (7/15) of 
the placebo cats (p = 0.04).
At 3 months, mean LUTS scores were 1.5 ± 0.7 in the HT-3 group and 2.9 ± 0.9 in the placebo group (p = 0.003). 
By 6 months, mean LUTS scores were 1.2 ± 0.6 in the HT-3 group vs. 2.8 ± 1.0 in the placebo group ( p = 0.002) 
(Table 2).
Mean DFI was longer in the HT-3 group than in the placebo group (162 ± 15 days [95% CI: 154–170] vs. 
110 ± 35 days [95% CI: 92–128], p = 0.01). Kaplan–Meier analysis also showed a significantly higher recurrence-
free probability over 6 months in the HT-3 group (log-rank p = 0.03) (Fig. 1). During follow-up, no episodes of 
urethral obstruction were noted.

Discussion
This randomized placebo-controlled study provides preliminary evidence that HT-3, when administered along -
side standardized MEMO over a 6-month evaluation period, was associated with statistically significant improve-
ments in clinical, laboratory, and behavioral outcomes in male cats with recurrent FIC.
In the short term (first 2–4 weeks), both groups improved; however, the HT-3 group lower LUTS scores over 
follow-up than the placebo group. The reduction in recurrence rate from 40% to 13.3% and the nearly 50% pro -
longation of DFI are clinically relevant findings, given the chronic and relapse-prone nature of FIC.
The observed changes in UCCR and GAG: Cr require nuanced interpretation. While a reduction in UCCR 
aligns with improved HPA axis adaptation to chronic stress, changes in the urinary GAG: Cr ratio should be 
interpreted with caution. Although historical literature documented low urinary GAG concentrations in certain 
cats with FIC17, contemporary evidence indicates that fluctuations in urinary GAG excretion may reflect urothe-
lial desquamation, shedding, or active mucosal repair processes rather than acting as a static structural marker of 
barrier enhancement18. Given that numerous controlled clinical trials evaluating oral, subcutaneous, or intravesi-
cal GAG administration have failed to demonstrate clinical superiority over placebos 7,19, the increase in urinary 
GAG: Cr observed in the HT-3 group should not be interpreted as definitive proof of direct structural restoration 
of the urothelial barrier.
Although recurrent FIC often follows a waxing-and-waning course, the trajectory is not uniformly progres -
sive in all affected cats. Clinical improvement observed during the present study may have been influenced not 
only by the study product but also by concurrent environmental standardization, hydration-related interventions, 
dietary factors, and the natural variability of disease expression20. Accordingly, the observed associations should 
be interpreted cautiously.
The current therapeutic approach to FIC is inherently multimodal. In the acute setting, treatment focuses 
on pain relief and restoration of urine flow in the presence of obstruction. Analgesics, such as opioids (e.g., 
buprenorphine) and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, are commonly employed to alleviate discomfort 3, 
while urethral obstruction necessitates catheterization, fluid therapy, and intensive monitoring to correct life-
threatening electrolyte and acid–base disturbances. In some cases, urethral relaxants, antispasmodics, or anti-
anxiety medications (e.g., tricyclic antidepressants) are used empirically, although evidence supporting their 
long-term preventive efficacy is limited, and concerns regarding adverse effects and the need for monitoring 
restrict their use to selected patients2,3,5,7.
For chronic management, MEMO is widely recognized as a fundamental element of long-term therapy. How-
ever, it is important to note that the landmark clinical evaluation of MEMO by Buffington et al.2,4, was an uncon-
trolled cohort study where a subset of cats received concurrent pharmacological interventions and the majority 
underwent a dry-to-canned dietary transition. Because cats in that historical cohort received multi-modal adjust-
ments beyond isolated environmental modification, separating the specific efficacy of spatial enrichment from 
nutritional and medical factors remains challenging. In parallel, prescription urinary diets with controlled mineral 
content, urine-acidifying properties, and enhanced moisture are commonly used to decrease the risk of crystal -
luria and promote adequate water intake; however, these diets alone do not fully address the stress-related and 
neuroendocrine aspects of the disease 2. Despite these measures, recurrence rates of 30–50% over 6–12 months 
are still reported in many cohorts, and a proportion of cats continue to experience waxing and waning signs that 
are distressing to both the patient and caregiver. Buffington3, This therapeutic gap has led to widespread interest 
in nutraceuticals and supplements that may offer additional benefits with a favorable safety profile.

A variety of nutritional supplements are currently marketed for feline urinary health, including oral glucos -
amine and chondroitin sulfate (intended to support the bladder GAG layer), N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, cran -
berry-containing products, omega-3 fatty acid formulations, and stress-modulating diets or treats enriched with 
L-tryptophan, alpha-casozepine, or milk protein hydrolysates7,8,17, However, the evidence base for many of these 
products remains limited. Randomized placebo-controlled trials of oral GAG precursors have reported no clear 
reduction in FIC recurrence compared with placebo, suggesting that GAG support alone is insufficient to over -
come the complex systemic nature of the disorder, particularly when issues of oral bioavailability and interindi -
vidual variability are considered7.
Cranberry-based supplements, widely extrapolated from human urinary tract infection literature, have attracted 
interest in feline medicine because A-type proanthocyanidins can inhibit bacterial adhesion to urothelial cells in 
vitro and may exert local antioxidant effects21. Nonetheless, controlled data in cats are sparse, and most available 
studies are small, open-label, or focus on surrogate outcomes rather than hard clinical endpoints such as recur -
rence rate or DFI. Similarly, diets or chews enriched with L-tryptophan or alpha-casozepine have been shown to 
reduce anxiety-related behaviors and may contribute to a reduction in LUTS in some cats; however, their effects 
appear modest and inconsistent when used as sole adjuncts, likely because they primarily target central stress 
pathways without directly modulating bladder inflammation or urothelial barrier function8.
Against this background, the present study is notable in that HT-3 was designed as a deliberately multi-target 
formulation, combining cranberry extract (urothelial and antioxidant support), DHA (pro-resolving anti-inflam -
matory actions), and L-lysine (immune and stress-related modulation), and was evaluated under rigorous ran -
domized, double-blind, placebo-controlled conditions with MEMO standardized in both groups. The observed 
reductions in LUTS, recurrence events, and UCCR, together with the increase in GAG: Cr and improvements in 
behavioral measures, suggest that a combined approach targeting stress, inflammation, and urothelial integrity 
may overcome some of the limitations observed with single-ingredient supplements or diets2,3.
This study had several limitations. First, only neutered male cats were included, which enhanced the internal 
validity but limited the generalizability to female cats, which may differ in both pathophysiology and clinical 
course. Second, the sample size was modest, and the findings should therefore be interpreted as preliminary 
pending confirmation in larger multicenter studies. Third, the primary analysis emphasized the composite LUTS 
score, which may not fully capture the distinct clinical meaning of individual urinary signs such as isolated 
hematuria or periuria. Fourth, the prior FIC episodes were identified from clinical records and may not have been 
evaluated using an identical diagnostic protocol at each previous presentation, Finally, all enrolled subjects were 
recruited exclusively from single-cat households. Because inter-cat aggression and social stress in multi-cat envi-
ronments represent common risk factors and comorbidities in cats with FIC, the efficacy of this intervention was 
not evaluated under multi-cat dynamic stressors, which limits the generalizability of these findings to multi-cat 
households.
Future research should explore the efficacy of HT-3 in female cats and mixed-sex populations, examine longer-
term outcomes beyond 6 months, and compare this multi-nutrient approach head-to-head with other dietary or 
pharmacological interventions. Mechanistic studies using more detailed neuroendocrine profiling or heart rate 
variability monitoring could further elucidate how these supplements modulate stress pathways in FIC.
Conclusions
In this preliminary pilot randomized placebo-controlled study, the study intervention was associated with favor-
able changes in clinical signs and selected exploratory measures in neutered male cats with recurrent FIC. The 
multi-nutrient formulation HT-3 demonstrated clinical utility as a supportive adjunct within a holistic FIC man -
agement strategy. The findings support further evaluation of this multi-nutrient approach in larger studies with 
stricter dietary control, longer follow-up, and validated clinical outcome tools.
