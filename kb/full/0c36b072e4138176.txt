The cascading effects of meteorological droughts on river ﬂows and water quality in mountainous
regions are poorly understood. Using a data-driven framework, we studied drought impacts on
streamﬂow, water temperature (WT), speci ﬁc conductance (SC) in the Upper Colorado River Basin
(1998-2022). Severe meteorological droughts in 2001-2002, 2012, 2018, 2020-2021 reduced annual
streamﬂows by 15%, increased WT by 9% and SC by 5% relative to normal adjacent years. Declines
were greatest in spring and summer, with reduced peak ﬂows. Streamﬂow and SC recovered until
2018, after which successive droughts prolonged impacts, whereas WT effects were transient.
Catchments with higher freshwater withdrawals, deciduous forest cover, air temperatures, residence
times, ﬁner soils had greater stream ﬂow declines, while higher subsurface ﬂows and in ﬁltration
enabled resilience. WT increases were associated with higher elevation, slopes, precipitation and
lower agriculture, shrub cover. Open water and reservoirs mitigated SC increases. Thus, catchment
attributes inﬂuence drought vulnerability in mountainous regions.
Meteorological droughts are periods with abnormally low water availability
that occur due to low precipitation and can be compounded by elevated air
temperatures1–3. Meteorological drought can p ropagate into hydrological
(reduced terrestrial water availability), agricultural (soil moisture de ﬁcits
affecting crops), and socio-economi c (water demand exceeding supply)
droughts, which can considerably impact ecosystem health and human
societies2,4–6. For example, between 1980 and 2022, drought events in the
United States (US) caused severe economic losses of more than 325 billion
USD7. The frequency, severity and duration of meteorological drought
events is predicted to increase due to climate change7–11.
One of the primary consequences of meteorological drought is reduced
stream ﬂows, which have been observed in many regions around the
world12–16. This can occur for various reasons including reduced recharge
from decreased precipitation2, higher temperatures leading to increased
evapotranspiration12,17,18, decreased groundwater or soil moisture
storage15,16,19, and anthropogenic activities such as increased surface water
abstraction or groundwater pumping20. The degree to which stream ﬂows
decline is governed by a combination of climatic factors (e.g., temperature,
precipitation) as well as biophysical (e.g., topography, geology, vegetation)
and anthropogenic (e.g., land use, reservoir management) catchment
attributes12–15,21. Decreases in stream ﬂow can have cascading impacts on
water quality and ecosystem health 2,22–26. For example, declines in water
quality have been observed during dro ught events with increased water
temperatures24,26,27 and salinity25,26. Changes in river thermal or salinity
regimes can have negative effects on local and downstream ecosystems and
aquatic life28. Thus, analyses of catchment v ulnerability to drought, and
corresponding water management decisions to mitigate negative impacts,
should consider the combined effects of changes in streamﬂow and water
quality.
Although there are numerous studies on the impacts of drought in
different types of watersheds, we stil l lack a clear understanding of how
meteorological droughts affect riverﬂows and water quality in mountainous

regions. Mountains are ‘water towers of the world’, contributing approxi-
mately 50% of stream ﬂows globally29. Mountain systems have distinct
hydrological processes that mediate streamﬂow and water quality compared
to lowland regions. In particular, snow is the primary form of precipitation,
with the majority of streamﬂow derived from snowmelt-driven runoff and
groundwater contributions30–32. Stream temperatures are inﬂuenced by air
temperature as well as melting snow and glacier ice33,g r o u n d w a t e r34,a n d
reservoir releases35. In addition, mountainous regions have steep terrain
with large elevation, climatic, and veg etation gradients, and small lower-
order streams, leading to substantial spatial variability in ﬂows and water
quality. Headwater catchments are frequently managed with reservoirs to
regulate downstream water availability and quality. Thus, warming tem-
peratures and shifting precipitation patterns make these regions particularly
susceptible to drought, posing uncertain risks to downstream water users.
The southwestern US, and particularly the mountains in the Colorado
River Basin (CRB), has been identiﬁed as a region susceptible to increased
risk of drought7. The CRB is a critical source of water for over 40 million
people in seven states, numerous tr ibal nations, and northern Mexico 36.
Snowmelt from headwater catchments in the Upper Colorado River Basin
(UCRB) supplies ~90% of the riverﬂows for the entire basin37,38.T h eU C R B
has experienced historically dry and warm conditions since 2000 18,39,40,
which combined with precipitation deﬁcits and consequent reductions in
snowpack have led to reduced stream ﬂow and changes in the timing of
snowmelt17,18,41–45. Several modeling studies have shown that future warming
in the UCRB will lead to earlier snow melt, increased evapotranspiration
losses, decreases in snowpack and gr oundwater, and consequently lower
streamﬂows, which could lead to irreversible aridi ﬁcation of the
region18,32,42,43,46–51. However, the moderate precipitation deﬁcits since 2000
only partially explain the sharp declines in streamﬂows41. The reservoirs in
the basin regulate riverﬂows, holding snowmelt and baseﬂow during high-
ﬂow periods52,53, and releasing water during low-ﬂow seasons. With recent
droughts, the ability of these reservoirs to sustain streamﬂows under future
conditions is in doubt54. The severe decline inﬂows over the last two decades
and the potential long-term shifts in hydroclimatic conditions 55,56 have
necessitated changes to governance and water apportionment across the
entire CRB. Although stakeholdersin the UCRB consider water tempera-
ture and salinity as important water quality parameters57,t h e s ea r em o n -
itored at fewer sites and remain understudied. A couple of studies found
elevated salinity58 and water temperature59 levels at several sites during the
severe drought in 2002. A recent study found that climatic conditions only
explain about 54% of the variability in streamﬂows and 40% of the variability
in salinity, with the remaining drivers being anthropogenic activities or
other watershed processes from 1986 to 202160. Thus, there is a general lack
of understanding of the effects of drought on water quality in the basin. As a
heavily managed basin that supplies critical water resources, the UCRB
exempliﬁes the challenges mountainous basins face during drought.
The natural and human factors that affect the vulnerability of catch-
ment ﬂows and water quality to meteorological drought remain poorly
understood, particularly in mountainous regions. To address this gap, we
investigated the impacts of severe meteorological drought episodes (hen-
ceforth referred to as drought) from 1998 to 2022 on streamﬂows (Q), water
temperature (WT), and salinity (SC) in catchments with different properties
in the UCRB. Our objectives were to (1)quantify the magnitude of changes
in Q, WT, and SC to droughts relativeto adjacent normal years (henceforth
referred to as catchment response to drought), (2) evaluate catchment
recovery following drought episodes, and (3) identify catchment attributes
that inﬂuence spatial variability in responses to drought.
We have developed a robust data-driven framework, DIRE (Drought
Impacts and Resilience Evaluation) to identify catchment response to spe-
ciﬁc drought episodes. Our analysis for this study utilizes long-term stream
monitoring data and a comprehensi ve dataset of 250 catchment char-
acteristics (Geospatial Attributes of Gages for Evaluating Stream ﬂow,
GAGES-II) comprising human (e.g., land use, water management) and
natural factors (e.g., climate, topography, vegetation) from the United States
Geological Survey (USGS). We also usean advanced deep learning model to
predict stream temperatures in unmonitored regions to address data lim-
itations. The DIRE framework ﬁrst applies an event-based metric to
quantify annual and monthly changes in Q, WT and SC (collectively
referred to as response variables) duringmeteorological drought episodes. It
then uses a combination of statistical methods to assess the persistence and
recovery of these variables from drought impacts. Finally, the framework
applies statistical methods, ense mble machine learning (ML) models,
explainable artiﬁcial intelligence (XAI), and network science to identify the
most important catchment attributes that inﬂuence changes in the response
variables during drought. This modular, data-driven approach provides
insights into mountainous watershedresponse to drought that are broadly
relevant beyond the UCRB to other regions around the world.

A sensitivity analysis testing different pre- and post-event baselines
showed that the results are generally consistent with using the 3-year
reference period (Table S.4). The notable differences were relative changes
that were moderately lower using a 2-year baseline for AT (− 22% instead
of − 25%), Q ( − 9% instead of − 15%), and SC (3% instead of 5% when
comparing the same sites). Using all normal years as a reference resulted in

normal years reference was not considered for SC and WT due to the low
number of sites with data availability.
Drought recovery and trends from 1998– 2022
Sites that had ﬂow increases during drought returned quickly to baseline
levels within 1–3 years (SI Fig. S.9). Most sites that experiencedﬂow declines
during drought recovered to their pre-event baselines within 10 years, with a
median recovery period of 3 y ears (median recovery for 2001 –02
drought = 4 years, for 2012 drought = 2 years; SI Fig. S.9). For the sites where
ﬂows declined, a small fraction (25 out202; SI Fig. S.9, SI Tables S.5 and S.6)
had not recovered to pre-drought baselines after the droughts in 2001–2002
(n = 20) and 2012 (n = 6; 1 site did not recover from both 2001–02 and 2012
droughts). However, after the 2018 drought, a majority of the sites (n = 117)
had not returned to pre-drought baselines as of 2022. The patterns for runoff
efﬁciency (RE) were different from Q (Table S.5), with a few sites not
recovering after 2001–2002 (n = 3), and more sites not recovering after the
2012 (n = 20) and 2018 (n = 35) droughts (Table S.6). In general, reservoir
storage declines during drought are noticeable for all drought periods (SI
Table S.5), with the larger reservoirs (Lake Powell, Flaming Gorge, Navajo,
Strawberry) having low levels for a few years after the droughts ended (SI
Fig. S.10).
Most sites with WT changes recovered quickly within 1 –2 years,
though a few took longer to recover. For SC, most sites recovered within 4
years of the 2001–02 and 2012 droughts. However, the 2018 drought had
more lasting impacts, with 12 sites that had not returned to pre-drought
levels as of 2022.
The Mann-Kendall tests showed that most sites did not have a sig-
niﬁcant trend between 1998 and 2022 for Q (n = 184) and RE (n = 182), with
ﬂows returning to near pre-drought levels after each event (SI Fig. S.11).
Fifteen sites had signiﬁcant decreasing trends for Q and RE, while a few sites
had an increasing trend (n =3f o rQ , n = 5 for RE). Most sites also did not
have a signiﬁcant trend in WT (32 out of 36) or SC (19 out of 23). Four sites
had signi ﬁcantly increasing trends in WT. Two sites had a signi ﬁcant
increasing and decreasing trend in SC, respectively. Of the 43 reservoirs in
the basin, 29 did not have a signi ﬁcant trend in storage levels, 12 had a
decreasing trend and 2 had an increasing trend. Generally, the difference
between the reference periods of the 2012, 2018, and 2021-2022 droughts
relative to the 2001 –02 drought was typically within 0 –15%, with the
exception of AT during the 2018 drought (Table S.7). Together, these results
suggest that there were no long-term baseline shifts during our period of
analysis for a majority of the sites across the basin.

Catchment attributes inﬂuencing drought response

precipitation, and elevation showed moderate to weak negative correlations

correlations (ρ between 0.2 to 0.3) with relative changes in Q. The attribute

and had lower median ﬂows as noted above (Section 2.2). Results for
catchments with negative relative changes are not presented. Since these
catchments comprise 84% of the dataset, their z-scores are substantially
lower and less informative. Thus, the z-score analysis is only used to

Given the limitations with the results from the Spearman correlation
and attribute z-score analyses, we used an XGBoost model with recursive
feature elimination and two importance scoring methods to identify
catchment attributes in ﬂuencing stream ﬂows, after excluding outlier
catchments (Methods, Section 5.6). Twelve attributes were identiﬁed using
the Shapley Values (RMSE=0.72; average test R2=0.42; average training
R2=0.96). In contrast, twenty attributes were identiﬁed using the permuta-
tion feature importance score (RMSE = 0.72; average test R2=0.42; average
training R2=0.98). The considerable difference in performance for training
and test sets suggests possible overﬁtting, despite the extensive hyperpara-
meter tuning, possibly due to the small size of the target dataset. However,
the top attributes selected were mostly consistent across numerous
ensemble runs, model con ﬁgurations (with and without outliers), and
scoring methods, providing con ﬁdence in the results. Feature rankings
varied slightly across the different model runs.
From the XGBoost model, factors associated with lower stream ﬂow
declines during drought were primarily higher base ﬂow index and soil
permeability with slight differences between the Shapley values (Fig. 4A)
and permutation feature importance (SI Fig. S.15 A). Lesser inﬂuences were
greater mean aspect northness, June precipitation, and average sand con-
tent. Greater declines were associated with higher freshwater withdrawal
rates, deciduous forest cover within the riparian zone and 100 m of main
stem, average silt and clay content, subsurfaceﬂow contact times (residence
time of inﬁltrated water in the saturated subsurface zone before discharging
into the stream), and mean annual air temperatures. August precipitation
and features associated with anth ropogenic development were also
important, but had weak or mixed directionality. The top three attribute
categories were consistent between the Shapley and permutation feature
importance scores (Fig. 4B and SI Fig. S.15 B). Attribute categories asso-
ciated with lowerﬂow declines during drought include Baseﬂow Index and
Aspect and, to a lesser degree, Soil Texture and Water Holding Capacity
(Fig. 4B, SI Fig. S.15 and S.16). Conversely, Deciduous Forests and Fresh-
water Use were associated with decreasedﬂows during drought, suggesting
these factors contributed to higher c atchment vulnerability. Across the
UCRB, baseﬂow index ranged from 50 –75% (SI Figs. S.16 and S.17, SI
Table S.8), with the lowest values in t he southern catchments. Deciduous
forest cover ranges from 0 –55%, with most catchments having less
than 20%.
These results reinforce the ﬁndings from the correlation and z-score
analyses, with the XGBoost model capturing more complex, non-linear
relationships betweenﬂows and catchment attributes. For these analyses, we
identiﬁed both individual attributes and aggregated importance scores using
a network clustering approach (see Methods Section 5.6) because many
catchment attributes are redundant (e.g., percent deciduous forest within
100m mainstem, deciduous forest within 100m riparian). Our interpreta-
tion focuses on the directionality and aggregate importance of the attributes,
rather than their individual rank order.

Discussion
Severe meteorological drought episodes between 1998 and 2022 in the
UCRB clearly caused negative impacts on water quantity and quality. Most
sites experienced annual decreases in stream ﬂow and increases in water
temperature and salinity during drought years compared to adjacent years
with normal conditions. The greatest impacts occurred during the spring
and summer months, while the fall and winter months were less affected.
Although meteorological drought intensities, quantiﬁed using the SPEI
(Fig. 1), were relatively uniform basin-wide, catchment responses varied
substantially due to differences in t heir biophysical and anthropogenic
attributes.
Across all episodes, we ﬁnd that average ﬂows (normalized by basin
area) declined by 15% during drough t compared to recent non-drought

In this study, we introduce DIRE, a data-driven framework that
combines ML with other computation al approaches to assess drought
impacts and recovery for different response variables. While prior drought
studies have implemented parts of this analysis, DIRE brings these elements
together into a single framework that is robust, modular andﬂexible, where
components can be swapped out to use alternate metrics or methods. We
also introduce methods to analyze factors inﬂuencing catchment drought
response. The network analysis enables clustering of redundant catchment
attributes and easier interpretation of high-dimensional data ( n = 247
catchment attributes). Our XGBoost modeling approach incorporates
extensive ensembles, recursive feature elimination, and attribute grouping
via clustering to address both redundancy in catchment attributes and the
stochasticity in model construction. This approach helps avoid mis-
identifying drivers of streamﬂow change using more simplistic ML or sta-
tistical modeling approaches. A z-score analysis enables comparison of
properties across different catchment groups, including those exhibiting
outlier behavior. Collectively, th e DIRE framework provides a general
scaffold to analyze the impacts and resilience to droughts or other short-
term disturbances on catchment functions.
Conclusions
Our study addresses key knowledge gaps on drought impacts in the UCRB,
and other mountainous regions. Weﬁnd substantial basin-wide declines in
streamﬂows, and increases in stream temperatures and speciﬁc conductance
during four severe meteorological droughts in 2001 –02, 2012, 2018 and
2020–21. To our knowledge, there are no prior studies that assess post-
drought recovery for these variables in the UCRB. Streamﬂow and speciﬁc
conductance recovered after droughtepisodes through 2018, with recovery
times ranging from 1 to 10 years and 1 to 6 years, respectively. However,
successive dry years since 2018 have resulted in prolonged impacts on Q and
SC. Water temperatures recovered more rapidly, typically within 1–2y e a r s
for all drought episodes. We show that in addition to climatic drivers,
natural and anthropogenic factors strongly inﬂuence catchment responses
at basin scale during short-term droughts. Notably, even when meteor-
ological drought intensities were similar across the basin, catchment
response varied in space and time due to their biophysical and anthro-
pogenic attributes. After accounting for redundancy, factors associated with
streamﬂow declines during drought include freshwater withdrawals, greater
deciduous forest cover, higher mean annual temperatures,ﬁner soils, and
longer subsurface residence times. Factors driving stream ﬂow resilience
include subsurfaceﬂows and properties that enhance inﬁltration, and to a
lesser extent northern aspect and June precipitation. Catchments with
higher slopes, elevation, precipitation, riparian woody wetlands and forests
had greater increases in WT. In contrast, higher shrub and agricultural land
cover and lower in ﬁltration were associated with smaller WT increases
(greater resilience). The presence of open water bodies and reservoirs
mitigate SC increases during drought. The DIRE framework provides a
robust, modular approach to assess drought impacts, recovery and resilience
that can be extended to other variables and disturbances.

This study focuses on water quantity and quality variables: streamﬂow (Q;

reference and non-reference classi ﬁcation from the GAGES-II dataset.

Pre-processing
We applied the following data availability criteria to adequately capture the
temporal evolution of drought, while accounting for the importance of
seasonality and snow melt: (1) A valid year of data should have at least 11
valid months of observations, and (2) each month should have at least
10 days of measurements for at least one variable. Once drought and
reference years were identiﬁed (Section 5.4), only sites with valid data for the
entire drought episode were used. Additionally, sites with streamﬂow data
were downselected based on the availa bility of corresponding catchment
attributes in the GAGES-II dataset. Flows were normalized by catchment
area (in square kilometers) obtained from GAGES-II. In contrast, all sites
with WT and SC measurements were included regardless of whether cor-
responding information was availa ble in GAGES-II, to maximize data
availability. We chose to retain sites close to dams and reservoirs to capture
the potential in ﬂuence of these structures on drought response. Due to
differences in gap lengths across sites and variables, we did not temporally
gap ﬁll records to maintain consistency and minimize uncertainties in this
analysis.
After applying the above-mentioned criteria for site selection within
the study period, there were 202, 24, and 23 sites with Q, WT, and SC
measurements, respectively (Table S.3). Most sites for each of the three
variables were human-impacted, but 24 sites for Q and one for WT were
pristine. The average physical attributes of the selected sites that had asso-
ciated GAGES-II data are summarized in SI Table S.8.
Only 24 sites with stream temperature observations met our data
availability criterion, and are located in the central and Eastern parts of the
basin. (Section 5.2). Hence, we used a deep learning model to estimate WT
for 12 additional sites, which expanded our analysis to 35 sites (SI Table S.9).
The continental-scale (CONUS) long short-term memory (LSTM) model is
described in refs.71, 100 and predicts WT time series in unmonitored sites
using dynamic meteorological data (e.g., air temperature, solar radiation,
day length), dynamic streamﬂows, and 274 static site attributes from the
GAGES-II dataset as inputs. This set of site attributes consists of all non-
categorical attributes available in GAGES-II. Though this work is focused on
the UCRB, recent literature has revealed that large continental, top-down
models are superior to region-speci ﬁc and local alternatives for deep
learning models like LSTM 101, which informed our choice to use a
continental-scale stream temperature model for this study. The LSTM
architecture was chosen because it is the most common deep learning
architecture for time series prediction of hydrologic variables in unmoni-
tored sites101. For further details on the LSTM recurrent neural network

Identiﬁcation of meteorological drought
We used the Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) to
identify meteorological droughts, which is widely used to incorporate the
effects of both precipitation deﬁcit and temperature increases105,106. The SPEI
was calculated for all sites that had meteorological data by subtracting the
potential evapotranspiration (PET) from precipitation (P) as a measure of
the water surplus or deﬁcit (P-PET). We used Thornthwaite’se q u a t i o n107 to

We calculated the average (P — PET) at each site across a window of
12 months, ranked the values, and calculated the anomalies based on the
1998-2022 average using the SPEI Python package113. We used the Grin-
gorten Plotting Position114, which is a non-parametric empirical plotting
approach to transform the data to a standard normal distribution and used
by many studies115–117, instead of assuming a probability distribution (e.g.,
gamma or log-logistic distributions).

and anything below − 0.8 as further levels of drought (moderate, severe,
extreme, and exceptional). Abatan et. al (2017)110 classiﬁed SPEI scores in
the UCRB between 1 and− 1a s“Normal”, with negative values beyond− 1

Drought impacts and recovery for streamﬂow, speciﬁcc o n -

Drought impacts have often been determined based on comparisons to
long-term baseline conditions, such as the last two to three decades pre-
ceding drought58,59 or over the last century18,120. For example, standardized
streamﬂow indices have been used to quantify stream ﬂow de ﬁcits and
drought duration in the UCRB from 1921 to 20203. Another approach for
determining the effects of a particular drought episode is based on identi-
ﬁcation of short-term changes relative to a pre- and post-event baseline. We
followed the latter by adapting methods outlined in van Vliet and Zwolsman
(2008)24 to calculate the impact of particular drought events on Q, WT, and
SC using event-based metrics. We chose this approach to avoid incorpor-
ating long-term trends in the variables and to decouple the impacts of
drought from the effects of warming 121 and human interventions (e.g.,
salinity control program). Oncesevere drought years were identiﬁed using
the SPEI (Section 5.4), reference years were identiﬁed as neighboring years
up to three years before and after drought episodes that had“Normal” SPEI
scores.
For this analysis, monthly, seasonal and annual median Q (normalized
by basin area), WT, and SC were calculated for each year. Median values
were chosen instead of average or cumulative values to avoid skew from
outliers, inconsistencies due to gaps in the data, and for comparison across
the three different variables. For each drought episode, we used the median
of pre- and post-event reference years together to represent baseline values
during the reference period. Thus, reference conditions were based on a
minimum of at least one“Normal” SPEI year to a maximum of six years. For
drought episodes that were more than one year long, we used the median of
the drought years. Seasons were deﬁned as in Carroll et al. (2024)32 based on
the water year and peak snow accumulation in the basin typically occurring
on April 1. The seasons are fall (Oct ober-December), winter (January-
March), spring (April-June), and summer (July-September).

temperatures category contains attributes like the basin average air tem-

aspect.
Spearman correlations were calcu lated between each catchment

The XGBoost model was applied to 165 out of 202 catchments that had

To reduce the number of input featu res, and particularly redundant
attributes in the GAGES-II dataset125, we used a recursive feature elimina-
tion (RFE) method with cross valida tion. Attributes were eliminated
according to their importance scores, computed using two different meth-
ods: (a) permutation feature importances and (b) Shapley values, calculated
using the respective functions in scikit-learn. The permutation feature
importance measures the decrease in model performance when values of
input features are randomly shufﬂed. A higher drop in performance cor-
responds to higher feature importance. This model-agnostic method cap-
tures the real contribution of each feature, including interactions with other
features, and gives a more realistic and interpretable estimate of how much
each variable actually affects predictions in practice127. Shapley values are
based on cooperative game theory and are computed by assigning a con-
tribution value to each feature by evaluating its average marginal impact
across all possible combinations of features128. Features were successively
eliminated based on the average importance of an ensemble of 30 inde-
pendent model runs using 80/20 train-test splits. Ten independent XGBoost
models (each averaging 30 trials computing feature importances with dif-
ferent random seeds) were trained for the RFE. The lowest-ranking input
