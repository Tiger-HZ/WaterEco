Development and external-validation of a mechanistic dynamic nutrient-
partitioning model for predicting growth and body composition in male 
guinea pigs (Cavia porcellus) of the Peru genotype 
Jaime Ricardo Rosero-Noguera , Juliana Carvajal and Sandra Lucía Posada-Ochoa 
GRICA Research Group, Faculty of Agrarian Sciences, University of Antioquia, AA 1226, Medellín, Colombia. 
 
A B S T R AC T 
Background and Aim: Guinea pig production has considerable economic, nutritional, and cultural importance in Andean food 
systems. However, no mechanistic dynamic model is currently available to simultaneously predict growth and chemical body 
composition in this species. This study aimed to develop and externally validate a mechanistic nutrient -partitioning model 
for predicting live body weight (BW), empty BW, and chemical body composition in male guinea pigs (Cavia porcellus) of the 
Peru genotype. 
Materials and Methods: A mechanistic dynamic model integrating dry matter intake, maintenance requirements, protein 
and energy utilization, nutrient partitioning, and tissue deposition was developed and implemented in R. External-validation 
was performed using two independent da tasets generated in Colombia. The first comprised longitudinal BW records from 
184 male guinea pigs raised under commercial conditions, whereas the second included whole -body chemical composition 
measurements from 24 male guinea pigs. Predictive performanc e was assessed using Lin’s concordance correlation 
coefficient (CCC), coefficient of determination, root mean square error (RMSE), relative root mean square error, mean 
absolute error, and mean bias (MB). 
Results: The model predicted live BW with almost perfect agreement between observed and predicted values (mean CCC = 
0.931; RMSE = 149.6 g; MB = −1.39 g). Final BW and empty BW were predicted with high accuracy (CCC = 0.99 and 0.98, 
respectively). Total body protei n and ash showed almost perfect agreement (CCC = 0.91 for both), whereas body water 
showed substantial agreement (CCC = 0.70). In contrast, body fat was poorly predicted (CCC = 0.04; RMSE = 121.9 g) and was 
systematically underestimated. Prediction errors increased in animals weighing >1000 g, indicating reduced accuracy during 
the later growth phase. 
Conclusion: This is the first mechanistic dynamic nutrient -partitioning model developed specifically for guinea pigs. The 
model provides a biologically interpretable framework for simultaneously predicting growth and chemical body composition 
and may support nutritional assessment, precision-feeding, and decision-making in intensive production systems. However, 
parameters governing lipid accretion require further refinement before the model can be broadly applied across genotypes, 
sexes, and production environments. 
Keywords: body composition, dynamic modeling, growth prediction, guinea pig, mechanistic model, nutrient partitioning, 
Peru genotype, precision-feeding. 
INTRODUCTION 
The guinea pig (Cavia porcellus) was domesticated primarily as a food resource more than 4000 years ago in 
the Andean region, encompassing present -day Colombia, Peru, Ecuador, and Bolivia [1]. Today, guinea pig 
production plays an important economic, nutritional, and cultural role in these countries, particularly among rural 
and smallholder households, where it contributes to household income, local food systems, and the supply of 
high-quality protein in communities in which access to other livestock products is limited by economic or 
geographical constraints [2]. The increasing intensification of guinea pig production has consequently created a 
growing demand for feeding strategies that improve nutrient -use efficiency, growth performance, carcass 
composition, and overall production profitability. 
Despite the increasing importance of guinea pig production, quantitative information regarding voluntary

feed intake, nutrient utilization, maintenance and growth requirements, and the dynamics of chemical body 
composition remains limited [3]. Growth in guinea pigs has generally been described using empirical nonlinear 
equations that relate body weight (BW) t o age [4]. Although these models effectively characterize phenotypic 
growth trajectories, they do not explicitly represent the underlying biological processes governing nutrient intake, 
maintenance requirements, nutrient partitioning, and tissue deposition [4–7]. Furthermore, empirical models 
generally predict BW without simultaneously estimating the accretion of individual chemical body components, 
including protein, fat, water, and ash. 
To date, no mechanistic dynamic nutrient-partitioning model has been developed specifically for guinea pigs 
to simultaneously predict live BW, empty BW, and chemical body composition. Consequently, current modeling 
approaches provide limited insight into t he biological mechanisms that regulate nutrient allocation among 
maintenance, protein deposition, lipid deposition, and overall tissue growth. This limitation restricts their 
application in evaluating nutritional strategies, optimizing nutrient utilization , and supporting precision -feeding 
programs under commercial production conditions. Therefore, developing a biologically based mechanistic model 
capable of integrating dry matter intake (DMI), nutrient requirements, protein and energy utilization, and body 
tissue accretion would represent a significant methodological advancement for growth prediction and nutritional 
management in guinea pigs [8–10]. 
We hypothesized that the growth and chemical body composition of male guinea pigs of the Peru genotype 
can be accurately predicted using a mechanistic dynamic framework that integrates DMI, maintenance 
requirements, protein utilization, energy utilization, and the differential accretion of body tissues. Therefore, this 
study aimed to develop and externally validate a dynamic nutrient -partitioning model to predict live BW, empty 
BW, and the accretion of protein, fat, water, and ash in male guinea pigs. Unlik e conventional empirical growth 
models that predict BW alone, the proposed mechanistic framework reconstructs empty BW from its individual 
chemical components, thereby providing a biologically interpretable platform to support nutritional evaluation, 
growth simulation, and the future development of precision-feeding strategies in guinea pig production systems. 
MATERIALS AND METHODS 
Ethical approval 
The animal procedures used to generate the chemical body composition dataset were reviewed and 
approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Antioquia, Colombia (Approval 
Act No. 138, dated February 9, 2021). Animal manag ement, euthanasia, sample collection, and whole -body 
chemical analyses were conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines governing the ethical 
use of animals in research. 
The BW data used for growth -model validation were obtained retrospectively from routine production 
records collected at a commercial guinea pig production facility in 2018. These records were generated during 
standard farm management and did not involve ad ditional animal handling, experimental treatment, or sample 
collection for the present study. Therefore, separate ethical approval was not required for the retrospective use 
of this dataset. 
Study period and location 
The study used two independent datasets generated in Colombia. The growth-validation dataset comprised 
routine BW records collected in 2018 from a commercial guinea pig production facility. The body composition -
validation dataset was obtained from an experiment conducted in 2021 at the University of Antioquia, Antioquia, 
Colombia. Model -development, simulation, statistical evaluation, and web application deployment were 
subsequently performed using the available experimental and production datasets. 
Study design 
This study followed a mechanistic model -development and external-validation design. A dynamic nutrient -
partitioning model was developed to predict live BW, empty BW (EBW), and the deposition of protein, fat, water, 
and ash in male guinea pigs. Model evaluation was performed using two independent datasets that were not used 
for model parameter estimation or calibration. 
The first dataset consisted of repeated live BW measurements from 184 male guinea pigs raised under 
commercial production conditions. This dataset was used exclusively to evaluate the model’s ability to predict 
growth throughout the production period. The second dataset comprised 24 male guinea pigs of the Peru 
genotype subjected to whole -body chemical analysis. This dataset was used to evaluate predictions of EBW and

Model description 
A mechanistic dynamic model was developed to predict growth and body composition in male guinea pigs 
based on nutrient intake and the efficiencies of protein and energy utilization. The model was based on nutrient-
partitioning principles and simulated dail y changes in EBW by tracking the differential deposition of protein, fat, 
water, and ash (Figure 1).

Initial live BW was used to estimate initial body composition through a series of empirical equations 
describing body protein, fat, water, and ash. Thereafter, BW and body composition were updated iteratively 
according to nutrient intake and the efficienci es of protein and energy utilization throughout the simulation 
period. 
The model was implemented using R version 4.5.2 [11] and deployed as an interactive web application using 
Shiny version 1.12.1 [12]. The simulator is publicly available at https://rrnoguera.shinyapps.io/PrediCuy/ and can 
be used to simulate guinea pig growth under different dietary scenarios.

DMI = 0.074 × BW0.6921

Model assumptions 
The proposed mechanistic model was developed to simulate growth and changes in chemical body 
composition in growing guinea pigs under controlled nutritional conditions. To maintain biological consistency 
while preserving model parsimony, several assumption s were adopted based on empirical evidence and 
established nutrient-partitioning principles. 
The model assumes that growth results from the accretion of protein, fat, water, and ash. Accordingly, EBW 
is calculated as the sum of these components, and BW is derived from EBW using a constant conversion factor. 
Initial body composition is estimated fr om the animal’s initial BW, whereas body water and ash are predicted 
from total body protein using previously published equations. 
DMI is assumed to depend primarily on BW, whereas the dietary concentrations of metabolizable energy 
and CP are considered constant throughout each simulation. Nutrient intake is first allocated to maintenance 
requirements, after which the remaining protei n and energy are partitioned toward protein and fat accretion 
using constant utilization efficiencies. 
The model does not explicitly account for the effects of genotype, environmental conditions, health status, 
or management practices on nutrient utilization. Therefore, its predictions should be interpreted within the 
biological and production conditions re presented by the datasets used for model -development and external -
validation. These assumptions were adopted to maintain model transparency while minimizing the number of 
empirical parameters required for simulation.

Experimental datasets 
Model evaluation was performed using two independent datasets generated under different production and 
experimental conditions in Colombia. The first dataset was used to evaluate the predictive performance of the 
model for BW during growth, whereas the sec ond dataset was used to assess the accuracy of EBW and chemical 
body composition predictions. Neither dataset was used for parameter estimation or model calibration, thereby 
ensuring a completely independent external-validation process. 
The first dataset comprised routine BW records collected from 184 male guinea pigs raised at a commercial 
production facility in 2018. The animals were weighed at birth and subsequently at 15, 28, 56, and 84 days of age. 
These data were used exclusively to evaluate the model’s predictive performance for BW throughout the growth 
period. 
Under the routine management protocol, the animals were housed in group pens and fed a diet comprising 
fresh perennial ryegrass (Lolium perenne) and commercial concentrate at a forage -to-concentrate ratio of 70:30 
on a dry matter basis. Diet composition remained constant throughout the production period and provided 16.8% 
CP and 2.62 Mcal of metabolizable energy/kg dry matter (Table 1). 
The second dataset comprised 24 male guinea pigs of the Peru genotype from an independent experiment 
conducted at the University of Antioquia, Medellín, Colombia, in 2021. The animals were euthanized to determine 
EBW and the absolute amounts of protein, ether extract, water, and ash. 
Chemical analyses were performed on the entire empty body after removal of the digestive contents to 
estimate chemical body composition. CP was determined using the Kjeldahl method (AOAC method 954.01), ether 
extract using Soxhlet extraction (AOAC method 9 20.39), dry matter by oven drying at 105°C (AOAC method 
934.01), and ash by combustion in a muffle furnace at 550°C (AOAC method 942.05) [16].

Model evaluation 
Model performance was assessed by comparing simulated outputs with observations from the two 
independent datasets. For each simulation, initial BW, dietary composition, and simulation duration were 
specified according to the corresponding experimental conditions. The model was then run at a daily time step to 
predict changes in BW, EBW, and the absolute amounts of protein, fat, water, and ash throughout the evaluation 
period. 
Predicted BW values were compared with the observed measurements obtained from the commercial 
growth dataset, whereas predicted EBW and chemical body composition were compared with the corresponding 
measurements obtained through whole-body chemical analysis. Because neither validation dataset was used for 
model parameterization or calibration, predictive performance was evaluated exclusively through external 
validation. All simulations used the same equations and parameter values, without recalibration or d ataset-
specific adjustment.

protein (P), body fat (F), body water (W) and body ash (A). Following established approaches for evaluating

systematic bias.

RESULTS 
Model performance for predicting growth 
The predictive performance of the model for BW was evaluated by comparing observed and predicted values 
throughout the growth period. The validation metrics indicated strong agreement between the observed and 
predicted growth trajectories. 
The mean CCC was 0.931, with individual values ranging from 0.789 to 0.998, indicating substantial to almost 
perfect agreement according to the established classification criteria [19]. The mean R² was 0.940 and ranged 
from 0.810 to 0.999. 
The mean RMSE was 149.6 g, ranging from 28.6 to 280.9 g, whereas the mean RRMSE was 24.2%. The mean 
MAE was 111.5 g. The mean MB was close to 0 (−1.39 g), indicating no overall tendency of the model to 
underestimate or overestimate BW across the growth period (Table 3).

Evaluation of BW dynamics demonstrated that the proposed mechanistic framework accurately reproduced 
the growth trajectory of guinea pigs maintained under controlled feeding and management conditions. The high 
concordance correlation coefficient (CCC = 0.9 31; Table 3) indicates strong agreement between observed and 
predicted values, confirming that the model adequately represents DMI and nutrient partitioning between 
maintenance and growth (Figure 3). From a biological perspective, the combination of high c oncordance and 
moderate prediction errors indicates that the model not only captured the overall growth trajectory but also 
maintained prediction errors within the range expected for experimentally validated mechanistic models [7, 22]. 
Furthermore, the neg ligible MB observed throughout the simulation period indicates no overall tendency to 
under- or overestimate BW, supporting the robustness of the model for simulating nutrient intake, maintenance 
requirements, and nutrient partitioning during growth [23, 24]. 
Although the overall MB was negligible, residual analysis revealed a systematic pattern associated with 
increasing BW. For animals weighing more than 1000 g, the model consistently overestimated BW, resulting in 
mild heteroscedasticity (Figure 3). This pat tern reflects the compensation between slight underestimation at 
lower BW values and overestimation at higher BW values, producing an overall MB close to zero. Consequently, 
residual analysis provides additional insight into structural deviations that are not evident from global 
performance statistics alone [24, 25]. 
The discrepancy between observed and predicted values during the final stages of growth is likely associated 
with physiological changes as animals approach mature BW. As maturity is reached, an increasing proportion of 
nutrients is allocated to maintenance , thereby reducing the metabolizable energy available for tissue accretion 
[26, 27]. Simultaneously, muscle growth approaches its biological limit, reducing the efficiency with which 
nutrients are converted into BW gain [28]. Mechanistic models developed f or monogastric species have 
demonstrated that the relationship between protein deposition and MEI above maintenance is curvilinear. Once 
the maximum capacity for protein accretion is reached, additional energy contributes predominantly to lipid 
deposition rather than further lean tissue growth, thereby altering nutrient partitioning and the growth response 
[29]. Therefore, the slight overestimation observed in heavier animals, together with the increased residual 
dispersion, suggests that although the model accurately describes the overall growth trajectory, it simplifies the 
physiological changes that occur during the late-growth phase. 
Model performance for predicting body composition 
Validation using an independent dataset of 24 male guinea pigs confirmed that the proposed mechanistic 
model accurately predicted most body composition components during growth. The high coefficients of

determination and concordance obtained for final BW (R² = 0.99; CCC = 0.99) and EBW (R² = 0.98; CCC = 0.98) 
indicate that the model provided accurate predictions of final BW and EBW while simultaneously representing 
the accretion of protein, fat, water, and ash (Table 4). Comparable predictive performance has been reported for 
mechanistic models developed for other livestock species [7, 23]. 
Total body protein was predicted with high accuracy, indicating that the mathematical representation of 
protein utilization efficiency adequately reflects the anabolic capacity of growing guinea pigs. This finding agrees 
with observations in other monogast ric species, in which protein accretion generally exhibits lower biological 
variability than lipid accretion because it is regulated by more stringent homeostatic mechanisms [30, 31]. 
Likewise, total body ash was predicted with high concordance, supporting the proportional relationship assumed 
between body protein and skeletal mineralization. In growing monogastric animals, ash deposition is closely 
associated with skeletal development and therefore follows a pattern similar to lean tissue accretion [32]. 
Prediction of total body water showed substantial agreement, although the positive MB indicated systematic 
overestimation. This discrepancy likely reflects the model's assumption of a fixed protein-to-water ratio. Although 
body water is physiologically rel ated to fat-free mass, the water-to-protein ratio changes during growth. In pigs, 
for example, this ratio gradually decreases as BW and body fat increase because of allometric changes in tissue 
composition and the progressive maturation of lean tissues [32 ]. Similar physiological changes may explain the 
systematic overestimation of body water observed in the present study. 
Prediction of body fat represented the principal limitation of the current model. Lipid accretion is highly 
dynamic and responds to changes in energy availability, nutrient utilization efficiency, and physiological status. In 
mechanistic models for monogas tric species, lipid deposition depends primarily on the metabolizable energy 
available above maintenance and on its interaction with the maximum capacity for protein accretion (Pdmax ). 
Once protein deposition approaches its physiological limit, excess energy is redirected predominantly toward de 
novo lipogenesis, thereby increasing body fat without proportional increases in lean tissue [29]. The lower 
predictive performance observed for body fat suggests that the current model does not fully represent this 
hierarchy of nutrient partitioning in guinea pigs, possibly because the parameters governing energy allocation to 
lipid deposition have not yet been sufficiently characterized for this species. 
The systematic underestimation of body fat suggests that the model allocates an insufficient proportion of 
available metabolizable energy to lipid deposition, particularly during the later stages of growth when protein 
accretion begins to plateau. Using constant values for the energetic cost of fat synthesis and the efficiency of lipid 
deposition, although common in mechanistic models, does not account for the biological variability associated 
with these processes. In pigs and other monogastric species, the efficiency of energy utilization for protein and 
lipid deposition varies according to nutrient source and nutritional status [29]. Therefore, the true efficiency of 
lipid deposition in guinea pigs, particularly in early-maturing genotypes, may differ from the fixed value assumed 
in the present model, thereby contributing to the observed discrepancy between predicted and observed body 
fat. 
The simultaneous underestimation of body fat and overestimation of body water reflects the mass -balance 
constraint inherent to the model structure. Because predicted EBW is reconstructed within the model as the sum 
of protein, fat, water, and ash, underest imation of one compartment may be accompanied by overestimation of 
one or more of the remaining compartments, particularly body water. This behavior is consistent with findings 
from dynamic nutrient -partitioning models developed for other species, in which lipid is generally the most 
variable body component, whereas protein and ash exhibit substantially greater biological stability [33]. 
CONCLUSION 
The present study developed and externally validated a mechanistic nutrient -partitioning model capable of 
simulating growth and body composition dynamics in growing male guinea pigs under controlled nutritional 
conditions. External validation demonstrated that the model accurately predicted BW and EBW while providing 
high predictive accuracy for body protein and body ash. Prediction of body water showed substantial agreement, 
whereas body fat represented the principal source of prediction error. Overall, the model successfully reproduced 
the biological processes governing nutrient intake, maintenance requirements, and nutrient partitioning during 
growth. 
The proposed model provides a biologically interpretable framework for predicting growth and body 
composition in guinea pigs using readily obtainable animal and dietary information. Because the model is based 
on physiological principles rather than empirical growth equations, it offers a valuable tool for evaluating feeding

strategies, estimating nutrient requirements, and supporting nutritional decision -making in research and 
commercial guinea pig production systems. The framework also provides a foundation for future precision -
feeding applications and simulation-based nutritional management. 
A major strength of this study is the use of two completely independent datasets for external validation, 
ensuring that model performance was evaluated without parameter recalibration or dataset-specific adjustment. 
The mechanistic structure of the model e nables direct biological interpretation of nutrient partitioning while 
maintaining relatively few empirical parameters, thereby enhancing model transparency and facilitating future 
refinement. 
The model was developed and validated using male guinea pigs maintained under controlled nutritional and 
management conditions. Consequently, its applicability to females, different genotypes, alternative production 
systems, or varying environmental condit ions requires further investigation. In addition, the reduced predictive 
accuracy for body fat indicates that the current representation of lipid accretion requires refinement, particularly 
during the later stages of growth when nutrient partitioning increasingly favors FD. 
Future studies should incorporate genotype -specific information, environmental and management effects, 
and dynamic descriptions of lipid accretion to improve predictive performance under diverse production 
conditions. Additional validation using independen t datasets representing different physiological stages, 
nutritional strategies, and production systems will further strengthen the applicability and robustness of the 
proposed modeling framework. 
The proposed mechanistic nutrient -partitioning model represents a biologically consistent approach for 
simulating growth and body composition in growing male guinea pigs. The strong external-validation performance 
for BW, EBW, body protein, and body ash de monstrates its potential as a practical tool for nutritional research 
and production management. Further refinement of the lipid deposition component is expected to improve 
prediction of body fat and enhance the overall capability of the model for supporti ng precision nutrition and 
decision-making in guinea pig production.
