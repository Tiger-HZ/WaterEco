 A B S T RA C T 
PM2.5 is a major air pollutant with diverse natural and anthropogenic sources, which 
significantly impacts human health and the environment. Remote sensing serves as a 
complementary resource for air-monitoring networks, providing valuable information on 
the spatiotemporal patterns of air pollutants at a global scale This study analyzes the 
feasibility of the use of MODIS MAIAC Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) remote sensing for the 
estimation of surface PM2.5 and explores the dynamic relationship between AOD and PM2.5 
in conjunction with regional factors such as heterogeneity of emissions sources, 
topography and meteorological variables in the Toluca Valley Metropolitan Area (TVMA), 
identified as the mo st polluted metropolitan area in Mexico. The five-year (2018-2023) 
temporal analysis during the dry seasons (Nov – May) revealed similar patterns between 
monthly average PM2.5 and AOD, with higher values observed during the Hot -Dry season. 
Spatially, 90% o f the monitoring sites exhibited a moderate linear correlation (0.4 < R < 
0.69) between AOD and PM 2.5, while one site showed a weaker correlation (0.33); 
associated with a heterogeneity of local emission sources and the presence of coarse-mode 
aerosols. Regarding meteorological factors, higher Boundary Layer Height (BLH) and 
lower Relative Humidity (RH), during the Hot -Dry season corresponded to stronger 
correlations, as increased convection facilitates the vertical mixing of particles, in contrast, 
higher RH during the Cold -Dry impacts aerosol optical properties. This study highlights 
the complex influence of emission sources and meteorological conditions on the 
relationship between AOD and PM 2.5, underscoring the importance of considering these 
factors when resorting to satellite data for air quality assessment.

INTRODUCTION 
 
Particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter smaller 
than 2.5 µm, commonly known as PM 2.5, is a criteria air 
pollutant, meaning that acceptable levels of exposure and 
ambient air quality standards have been established [1]. 
According to Valle -Hernández et al. (2023), PM 2.5 can be 
emitted from natural sources such as wind resuspension, 
volcanic activity and wildfires, and from anthropoge nic 
sources as well: industries, vehicular emissions from 
combustion and road emissions; area emission sources such 
as homes and commercial premises with their own 
combustion emissions; and also, emissions from the burning 
of crop residue and dust resuspen sion from agriculture and 
livestock significantly contribute to PM2.5. In addition to these

primary sources, PM 2.5 is generated by secondary sources 
through atmospheric oxidation reactions of primary 
pollutants such as NO 2 and SO 2 to produce secondary 
aerosols, SO42- and NO3- [2]. 
Given this diversity of emission sources, ambient PM 2.5 
concentrations are a major international concern. According 
to the World Health Organization, 99% of the global 
population breathes air with concentrations of pollutants that 
exceed the limits set in the Air Quality Guidelines (AQG) [3]. 
For the specific case of PM 2.5, these AQG limits are 15 µg/m 3 
as a 24 -hour average and 5 µg/m 3 as an annual average [4]. 
The limits are established on the basis of ep idemiological 
evidence linking PM 2.5 exposure to the exacerbation and 
development of allergies, respiratory, cardiovascular and 
neurological conditions, and even cancer [5]. The health 
878 Environ Res Tec, Vol. 9, Issue 5, pp. 877-885, October 2026 
effects above are related to the diverse composition of PM 2.5, 
which in cludes toxic substances such as heavy metals, 
Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs), dioxins and 
furans, among others [6]. 
Fine particles also have a considerable impact on the 
environment. Their massive presence in the atmosphere 
affects the natural cy cle of cloud formation: particles may 
serve as condensation nuclei, increasing cloud droplet 
concentration, but also reducing the cloud droplet effective 
radius (re), leading to smaller droplets, which reduces 
precipitation efficiency and prolongs cloud lifetime [7]. PM2.5 
composition can also diminish the pH of the rain, caused by 
the dissolution of SO42- and NO3- (secondary aerosols) into the 
cloud droplets, forming the acids H 2SO4 and HNO 3, a 
phenomenon known as acid rain [8]. 
The term aerosol applies to the system composed of liquid or 
solid particles suspended in a gas, typically air [9]. Aerosols 
interact with solar radiation, scattering or absorbing it, 
depending on their composition. The degree to which 
aerosols prevent the transmission of light from the ground 
surface to the top of the atmosphere is known as Aerosol 
Optical Depth (AOD) or Aerosol Optical Thickness (AOT). AOD 
can also be considered a global measure of aerosols 
distributed within an air column and is strongly correlated 
with PM concentrations [10]. 
Aerosol remote sensing is performed using specific sensors 
on satellites that measure the amount of light that reaches the 
Earth's surface and how much of such light is reflected by 
aerosols [11]. These measurements are recorded as images 
where each pixel contains a value of the signal intensity 
captured by the sensor. Depending on the sensor technology 
and retrieval algorithm, pixel sizes can represent from 1 to 
100 km. MODIS (Moderate Resolution Imaging 
Spectroradiometer) is a sensor instal led on two NASA 
satellites, Terra and Aqua, which measures parameters of the 
Earth's land, oceans and atmosphere, including AOD. 
Originally developed for MODIS [12], the Multi -Angle 
Implementation of Atmospheric Correction (MAIAC) is an 
algorithm that uses time series analysis to improve 
atmospheric correction, cloud detection and aerosol retrieval. 
This retrieval algorithm combines the Aqua and Terra 
products and enhances their AOD measurements with the 
aforementioned corrections, which turns it into a hig h-
resolution product (1 km) that has been widely used for 
spatiotemporal AOD analysis [13-15]. 
Remote sensing serves as a complementary resource for air -
monitoring networks, providing valuable information on the 
spatiotemporal patterns of aerosols at a glo bal scale [16]. To 
estimate ground-level PM2.5, it is necessary to conduct remote 
calibrations by comparing MAIAC AOD values with respective 
PM2.5 ground measurements from the local air -monitoring 
network, and also analyze the factors that influence this 
relationship. In this context, this study aims to evaluate the 
feasibility of using MODIS MAIAC AOD for PM 2.5 estimation 
within the Toluca Valley Metropolitan Area (TVMA). The 
TVMA is a critical area of interest as is the most polluted city 
in Mexico, with concentrations comparable to those found in 
Iraq, Shanghai, and Vietnam [17], and has been largely 
unaddressed in this specific context. While previous studies 
have confirmed the general relationship between satellite -
derived AOD and PM2.5, a comprehensive, multi-year analysis 
integrating specific regional factors —including local 
topography, diverse emission sources (industrial, vehicular, 
and agricultural), and seasonal meteorological variations—is 
often lacking. Consequently, this study represents a cruc ial 
first step in analyzing the feasibility of remote sensing and 
explores the dynamic relationship between AOD and PM2.5 in 
conjunction with the aforementioned regional factors. The 
findings, therefore, serve as a valuable reference case for 
implementing remote sensing measurements in similar 
complex urban environments. 
 
MATERIALS AND METHODS 
 
Study Area 
 
TVMA is located in the central area of Mexico, 60 km west of 
Mexico City between 18°59′07′′ and 19°34′47′′ North latitude 
and 99°38′22′′ and 99°56′13′′ West longitude (Fig ure 1); it 
has an extension of 2,410.5 km 2 and has a population of 2.3 
million inhabitants [18]. The poor quality of air in TVMA is 
attributed to several factors, including its high altitude (2610 
masl) and its location withi n a semi -closed valley, which 
hinders the dispersion of air pollution [19]. Additionally, over 
a thousand industries are established here and there are 1.1 
million registered motor vehicles in circulation [18]; for their 
part, agricultural fields account f or over a half of the TVMA 
territory, and contribute with emissions from wind erosion 
and the burning of crop residues. The TVMA experiences 
three distinct climatic seasons: Cold -Dry Season (CDS) from 
November to February; Hot-Dry Season (HDS) from March to 
May; and Rainy Season (RaS) from June to October. The 
seasons with the highest PM 2.5 concentrations are CDS and 
HDS [20]. The study area was limited to the TVMA area 
presented in Figure 1, based on the coverage of the air -
monitoring network.

Monitoring Data 
 
Currently, the TVMA air -monitoring network reports hourly 
ambient concentrations of criteria air pollutants from 8 
monitoring stations. The ININ monit oring station from 
Mexico City's air monitoring network was also included due 
to its proximity to TVMA. The SC site ceased operations in 
2021. PM 2.5 data was limited to CDS and HDS seasons 
(November to May) over a five -year period (2018 to 2023). 
The Rainy season was avoided because of the effect of rains 
on AOD remote sensing such as even higher AOD missing data 
caused by cloud coverage and the diminishing of PM2.5, which 
is partially precipitated by rain. Daily PM 2.5 concentrations 
were calculated as an a verage of the MAIAC acquisition time 
period, 15:00 – 18:00 (GMT -6 AM) to maintain the 
representativeness of the comparison between PM 2.5 and 
AOD, as PM2.5 emission patterns are hourly specific (Figure 2).

Satellite Data 
 
Land AOD spectral data was obtained from the Level 2 
product MCD19A2 Version 6.1, which combines data from 
MODIS Terra and Aqua with the MAIAC correction algorithm, 
produced daily at 1 km pixel resolution [21]. The data was 
downloaded from the Worldview NASA browser [22], 
selecting the daily images of the months with the highest 
TVMA PM2.5 concentrations (Nov to May) from 2018 to 2023. 
The downloaded images corresponded to the granule 
covering the TVMA area. The Science Dataset layer used was 
the green band AOD at 0.55 µm. Data validation was 
performed on the basis of QA SDS values. 
 
Collocation Process Between MAIAC AOD and PM 2.5 
Monitoring Data 
 
AOD data is reported at a 1 km x 1 km grid, while ground-level 
PM2.5 monitoring data are point values. Therefore, it was 
necessary to collocate both variables at the monitoring 
stations’ locations in order to assess the relationship between 
the surface -level PM 2.5 and columnar AOD. The satellite 
images were reprojected to the WGS84 geodetic coordinate 
system and AOD and QA data were obtained by using the QGIS 
point sampling tool from the pixel corresponding to the 
location of each monitoring station. QA values were used to 
exclude pixel values with high uncertainty. 
 
Spatiotemporal Analysis 
 
The study employed several analyses to investigate the AOD–
PM2.5 relationship. Spatial correlation analysis was conducted 
for each monitoring site (Fig ure 1) to identify locatio ns with 
the best agreement and diagnose possible causes of low 
correlation. Temporal analysis evaluated changes in 
correlations between hot and dry seasons (November –May), 
it was intentionally restricted due to the severe data scarcity 
during the rainy sea son (June –October), where persistent 
high cloud cover and humidity resulted in AOD retrieval rates 
below 10%, thus violating the clear -sky assumption of the 
satellite algorithm. Finally, Relative Humidity (RH) and 
Boundary Layer Height (BLH) data were inte grated into the 
analysis to identify and explain the meteorological factors 
influencing the spatiotemporal correlation between PM2.5 and 
AOD. 
 
Meteorological Assessment 
 
For meteorological context, Relative Humidity (RH) data 
were collected from the TVMA air -monitoring network at 
each station, while Boundary Layer Height (BLH) was 
retrieved from the Modern -Era Retrospective Analysis for 
Research and Applications (MERRA-2) product M2TMNXFLX. 
Due to the coarse spatial resolution of the MERRA-2 data (0.5° 
×0.625°), a direct spatial regression with the 1 km MAIAC 
AOD product was infeasible. Consequently, BLH was 
restricted to the temporal analysis, where an average TVMA 
value was used solely to differentiate and characterize the 
climatic seasons. Similarl y, an average RH value was 
calculated for temporal characterization. However, because 
site-specific RH values were available, a spatial analysis was 
feasible using Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) 
interpolation by season for comparison with the site -level 
AOD–PM2.5 correlation coefficients. 
 
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
 
MAIAC AOD Value Retrieval Rate 
 
During the study period (2018 -2023), there was a high 
missing rate of satellite-retrieved AOD, mostly related to orbit 
patterns and cloudiness [23]. The AOD retrieval percentage 
by site is summarized in Table 1. 
As shown in Table 1, the percentage of missing AOD values 
was above 50% at all sites, primarily due to inconsistent 
orbital patterns. There was also missing data owing to 
cloudiness in November and D ecember, and surface 
reflectivity, especially in April and May. Low AOD recovery is 
not unusual in remote sensing; for example, Xiao et al. (2017) 
also reported 40% recovery in China [24] as well as Palacios 
et al., (2022) documented recovery rates lower t han 20% 
driven by the high frequency of intense cloud cover the 
Amazonian tropical rainforest [25]. Acknowledging the high 
percentage of missing values, the inherent benefits of remote 
sensing’s continuous spatiotemporal coverage justify 
proceeding with the analysis. For subsequent modeling steps 
aimed at PM 2.5 estimation, a robust assessment of various 
missing-data imputation techniques will be essential. 
Methods, including Chemical Transport Models (CTMs) 
coupled with surface measurements [26] or those detailed by 
Chen et al. (2020) [27], must be evaluated prior to the final 
modeling process. However, as an exploratory study, the 
dataset was utilized exactly as retrieved from the satellite 
product (i.e., non-imputed data). This approach establishes a 
clear baseline for the actual data availability and correlational 
factors, thereby verifying the feasibility of using satellite AOD 
measurements within the TVMA. AI modeling is planned as 
the subsequent research stage. 
 
Temporal Variation 
 
Similar patterns were observed between the TVMA PM 2.5 
monthly average and the AOD from MAIAC at 550 nm during 
the dry-seasons (Nov-May), showing a good match between 
both variables (Fig ure 3). A statistically significant, strong 
positive correlation was observed between the two variables 
using the Pearson test (R=0.78, p<0.01). The temporal 
variation analysis among the dry -seasons revealed that the 
highest PM2.5 and AOD values occurred in May, that is, during 
the HDS (March-May). 
During HDS in TVMA, air pollution i s exacerbated by high -
pressure systems typical of this time of the year, 
characterized by high solar radiation and low wind speed,

which are related to stagnation of pollutants and increased 
formation of secondary aerosols. During this season, air 
quality is also diminished by agricultural burning of crop 
residues to prepare the land for planting just before the rainy 
season, as TVMA agriculture is rainfed. Forest fires are also 
frequent in this season, many of them caused by uncontrolled 
agricultural burni ng. CDS also presents high concentrations 
related to the burning of wood and charcoal for heating 
homes and the use of fireworks during religious festivities; all 
these emissions are trapped under the thermal inversions 
typical of this time of the year, ca using pollutant 
accumulation. This tendency aligns well with the findings of 
Neria-Hernández et al. (2024) [20]. Fig ure 4 shows the 
differences between the satellite images corresponding to the 
CDS and HDS. The colorimetric scale of Fig ure 4a clearly 
shows higher AOD values during HDS in contrast to the CDS 
presented in Fig ure 4b, a pattern consistent with the 
temporal variation presented in Figure 3.

Correlation Between MAIAC AOD and PM 2.5 Monitoring 
Data 
 
After the collocation process of AOD data and surface PM2.5 at 
each monitoring site, a Pearson correlation assessment was 
conducted between both variables. The scatter plots for each 
site and their respective Pearson correlation values (R) are 
presented in Fig ure 5. All correlation coefficients were 
determined to be statistically significant using a two -tailed 
test (p < 0.01). From the 10 monitoring sites considered, 90% 
presented a moderate linear relationship between AOD and 
PM2.5 (0.4 < R < 0.69), while only one site, (SM) showed a 
weak correlation (0.1 - 0.39), this correlation coefficient 
interpretation was based on Schober et al., (2018) [28]. 
Figure 6 presents the spatial distribution of each site's 
Pearson correlation value, as well as land use, industrial 
sources and main roads in order to discuss the facto rs 
affecting the AOD and PM2.5 correlation values. 
The sites with the highest correlations (R>0.6) values (SC and 
MT) are located within urban and suburban areas at school 
districts, however, they are distant from industrial sources. 
These sites are influe nced by vehicular sources of aerosols 
and, in the case of the SC site, biomass burning from coal and 
wood used for cooking and agricultural burning from small 
parcels near the site. In contrast, the site with the weakest 
correlation (R = 0.33), SM, is affected by several sources; it is 
close to an industrial area (less than 3 km), main roads with 
heavy traffic and wind -dragged emissions from distant 
agricultural burnings. These facts align with the findings of 
Paciorek & Liu (2012) that a heterogeneity of sources at local 
level can contribute to weak correlations [29], as observed at 
the SM site. 
According to Zheng et al. (2017), the presence of coarse-mode 
aerosols (PM 10) increases AOD values, diminishing their 
relationship with PM 2.5 [30]. This may be the case of the CA, 
XO and AJ sites, located outside the urban area and 
surrounded by agricultural fields that remain uncovered 
during dry seasons, when coarse PM resuspension is 
frequent. The ININ site is a special case since it is located in a 
forest preservation area with particular climatic conditions, 
which will be assessed further.

Meteorological Factors Assessment 
 
Table 2 presents the comparison between the TVMA seasonal 
average values of MERRA -2 Boundary Layer Height (BLH) 
and surface RH with the seasonal means of the R values per 
site, the correlation coefficients for all seasonal analyses were 
found to be statistically significant (p<0.01). Hot Dry Season 
(HDS) presented higher R values, as well as higher BLH and 
lower RH. In contrast, during Cold Dry Season (CDS) 
(November-February), RH was higher, BLH lower and 
correlations were lower, as shown in Table 2. 
At higher BLH, there are more convection processes, which 
cause particles to be lifted up into the atmosphere more 
easily, leading to better correlations between AOD columnar 
measurements and PM2.5 [31]. Conversely, in winter, at lower 
BLH, vertical mixing is reduced, resulting in low aerosol 
concentration in the upper atmosphere and high surface 
aerosol concentration, thus weakening the AOD and PM 2.5 
correlation [32]. 
Regarding the effect of RH on correlation coefficients, the 
2018-2023 RH average per site and season and the respective 
correlation values between PM2.5 and AOD were plotted 
(Figure 7), resulting in a moderately negative correlation 
(R=0.54, p < 0.05). RH has a high impact on the correlation 
between PM2.5 and AOD, as high RH affects the water uptake 
process of aerosols, which can cause changes in ae rosol size 
distribution, chemical composition and extinction 
characteristics [30]. As a result of the hygroscopic growth of 
aerosols, larger particles scatter and absorb more solar 
radiation (extinction) leading to a higher AOD, which includes 
the water ab sorbed by the aerosols, this can lead to an 
overestimation of the actual dry aerosol concentration near 
the ground if not properly accounted for [33]. 
To analyze the spatial variability of RH, the 2018 -2023 
average RH was plotted for each site and interpol ated using 
the Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) algorithm, then 
compared with the R values per season, as shown in Figure 8.

Regarding the relationship between the spatial variability of 
RH and the AOD and PM 2.5 correlation per season (Figure 8), 
Pearson correlation values per site were higher during HDS 
than during CDS; besides, in HDS, RH was 20% lower in 
comparison with RH in CDS. Two site s, CA and ININ, 
presented atypical patterns. The CA site exhibited a higher R 
value in CDS than in HDS. This could be caused by the 
diversity of PM2.5 emission sources near the site in HDS, since 
this site is heavily impacted by agricultural burnings and 
wildfires and secondary aerosol formation, meanwhile 
during CDS, the emission is mainly from PM resuspension. In 
the case of the ININ site, which has the highest RH due to its 
location within a forest preservation area between TVMA and 
Mexico City, the corr elation values are still moderate. This 
might be associated with the remote location of this site and 
the absence of local PM 2.5 emission sources. Therefore, the 
PM2.5 measured at this site is likely transported by wind from 
urban environments. As shown in Figure 9, the ININ PM 2.5 
hourly profile differs from the TVMA profile; the five -hour 
difference between the maximum concentrations is evidence 
of the transfer of air masses with PM 2.5, which is well mixed, 
increasing the AOD and PM2.5 correlation. The wind rose plot, 
elaborated from 10:00-17:00 h, indicates that the main wind 
direction originates from TVMA. This has been evidenced by 
Hernandez-Moreno et al. (2024), who analyzed the PM 2.5 
mass exchange between three metropolitan areas in central 
Mexico [34].

Further Steps 
 
While our study focuses on establishing the baseline 
feasibility and spatiotemporal dynamics of the direct AOD –
PM2.5 correlation, it is essential to contextualize these 
findings against recent methodological advancements in air 
quality estimation. Studies employing data fusion and 
machine learning (ML) models —such as Random Forest, 
Gradient Boosting, or Deep Learning —have significantly 
improved the acc uracy of PM 2.5 predictions by integrating 
AOD with a wide array of auxiliary inputs, including Boundary 
Layer Height, land-use data, and meteorological variables [26, 
27, 32]. Specifically, current research demonstrates that these 
complex ML approaches can achieve high predictive skill 
884 Environ Res Tec, Vol. 9, Issue 5, pp. 877-885, October 2026 
(often reporting cross -validation R 2 values exceeding 0.80) 
by effectively capturing the non -linear relationships ignored 
by simple linear correlation as reported by Lolli, (2025) [35]. 
Our identified moderate correlations (R <0.70) across the 
TVMA, particularly the weaker relationships in 
heterogeneous industrial areas, reinforce the necessity of 
moving beyond simple linear models. These baseline findings 
serve as a critical validation step, confirming that the non -
linear, loc ally specific factors identified here (e.g., source 
heterogeneity, coarse -mode aerosols) are precisely the 
variables that must be successfully integrated into advanced 
spatiotemporal ML models to achieve the high predictive 
accuracy required for operational air quality forecasting. The 
high predictive power of these applications is exemplified by 
Gutiérrez-Ávila et al. (2022), who developed a ML model for 
PM2.5 concentration within Mexico City. The model 
demonstrated robust performance, achieving overall cr oss-
validated Mean Absolute Errors (MAE) of 3.68μg/m 3 and 
9.20μg/m3 for daily mean and maximum daily 1 -hour PM2.5, 
respectively. This level of predictive accuracy is essential for 
proper health impact assessment [36]. 
 
CONCLUSIONS 
 
This study assessed the dynamics of the spatiotemporal 
correlation between Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) values 
obtained from MODIS MAIAC satellite data and ground -level 
PM2.5 measurements, which allowed for the identification of 
some of the main factors that affect th is relationship. 
Temporal analysis during the dry seasons (Nov – May) 
revealed a consistent monthly pattern between PM 2.5 and 
AOD, with both variables exhibiting higher concentrations 
during the Hot-Dry season (March-May). This trend is driven 
by meteorolo gical conditions characteristic of this period, 
namely low RH and higher Boundary Layer Height (BLH). 
The spatial correlation analysis revealed a moderate linear 
relationship between AOD and PM2.5 (0.4<R<0.69) across 
90% of the monitoring sites, with stat istical significance 
confirmed at the 1% level (p < 0.01). Specifically, sites that 
yielded higher correlation coefficients (R ranging from 0.50 
to 0.62) were generally situated in urban and suburban 
sectors dominated by vehicular emissions. Conversely, th ose 
locations with weaker correlations (R=0.33–0.49) were often 
attributed to a more complex and heterogeneous mix of 
emission sources, such as contributions from industrial 
activity, heavy -duty road traffic, agricultural burning, and a 
notable presence of coarse-mode aerosols. This emphasizes 
that the local emission context is a crucial factor that 
influences the AOD-PM2.5 relationship. 
Meteorological factors, specifically BLH and RH, were 
identified as key influences on the correlation between PM 2.5 
and A OD. Higher BLH and lower RH during the Hot -Dry 
season were associated with stronger correlations, likely due 
to enhanced vertical mixing of pollutants. Conversely, lower 
BLH and higher RH during the Cold-Dry season led to weaker 
correlations, since increas ed humidity can alter aerosol 
properties and diminish the AOD -PM2.5 relationship. The 
moderate negative correlation observed between RH and the 
correlation coefficients further supports this finding. The 
atypical patterns observed at the CA and ININ sites highlight 
the importance of considering localized emission 
characteristics and air mass transport phenomena when 
interpreting AOD-PM2.5 correlations. 
Overall, this study confirms the applicability of MAIAC AOD 
data to the further estimation of PM 2.5 concentrations in the 
TVMA and also, at once, highlights the complex interplay of 
emission sources and meteorological variables that modulate 
this relationship. Future studies should focus on developing 
more sophisticated models that integrate these influencing 
factors for the purpose of improving the accuracy of satellite-
based PM2.5 estimations, particularly in regions with diverse 
pollution sources and varying meteorological conditions.
