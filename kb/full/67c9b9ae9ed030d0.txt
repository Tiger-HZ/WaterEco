Abstract
Cyanobacteria are prokaryotic and photosynthetic organisms contained in various aquatic ecosystems. Multiple 
studies confirm the risks caused by their presence in aquatic bodies due to their ability to generate cyanotoxins. 
Similarly, it has been shown that these bloom-forming species can regulate their flotation through gas vacuoles, 
which represents a competitive advantage over other phytoplankton species, favoring their proliferation. The 
application of ultrasound (US) can break these gas vacuoles and facilitates the sedimentation of cyanobacterial cells, 
interrupting their vital functions, such as photosynthesis and nutrient absorption; however, it can also release 
cyanotoxins contained within cyanobacteria. In this work, low-frequency US was used to assess its potential in 
inactivating and removing cyanobacteria and cyanotoxins from a Colombian reservoir. The efficiency of the 
sonication process, using different exposure powers (10, 30, and 50 W) and times (5, 10, 20, and 30 min) at a 
frequency of 40 kHz was evaluated through microscopy, cytometry, and sedimentation tests, as well as by the 
analysis of cyanotoxins in water. The sonication process was optimized by using the response surface methodology 
through a full-factorial design of experiments. It was found that power and exposure time had a significant and 
directly proportional effect on the sedimentation of cyanobacterial cells after the sonication process (p-values equal 
to 0.0019 and 0.0050, respectively). A settling efficiency percentage of up to 74.9% was reached, while cell 
sedimentation was achieved in 5 min and its effect was significantly sustained for the following 72 h under optimal 
operating conditions (50 W of power and a contact time of 30 min). Nevertheless, under these conditions, 
cyanotoxins were not efficiently removed, since no significant degradation rates (< 10%) were observed for 
microcystin (MC) in the surface water matrix. In this regard, for scaling up purposes, even though US has been 
reported to be effective for cyanobacterial and cyanotoxin control, further and careful research must be conducted to 
avoid reaching erroneous conclusions that would limit the actual application of the US process in natural water 
matrices.
Keywords: Cyanobacteria; bloom; cyanotoxins; Microcystis spp.; advanced oxidation process; sonication; gas 
vacuole.
1. Introduction
Cyanobacteria exist in terrestrial and aquatic environments (Kulabhusan and Campbell, 2021; Guimarães et al., 
2025). They are photosynthetic gram-negative microorganisms that come in filamentous, unicellular, colonial or 
multicellular forms, and inhabit all possible environments, even under conditions of precarious light and available 
nutrients (Gaysina et al., 2019; Vidal et al., 2021). Indeed, their complexity and adaptability represent a great 
challenge (Gobler, 2020), since their presence has a negative impact on the environment due to the accumulation of 
toxins (Pham and Utsumi, 2018). Globally, climate change together with the eutrophication of water bodies have led 
to their excessive increase (Glibert, 2020; Paerl and Barnard, 2020). Microcystis spp., one of the most widespread

cyanobacteria, can proliferate and form blooms on the water surface (Caramés, 2016; Carmichael, 2024), whose 
harmful effects depend on the physiological mechanism of intoxication, as well as on the route and amount of 
exposure (Mohan et al., 2023).
Several biological, chemical, and physical treatments have been used to reduce and eliminate the proliferation of 
cyanobacteria and cyanotoxins in natural aquatic ecosystems (Cobo, 2015). These include the reduction of 
discharges with a high nutrient load, sediment coating agents, increased herbivory pressure, adsorption, algaecides, 
filters, wetlands, aeration or mixing, and ultrasound (US) (Joyce et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2021; Choi et al., 2022; 
Peng et al., 2024a). Each of these methods has certain limitations, even though they work to some degree. However, 
US has recently attracted attention due to its simple operation, low impact on ecosystems and the environment and, 
above all, because of its efficiency in controlling the proliferation of cyanobacteria and cyanotoxins (Park et al., 
2017; Yao et al., 2020; Munoz et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2025).
The reduction in algal growth occurs through the structural or functional rupture of the cells (Rajasekhar et al., 
2012a). Thus, there are two routes of inactivation, the destruction of cell integrity and that of the photosynthetic 
system. The former increases the sedimentation tendencies of the cell, while the latter decreases the photosynthesis 
function, i.e., the absorption of carbon dioxide (CO2) and the release of oxygen (O2) (Peng et al., 2020; Peng et al., 
2024b). The gas vacuoles are within the cell structure, whose function is to maintain the buoyancy of cyanobacteria, 
so that the cells can remain in water at the appropriate depth to receive enough O2, solar radiation, and nutrients 
(Den Uyl et al., 2021). Therefore, the collapse of the cavitation bubbles breaks the gas vacuoles, inhibiting 
physiological activities (Kurokawa et al., 2016; Schneider et al., 2015; Tan et al., 2018). Nevertheless, these 
aerotopes can be regenerated in a relatively short time after US application ceases (Li et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2024).
Once US is applied to water, various chemical and physical effects occur. These include the effect of pyrolysis, 
oxidation due to the action of free radicals and mechanical shearing produced by cavitation (Chen et al., 2020; Wu et 
al., 2025). At a high power but a low frequency, acoustic cavitation leads mainly to high shear forces or mechanical 
effects, resulting in cell rupture. On the other hand, at high frequencies, a larger amount of free radical species is 
produced and, therefore, the chemical damage to the cells can be significant (Wu et al., 2012), with the subsequent 
release of cyanotoxins. Consequently, the impact of US frequency must be such that it destroys the internal cellular 
structure of the cyanobacteria but not the external one. By maintaining the integrity of the cells and avoiding cell 
disruption, the release of harmful intracellular compounds is avoided (Huang et al., 2020), limiting the amount of 
extracellular cyanotoxins in the aquatic environment (Kong et al., 2019; Greenstein et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2025).
While there has been significant advancement in the utilization of US-based technologies in cyanobacteria and 
cyanotoxin control (Amabilis-Sosa et al., 2026; Joshi et al., 2026), as far as the authors’ knowledge the vast majority 
of previous studies have been confined to in vitro studies using axenic monoclonal cultures in widely standardized 
laboratory setups. This conventional method of treatment has left a critical knowledge gap in the externalization of 
findings to environment, particularly when the environmental effects of these methods are regarded to vary due to 
the low environmental complexity and physical robustness of natural phytoplankton communities (Zhu et al., 2026). 
To overcome this limitation and meet the demand to validate these technologies under real-world conditions, the US 
treatment of a natural cyanobacterial bloom is of utmost importance and can be optimized to make the acoustic 
treatment of a growing cyanobacterial and cyanotoxins in a natural bloom for resistance evaluation. This approach 
facilitates inclusion of phenotypic variability, including variation in Microcystis spp. macrocolonies, protective 
influence of mucilage, and interactions with dissolved organic matter (DOM) and coexisting species, by using real 
aqueous matrices. Acoustic dosimetry must be optimized with the aim of inducing biomass sedimentation via 
mechanical collapse of aerotopes (Bohrerova et al., 2023). This technique is intended to operate below the 
destructive cavitation cut off, thus preventing cell shear, and in turn, unwanted liberation of cyanotoxins into the 
aquatic medium (Joshi et al., 2026). Additionally, research should adopt a comprehensive study design with a 
regression analysis through response surface methodology (RSM) to mitigate the high intrinsic variability of natural

samples. This statistical instrument facilitates assessment along with maximizing the optimization and high accuracy

Under this scenario, the present study seeks to observe different operating conditions, focused on the variation of the 
power and the exposure time of low-frequency US (40 kHz), and to determine under which ranges a) the cell 
membrane remains intact, b) the gas vacuole collapses, c) the cyanobacterial cell lysis occurs, and d) the 
effectiveness of the US treatment under optimal conditions for cyanotoxins removal. Through this, it is intended to 
have an approach towards the viability of the implementation of this advanced oxidation process (AOP) in the 
treatment of natural water containing cyanobacteria aiming at discerning its safe and sustainable feasibility for the 
subsequent scaling up of the technology.
2. Material and methods
2.1. Reactants and chemicals
Analytical-quality chemical reagents were used for all experimental assays. Solutions were prepared in ultrapure 
water, which was obtained by a Milli-Q purification unit (Millipore Pty Ltd, USA). For cyanotoxin detection and 
quantification, solid phase extraction (SPE) protocol was followed by using HPLC-grade methanol (CH3OH ≥ 
99.8% purity). Further high-performance liquid chromatography analyses were conducted with analytical-grade 
ammonium formate (NH4HCO2, > 99.9% purity), ultrapure gases (argon (Ar), and nitrogen (N2)) and certified 
reference standards for the targeted cyanotoxins (MC-LR, MC-RR, MC-YR, and NOD, standing for microcystin-
LR, microcystin-RR, microcystin-YR, and nodularins, respectively) (with a purity greater than 95%), which were 
provided by Eurofins Abraxis (Warminster, USA).
2.2. Surface water sampled
To carry out the sonication tests, surface water samples were collected from a Colombian reservoir, by means of 
spot sampling and avoiding the dissipation of the algal bloom (Fig. 1). Two types of samples were taken, a 
superficial one from the photic zone (PZ) and another integrated up to the limit of the photic zone (LPZ), by using a 
Kemmerer bottle (Wildco, USA). The taxonomic classification was carried out using the dichotomous keys of 
Komárek & Anagnostidis (1989), Komárek & Anagnostidis (1999) and Hoffmann et al. (2005) to discern the 
presence of Microcystis spp., which is mainly the species of interest. Additionally, the matrix was characterized by 
physicochemical and microbiological variable measurements following APHA standard analytical methods (Lipps et 
al., 2023). Furthermore, the presence of cyanotoxins (e.g., MC-LR, MC-RR, MC-YR, and NOD) was identified and 
quantified.

2.3. Sonochemical reactor and sampling
The experimentation was carried out using a low-frequency US equipment (Meinhardt, Germany), which allows 
operation at 5 different powers (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 W). The cylindrical reactor has a 500 mL glass vessel. In Fig. 
S1, an image of the sonochemical reactor used and their components can be found. This reactor generates US waves 
at 40 kHz through a transducer. The low-frequency regime (20 to 40 kHz) is well documented to induce gas vesicle 
collapse without total lysis. At 40 kHz, the thermodynamics of the system is dominated by transient cavitation, as 
the uneven collapse of microbubbles creates very high mechanical shear stress and hydrodynamic shock waves. This 
low-frequency range has been shown to be effective in disrupting easily susceptible structures like gas vacuoles 
(Amabilis-Sosa et al., 2026). In contrast, US frequency >100 kHz induces the predominant mechanism to achieve 
stable cavitation, which mainly favors sonochemical effects (e.g., water thermolysis and reactive free radical 
generation). These oxidizing processes attack the cytoplasmic membrane directly, increasing cell lysis rates and the 
subsequent risk of massive release of intracellular toxins into the environment (Joshi et al., 2026).
On the other hand, the calorimetric method was used to evaluate the real power dissipated in the solution mostly in 
the form of heat. It was found that ~ 10% of the power generated was lost (Parra-Enciso et al., 2022). For its control, 
the reactor vessel has a water recirculation system, which mitigates the increase in the temperature of the water to be 
treated. During experimentation, the temperature was maintained around 27 ±3 °C by using a cooling system at a 
temperature of 21 ±1 °C. It is important to note that although acoustic cavitation induces hot spots at a microscale, 
which are transient extreme microbubble collapse states, the rapid heat dissipation in this microscale phenomenon 
prevents thermal changes to the fluid at a macroscopic level (Suo et al., 2025).
During experimentation, 50 mL aliquots were extracted after the established exposure times (5, 10, 20, and 30 min), 
in addition to a control sample (0 min), and supernatant was used for further analyses concerning cell removal. 
Before withdrawing aliquots, the entire sample volume was shaken to maintain individual cells and colonies with 
different sizes in suspension to counterbalance buoyancy and sedimentation. This agitation was driven to prevent 
shear forces that would cause premature cell lysis, mucilage dissociation or colony rupture prior to acoustic 
treatment. Subsequently, subsamples were collected from the center of the container. The uniformity of this process 
was demonstrated in the replicates (n = 3) of the control sample, which was not subjected to US treatment.
2.4. Design of experiments for optimization purposes
To evaluate the US effect on cyanobacteria sedimentation, the natural water matrix was subjected to different 
sonochemical experiments. At 40 kHz, the power and exposure time were increased, obtaining samples under 
minimum, medium and maximum powers of 10, 30, and 50 W, and at time intervals of 5, 10, 20, and 30 min. 
Additionally, a control sample (0 min) was obtained for each experiment, which was carried out in triplicate. A total 
of 15 samples were withdrawn, and the cell density was determined at the beginning and at the end of each 
treatment.
To optimize the operating conditions for obtaining the maximum response (i.e., the percentage of settling of the 
cyanobacterial cells, denoted as R (%)), as described by Equation (1), the results found from the sonication process 
were analyzed through Statgraphics Centurion 19® software (Statgraphics Technologies, Inc. 2020) (Statpoint, 
USA). For this, RSM was used, by means of a 3-level factorial design (32), which shows the effects of 2 individual 
factors, the power and the exposure time, including 3 central points. Applying a full factorial design within RSM 
allows modelling factors and responses, instead of obtaining only means of the data analyzed. It is highlighted that 
the most efficient way to estimate experimental error involves replicating the center point, instead of replicating the 
entire factorial design, due to unnecessary expenses are incurred for personnel, reagents, supplies, and energy,

2.5. Analysis methods and procedures
2.5.1. Cell counts by microscopy
An Eclipse E200 model phase contrast trinocular optical microscope (Nikon, USA) was utilized for counting the 
number of cells and colonies of Microcystis spp. In addition, the IC Measure program v. 2.0.0.286 (2021) (The 
Imaging Source, USA) was used to facilitate its visualization, through the USB connection of a Z14-HD camera 
(Nikon, USA) of 14 MP coupled to the microscope. For counting, a Neubauer camera (Boeco, Germany) was used, 
which has two fields and allows 2 counts to be made simultaneously. In this regard, 3 quadrants of both fields were 
randomly selected, with 16 grids each. Observations were made at 400X and 100X total magnification for cell and 
colony number, respectively. Each sample was completely homogenized and 30 µL was used. To calculate the cell 
density per mL, the average value obtained was multiplied by the dilution factor of the chamber, which was 5000. In 
the case of the controls, it was necessary to dilute by a factor of 10, taking 100 µL of sample to a 1 mL Eppendorf 
and volumizing with distilled water.
2.5.2. Flow cytometry
To evaluate the impact on the function and structure of cyanobacterial cells and, therefore, on the viability of these 
microorganisms, a cell analysis was performed using flow cytometry. A LIVE/DEAD Backlight bacterial viability 
kit reference L7007 was used and was purchased from Molecular Probes (Eugene, USA). This consists of a mixture 
of two stains, SYTO 9 5 mM and propidium iodide 10 µg/mL (green and red fluorescent nucleic acids, 
respectively). Thus, bacteria with intact and damaged cell membranes stain fluorescent green and red, respectively. 
The background remains virtually non-fluorescent. To supply a baseline cellular fluorescence, an unstained control 
sample was measured. Additionally, a filter for the detection of chlorophyll was activated, at wavelengths of 650, 
700, and 750 nm, considering the maxima excitation/emission of the cyanobacteria. Finally, immediately after each 
assay, 200 µL of sample were taken and 1 µL of SYTO 9 and 1 µL of PI were added. Then, the suspension was 
homogenized by means of a vortex shaker and incubated in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. Reading was 
performed on a CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter, USA) using a blue laser at 488 nm to excite the dyes. SYTO 9 was

2.5.3. Sedimentation tests
Regarding the buoyancy of the cyanobacterial cells, sedimentation tests were carried out. These consisted of 
observing the cell position in the water column, after the sonication process. For this purpose, a 10 mL sample was 
taken in a 15 mL falcon, and its evolution was monitored after 8, 24, 48, and 72 h. The assembly was left in a space 
with natural light and free from external disturbances that could cause the cells to resuspend.
2.5.4. Cyanotoxin analysis
Cyanotoxin detection and quantification, particularly MC-LR, MC-RR, MC-YR, and NOD, were performed by 
using a high-resolution ACQUITY UPLC H-Class equipment with a Xevo TQD triple quadrupole mass 
spectrometer (UPLC/MS/MS) (Waters, USA). After purification, target analytes were isolated and enriched with 
SPE prior to performing instrumental evaluation. Acoustic cavitation treatment was done directly on the raw, 
unfiltered form and, subsequently, the analysis required the removal of the dense algal biomass. This required 
passing the matrix through 0.45 μm glass microfiber filters before commencing the SPE process.
Analyte extraction and enrichment
SPE was performed in Oasis HLB (Hydrophilic-Lipophilic Balance) cartridges at a dosage of 60 mg/3 mL (Waters, 
USA). First, manifold lines were flushed with 90:10 water to methanol to avoid cross-contamination. Cartridge was 
activated by successive gravity-fed loading of 10 mL of HPLC-grade methanol and 10 mL of ultrapure water, 
maintaining continuous solvation in the sorbent bed. After conditioning, 50 mL of the clarified aqueous samples 
were drawn through the stationary phase under vacuum with controlled flow of 5 mL/min. Then, target compounds 
were desorbed into 25 mL glass receptacles using 10 mL of HPLC-grade methanol. To concentrate the extract, the 
eluate was evaporated to about 1 mL in a gentle air run for about 2 h. This concentrated fraction was pipetted to a 5 
mL volumetric suspension flask; the initial container was rinsed with small volumes of 90:10 methanol-water 
solution to ensure complete recovery and standardized for dilution using the same solvent. Final preparation was 
maintained in a 2 mL amber vial fitted with specialized UPLC-compatible preslit septum, resulting in a net 
preconcentration factor of 10.
Instrumental analysis and quantification
For every sample withdrawn after the sonication treatment at optimal conditions, 30 μL were injected into 
UPLC/MS/MS. Chromatographic resolution was obtained on a Kinetex C18 column (2.1 mm internal diameter × 50 
mm length; and 1.7 μm particle size); maintained at 30 ºC. The mobile phase was transported during 10 min at 0.25 
mL/min and the autosampler chamber was cooled to 15 ºC. Eluent was composed of an aqueous phase 
(water/methanol, 95:5 v/v) and an organic phase (methanol/water, 95:5 v/v) that were both buffered with 5 mM 
ammonium formate. In mass spectrometric scanning, ESI+ mode at 3.5 kV was used for the detection of mass 
spectrometry. The ion source and desolvation temperatures were set at 150 ºC and 350 ºC, respectively. N2 was used 
for both desolvation (650 L/h) and for the cone (50 L/h). Each target cyanotoxin had its own calibration function 
developed for it over a dynamic range from 1 to 50 µg/L (for 1, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 µg/L concentration) from a 
200 µg/L methanolic mixed stock standard. As the lowest calibration value providing acceptable accuracy and 
precision, the limit of quantification (LOQ) was 1 µg/L, given the preconcentration of the material (10-fold). All 
analyte signals below this threshold were defined as <LOQ.

3.3. Effect of US on cell integrity
Flow cytometry was used in combination with fluorescent probes to determine the cell integrity. Using a chlorophyll 
filter or detector, the cell counts were discretized from the total number of events, for each sonication experiment. 
Approximately 6000 events were recorded for the control samples, in the viable and non-viable regions. In Fig. 5, 
the positive count for chlorophyll at 650 nm is observed after the sonication tests under different powers and at 40 
kHz. The increase in the count as the exposure time is prolonged is evident. This is due to the cell disintegration 
described above. Nevertheless, under the maximum power of 50 W, a rapid decrease in the count is reached from 5 
min, but even so the initial count is exceeded at the end of the 30 min of US treatment.
Although the treatment effectively induces colony breakdown, as shown by the increase in the number of single 
cells displayed in Fig. 5, such an alteration of morphology presents a ecological trade-off. The disruption of very 
large colonial aggregates is effective in inhibiting the production of scum on the surface. However, the increase in 
number of single cells after treatment substantially increases their bioavailability to filter-feeding zooplankton (e.g., 
large-bodied cladocerans). Although this higher pressure could theoretically make top-down biological control of 
the cyanobacterial population easier, it can also result in quicker accumulation, uptake and trophic transfer of 
intracellular cyanotoxins to the aquatic food chain. In addition, a high density of dispersed single cells is a challenge 
for drinking water treatment plants, since unicellular cyanobacteria can easily bypass conventional physical barriers, 
such as intake screens and sand filters, and have lower settling velocities during conventional coagulation-
flocculation processes than larger colonies, thus requiring supplementary downstream removal strategies.

4. Discussion
4.1. Effectiveness of the treatment for cyanobacteria and cyanotoxin control
Several methods have been used to evaluate the effects or cell damage caused in the control of algae. Among these, 
the most prominent are chlorophyll-a (Chl-a), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM), flow cytometry (Franklin, 2021; Park et al., 2019). Nonetheless, despite the potential of 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
combining flow cytometry with fluorescent molecular probes to evaluate the integrity of cyanobacterial cell 
membranes (Aguilera et al., 2021; Kozik et al., 2019), distinguishing between particles and organisms can pose a 
challenge (Olusoji et al., 2021). Thus, it is crucial to grasp the analytical methods' mechanisms to prevent 
misinterpretation. Typically, algal biomass is quantified through cell counts under a microscope and Chl-a 
concentrations, while optical density (OD) serves as an indicator of cell density. Additionally, cyanotoxin analysis is 
required to evaluate the effectiveness of an specific treatment for controlling cianobacterial cells and cyanotoxins in 
aqueous environments.
Joyce et al. (2010) evaluated the effect of several frequencies from low (20 kHz) to high (1146 kHz) values on the 
removal of Microcystis aeruginosa. The removal efficiencies were from the highest to the lowest ones in the order 
20 < 1146 < 864 < 580 kHz. In Fig. 5 and 6 of the present work, similar results were presented, where the cell count 
obtained by flow cytometry increased with the exposure time. However, Park et al. (2017) stated that low-frequency 
US (20-40 kHz) can cause the breakdown of algae clumps, which can change the OD of a test solution. Likewise, 
Wang et al. (2014) obtained a rise in the cell number in the first 2 min of treatment at 20 kHz, by breaking the cell 
aggregates to later form individual cells. Additionally, they reported that if there is enough US energy to act on 
individual cells, some of these divide into cell fragments. In this regard, the cell fragment production and the 
agglomerated cell dispersion cause an increase in cell counts, which explains the convenience of comparing them 
with the counts under the microscope.
Similarly, Peng et al. (2020) observed a scarce impact on algal cells when subjected to 20 kHz at 0,038 W/mL, 
obtaining an increase in the cell number after 2 min and reaching a kill rate of 10.5% at 15 min of exposure. 
Therefore, 20 kHz US energy input was probably not sufficient to remove Microcystis aeruginosa cells. 
Nevertheless, Wu et al. (2012) measured the cell reduction by means of a Neubauer camera and validated the data 
obtained using OD680. Under these conditions, 91.5% reduction was obtained, while OD measurements showed a 
reduction of only 44.6%. According to Park et al. (2017), this difference is probably ascribed to the fact that in the 
Neubauer camera the remaining whole number is measured, while the OD measures the total number of organisms 
that are suspended, counting the cells in suspension that are broken. This explains the fact of that reduction rates of 
up to 74.9% in cell concentration were obtained through counting with the Neubauer camera, while counting 
through flow cytometry shows a significant increase and, despite a subsequent reduction.
On the other hand, it has been shown that by augmenting the US power (or intensity), the algae cell concentration is 
further reduced (Dehghani, 2016). Nevertheless, algae cells, including Microcystis spp., can be easily disrupted by 
high intensities. Liu et al. informed that ~ 90% of algal cells were disrupted after sonication (40 kHz, 1 W/mL) for 1 
min of treatment (Liu et al., 2016). In turn, Rajasekhar et al. (2012b) observed that low-frequency sonication with 
high intensity (20 kHz and 0.32 W/mL) was effective in inhibiting the growth of Microcystis aeruginosa cells. Other 
studies have indicated that increasing the intensity can be counterproductive, since despite accelerating the rate of 
algae cell removal, it can lead to cell disruption. Zhang et al. (2006a) found that toxin release was significantly 
increased under power greater than 48 W; therefore, they recommended the use of lower US power. Additionally, 
Peng et al. (2020) indicated that US can result in the release of organic matter that is contained within the cell, which 
mainly consists of protein compounds, chlorophyll, and humic substances. Similarly, excessive ultrasonic treatment, 
according to the findings reported by Tan et al. (2018), can cause an increase in dissolved nutrients (N and P) due to 
cell alteration. The use of 35 kHz at 0.035 W/mL for 20 min is suggested by the referred authors. This is expected to 
effectively inhibit the physiological activities of Microcystis spp. without generating marked cell disruption, 
avoiding the release of cell inclusions that can generate adverse effects. Hence, the green coloration of the water 
obtained in the current study (Fig. 9) after 20 min can be explained by the release of photosynthetic pigments under 
30 and 50 W, evidencing a greater effect on cell integrity after prolonged sonication.
Consequently, the preferable form of removal of cyanobacterial cells is through the buoyancy loss due to the 
ultrasonic collapse of the gas vacuoles, while maintaining cell integrity. This is the form of removal most conducive 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
to the aquatic environment safety (Hu & Rzymski, 2019). However, low-frequency US can cause gas vacuoles to 
resonate and collapse (Chen et al., 2020). Thus, the inhibition of cyanobacterial cell growth is mainly attributed to 
the effect of sonication on gas vacuoles. For this reason, Rajasekhar et al. (2012b) found in other algae species, such 
as Chlorella spp., the lack of such organelles. Additionally, the implosion of the cavitation bubbles produces the 
photosynthetic system rupture of algae cells, as well as the structure of phycobiliprotein and chlorophyll, affecting 
the energy metabolism and substance conversion. This improves cell sedimentation by reducing photosynthesis 
(Peng et al., 2020). Similarly, Chen et al. (2020) demonstrated a decrease in the rate of O2 production from 
photosynthesis. Meanwhile, Zhang et al. (2006b) found that 25 kHz at 0.32 W/mL resulted in a decrease in the 
content of Chl-a, phycobiliprotein, and the rate of O2 release from photosynthesis, after 5 min of treatment. It was 
also reported that US caused a higher damage to the phycobiliprotein in comparison with that caused to Chl-a (Chen 
et al., 2020).
US treatment also produces morphological changes in Microcystis spp., making it possible to demonstrate its impact 
on the cell structure. Thus, Peng et al. (2020) managed to verify the rupture of the gas vacuoles, by observing a 
decrease in the cell size and wrinkles on the cell surface through SEM images. Likewise, Park et al. (2019) observed 
that, although there was no perceptible alteration of the cell membrane by SEM images, cell damage was clearly 
shown in TEM images, even under the lowest frequency of 36 kHz at 300 W, with a noticeable reduction or 
shrinkage of the gas vacuoles. In the present study, it was also possible to detail the cell structure (Fig. 8) by optical 
microscopy, indicating that the use of 40 kHz under the different US powers evaluated (10, 30, and 50 W) resulted, 
in addition to the gas vacuole collapse, in a loss of cell organelles even at the minimum exposure time of 5 min (Fig. 
9). In fact, Lee et al. (2001) obtained the settlement of algal cells at 28 kHz and 120 W in 30 s.
It should be noted that, after the gas vacuole collapse, the cells can be recovered under optimal conditions of 
aeration and light irradiation; it is taking approximately 3 d for the regeneration of the gas vacuoles to be sufficient 
to provide buoyancy again (Lee et al., 2000; Walsby, 1992). Thus, Li et al. (2020) found that the distribution of the 
cells of the sonicated group was almost restored after 72 h to the level of the control group, with the buoyancy 
capacity recovering. In turn, Rodriguez-Molares et al. (2014) refuted that the sedimentation phenomenon by US is a 
short-term effect, indicating that 14 d of incubation after sonication (10 min at 8.24 W and 21.5 kHz) led to a slight 
recovery of cells in the upper layer, but these were still 82% smaller than in the control sample. Therefore, as shown 
in Fig. 10, only a small percentage of cells migrated to the surface after 72 h in the sample sonicated under minimum 
power (40 kHz - 10 W) for 5 min. Nonetheless, further studies are required to determine the efficiency of the 
treatment when applied under different environmental and US conditions.
Furthermore, regarding the use of propidium iodide (PI) as a viability marker following low-frequency US exposure, 
it is well established that acoustic cavitation can induce transient membrane permeabilization, potentially allowing 
PI entry into cells that retain the capacity for membrane repair and subsequent recovery. Consequently, classification 
as non-viable based solely on PI inclusion immediately post-treatment may overestimate irreversible damage by 
failing to distinguish cells with reversible lesions from those committed to cell death (Trinh & Lee, 2022; Rosenberg 
et al., 2019). This limitation is inherent to single-time-point membrane integrity assays and is particularly relevant 
when physical perturbations, such as US, are applied. Without a recovery period or an independent validation of 
irreversibility, this approach cannot definitively discriminate between these two populations. In this regard, future 
studies should incorporate temporal sampling or complementary viability metrics to address this issue.
Moreover, regarding the marginal effects of US under the experimental optimal conditions found here, normalized 
concentration curves (C/C0) show that the degradation kinetics for the MC-LR, MC-RR, and MC-YR variants are 
practically negligible. In this context, the limited effectiveness of US treatment is attributed to an imbalance between 
providing acoustic energy and the chemical loading of the matrix (Loaiza-González et al., 2025). The high initial 
concentration had a limiting effect, as acoustic cavitation and free radical generation depend greatly on the 
relationship between intensity (or power) and analyte concentration. The concentrations of cyanotoxins in the 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
natural matrix were found to be extremely high in this study, particularly for MC-LR. Thus, the thermodynamics 
and kinetics of the assessed AOP prove that the more pollution is initially present, the much larger an energy input is 
needed to keep degradation rates (Klemenčič & Eleršek, 2025). In this regard, a power of 50 W for 30 min of 
treatment was insufficient to oxidize the high cyanotoxin load. On the other hand, competitive cell lysis can occur, 
since the US waves produce shear stress leading to lysis of the surviving cyanobacterial cells. The intracellular 
toxins can be thus continuously released to the aqueous medium. This continual influx of newly dissolved 
metabolites to the solution counteracts or hides the effect of any possible simultaneous chemical degradation 
occurring as a result of reactive oxygen species (ROS) attack and can be responsible for the fluctuation of the 
concentrations measured and their remain as stable values instead of decreasing significantly.
The need to focus efforts on the field is highlighted; since the application of US is demonstrated to be an effective 
control method against the proliferation of algae, especially cyanobacteria, but it also has a possible negative impact 
on other important aquatic organisms for the ecosystem, such as zooplankton, fish and submerged plants (Chen et 
al., 2020; Cobo, 2015). Even though, in this study, the activity of various protozoa was evidenced under the 
microscope after the sample sonication under the three powers evaluated (10, 30, and 50 W), research is needed to 
discern if this control method can be used as a low impact technology on aquatic organisms and obtaining an ideal 
adjustment of the operating parameters so that water quality improvement is required.
4.2. Scaling-up limitations and future perspectives
It is important to note that the high retention of cell viability (~80%) in the sediment postulates a latent risk of 
phenotypic resuspension after the 72-h observation period. The ability of these populations to synthesize gas 
vesicles and restore their cell density to values lower than those of the surrounding medium could generate re-
floating events (Bohrerova et al., 2023). Therefore, for larger-scale applications, induced sedimentation must 
necessarily be coupled with purge systems or physical removal of sludge generated before exceeding the 
physiological latency window for vesicular regeneration (Peng et al., 2024b).
Furthermore, settling velocities are strongly linked to energy dose (Fig. 9), and visible optical divergences in the 
clarification of the post-treatment water column are clearly observed. A direct correlation occurs between the 
applied power and the magnitude of the structural collapse of the intracellular gas vesicles. When applied low 
powers (10 W), partial vesicular collapse creates a suboptimal density differential, retaining suspended biomass. In 
contrast, at powers of 50 W, the critical pressure of the vesicles is exceeded to maximize effective biomass density 
and speed of the precipitation process. However, both empirical quantification of terminal settling rates and their 
kinetic modeling are key areas for further research to enable scaling up of the low-frequency US process 
(Habtemariam et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2026).
On the other hand, it is recommended to quantify the change in the concentrations of dissolved organic carbon 
(DOC), total nitrogen (TN), or total phosphorus (TP) in the post-treatment water column. Cyanobacterial cells may 
rupture, leading to the leaching of intracellular nutrients into the bulk. Such bioavailable nutrient input may have a 
latent risk of local re-eutrophication as it can cause the population growth of the cyanobacterial fraction surviving 
the stress of the treatment (Ahmad et al., 2025). Therefore, systematic monitoring of nutrient dynamics (DOC, TN, 
and TP) in tandem with long-term growth trials should become a prerequisite before projecting the environmental 
scalability of low-frequency US for the treatment of cyanobacteria and their cyanotoxins in water.
US treatment resulted to be effective at promoting the settling of Microcystis spp., within the experimental domain. 
Nonetheless, collateral effects on other aquatic living beings might occur. Exposure to US waves exerts a selective 
pressure on planktonic organisms (Joshi et al., 2026) as mentioned above. The success of US is the forced collapse 
of intracellular gas vesicles, as buoyancy-dependent cyanobacteria are particularly affected by US. Non-target 
phytoplankton without those structures, like chlorophytes and diatoms, tend to have a higher threshold of resistance 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
to acoustic stress at low energy levels, potentially fostering the transition to nontoxic algal species once the bloom is 
removed. Zooplankton, residing in the environmental matrix, may be vulnerable to mechanical stresses resulting 
from acoustic cavitation events and induced microcurrents (Li et al., 2026). Whether planktonic micro-invertebrates 
are affected directly by physical damage, immobilization or modification of feeding behavior may depend on such 
frequency and strength. Moreover, the rapid induction of Microcystis spp. sedimentation indicates a shift in the 
availability of suspended biomass with the potential to affect the local trophic cycle, influencing non-target 
biodiversity. Moreover, although some protozoa in the initial microscopic observation showed residual motility 
activity after irradiation, exposure to low-frequency US (40 kHz) can induce pronounced structural damage in non-
target organisms, particularly microcrustaceans and filter-feeding zooplankton (e.g., cladocerans and rotifers). 
Consequently, the ecotoxicological effect and perturbation ascribed to the treatment system assessed are suggested 
to enable the scaling of procedures through quantitative survival assays of meso- and microzooplankton.
In addition to biological effects, US might also lead to physicochemical alterations of the water matrix. Physically, 
US may promote the disruption of microbial flocs and the fragmentation of particulate organic matter (POM), which 
can generate temporary fluctuations in turbidity and alter natural sedimentation dynamics (Li et al., 2026). At a 
chemical level, cavitation might result in the formation of ROS, which could react with DOM or generate local 
oxidative stress (Wu et al., 2025). Thus, the practical implementation of this technology on an ecosystem scale needs 
to be optimized with respect to power density along with intermittent exposure times to achieve the best possible 
disruption of cyanobacteria while simultaneously minimizing unwanted physicochemical and ecological disruptions.
Finally, regarding the cost analysis, the energy consumption under optimal laboratory conditions is estimated to be 
50 kWh/m3. It is observed that the energy density is considerably high compared to conventional water treatments 
(e.g., the associated cost for traditional coagulation/flocculation is typically < 1 kWh/m3), or even with other AOPs 
for cyanobacteria mitigation (usually in the range of 1-10 kWh/m3) (Ortiz et al., 2026). Using low-frequency US 
under the optimal conditions found here to treat the full volume of a reservoir in its current batch configuration 
would not be economically feasible. In this regard, further developments are needed for technical applicability and 
economic viability in real-world applications (Amabilis-Sosa et al., 2026). Among these approaches, switching from 
batch reactors to flow-through systems, testing pulsed rather than continuous energy application regimes, and 
limiting treatment to high biomass accumulation areas (i.e., retention bays or littoral zones) can be named to 
substantially reduce the effective volume of treatment and, consequently, total operating costs.
5. Conclusions
Low-frequency US at 40 kHz under 10, 30, and 50 W allowed a high percentage of settling and inactivation of 
cyanobacterial cells. The rate of cell density reduction was proportional to the increase in the sonication power. In 5 
min of treatment, a reduction efficiency of around 50% of cells was achieved under the three power levels evaluated. 
The maximum percentage of reduction obtained was 74.9%, after 30 min at 50 W. Likewise, the effect of 
disintegration in the cells was observed, reducing the number and size of the colonies. Additionally, collapse of the 
gas vacuoles was achieved even when using the minimum power of 10 W. Sedimentation of the cells was observed 
to occur after 5 min of treatment and this effect was significantly sustained during the following 72 h.
Microcystis spp. under low-frequency US (40 kHz) at the optimal operating conditions (50 W and 30 min of 
treatment) resulted to be controlled by the loss of buoyancy associated with the collapse of gas vacuoles, even at the 
lowest power evaluated (10 W). However, the cell damage caused, especially after 20 min of sonication, produced 
the release of cell inclusions, which can lead to detrimental repercussions on the environment and human health. On 
the other hand, under these optimal operating conditions, cyanotoxin (MC-LR, MC-RR, and MC-YR) removal rates 
were insignificant (<10%), posing a risk to human health if subsequent treatments are not conducted.

Thus, to discern whether US induced cyanobacteria reduction and sedimentation without negatively causing massive 
destruction of the cell structure and if it is an effective treatment under the experimental conditions evaluated here 
for the cyanobacterial bloom control, further research is needed to probe the safe and sustainable feasibility of US 
for the treatment of water polluted with cyanobacteria, particularly for scaling up purposes.
