Abstract
Monitoring water quality is challenging in data-scarce regions. This study examines the use of
remote sensing to assess key water quality parameters in Lake T ana, Northwestern Ethiopia, to
support the sustainable management of Lake water resources. The Moderate Resolution Imaging
Spectroradiometer satellite imagery was applied for long-term spatiotemporal assessment of total
suspended solids (TSS) and turbidity. The results show that annual mean TSS increased from
350 mg l−1 in 2000–640 mg l −1 in 2020, while turbidity increased from 120 to about 500 NTU
before both parameters declined during 2020–2023. Seasonal analysis indicates that the highest
TSS and turbidity occurred during the peak rainy month of July. The spatial variation revealed
that elevated TSS and turbidity levels were concentrated primarily at the inlets of the major feeder
rivers to Lake T ana. This reflects a substantial influx of sediment into the Lake during rainy sea-
sons. During post-rainy months, TSS is redistributed across different sections of the Lake by wind.
The findings of this work demonstrate the ability of remote sensing to provide long-term monitor-
ing of water quality parameters, offering valuable insights for the sustainable management of Lake
water resources.
1.Introduction
Lakes play a vital role in supporting a wide range of environmental and biological services (Hou et al
2017). Globally, they are the sole source of drinking water, irrigation, fisheries, power generation,
regional climate regulation, and biodiversity conservation (W oolway et al 2020, Makanda et al 2022,
Mishra 2023). However, the quality of Lake water is progressively deteriorating due to both natural and
anthropogenic factors (Shi et al 2018, Walton et al 2024). Climate change, combined with human activi-
ties, is contributing significantly to declines in water quality Solheim et al 2010, Li et al 2015, Mujere and
Moyce 2017). V ery rapid population growth, land use/cover changes, agricultural intensification, urban-
ization, and poor watershed management accelerate the deterioration of Lake water quality, particularly
in developing nations (Dodds and Smith 2016). The degradation of quality can be exacerbated by cli-
mate variability, including rising temperatures, increased evaporation, and altered precipitation patterns,
which affect runoff and sedimentation (Y adava et al 2023).
T otal suspended solids (TSS) and turbidity are the two primary indicators of water quality. They are
essential metrics for tracking water quality and evaluating the extent of pollution (Kusari and Ahmedi
2013, Wu et al 2014). These parameters are widely used to track surface water quality, as they respond
rapidly to watershed disturbances and can be effectively retrieved from satellite data (Dorji and Fearns
2017, Adjovu et al 2023, Friedmann et al 2024). Extreme hydroclimate events, such as intense rainfall,
can increase soil erosion and sediment transport from upper catchments into the Lake. These events
increase TSS and turbidity in the Lake, which affect water quality (Delpla et al 2009). Water carries
pollutants and suspended sediments/solids, which decrease light penetration through the water column

and affect the aquatic system (W omber et al 2021). Elevated TSS and turbidity also increase water treat-
ment costs and pose risks to public health by facilitating the transport of pathogens and contaminants
(Zimoch and Paciej 2020).
Lake T ana is the largest Lake in Ethiopia and is used for drinking water supply, irrigation and trans-
portation. The Ethiopian government selected the Lake as a development corridor. However, Lake T ana’s
water quality is declining due to increased pressure from surrounding catchment land-use changes, agri-
cultural expansion, deforestation, and urban development. Studies have highlighted that Lake T ana’s
water quality is deteriorating (Goshu and Akoma 2010, Ligdi et al 2010, W ondim 2016, Tibebe et al
2019, Abegaz et al 2023, Engdaw et al 2025b). This leads to proliferation of water hyacinth and worse
conditions for human and animal health. Many previous water quality studies in Lake T ana (e.g. Goshu
et al 2017, Tibebe et al 2019, W omber et al 2021) primarily relied on grab sampling, focusing on local-
ized areas around towns and at the confluence of a single river. Their findings indicated increased pol-
lutant accumulation in the Lake, attributed to elevated nutrient inflows, which have negatively impacted
water quality. However, the limited geographic scope of these studies did not provide a comprehensive
assessment of Lake T ana as a whole. Moreover, the field sampling analysis is limited to temporal varia-
tion and does not account for the long-term dynamics of TSS and turbidity. Those gaps highlight the
need for more extensive spatiotemporal assessment (Zhao et al 2020, Yunus et al 2021). Thus, an effec-
tive alternative is to use satellite datasets, which can reduce sample size and effectively address spatial
and temporal variations (Glasgow et al 2004). Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS)
imagery has been widely used for long-term monitoring of inland water quality due to its high tempo-
ral resolution and consistent data coverage within 8 d (T arrant et al 2010, Shi et al 2018, Cao et al 2023).
It has demonstrated the capability to estimate TSS and turbidity at regional and global scales for open
water bodies, particularly for Lakes (Chen et al 2015, Rocha et al 2024).
Therefore, this research aimed to (i) examine the long-term spatio-temporal trends of TSS and tur-
bidity (2000–2023) and (ii) delineate the hotspot sites highly sensitive to TSS and turbidity. The study
findings will serve as guidance for decision-making and implementation of sustainable management of
Lake T ana and similar Lake water resources globally. The results are expected to support extension work-
ers, water resource managers, and environmental planners involved in Lake water resource planning and
management activities.
2.Materialsandmethods
2.1.Studysite
Lake T ana is the largest Lake in Ethiopia, located in the northwestern highlands, and serves as the pri-
mary source for the Blue Nile River. The Lake covers an area of approximately 3000 km 2. It is geo-
graphically situated in the 37 UTM zone between 12 ◦0′ latitude and 37 ◦15′ longitude (figure 1). The
Lake drains a wide catchment area (T ana Sub-Basin) and has an average depth of 9 m. Many permanent
rivers, including Gilgel Abay, Gumara, Ribb, Megech, and small tributaries, feed the Lake. The T ana Lake
water is utilized for drinking, hydropower, agriculture, fishing, tourism, and transportation. The govern-
ment established a growth corridor with many irrigation schemes around Lake T ana and its surrounding
watersheds. The catchments of the Lake Basin experience a unimodal rainfall regime with peak precipita-
tion during Kiremt (Ethiopian rainy season) from June to August.
2.2.DatasourcesandacquisitionformonitoringTSSandturbidity
MODIS provides long-term, continuous Earth observation data that are particularly useful for large
inland water bodies due to its high temporal resolution and radiometric stability (Zhou et al 2018,
Xiong et al 2020). The current study utilized the multitemporal MODIS T erra Surface Reflectance prod-
uct (MOD09A1) to estimate the TSS and turbidity levels over Lake T ana. It was applied to analyze the
reflectance values in specific spectral bands using the Google Earth Engine platform (GEE). The GEE
cloud-computing environment enables efficient handling of large time-series datasets and supports
reproducible analysis of long-term spatiotemporal dynamics for environmental assessments (Zhao et al
2021, Ghosh et al 2022, Chen et al 2024). MODIS-T erra images have been obtained at a spatial resolu-
tion of 250 m and a temporal resolution of 8 d. The images were preprocessed, and near-infrared (NIR)
reflectance values were extracted from single bands. Quality assurance layers were applied to mask clouds
and poor-quality pixels to reduce noise and uncertainty in the reflectance data.

Atmospheric correction was applied to the MODIS images to remove atmospheric interference and
obtain accurate surface reflectance values. This step is crucial for reducing the effects of aerosol scatter-
ing and gaseous absorption, thereby improving the reliability of satellite-derived water quality parameters
(V ermote and Kotchenova 2010). Only high-quality water pixels were retained for analysis to minimize
uncertainties associated with atmospheric contamination and mixed pixels. These preprocessing steps
substantially reduced noise and improved the reliability of the retrieved water quality parameters.
2.3.RetrievalofTSSandturbidity
Suspended sediments strongly influence the spectral reflectance of inland waters, particularly in the NIR
region. In the NIR, water absorption is dominant, whereas scattering is primarily controlled by sus-
pended particulate matter. Consequently, NIR surface reflectance is expected to exhibit an approximately
linear relationship with suspended sediment concentration. This relationship has been widely validated
in previous studies and forms the theoretical basis for satellite-based retrieval of TSS and turbidity in
optically active inland waters (Sutherland et al 2000, Doxaran et al 2002).
In the current study, GEE scripts were developed to extract surface reflectance values from the
MODIS NIR band for each image acquired during the study period (2000–2023). The extracted NIR
surface reflectance values were subsequently converted to TSS and turbidity using previously developed
and validated empirical regression models for Lake T ana. These models were established by relating
in situ measurements of TSS and turbidity reported in earlier studies (Goshu et al 2017, Tibebe et al
2019, W omber et al 2021, Leggesse et al 2024, Nkwasa et al 2024) to the corresponding MODIS surface
reflectance values. The resulting long-term time series enabled the assessment of monthly and annual

2.4.Temporaltrend,seasonalanalysisandhotspotidentification
Monthly and annual composites were generated from all valid MODIS observations between 2000 and
2023. Monthly mean TSS and turbidity maps were produced by averaging all cloud-free observations
within each calendar month. Annual mean values were then calculated from monthly composites to
investigate long-term temporal variability. The long-term temporal trends of TSS and turbidity were
evaluated using annual averages. Seasonal variability was examined using mean monthly composites.
Differences between wet and dry seasons were also investigated to understand the influence of seasonal
rainfall on sediment transport dynamics. Spatial distribution maps of TSS and turbidity were produced
using long-term monthly averages derived from MODIS. These maps enabled identification of spatial
variations across Lake T ana throughout the study period. Hotspot areas were identified that consis-
tently exhibited elevated TSS and turbidity concentrations. Persistent hotspot regions were delineated
by comparing long-term mean monthly spatial maps and identifying areas with recurrent high sediment
concentrations.
2.5.ValidationoftheretrievedTSSandturbidity
Field measurements of TSS and turbidity were not conducted in the current study. Consequently, indi-
rect validation was performed using three complementary approaches. First, the spatial and seasonal pat-
terns of MODIS-derived TSS and turbidity were compared with previously published field investigations
(Goshu et al 2017, Tibebe et al 2019, W omber et al 2021, Leggesse et al 2024, Nkwasa et al 2024) con-
ducted in Lake T ana. Second, the empirical relationships used to estimate TSS and turbidity have been
extensively validated in previous studies of inland lakes, where coefficients of determination ( R2) typi-
cally range from 0.65 to 0.85 (Doxaran et al 2002, Shi et al 2018). Finally, the physical consistency of the
retrieved TSS and turbidity values was assessed by comparing them with long-term monthly and annual
rainfall records. Increased rainfall is expected to enhance soil erosion and sediment transport into the
lake, resulting in elevated suspended sediment concentrations. Agreement between rainfall variability and
the derived TSS and turbidity, therefore, provides additional confidence in the retrieved products.
3.Resultsanddiscussion
3.1.MeanmonthlyandannualTSSandturbidityvariations(2000–2023)
MODIS satellite images processed with the GEE platform were used to assess surface reflectance over
Lake T ana. Reflectance values were used to calculate TSS and turbidity for more than 2 decades, from
2000 to 2023, due to the scarcity of field-measurement data. The use of these long-term satellite observa-
tions enabled consistent monitoring of sediment dynamics and water turbidity across the entire surface
of Lake T ana. This overcomes the temporal and spatial limitations of conventional field-based sampling,
and no sampling was conducted in the current study. The results show an increasing annual trend in
TSS from 2000 to 2020 (figure 2(b)) and a somewhat declining trend over the three years (2021, 2022,
and 2023). This long-term increasing trend suggests a gradual intensification of sediment inputs to the
Lake. The sediment entering the Lake is due to land/use changes, agricultural expansion, and soil degra-
dation across the Lake T ana Sub-Basin over time. T urbidity has the same trend as TSS, increasing from
2000 to 2020. The TSS concentration could increase turbidity levels. At shallow depths, sediment resus-
pension further elevated turbidity, particularly during post-rainy seasons (Kebedew et al 2020). Wind and
wave action in the shallow part of the Lake reintroduce bottom sediment into the Lake’s water column.
This further increases the lake’s turbidity. A general upward trend shifted in 2021, with a concerning
decline in TSS values that persisted through 2023, as illustrated in figure 2(b). The recent annual decline
in TSS and turbidity levels may be due to decreased rainfall intensity and effective watershed manage-
ment practices that reduce sediment erosion entering the Lake. The recent Ethiopian green legacy (since
2019) may improve watershed management and reduce soil erosion. Ethiopia’s massive reforestation
campaigns aim to reduce soil erosion in the Upper Blue Nile Basin (Beyene and Shumetie 2023).
Seasonal analysis reveals that TSS concentrations and turbidity levels are significantly elevated during
the rainy seasons, particularly in June, July, and August (figure 3(a)). This is related to higher sediment
loading from nonpoint sources in intensively agricultural watersheds. Rainy seasons transport soil nutri-
ents into the Lake from surrounding catchment areas (Setegn et al 2008, T affese et al 2014). This sea-
sonal variation highlights the critical impacts of precipitation on sediment transport mechanisms and

to enter the lake, elevating TSS and turbidity during these months. The expansion of water hyacinth
traps fine sediments and can lower TSS concentrations in parts of the Lake where it has expanded
(Kebedew et al 2023).

3.2.SpatialvariationofTSSandturbidityandhotspotsites
The monthly mean spatial MODIS-derived TSS concentration is shown in figure 3. The spatial patterns
are mainly influenced by the Lake’s feeder rivers. The section of the Lake surrounding the inlets of Gilgel
Abay, Gumara, Ribb, Megech, Arno, and other small tributaries produced anomalies characterized by ele-
vated TSS levels (figure 3). The spatial distribution maps for June, July, and August show elevated TSS
hotspots at the river inlets of major feeder rivers to the Lake. This indicates strong hydrological connec-
tivity between the catchment and the Lake. The result aligns with studies by Lemma et al (2018) and
Zimale et al (2018), indicating that sediments are transported to the Lake and significantly increase TSS
concentrations at the inlets during rainy seasons. The Gilgel Abay River is the major sediment-inflow
path to the Lake and is the main hotspot region (figure 3). According to Kebedew et al (2021), 60% of
the inflow comes from this river. Hence, it carries a large amount of sediment to the Lake and has the
dominant role in sediment delivery during high-flow conditions. The high concentration of TSS around
the Gilgel Abay River may be due to the steep-slope geomorphology, which promotes rapid runoff into
the Lake. Additionally, the limited floodplain development at the Gilgel Abay outlet reduces sediment
deposition before joining the Lake. Elevated, steep-slope regions are prone to soil erosion, contribute sig-
nificantly to sediment loads, and increase TSS in aquatic environments (Lewin et al 2017, W ohl 2020).
The sediments cannot be deposited on land near the Gilgel Abay River inlet. This results in direct sed-
iment entering the Lake water column. Although it is the largest supplier, its sediment typically spends
less time near the inlet and migrates more quickly toward its outlet in the Blue Nile River (Abay River)
at the southern Gulf. W omber et al (2021) describes a highly mobile river that generates a sustained
plume of high turbidity in the southern Gulf. Similarly, in the current study, the southern part of the
Lake at the outlet of the Abay River showed elevated TSS and turbidity, indicating sediment transport to
the lower catchments and to lower Nile River countries.
The eastern and northern borders of Lake T ana, particularly the Dembia floodplain (influenced by
the Megech River) and the Fogera floodplain in the east (primarily impacted by the Gumara and Rib

rivers), experience significant flooding during the rainy seasons. As a result of these seasonal floods, a
substantial amount of sediment and suspended materials is deposited onto the floodplain lands and
the Delta development, rather than being carried into the Lake. This process results in lower sediment
input to the Lake from the Gumara and Ribb rivers than from the Gilgel Aaby river. The development
of Deltas increased over time with the entry of the Gumara (Abate et al 2015). These sediment dynamics
explain the lower TSS estimate in the Gumara and Ribb River inlets compared to the Gilgel Abay River
inlet. During peak flow, the floodplains absorb excess water and facilitate sediment deposition before
the water reaches the Lake. Thereby reducing the overall TSS levels in the Lake water column in these
regions. Kebedew et al (2023) applied hydrodynamic modeling to demonstrate that the Lake current gen-
erally transports sediment and nutrients from the eastern river inlets (Rib and Gumara) towards the
northeast. In the central part of the Lake, the TSS concentration is minimal across all months. This is
due to a lack of sediment sources and being far from the feeder rivers. The sediments carried by feeder
rivers settle before reaching those parts of the Lake.
Wind plays a role in sediment distribution and spatial dynamics in Lakes (Hofmann et al 2011, Nutz
et al 2015). Wind-generated waves can resuspend settled sediments in the lake’s water column (Larsen
and MacDonald 1993, Hofmann et al 2011). This refers to wind-induced sediment resuspension, espe-
cially in shallow Lakes where bottom sediments are easily reworked by wave action. In Lake T ana, winds
affect sediment distribution, particularly pronounced during the windy months (post-rainy months) of
September, October, November, and December, when strong winds are more frequent. During these
post-rainy periods, characterized by reduced water depth and exposed shallow zones, the susceptibility
of bottom sediments to resuspension increases. The sediment particles are lifted and carried in the direc-
tion of the wind (Engida 2010, Kebedew et al 2020). This enhances lateral sediment transport across the
Lake surface. The sediments are typically redistributed toward the northeast and east (figure 5, yellow
arrows) and are consistent with the work of Kebedew et al (2020). This pattern clearly shows wind direc-
tion over the Lake during windy dry months and post-rainy seasons. As a consequence, sediments tend
to accumulate more significantly in the north and eastern regions of the Lake (figure 5, yellow arrows).
Most of the turbidity is caused by high TSS entering the lake. In Lake T ana, turbidity is caused by
both natural and anthropogenic factors (Goshu et al 2017, Tibebe et al 2019). The high level of TSS is
mainly associated with sediment inflow to the Lake. TSS levels determine water clarity. Runoff carries
weathered rock debris and soil erosion from agricultural areas into the Lake. This increases the level
of turbidity. T urbidity varies across time and space because it depends on factors such as precipitation
amounts, hydrological characteristics, landscapes, and seasons (Goshu et al 2017, Tibebe et al 2019).
Steep-slope and mountainous areas are more prone to erosion during rainfall, leading to higher tur-
bidity in those sections of the Lake than in flatter regions. The western parts of the Lake, where steep
catchment topography generates high sediment transport to the Lake, are directly affected. The area of
Lake T ana near the Gilgel Abay River inlet consistently exhibits higher turbidity (figure 5), as evidenced
by elevated TSS (figure 4). This is a direct consequence of the river’s sediment load, particularly dur-
ing rainy seasons when runoff increases intensely. As shown in figure 5, high turbidity values have been
observed from June to August. This is because those months are rainy and load the lake with high sed-
iment. Spatially, the major river inlets to the Lake are characterized by high turbidity. In particular, in
the western part of the Gilgel Abay River inlet, highly elevated turbidity is observed, similar to TSS lev-
els. The eastern parts show a lower level than the western part because, in the eastern part, sediments
are deposited on gentle slopes (e.g. the Fogera floodplain), and the level of sediment joining the Lake is
lower than in the southwestern Gulf.

4.Conclusions
The use of remote sensing technology enables wide-scale, long-term, and cost-effective monitoring of
environmental parameters in large water bodies. In this study, the key open-water quality parameters,
TSS and turbidity, were assessed to support sustainable management of water resources in Lake T ana,
Northwestern Ethiopia. High-temporal-resolution MODIS satellite imagery was used to track the long-
term dynamics of TSS and turbidity and to delineate hotspots. The results show an increasing trend in
TSS and turbidity over two decades (from 2000 to 2020), followed by recent declines from 2020 to 2023.
The recent decline may indicate reduced rainfall intensity, the recent green legacy in Ethiopia, and good
watershed conservation in the upper catchments. Significant variation in TSS and turbidity is observed
between wet and dry months. The parameter reaches its peak concentration during the rainy seasons of
June, July, and August. This is due to a high sediment load in the Lake from runoff from agricultural
upper catchments. In dry months, the TSS concentration is low, and the Lake water is mostly clear.
Spatially, the inlets of the major feeder rivers of Lake T ana, including Gilgel Abay, Gumara, Rib, and
Megech, exhibited very high concentrations. Those parts of the Lake are determined as hotspot sites. In
particular, the Gilgel Abay River is the dominant sediment feeder to the Lake, as the catchment is slop-
ing and has characteristic terrain. The sediments enter the Lake directly, without first being deposited on
land. The study supports the sustainable management of Lake waters and provides insights for improv-
ing water quality in Ethiopia and globally.
