As California faces increasing water scarcity due to climate change and population growth, the state’s recent Direct Potable Reuse (DPR) regulations, effective October 2024, represent a significant advancement in sustainable water management. A defining feature of DPR is the removal of the environmental buffer, necessitating engineered treatment systems capable of meeting log removal values of 20 for enteric viruses, 14 for Giardia, and 15 for Cryptosporidium. This chapter examines the microbial challenges underlying three important pillars of DPR treatment: ensuring robust pathogen inactivation across the multi-barrier treatment train, optimizing biological filter performance, and maintaining long-term biostability in distribution systems. First, we evaluate how redundant treatment barriers from ozone and UV advanced oxidation to disinfection achieve the required log removal values at full scale, verified by quantitative microbial risk assessment, and consider the limitations of validating 6-log treatment process credits. Second, we explore the microbial ecology of biological activated carbon filters, including community composition, deterministic and stochastic assembly dynamics, and the role of pre-ozonation in shaping downstream microbial communities. Third, we evaluate biostability in distribution systems, where the interplay of various factors, including nutrient limitations, disinfectant residuals, and water age, determines regrowth potential, and where emerging frameworks prioritize biological control over continuous chemical disinfection. By linking microbial ecology to regulatory compliance and public trust, this chapter provides engineers and policymakers with a framework for designing sustainable DPR systems that protect public health and secure California’s water future.
