Abstract
Water deficit is a major constraint for sesame (Sesamum indicum L.) production in arid and semi-
arid regions. This study evaluated the effects of foliar application of calcium nitrate (Ca(NO₃)₂; 0, 
5, and 10 mM) and putrescine (Put; 0 and 0.5 mM) on the physiological and biochemical responses 
of sesame under three irrigation regimes (100%, 75%, and 50% of crop water requirement) during 
the 2018–2019 growing seasons. The experiment was conducted under field conditions using a 
split-plot factorial design with three replications. Severe water deficit (50% irrigation) 
significantly reduced chlorophyll content by 38.6%, relative water content by 27.2%, and 
membrane stability index by 28.6% compared with full irrigation (P ≤ 0.01). Foliar application of 
10 mM Ca(NO₃)₂ increased total chlorophyll by 19.3% and relative water content by 17.1% (P ≤ 
0.05), whereas 0.5 mM Put improved membrane stability by 30.8% under severe stress (P ≤ 0.01).

to 60.6%, soluble carbohydrates by 45.6%, proline by 34.3%, catalase activity by 32.8%, and

application of Ca(NO₃)₂ and Put can mitigate drought-induced injury in sesame through 
coordinated improvements in water status, membrane stability, and antioxidant defense. These 
findings suggest that this treatment combination may serve as a practical complementary 
agronomic strategy for improving sesame performance under water-limited conditions.

1. Introduction
Anthropogenic activities driving global climate change profoundly alter environmental conditions, 
significantly impacting the survival and adaptability of living organisms. Plants, constrained by 
their sessile nature, are particularly vulnerable to environmental imbalances compared to motile 
organisms. Abiotic stresses, including drought, salinity, and extreme temperatures, severely limit 
agricultural productivity. Notably, drought stress markedly reduces the yield of sesame (Sesamum 
indicum L.), a critical oilseed crop and the fourth most significant food commodity globally¹–³. 
The rising global demand for oilseeds, driven by population growth, dietary shifts, and biodiesel 
production, underscores their economic and nutritional importance as primary sources of vegetable

oils and animal feed⁴. Beyond the nutritional value of sesame, its seeds and oil have historically 
been utilized therapeutically to treat metabolic, inflammatory, and infectious conditions, including 
ulcers, asthma, wounds, amenorrhea, and hemorrhoids⁵. In the pharmaceutical and cosmetic 
industries, sesame oil serves as an essential solvent, drug carrier, skin emollient, natural UV 
protectant, and ointment base, while its blooms and stems yield fragrances for perfume 
production⁶. Additionally, myristic acid derived from sesame seeds is a common ingredient in 
cosmetics⁶. Consequently, enhancing the resilience of sesame to abiotic stresses is critical for 
sustaining global food security and agricultural productivity.
Calcium ions (Ca2+) serve as critical secondary messengers in all living organisms, orchestrating 
diverse cellular signaling cascades⁷,⁸. In plants, calcium is essential for optimal growth and stress 
responses, modulating cellular processes by activating calcium channels, increasing cytosolic 
calcium concentrations, and initiating downstream signaling pathways⁹–¹¹.
Polyamines (PAs) are low-molecular-weight, positively charged aliphatic nitrogenous compounds 
found in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms¹². Among these, putrescine (Put), spermidine, 
and spermine are highly prevalent in plants, whereas cadaverine is less abundant¹³. PAs play 
crucial roles in plant growth, development, and stress mitigation due to their anti-aging, acid-
neutralizing, and antioxidant properties, as well as their capacity to stabilize cell membranes and 
cell walls¹⁴. Foliar application of putrescine has been documented to ameliorate physiological 
performance under drought conditions in species such as garden thyme (Thymus vulgaris L.)¹⁵, 
cotton (Gossypium barbadense L.)¹⁶, wheat (Triticum aestivum L.)¹⁷, and sugar beet (Beta vulgaris 
L.)¹⁸.
Exogenous calcium enhances drought tolerance through the stabilization of cell walls and 
membranes, the regulation of stomatal behavior and ion homeostasis, and the induction of stress-

responsive gene expression. Putrescine, a low-molecular-weight polyamine, mitigates drought 
damage by stabilizing membranes and macromolecules, modulating the expression and activity of 
antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, CAT, and APX, reducing protein and chlorophyll 
degradation, and altering stomatal conductance and osmotic adjustment17, 18, 19. Together, Ca2+ and 
Put target overlapping physiological processes that are central to drought tolerance, suggesting 
that their co-application may provide additive or beneficial interactive effects compared to 
individual applications. Ca2+ binds to cell-wall pectins and stabilizes membrane structures, thereby 
reducing leakage under dehydration 20. Put interacts with membrane phospholipids and proteins, 
further reducing permeability and protecting the photosynthetic apparatus17. The combination can 
therefore produce greater physical stabilization than either treatment alone. Put upregulates 
antioxidant systems, including SOD, CAT, and APX, and can reduce ROS accumulation 17. 
Calcium application also influences antioxidant capacity and stabilizes enzymes; together, they 
may increase antioxidant activity and reduce oxidative damage under water deficit²¹. Despite the 
agronomic importance of sesame and its high sensitivity to water deficit, the mechanisms by which 
exogenous calcium and putrescine improve drought tolerance in this crop remain insufficiently 
understood. Although previous studies have demonstrated the beneficial roles of calcium or PAs 
in alleviating drought-induced damage in various plant species, evidence regarding their combined 
application to sesame remains limited. In particular, little is known about whether foliar-applied 
calcium nitrate and putrescine can exert additive or beneficial interactive effects on the growth, 
physiological performance, and biochemical responses of sesame under drought conditions. 
Therefore, the novelty of the present study lies in evaluating the individual and interactive effects 
of calcium nitrate and putrescine on drought tolerance in sesame. Accordingly, this study aimed 
to elucidate the growth, physiological, and biochemical responses of sesame to the foliar

2. Results
2.1. Physiological characteristics
2.1.1. Leaf pigments
An analysis of variance showed that, at P ≤ 0.01, moisture levels (ML) had a significant impact on 
the pigments found in the leaves of sesame, including Chl a, Chl b, Chl T, and Car (Table 2). 
Ca(NO3)2 did not significantly affect Chl a, Chl b, or Car, however, it did exhibit a significant effect 
on Chl T at P ≤ 0.05. Overall, Ca(NO₃)₂ had no significant effect on most leaf pigments (Table 2). 
Furthermore, there was no discernible year effect on leaf pigments. At P ≤ 0.05, the ML×Put 
interaction significantly affected Chl T. At each ML, Put spraying had a significant impact on Chl 
T (Fig. 2). Conversely, at P ≤ 0.01 (Table 2), the interaction effects of Ca×Put had a significant 
impact on Chl T. Significant impacts were seen on Chl b and carotenoid contents at P ≤ 0.01 and P 
≤ 0.05, respectively, due to the interaction effects of Y×Ca×Put. Additionally, Table 2 show the 
interaction effects of ML×Ca×Put on Chl a and Chl T at P ≤ 0.01. Table 3 show that a significant 
reduction in leaf pigment concentration was seen at low ML (50% water requirement). Compared 
with the 100% irrigation treatment, the 50% treatment decreased Chl a, Chl b, and Chl T by 38.7%, 
38.4%, and 38.6%, respectively. Moreover, carotenoid content in sesame leaves increased by 40.9% 
under the 50% water requirement treatment compared with the control (Table 3). Chl T was shown 
to significantly rise with increasing Ca(NO 3)2 concentration. The 10 mM Ca(NO₃)₂ treatment 
increased Chl T by 19.3% compared with the control (Table 3).
The highest Chl b and carotenoid contents were observed at 10 and 5 mM Ca(NO₃)₂ and Put, 
respectively. Chl b increased by 18.1% and 40.7% in 2018 and 2019, respectively, whereas

2.1.2. RWC and MSI
ANOVA revealed that moisture level (ML) significantly affected leaf relative water content 
(RWC) and membrane stability index (MSI) at P ≤ 0.01 (Table 6). The lowest values of leaf RWC 
and MSI were recorded under the 50% water requirement treatment. Specifically, under this level, 
leaf RWC and MSI declined by 27.2% and 28.6%, respectively, compared with the control (Table 
7). Leaf RWC and MSI were also significantly influenced by Ca(NO3)2 application (Table 6). The 
highest values of both traits were observed at 10 mM Ca(NO 3)2. At this concentration, leaf RWC 
and MSI increased by 17.1% and 28.8%, respectively, relative to the control (Table 7). The 
interaction between ML and Ca had a significant effect on leaf RWC at P ≤ 0.05 (Table 6). Across 
all moisture levels, application of 10 mM Ca(NO 3)2 resulted in the highest leaf RWC (Fig. 4), 
confirming the positive role of calcium nitrate in improving leaf water status under different 
irrigation regimes. Moreover, the ML × Put interaction significantly affected leaf RWC at P ≤ 0.05 
and MSI at P ≤ 0.01 (Table 6). Mean comparison of this interaction showed that application of 5 
mM putrescine produced the highest RWC under 100%, 75%, and 50% ML, corresponding to 
increases of 26.0%, 21.3%, and 23.7%, respectively, compared with the relevant controls (Table 
8). Likewise, the highest MSI values were observed at 5 mM putrescine under 100%, 75%, and 
50% ML, with corresponding increases of 16.2%, 27.1%, and 30.8%, respectively (Table 8).

2.1.4. LAI and CT

3. Discussion
Water deficit is a major factor limiting plant growth by disturbing physiological and biochemical 
processes¹⁹,²². The results showed that water deficit adversely affected the physiological and 
biochemical processes of sesame. PAs are a group of plant growth stimulants that play a critical 
role in different developmental and physiological processes, especially under stress 
conditions¹⁹,²³–²⁵. These factors encompass the enhancement of ATP synthesis, Mg-ATPase 
activity, Ca-ATPase activity, enzymatic and non-enzymatic activity, improvement of nitrate 
reductase activity, N content, chlorophyll content index, chlorophyll stability index, improvement 
of Fv/Fm, and increase in Rubisco activity¹⁹,²⁴,²⁶. Drought-tolerant plants generally accumulate 
higher levels of PAs under water deficit¹⁹,²³,²⁴,²⁷,²⁸. Furthermore, it has been observed in numerous 
studies that drought-resistant plants exhibit increased levels of PAs when subjected to water 
shortage conditions¹⁶,²⁸–³⁰. Appropriate calcium application can regulate cellular responses under 
stress conditions³¹. Calcium application has been reported to improve drought responses in maize 
(Zea mays)³², Catharanthus roseus³³, and sugar beet (Beta vulgaris)²⁰.

Results of this study revealed that spraying Put and Ca(NO₃)₂ increased total chlorophyll content 
in sesame under water deficit. Similar results were reported in onion following foliar spraying with 
Put, where photosynthetic pigments significantly increased compared with untreated plants³⁰. 
Furthermore, higher chlorophyll content and improved photosynthetic apparatus were reported 
following application of Put in two citrus rootstocks³⁴, thyme (Thymus vulgaris L.)³⁵, and pepper 
(Capsicum annuum L.)³⁶. Foliar application of calcium has also been shown to improve 
chlorophyll content and photosynthetic apparatus³⁷,³⁸.
Foliar application of Put and Ca(NO₃)₂ improved the relative water content and membrane stability 
index of sesame leaves under water deficit. PAs can enhance plant stress tolerance by stabilizing 
membranes and cell walls, buffering cellular pH, and contributing to antioxidant defense¹⁹. PAs 
have the ability to control the dimensions of potassium channels and pores in the plasma membrane 
of guard cells; as a result, they significantly affect stomatal opening and closure. PAs can regulate 
water loss in plants³⁹. Multiple studies have demonstrated that applying Put directly to leaves at 
the correct dosage can stimulate physiological processes and promote the production of osmotic 
adjustment compounds, including free amino acids, soluble sugars, and proline¹⁹,²⁴,²⁷,³⁴,³⁶. Foliar 
application of calcium has been reported to improve relative water content and membrane 
stability⁴⁰–⁴². Foliar application of Put and Ca(NO₃)₂ also decreased electrolyte leakage and 
improved osmotic potential in sesame under water deficit.
Put and Ca(NO₃)₂ application increased osmolyte accumulation in drought-stressed sesame. Foliar 
application of Put has been reported to increase osmolyte accumulation³⁴–³⁶. Furthermore, similar 
results were observed following foliar application of calcium³²,³⁷,³⁸. The significant interaction 
effects observed among moisture level, calcium nitrate, and putrescine indicate that the response 
of sesame plants to foliar treatments depended strongly on drought severity. Although the

enhanced efficacy of the co-treatment under stress conditions points toward complementary modes 
of action for calcium nitrate and putrescine, a formal inference regarding the benefits of combined 
application cannot be made statistical interaction analysis. Calcium mainly contributed to cellular 
stabilization and stress signaling, whereas putrescine appeared to enhance membrane protection, 
osmotic adjustment, and antioxidant defense. Therefore, the interaction between these compounds 
likely reflects coordinated physiological regulation rather than independent action of each 
treatment.
Results of this study demonstrated improvement of CAT and POD activity by foliar application of 
Put and Ca(NO₃)₂ under both well-watered and water-stress conditions. Numerous reports have 
demonstrated increased enzymatic antioxidant activity, such as CAT, SOD, and POD, following 
foliar application of Put¹⁹,³⁵ and calcium²⁹,⁴³–⁴⁷. The physiological improvements observed in 
sesame plants under drought stress following the co-application of calcium nitrate and putrescine 
point toward a combined beneficial response, suggesting that these treatments may modulate 
different, yet complementary, pathways in the stress defense network. First, Ca²⁺ has been 
extensively reported in previous literature to act as a secondary messenger in plant stress signaling. 
Exogenous Ca(NO₃)₂ application may potentially influence intracellular Ca²⁺ homeostasis and 
Ca²⁺-dependent signaling cascades (such as calcium-dependent protein kinases, CDPKs), although 
such pathways were not directly assessed in the present study. In previous studies, such pathways 
have been reported to influence the expression of stress-responsive genes and stomatal behavior. 
It is therefore plausible that Ca²⁺-related signaling may contribute to the maintained RWC and 
reduced transpiration losses observed in this study, although these molecular events were not 
measured here.

Furthermore, Ca²⁺ is suggested, based on previous literature, to interact with phospholipid head 
groups of the plasma membrane and with cell-wall pectins, which could contribute to structural 
integrity and to the reduced EL observed under severe water deficit. Second, Ca²⁺-mediated 
signaling has been linked in previous studies to ROS scavenging. Under drought, disruption of the 
photosynthetic electron transport chain may lead to ROS accumulation and lipid peroxidation. 
Ca²⁺-associated signaling may, in turn, influence the activity of key antioxidant enzymes, although 
transcript-level regulation and ROS dynamics were not directly examined in this study. 
Concurrently, exogenous Put contributes to this defense. As a positively charged polyamine at 
physiological pH, Put has been reported to interact with negatively charged macromolecules, 
including nucleic acids, proteins, and membrane phospholipids. Such interactions are suggested to 
shield membranes from ROS-mediated damage and may therefore contribute to the membrane 
stability observed in this study, although direct binding interactions were not measured. The co-
application of Ca²⁺ and Put was associated with enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity in several 
measured traits. This pattern is consistent with, but does not conclusively establish, complementary 
physiological effects between the two treatments.
Previous studies have suggested that polyamines may influence transient apoplastic H₂O₂ 
production through amine oxidase activity, which can act as a signaling intermediate for 
downstream antioxidant enzymes such as CAT and POD. Calcium has likewise been reported to 
affect antioxidant enzyme activity. Such pathways may potentially contribute to the CAT and POD 
responses observed here, but they were not evaluated directly in the present experiment. 
Consequently, the combined application of Ca(NO₃)₂ and Put was associated with improved 
membrane stability and antioxidant-related traits. These results may be consistent with 
complementary effects in which calcium- and putrescine-associated processes potentially

in this study. The combined treatment was associated with improved chlorophyll content, growth, 
and yield-related performance under moisture deficiency. These responses may be consistent with 
improved physiological protection, potentially involving antioxidant and membrane-related 
processes; however, oxidative stress and direct protection of the photosynthetic apparatus were not 
measured in this study. It should be noted that the Pearson correlation coefficients reported in this 
study represent statistical associations between the physiological and biochemical traits rather than 
direct causal relationships. While strong correlations were observed between parameters such as 
antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT and POD) and membrane integrity markers (EL and MSI), 
these associations do not inherently prove direct causality. Instead, they suggest that these 
physiological responses are co-regulated and function as part of an integrated network under 
drought stress. The statistical associations are consistent with a proposed framework in which 
putrescine-associated membrane protection and calcium-related signaling may contribute to the 
observed improvements in water relations and growth. This interpretation remains tentative 
because the underlying molecular processes were not directly measured.
The multiple regression models provided significant insights into the physiological determinants 
of sesame yield under drought stress. The inclusion of traits such as MSI and RWC as primary 
predictors in the model highlights their critical role in maintaining yield stability. From a biological 
perspective, the positive regression coefficients for these parameters suggest that grain yield is 
predominantly governed by the plant's ability to maintain cellular hydration and membrane 
integrity. Practically, these regression models serve as a predictive tool for identifying key 
selection criteria in sesame breeding programs. The high coefficient of determination (R 2) 
indicates that a substantial portion of yield variation can be explained by a few key physiological 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
traits. This suggests that instead of measuring numerous parameters, researchers and breeders can 
focus on monitoring MSI and RWC as reliable proxies for drought tolerance and yield potential in 
sesame treated with exogenous protectants like calcium and putrescine.
From a practical perspective, the foliar application of calcium nitrate and putrescine represents a 
potentially useful treatment for sesame growers, as both compounds are commercially available 
and utilized at very low concentrations (10 mM and 0.5 mM, respectively), keeping chemical input 
costs minimal. However, the overall cost-effectiveness and scalability under commercial field 
conditions will depend on local labor costs, spraying equipment (such as tractor-mounted sprayers 
or agricultural drones), and region-specific input prices. To optimize the physiological benefits of 
these protectants, application should target periods of low evaporative demand (early morning or 
late evening) to ensure efficient foliar uptake, and site-specific trials are recommended to validate 
economic returns prior to commercial adoption.
4. Conclusion
Overall, foliar application of putrescine (Put) and calcium nitrate (Ca(NO₃)₂), particularly in 
combination, was associated with improved physiological and biochemical traits of sesame under 
the water deficit conditions tested. The improvements observed in traits related to water status, 
membrane stability, osmotic adjustment, antioxidant activity, and photosynthetic pigments suggest 
that these compounds may be associated with improved drought-related physiological status and 
could potentially contribute to plant adaptation under limited water availability. Beyond the 
specific results obtained here, these findings highlight the potential of exogenous protectants as 
supportive agronomic tools that may improve stress-related physiological traits in water-limited 
environments, especially in arid and semi-arid regions where drought is a major constraint to crop 
productivity. However, these results should be interpreted within the limits of the present study, 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
which was conducted under specific field conditions, during two growing seasons, and on a single 
cultivar. Therefore, the broader applicability of these treatments may vary depending on genotype, 
climate, soil conditions, and management practices. Further research is needed to evaluate the 
consistency of these responses across diverse sesame cultivars, environments, and larger 
production systems. Future studies should also investigate the long-term agronomic and economic 
feasibility of these foliar treatments, optimize their timing and dosage under different drought 
intensities, and further clarify the physiological and molecular mechanisms that may underlie their 
combined effects. Such efforts will be essential for determining the practical value of Put and 
Ca(NO₃)₂ application as part of drought management strategies in sesame production.
5. Materials and methods
5.1. Experimental site and design, agricultural operations, and treatments
This study was conducted on the Oltan variety of sesame, obtained from the Khorasan Razavi 
Agricultural and Natural Resources Research Center. The research was carried out at Birjand 
University’s research farm, located at coordinates 32°52′15″N, 59°07′23″E, at an altitude of 
1411 m. The study was conducted throughout the growing seasons of 2018 and 2019. The 
experiment was conducted using a split-plot factorial design within a randomized complete 
block design with three replications. Irrigation levels were set at 100%, 75%, and 50% of the 
crop water requirement, serving as the main plot factor. Foliar applications of Put, 0 and 0.5 
mM17, and calcium nitrate (Ca(NO₃)₂), 0, 5, and 10 mM45 were applied as subplot factors. 
Various studies have demonstrated that a wide range of concentrations of both putrescine and 
calcium nitrate can influence the growth and physiological characteristics of plants under 
moisture deficit conditions. Recent investigations have confirmed that the specific 
concentrations selected in our study (i.e., 0.5 mM putrescine and 10 mM calcium nitrate) fall 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
well within these effective ranges and are highly suitable for mitigating the adverse effects of 
drought stress in crops 17,45, 48, 49. Soil samples were collected from 0–30 cm depth prior to 
planting to assess the physical and chemical properties of the experimental site. Weather data, 
including precipitation and temperature, were recorded for the 2018 and 2019 growing seasons.
According to the soil analysis results, the recommended application of urea was 250 kg ha⁻¹, 
split into two doses at sowing time and two months after emergence. Additionally, 100 kg ha⁻¹ 
of P₂O₅ and K₂O was applied to all experimental plots before seeding. To prepare the seedbed, 
the field underwent a series of steps. First, a mouldboard plough was used to plough the field to 
a depth of 30 cm. This process was repeated twice using disk harrowing. Finally, specialized 
leveling equipment was used to level the soil surface. In both 2018 and 2019, sesame seeds were 
planted on June 18. Each main plot measured 17.5 m × 3 m. Seeds of the Oltan sesame cultivar 
were sown at a row spacing of 50 cm, intra-row spacing of 5 cm, and planting depth of 3 cm, 
achieving a target plant density of 40 plants m⁻². Approximately one week following seeding, 
the crops began to emerge from the soil surface. Plants were thinned at the four-leaf stage, two 
weeks post-emergence and three weeks post-planting, to achieve the target density.
The FAO-56 technique employed the CROPWAT program, specifically the Penman–Monteith 
method, to determine the optimal irrigation schedules, including the timing and quantity of 
irrigation required50. The water treatments were initiated at the four-leaf stage and applied 
weekly until the end of the growing season. The required volume of irrigation water was 
determined using a smart water volume meter and delivered separately to each plot. Foliar 
applications of putrescine, NH₂(CH₂)₄NH₂, 88.15 g mol⁻¹, colorless solid crystal, Sigma-Aldrich, 
USA, and calcium nitrate, Ca(NO₃)₂, 164.08 g mol⁻¹, white powder, Merck, Germany, were 
administered two days after inducing stress conditions (after post-thinning) and at the onset of 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
the pollination stage, respectively. Foliar applications were performed using a rechargeable 
sprayer equipped with a flat-fan nozzle, 8002, at 1 bar pressure. Spraying was conducted near 
dusk to ensure solution stability. Two foliar treatments were applied, with a four-week interval 
between applications51. For physiological and biochemical measurements, multiple plants were 
sampled from each plot, and the data were averaged to obtain one value per plot before statistical 
analysis. Accordingly, the plot was considered the experimental unit, whereas individual plants 
served only as subsamples. Thus, the ANOVA was performed using plot means, not individual 
plant measurements, to avoid pseudoreplication.

5.3. Measurement of biochemical characteristics
5.3.1. Soluble carbohydrates
The method of Irigoyen et al.⁵8 was used to measure soluble carbohydrates. Briefly, 5 mL of 95% 
C2H6O and 0.1 g of fresh leaf was ground in liquid nitrogen. After being gathered, the supernatants 
were put into vials. After twice washing the extract sediment with 5 milliliters of 70% C2H6O, the 
supernatants were poured into vials. For ten minutes, each vial was centrifuged at 3500 g. 3 mL of 
freshly made C 14H10O (anthrone) (150 mg anthrone + 100 mL 72% H 2SO4) and 0.1 mL of the 
alcoholic extract were combined, and the mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for ten 
minutes. Following cooling, 1.5 mL of supernatants and 1.5 mL of calibration solution (consisting 
of 1 mL of 70% C2H2O and 3 mL C14H10O) were combined. Lastly, the UV–vis spectrophotometer 
model T70 was used to measure the extract solution's absorbance at 625 nm.

The method of William⁵ 9 was used to determine calcium content of leaves. The leaves were

ash and dissolve it in a hot water bath. Filter paper (Whatman No. 42) was used to filter the mixture 
before pouring it into a 100 mL volumetric flask. Then, it was made up to 100 mL with distilled 
water. The calcium content was measured by adding distilled water to a 1 L flask containing 2.5 
grams of calcium carbonate until the solution reached 1000 ppm. 0.5, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 7.5, and 10 mL

5.3.3. Proline
Proline content of leaves was determined using the acid ninhydrin method 60–⁶2. One milliliter of 
3% w/v sulfosalicylic acid was used to homogenize leaf samples before filtering. In a boiling water

extract were combined and left for one hour. The test tubes were then submerged in an ice bath.

was stated as µg g-1 FW.
5.3.4. Prepare enzyme extracts
In an ice-filled container, 1 g of fresh leaf weight was homogenized in 5 mL of phosphate 
potassium buffer (pH 7.5) and 1 mM EDTA. Following a 20-minute centrifugation at 15,000 g for 
4 °C, the supernatant was promptly frozen under liquid N 2 and kept at -70 °C for the subsequent 
experiments.

The methods of MacAdam et al.⁶4 and Hammami et al.⁶3 were used to measure POD activity. 0.81

5.4. Data analysis
Data from the two growing seasons were first evaluated for homogeneity of variance using 
Bartlett’s test. Since the variance was homogeneous across years, a combined analysis of variance 
(ANOVA) was performed. The experiment was conducted as a split-plot factorial based on a 
randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications. In the combined model, year 
and block within year were considered random factors, while irrigation regime (main plots), 
calcium nitrate, and putrescine (subplots) were treated as fixed factors. The complete ANOVA 
model included the main effects of year, irrigation, calcium nitrate, and putrescine, along with all 
possible two-way, three-way, and four-way interactions. Specifically, the error term for the main 
plot (irrigation) was the interaction between block (year) and irrigation, while the sub-plot error 
was used to test the effects of calcium nitrate, putrescine, and their interactions with irrigation and 
year. All analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, 
USA). Normality of residuals and homogeneity of variance were verified using the Shapiro-Wilk 
and Levene’s tests, respectively, prior to further inference. The experimental plot was defined as

the statistical unit (n = 3), and subsamples within each plot were averaged before analysis. Mean 
comparisons for both main effects and interaction effects were conducted using the Fisher’s 
Protected Least Significant Difference (FLSD) test at P ≤ 0.05. For significant interactions, mean 
separations were performed on all treatment combinations to identify significant differences 
among the factor levels. Additionally, a stepwise multiple linear regression analysis was employed 
to assess the relationships between the measured traits.
