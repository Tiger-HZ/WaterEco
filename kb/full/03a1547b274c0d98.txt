Abstract
Concrete waste recycling is increasingly recognized as a strategy for reducing the 
environmental burdens associated with end-of-life construction materials, landfilling, 
and virgin material extraction. Within the transition to a circular economy, recycled 
concrete aggregate has gained growing attention as a secondary aggregate resource for 
concrete production. However, before recycled aggregate concrete (RAC) can be widely 
promoted as an environmentally preferable alternative, its environmental performance 
must be evaluated through rigorous, transparent, and standardized life cycle assessments 
(LCAs). This paper presents a PRISMA-guided systematic review of 37 RAC LCA studies 
and conducts an ISO-informed methodological audit across the four phases of the ISO 
14040/44 framework. The review identifies recurring limitations: 82% of system boundary 
instances were limited to cradle-to-gate, constraining full life cycle evaluation; 50% of 
functional unit instances adopted a volumetric unit of 1 m³ of concrete, which may not 
fully capture functional equivalence; and 65% of studies did not report how allocation and 
multifunctionality were handled, limiting transparency and reproducibility. Additional gaps 
were observed in inventory data representativeness, impact assessment method reporting 
and justification, interpretation practices, and data quality analysis. By linking identified 
limitations to ISO-aligned recommendations, this review provides practical guidance for 
improving the robustness, transparency, comparability, and credibility of future RAC LCA 
studies and circularity claims.

Introduction
Construction and demolition waste (CDW) remains one of the largest waste streams, 
accounting for a substantial share of total solid waste in many regions [ 1, 2]. CDW 
comprises a heterogeneous mix of inert and non-inert materials, as well as hazardous and 
non-hazardous streams, typically including concrete, clay bricks, mortar, wood, plastics,

and steel [3]. CDW generation varies across regions, with China, the United States, India, 
and the European Union as the dominant contributors [ 4]. With population growth, rapid 
urbanization, and continued infrastructure expansion, CDW management is expected to 
remain a critical challenge in the coming decades [5].
Recent policy frameworks increasingly promote the reduction, reuse, and recycling of 
CDW to reduce the extraction of virgin materials and lower the environmental impacts asso-
ciated with primary production and transportation [ 5]. Given the high global demand for 
concrete [6] and the high repurposing potential of CDW [ 7], incorporating recycled CDW 
into concrete offers a promising sustainable pathway to support circular construction. A key 
advantage of CDW recycling is reduced extraction of natural aggregate (NA), thereby limit-
ing new mining [ 8, 9]. CDW recycling also contributes to waste diversion and can reduce 
the environmental burdens associated with landfilling [10, 11].
The majority of CDW consists of concrete waste (CW), typically exceeding 70% 
(Fig. 1). Consequently, CDW recycling primarily involves CW recycling. In this context, 
research and practical applications have predominantly focused on recycled coarse 
aggregate (RCA) as a substitute for natural coarse aggregate (NCA) [ 9, 12]. Existing 
technical standards predominantly provide guidance for RCA use in concrete, while 
providing limited provisions for recycled fine aggregate (RFA) or recycled concrete 
powder (RCP) [ 13]. Therefore, recycled aggregate concrete (RAC) incorporating 
RCA provides a practical and comparatively mature reference case for refining and 
harmonizing methodological practices in life cycle assessment (LCA) studies of recycled 
concrete products.
 
LCA is widely used as a systematic approach for quantifying the environmental 
impacts of a product system [ 15]. In construction, LCA is increasingly used to inform 
early-stage design decisions and to evaluate end-of-life (EOL) strategies, including

recycling, reuse, and disposal [ 5, 16, 17]. Applications of LCA to RAC have a relatively 
long research history, with studies demonstrating that the environmental performance 
of RCA substitution is highly sensitive to LCA modeling assumptions. For example, 
early studies have shown that system boundary definition, transport distance, allocation 
approach, avoided-burden modeling, functional equivalence, recycling system design, and 
recycling-loop representation can substantially influence LCA outcomes [ 18–21]. These 
early contributions indicate that methodological variability is not a recent issue, but a 
long-standing challenge in RAC LCA studies.
Despite this long research history, RAC LCA outcomes remain insufficiently robust to 
support confident investment in high-quality RCA production for upcycling CW for appli -
cation in new concrete rather than downcycling into road base or fill [ 9]. While RAC mix 
design and mechanical performance have been extensively investigated, environmental 
assessment has received comparatively less attention, and studies that apply LCA often 
consider only a limited number of mix designs [22].
Although LCA has been standardized by the International Organization for 
Standardization (ISO), ISO 14040:2006 [ 23] and ISO 14044:2006 [ 24] provide a 
general LCA framework rather than prescriptive rules for key methodological choices, 
including functional unit (FU) definition, system boundary selection, life-cycle stage 
inclusion or exclusion, allocation approaches, and interpretation [ 25, 26]. Consequently, 
LCA practitioners often apply diverse methodological assumptions, creating ambiguity 
regarding the comparative environmental performance of RAC systems [ 25]. This issue is 
particularly important in the context of the circular economy (CE), where the distinction 
between closed-loop and open-loop recycling can substantially influence the interpretation 
of LCA results.
Reflecting these challenges, previous review studies have examined LCA practices 
in CDW management, CW recycling, and RAC systems, revealing significant 
methodological inconsistencies. Zhang et al. [ 27] identified key sources of variability 
in RAC LCA studies, including mix design method, FU definition, inventory 
allocation, CO₂ uptake through carbonation, and transport distance. Xing et al. [ 28] 
similarly highlighted that RAC LCA outcomes are strongly influenced by variations 
in the mix design and LCA modeling mechanisms. Marinković et al. [ 26] further 
emphasized the need to connect the sustainability assessment of RAC systems with 
LCA methodologies, standards, and design practice. Broader CDW and CW recycling 
reviews have also reported inconsistencies in FU definition, system boundary 
selection, impact category selection, life cycle impact assessment (LCIA) methods, 
CE integration, allocation modeling, inventory data quality, and recycled material 
quality considerations [ 5, 25, 29, 30].
Existing reviews have primarily focused on identifying variations in methodologi -
cal choices and comparing their influence on LCA. Limited attention has been given to 
critically evaluating whether the adopted methodological choices in RAC LCA studies 
are transparently reported, adequately justified, consistently applied, and reproducible in 
accordance with the widely acknowledged ISO 14040 [ 23] and ISO 14044 [24] principles. 
This represents an important gap as studies may claim ISO compliance yet provide insuffi -
cient methodological detail to support reproducibility, comparability, and decision-relevant 
interpretation.

To address this gap, the present study conducts an ISO-informed methodological audit 
of RAC LCA studies, evaluating key methodological components including FUs, system 
boundaries, allocation and multifunctionality handling, inventory development, LCIA 
methodologies, software tool use, interpretation practices, and data quality assessment. 
The audit identifies persistent inconsistencies in the operationalization of ISO 14040/44 
principles across RAC LCA studies. Building on these findings, the study develops 
ISO-aligned and RAC-specific methodological guidance to support more transparent, 
consistent, reproducible, and decision-relevant LCA outcomes for circular concrete 
systems.
Methods
This research employs a systematic review approach to critically examine contemporary 
LCA practices for RAC. The review followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items 
for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) framework to guide screening, selection, and 
reporting, as it is widely used in systematic reviews to ensure transparent and systematic 
evidence reporting [31–33].
Literature Search Strategy
The literature search was primarily conducted using the Scopus database, which is one of 
the world’s largest curated databases of high-quality peer-reviewed research [ 34]. A com-
prehensive search query was developed for the literature search process using key terms 
including ‘construction and demolition waste’, ‘life cycle assessment’, and ‘recycl*’, 
together with related keywords (Supplementary Information S1).
Study Selection Criteria and Screening Process
The review included peer-reviewed English-language journal articles published between 
January 2015 and May 2025 to capture contemporary LCA practice and recent method -
ological advances. The initial Scopus search yielded 302 records, and three additional 
studies were identified through backward and forward snowballing. The title and keyword 
screening stage focused on retaining studies that explicitly addressed CDW management 
and recycled concrete with an LCA focus, resulting in 224 records. The abstract screening 
stage focused on retaining studies that appeared to assess RCA integration in concrete from 
an LCA perspective, yielding 140 records. During full-text screening, 103 records were 
excluded where detailed review showed that they did not focus on recycled concrete LCA, 
did not incorporate RCA as a recycled input material, or lacked core LCA methodological 
information, including the FU, system boundary, and LCIA, to enable meaningful extrac -
tion, classification, and comparison within the audit framework. The full screening and eli-
gibility criteria are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Following systematic screening 
and eligibility assessment, 37 studies were retained for in-depth review and are listed in 
Supplementary Table S2. The full screening process is summarized in the PRISMA flow 
diagram (Fig. 2).

Data Extraction and Organization
The final dataset was systematically extracted to capture descriptive and methodological 
information from the reviewed studies. Key extracted variables included study descriptors, 
such as keywords, publication year, and geographic region, as well as LCA methodological 
elements, including FUs, system boundaries, allocation and multifunctionality handling, 
life cycle inventory (LCI) data sources, LCIA methods, impact categories, software tools, 
interpretation practices, and data quality analysis. This structured extraction process formed 
the evidence base for the descriptive overview, quantitative summary, and qualitative 
methodological synthesis.

Counting and Reporting Convention (Study-Level vs. Instance-Level)
Because some methodological elements were reported more than once within a single study 
(e.g., multiple FUs), both study-level and instance-level counts were used in the quantita -
tive synthesis. Accordingly, percentages were calculated using the appropriate denominator, 
and the terminology (“studies” vs. “instances”) is used consistently throughout the paper. 
For study-level reporting, the default denominator was N = 37. For instance-level reporting, 
the denominator varies with the total number of reported instances for the relevant element. 
These totals are clearly indicated in the relevant text, tables, and figures.
Descriptive Overview and ISO-Informed Methodological Audit
Following the systematic identification, screening, and selection of relevant RAC LCA 
studies, the analysis combined a descriptive overview of the reviewed studies with an ISO-
informed methodological audit. The descriptive overview briefly summarized publication 
years, keyword patterns, and geographic distribution to contextualize the reviewed studies 
and provide a general overview of research patterns.
The ISO-informed methodological audit examined RAC LCA methodological practices, 
structured around the four phases defined in ISO 14040 and ISO 14044: goal and scope defi-
nition, life cycle inventory analysis, life cycle impact assessment, and life cycle interpreta -
tion [23, 24]. The audit focused on whether LCA methodological choices were explicitly 
reported, adequately justified, consistently applied, and sufficiently transparent to support 
reproducibility and comparison across RAC LCA studies. This combined approach enabled 
the review to integrate a transparent systematic review process, a concise contextual over -
view, and a detailed qualitative synthesis of key methodological practices, reporting pat -
terns, ISO-informed gaps, and ISO-aligned recommendations.

LCA Methodological Practices
LCA is defined by ISO as the “compilation and evaluation of the inputs, outputs, and the potential 
environmental impacts of a product system throughout its life cycle” [23]. In accordance with 
ISO 14040 and ISO 14044, an LCA consists of four phases: (i) goal and scope definition, (ii) life 
cycle inventory analysis, (iii) life cycle impact assessment, and (iv) life cycle interpretation [23, 
24]. To enhance the paper’s thematic coherence and analytical clarity, the findings are structured 
around these phases. Where relevant, reference is also made to the EN 15804:2012+A2:2019 
standard, as it provides specific guidance for LCA of construction products [36].
Goal and Scope Definition
The goal of an LCA should define the intended application, the rationale for undertaking the 
LCA, the target audience, and whether the findings will be used in comparative assertions 
disclosed to the public [ 23]. The scope of an LCA encompasses a wide range of aspects. 
This review focuses on four principal elements: system approach, FU, system boundary, and 
allocation and multifunctionality handling.
System Approach (Attributional/Consequential) The two main system approaches used in LCA 
are attributional LCA (ALCA) and consequential LCA (CLCA) [37]. ALCA assesses a product’s 
environmental impacts within the current system and is commonly used to compare products 
or processes under existing conditions. In contrast, CLCA assesses the environmental conse-
quences of a decision or change in the product system (e.g., an increase in RCA demand) [25].
Among the reviewed studies, none explicitly stated the system approach adopted. However, based 
on the background databases used, the FUs defined, and the reported LCA outcomes, 30 studies 
can reasonably be classified as ALCA studies. The remaining seven studies [9, 22, 38–42] applied 
a substitution-based approach using avoided-burden credits to address recycling-related multi-
functionality. This is generally interpreted as an implementation of system expansion. Although 
some authors interpret the use of system expansion as an indicator of CLCA [43], Schaubroeck

Functional Unit (FU) ISO defines the FU as “quantified performance of a product system 
for use as a reference unit” [23]. It provides the basis for quantifying all inputs and outputs 
associated with the product system [25]. Therefore, the FU must be consistent with the goal 
and scope definition of the study [23]. In this review, six different FUs have been identified 
(see Table 1). The volumetric FU (1 m³ of concrete) was the most frequently applied (21 
instances), followed by the strength-based FU (11 instances). The complete list of refer -
ences corresponding to each FU category is provided in Supplementary Table S3.
System Boundary Concrete undergoes several life-cycle phases, including the production, 
construction, use, EOL, and material recovery. Consequently, system boundary selection 
must be carefully aligned with the study goal and scope, as it determines which stages 
are included in the LCA modeling [ 23]. The most common system boundaries in concrete 
LCAs are cradle-to-gate, cradle-to-grave, and cradle-to-cradle. Cradle-to-gate covers unit 
processes from raw material extraction to concrete production. Cradle-to-grave extends the 
scope to include use and maintenance phases and EOL processes (e.g., demolition, trans -
port, and disposal). Cradle-to-cradle further extends the boundary by including EOL recov-
ery and the reintegration of recovered materials into a subsequent product system, thereby 
enabling CE-related benefits and burdens to be represented. In this review, 31 instances 
were limited to cradle-to-gate, while cradle-to-grave (4 instances) and cradle-to-cradle (3 
instances) boundaries were explored minimally (see Fig. 6(a)).
However, the sustainability of RAC systems cannot be evaluated solely on a cradle-to-gate 
basis. Given the role of RCA use as a CE strategy, greater attention is needed to extend sys-
tem boundaries beyond EOL to incorporate cradle-to-cradle scenarios. In such cases, a key 
challenge is determining how environmental burdens and potential credits should be shared 
between the previous concrete life cycle and the subsequent life cycle in which recovered 
CW is processed into RCA and used to produce RAC. This directly relates to the broader 
discussion of allocation and multifunctionality handling.

Allocation and Multifunctionality Handling CW recycling commonly generates multiple out-
puts, with RCA being only one co-product. Where subdivision or system expansion is not feasi-
ble, this multifunctionality requires environmental burdens to be allocated among co-products 
[25]. In addition, CW recycling introduces a system-level multifunctionality, as burdens and 
potential credits may need to be distributed across consecutive life cycles when recovered CW 
is processed into RCA and used in new concrete. To handle cross-life-cycle multifunctionality, 
the primary approaches are cut-off and substitution-based avoided-burden, while approaches 
based on physical relationships or economic value are less frequently applied. In contrast, allo-
cation within multi-output processes is typically based on measurable physical relationships 
(e.g., mass, volume, or energy content) or the economic value of co-products [25].
In this review, most studies (24 of 37) did not report how allocation and multifunctional -
ity were handled, creating uncertainty regarding how environmental burdens and potential 
credits were assigned between co-products and/or across life cycles. Among the studies 
that specified an approach for cross-life-cycle multifunctionality handling, the substitution-
based avoided-burden approach was applied in seven instances, the cut-off approach in 
seven instances, and economic allocation in one instance. Only one instance addressed 
multi-output allocation using mass allocation. The distribution of these approaches is shown 
in Fig. 6(b), while the principles of each approach are summarized in Table 2, and the asso-
ciated references are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

LCI results to the selected impact categories, while characterization means converting those 
results into category indicator outcomes relating to the defined FU [23, 24].

Life Cycle Impact Assessment Approach LCIA commonly uses two modeling approaches. 
The midpoint (problem-oriented modeling) approach evaluates impacts at an intermediate 
stage along the cause-effect chain and typically includes indicators such as global warming 
potential (GWP), ozone depletion potential (ODP), acidification potential (AP), and can 
include more than 15 other indicators [ 30, 46]. The endpoint (damage-oriented modeling) 
approach links impacts further down the cause-effect chain to areas of protection such as 
human health, ecosystem quality, or resource depletion [ 46]. Among the reviewed studies, 
20 did not report the LCIA modeling approach used. However, based on the LCIA methods 
and the impact categories used, these can be classified as midpoint studies. In addition, 15 
studies explicitly reported using a midpoint approach, one reported an endpoint approach, 
and one used a combined midpoint-endpoint approach. 
Life Cycle Impact Assessment Methods A wide range of LCIA methods are available, each 
relying on distinct characterization models that link emissions or resource use to the poten-
tial impact categories. In this review, 9 studies (out of 37) did not report the LCIA method 
used. Among the reported LCIA methods, variations of the CML method were used in 11 
instances. Other widely used methods included the IPCC (6 instances), TRACI (4 instances), 
ReCiPe (3 instances), and IMPACT 2002+ (2 instances). Two instances reported the use 
of the Environmental Product Declaration (EPD) 2018 [ 47] and EN 15804:2012+A2:2019 
[48] methods. However, these are not typically considered as stand-alone LCIA methods in 
the same sense as CML or ReCiPe, but rather reporting/standardization frameworks. The 
mapping of LCIA methods to individual studies is provided in Supplementary Table S6, and 
their overall distribution is shown in Fig. 7.

Life Cycle Interpretation
According to ISO standards, the interpretation phase of an LCA involves three core ele -
ments: (i) identification of significant issues emerging from the LCI analysis and LCIA, (ii) 
evaluation of the study through completeness, sensitivity, and consistency checks, and (iii) 
drawing of conclusions, limitations, and recommendations [23, 24].
In the reviewed studies, the interpretation phase was generally used to identify 
environmental hotspots and to compare the environmental performance of RAC with that 
of conventional concrete (CC). Cement production was most frequently identified as the 
dominant contributor to environmental impacts, particularly for GWP, while transportation 
was also highlighted as an influential process in several studies [ 26, 41, 50, 56]. For RAC 
systems, the interpretation of results was commonly linked to RCA replacement level, 
transport distance, RCA processing method, cement adjustment, and allocation approach 
[22, 56].
The comparative findings showed that RAC did not consistently perform better than 
CC across all impact categories [ 9, 22]. Environmental benefits were more commonly 
reported when RCA replaced NCA without requiring significant additional cement, when 
RCA transport distances were relatively short, and when avoided landfill disposal was 
considered [ 9, 56]. In contrast, the environmental advantage of RAC was reduced or 
reversed when additional cement was needed to maintain functional equivalence, when 
RCA processing was energy-intensive, or when longer transport distances were assumed 
[22, 41]. Therefore, the environmental performance of RAC should be interpreted as 
highly scenario- and method-dependent, rather than assumed to be universally beneficial, 
highlighting the importance of transparent and carefully justified methodological decisions 
in RAC LCA studies.

Data Quality Analysis
To enhance the robustness and reliability of LCA outcomes, additional analyses such as 
sensitivity and uncertainty analyses are often recommended. These analyses help assess 
the stability of LCA outcomes and the level of confidence with which conclusions can be 
drawn [25, 30].
Sensitivity analysis examines how changes in input data, assumptions, and methodologi-
cal choices influence LCA outcomes [ 24]. In this review, 10 studies conducted sensitivity 
analysis as presented in Supplementary Table S8. The most frequently examined param -
eter was transport distance (for RCA or NA), while other parameters included mix design 
factors (e.g., additional cement demand, RCA proportion, cement type), recycling process 
configurations (e.g., fixed versus mobile treatment facilities), energy system (electricity grid 
composition, kiln fuel, renewable sources), different allocation approaches, transport mode/
type, and CO₂ uptake through carbonation.
Uncertainty analysis assesses how variability in input data and model assumptions prop-
agates through the LCA model and affects the reliability of outcomes. In this review, only 
two studies incorporated an uncertainty analysis. Mostert et al. [55] applied it at the building 
level, while Mistri et al. [58] used a Monte Carlo simulation to assess the influence of input 
variability in the context of developing India-specific LCA databases. Overall, the limited 
use of sensitivity and uncertainty analyses suggests that the robustness of RAC LCA conclu-
sions was often only partially evaluated, a point to consider when interpreting the reported 
environmental benefits and trade-offs.
Discussion
Emerging Trends in RAC LCA Practice
The analysis revealed a gradual increase in RAC LCA studies over the past decade, with 
68% of studies published between 2021 and 2025. This reflects the increasing recognition 
of LCA as a tool for assessing the environmental sustainability of recycled materials, in line 
with global shifts toward CE.
Geographical analysis indicates that China, Italy, and Spain are leading contributors to 
RAC LCA literature, suggesting that countries with high CDW generation and/or more 
established regulatory frameworks are shaping current LCA practice. While Europe and 
East Asia dominate the research landscape, African nations (except Egypt) and most parts 
of South America, Central and Eastern Europe, and the Middle East are underrepresented. 
Limited contributions from North America and large parts of the Asia–Pacific region fur -
ther indicate that the evidence base is largely Euro–Sino–centric. This concentration may 
constrain methodological diversity and limit the contextual transferability of LCA findings.
The keyword co-occurrence map shows strong thematic clustering around core environ-
mental CE topics. The prevalence of terms such as “life cycle assessment”, “construction 
and demolition waste”, “recycled aggregate”, “recycled concrete aggregate”, and “sustain -
ability” reflects an emphasis on environmental evaluation of material recovery pathways in 
recycled concrete production. However, the relative absence of keywords related to social 
1 3

Circular Economy and Sustainability (2026) 6:332 
or economic sustainability, policy integration, data harmonization, or regional adaptation 
indicates that most studies remain focused on the environmental dimension of sustainability. 
Future research could therefore benefit from more multidimensional approaches, including 
stronger integration of socio-economic considerations, context-specific datasets, and policy 
linkages to support broader sustainability appraisal.
Methodological Gaps and Reporting Inconsistencies in RAC LCA Practice
This section discusses the key methodological gaps and reporting inconsistencies identified 
across the reviewed studies. These issues relate to system approach selection, FU definition, 
system boundary selection, allocation and multifunctionality handling, inventory analysis, 
impact assessment, software usage, interpretation, and data quality analysis. In the context 
of CE implementation, these gaps are important because they affect how reliably RAC LCA 
studies can represent material recovery, functional equivalence, cross-life-cycle burden 
sharing, waste diversion, avoided use of virgin materials, and EOL recycling. Therefore, the 
identified methodological limitations should be understood not only as reporting issues, but 
also as factors that may affect the credibility of CE claims for RAC systems. A summary 
of these limitations is provided in Supplementary Table S9, while detailed discussions are 
presented in the following subsections.
System Approach Selection (Attributional/Consequential)
Although ISO standards emphasize the importance of clearly reporting and justifying the 
choice between an attributional and a consequential approach, none of the reviewed studies 
explicitly stated their system approach. Given that the selected approach can substantially 
influence the interpretation of LCA outcomes [25], clearer reporting would improve meth -
odological clarity and comparability.
The dominance of attributional modeling reflects a prevailing focus on representing 
existing production processes, rather than explicitly exploring market or behavioral con -
sequences of RCA integration. This may reflect the status of RCA integration into concrete 
as a developing rather than fully mainstream market, where most LCA frameworks are 
designed to compare product-level environmental performance under existing conditions. 
While ALCA provides a robust baseline for assessing and comparing environmental perfor-
mance, it offers limited insight into the dynamic effects of policy interventions, technology 
adoption, or market substitution linked to increased RCA use. Consequently, the limited 
application of CLCA approaches suggests an opportunity for system-level modeling that 
captures feedback mechanisms such as shifts in aggregate demand, transport logistics, and 
waste management practices to better inform environmental management and CE decision-
making for recycled construction materials.
Functional Unit (FU) Definition
Among the reported FU instances ( N = 42), the volumetric FU (1 m³ of concrete) was the 
most common (50% of instances). However, this FU may not fully capture functional equiv-
alence between RAC and CC [22]. ISO 14040/44 requires that the FU define the product’s 
intended function and quantify it in terms of relevant performance characteristics [ 23, 24]. 
1 3
Page 17 of 32 332 
Circular Economy and Sustainability (2026) 6:332 
In addition, the EN 15804:2012+A2:2019 standard, which is widely applied in EPDs for 
construction products, classifies volumetric measures such as 1 m³ of concrete as declared 
units and recommends their use primarily when the product’s function or application is not 
fully defined [36].
Even where performance differences exist, volumetric comparisons may remain appro -
priate for applications such as non-structural filling or bulk use, where functional require -
ments are relatively uniform. However, this approach becomes more problematic for 
higher-value structural concrete applications, where performance equivalence is critical. 
In such contexts, functional requirements are typically predefined by design specifications 
(e.g., a specified strength grade for columns). Therefore, continued reliance on volumetric 
FU without integrating performance parameters into the FU definition may limit compara -
bility across studies and reduce the decision relevance of comparative LCA conclusions.
This issue is less critical for materials such as steel, where closed-loop recycling and 1:1 
substitution are generally feasible without compromising functional performance compared 
with virgin material. However, in concrete, many studies have shown that a 1:1 volumet -
ric replacement of NCA with RCA often reduces functional performance [ 42]. If such dif-
ferences are not considered explicitly, comparative LCAs may be affected by functional 
inequivalence.
To achieve equivalent performance, RAC mixtures typically require higher cement con-
tent or superplasticizers, which alter mix design and influence the overall LCA outcome 
[44, 45]. Accordingly, defining an FU that integrates performance parameters supports more 
technically meaningful and environmentally robust comparisons between RAC and CC sys-
tems [41].
Performance metrics typically integrated into the FU of RAC LCA studies include com-
pressive strength, workability, and durability [41, 56]. Accordingly, several studies adopted 
FUs that more closely aligned with ISO principles: strength-based FU (26% of instances), 
strength–workability FU (7% of instances), and strength–durability FU (7% of instances) 
(Table 1). In other studies (7% of instances), the environmental impact of 1 m³ of concrete 
was normalized to predefined performance parameters. In this review, studies that initially 
defined a volumetric FU but subsequently ensured functional equivalence through mix 
design adjustments were categorized accordingly, as their comparative assessments were 
ultimately performance-consistent. Nevertheless, explicitly defining the intended functional

function unless the FU itself specifies the required strength, durability, exposure condition, 
and reference service life. Moreover, such normalization may implicitly assume a linear 
relationship between environmental impact and the selected performance parameter, even 
though the functional value of concrete may not scale proportionally with that parameter. 
Therefore, if mass-based or performance-normalized impact FUs are adopted, their selec -
tion should be explicitly justified, and comparative results should be interpreted cautiously.
System Boundary Definition
Among the instances of a reported system boundary ( N = 38), 82% were cradle-to-gate, 
while 10% were cradle-to-grave and 8% were cradle-to-cradle. In most cases, cradle-to-
gate studies considered recycling substitution only at the material input stage and excluded 
further recycling scenarios. This tendency may reflect ongoing uncertainties regarding the 
recyclability of RAC at EOL, as material quality has been reported to decline with succes -
sive cycles.
The predominance of cradle-to-gate boundaries is justified by several studies based on 
the following rationale. Some studies assume functional equivalence, suggesting that RAC 
and CC systems perform similarly during the construction, use, and demolition phases [ 6, 
11]. Others argue that for concretes designed for the same application, evaluating only the 
production phase is sufficient, as subsequent life-cycle stages are expected to have limited 
influence on LCA outcomes [56, 59, 60]. In some studies, the later stages were excluded pri-
marily as a system boundary choice [52, 57, 61]. Additionally, certain authors contend that 
post-gate emissions are minor relative to those from raw material extraction and production 
1 3
Page 19 of 32 332 
Circular Economy and Sustainability (2026) 6:332 
phases [11], while others excluded later phases due to data limitations or unavailability [62]. 
While such simplifications reduce LCA modeling complexity, they can limit insight into 
service-life and EOL sustainability trade-offs.
More importantly, the dominance of the cradle-to-gate boundary can influence how RAC is 
interpreted as a CE strategy. When only production-stage burdens are considered, RAC benefits 
are often predominantly represented through reduced virgin aggregate extraction and the inte-
gration of RCA as an input material. However, this boundary may exclude several mechanisms 
that are central to circularity, including differences in durability and maintenance requirements 
during use, carbonation uptake during service and after demolition, EOL crushing and recovery 
processes, residual waste disposal, and potential avoided landfill burdens. As a result, cradle-to-
gate studies may either overstate RAC benefits by ignoring possible service-life or maintenance 
penalties or understate benefits by excluding carbonation uptake, future material recovery, and 
avoided disposal impacts. Therefore, CE claims based solely on cradle-to-gate results should 
be interpreted with caution unless the study’s goal is limited to a production-stage comparison 
and functional equivalence between RAC and CC is clearly justified.
Only a minority of studies attempted full life cycle coverage, including those adopting a 
cradle-to-grave system boundary. For example, Colangelo et al. [ 44] omitted the construc-
tion, use, and maintenance phases and considered only the demolition stage. Pavlů et al. [6] 
modeled two use cycles of RAC within an open-loop system, considering demolition and 
landfill at the end. Alberto López Ruiz et al. [13] omitted the construction, renovation, and 
use stages, modeling only the waste-disposal stage. Similarly, Aman et al. [ 63] restricted 
their assessment to landfill processes and the associated emissions occurring during the 
EOL stage. Collectively, these examples indicate that even where cradle-to-grave boundar-
ies are adopted, construction and use stages are often excluded, which may limit interpretive 
depth and the identification of environmental sustainability trade-offs.
RAC can differ from CC in several aspects that may affect these frequently omitted life-
cycle stages. RAC typically exhibits higher carbon sequestration potential due to greater 
porosity and residual cement paste [ 49, 64], and this potential can vary with RA treatment 
methods [14, 22]. Durability performance may also vary with exposure conditions [ 41]. 
Nevertheless, most LCA studies assume identical service lives under non-aggressive envi -
ronmental conditions for both RAC and CC [11, 49]. Such assumptions may be reasonable 
for specific applications where equivalent performance has been demonstrated, but they may 
also overlook material-specific degradation mechanisms, maintenance requirements, and 
carbonation behavior. This can lead to simplified representations of the long-term environ -
mental performance of RAC, particularly in structural or exposure-sensitive applications.
There also remains ambiguity in how cradle-to-cradle boundaries are defined and applied. 
In principle, a cradle-to-cradle boundary should extend beyond the current product life cycle 
and explicitly represent EOL recovery and the reintegration of recovered materials into a 
subsequent product system. However, in several reviewed studies, the term cradle-to-cradle 
was used even when the model primarily represented recycled-material inputs at the produc-
tion stage. For example, Pu et al. [49] presented their system boundary as cradle-to-cradle; 
however, the construction, service, and demolition phases were excluded. In practice, the 
model primarily represented concrete production using RA, including aggregate treatment 
processes, transport, concrete mixing, and carbonation uptake, rather than a fully modeled 
cradle-to-cradle concrete life cycle. Therefore, the adopted system boundary appears to 
align more closely with a cradle-to-gate framework incorporating RCA recycling input.

A similar interpretation appears in Aman et al. [ 63], which framed the RCA integration 
modeling scenario as a cradle-to-cradle approach. However, the operational model for this 
scenario ended at the concrete batching plant due to a lack of data on downstream construc-
tion-site transport and CDW incorporation. Thus, although the study conceptually invoked 
a cradle-to-cradle framework, the modeled system more closely reflects a cradle-to-gate 
approach incorporating RCA input.
Ding et al. [56] described their study as a closed-loop cradle-to-cradle LCA because CW 
was modeled as either being landfilled or recycled into RAC. Unlike previous studies that 
incorporated RCA as a recycled input at the production stage, Ding et al. [ 56] explicitly 
included waste-disposal and recycling-related processes at EOL, including landfill burdens 
and RCA recovery. However, the construction, service, and demolition phases were omitted 
for modeling simplicity, as they were assumed to be equivalent for CC and RAC under non-
aggressive exposure conditions.
These observations indicate that cradle-to-cradle terminology is applied inconsistently 
across RAC LCA studies. In some studies, it primarily reflects recycled material input at 
the production stage, while in others it represents partial closed-loop modeling that includes 
selected EOL or recovery processes. However, these approaches do not always fully rep -
resent the broader closed-loop logic typically implied by cradle-to-cradle framing. These 
inconsistencies highlight the need for clearer and more standardized boundary definitions 
and reporting, particularly when CE perspectives are invoked.
Allocation and Multifunctionality Handling
Allocation and multifunctionality handling are among the most challenging methodologi -
cal aspects in contemporary LCA practice [27, 65]. In principle, closed-loop recycling can 
often reduce or avoid the need for cross-life-cycle multifunctionality handling, provided 
that the recycled material displaces an equivalent virgin material and maintains functional 
equivalence [15]. However, most concrete recycling frameworks are open-loop in nature, 
with RCA often entering different concrete production systems or being downcycled to 
lower-grade applications due to reduced material performance [ 49, 63]. In such cases, 
transparent treatment of cross-life-cycle burdens and potential credits is required to assign 
responsibility between the original and subsequent life cycles, as CE systems imply shared 
responsibility for material recovery. Additional complexity arises from multi-output recy -
cling processes, where several co-products (e.g., RAs, steel, fines, and residual waste) are 
generated, potentially requiring multi-output allocation.
RAC inherently involves at least two interlinked life cycles and therefore requires a 
transparent treatment of cross-life-cycle burdens and credits. Despite this, nearly two-thirds 
of the reviewed studies did not explicitly specify how allocation and multifunctionality 
were handled. Such omissions can reduce interpretability and transparency, as the selected 
approach can substantially influence LCA outcomes and comparative conclusions [41]. This 
transparency gap highlights persistent challenges in ISO-aligned LCA reporting among 
RAC studies [23, 24].
ISO recommends avoiding allocation where possible, either by dividing unit processes 
into two or more sub-processes and collecting input–output data for each sub-process, or by 
expanding the product system boundary (system expansion) to include additional functions 
related to co-products [24]. Consequently, although system expansion is often discussed in

literature alongside allocation, it is formally an allocation-avoiding approach. When alloca-
tion cannot be avoided, it should preferably be based on physical relationships that reflect 
actual input–output changes. Where such physical relationships cannot be established, allo-
cation may instead be based on alternative criteria, such as economic value [ 24]. ISO fur-
ther requires consistent allocation rules across comparable processes, with mass and energy 
balances maintained between inputs and outputs. These principles also extend to reuse and 
recycling systems, where clear differentiation between closed-loop and open-loop recycling 
is needed to ensure methodological consistency and integrity [ 24]. Whether the method -
ological issue concerns cross-life-cycle burden and credit sharing or multi-output recy -
cling, the processes subject to allocation, the allocation basis adopted, and the treatment of 
avoided burdens should be reported transparently to avoid skewed claims about circularity.

Life Cycle Inventory (LCI) Analysis
The findings indicate that RAC LCA studies commonly rely on mixed inventory data 
sources, reflecting both the complexity of RAC systems and the limited availability of stan-
dardized datasets for recycling-related processes. The use of primary data in approximately 
57% of studies suggests that context-specific data are increasingly recognized as important, 
particularly for key foreground processes such as waste collection, transport, sorting, crush-
ing, screening, and RCA production.
However, the continued reliance on literature-derived inventory data, used in approxi -
mately 70% of studies, raises concerns regarding contextual accuracy and regional repre -
sentativeness. Several studies were based entirely on literature-derived datasets [ 22, 41, 
52, 53, 61, 63], which may limit their ability to represent specific technologies, operational 
conditions, regional transport options, the mix of electricity, the specific energy demand and 
practices of the recycling plant, the composition of the cement, and variability in the quality 
of RCA. These factors should be considered methodological sensitivity points, as varia -
tions can substantially influence comparative LCA outcomes for RAC and CC. As noted by 
Colangelo et al. [44], relying solely on literature or secondary data can be a methodological 
limitation of LCA when such datasets are not aligned with the actual system being assessed.
The frequent use of proprietary databases, particularly Ecoinvent, can improve com -
patibility with major LCA software and provide broad data coverage. However, restricted 
access to the underlying inventory datasets in proprietary databases may reduce transpar -
ency, reproducibility, and cross-study comparability of RAC LCA outcomes. In contrast, 
the use of regional or public datasets remains comparatively limited, despite their impor -
tance for improving geographical representativeness, particularly for electricity genera -
tion, transport systems, aggregate production, supply chains, and recycling technologies. 
Overall, these findings suggest that inventory data quality remains a critical methodological 
challenge in RAC LCA studies, and that key inventory parameters should be interpreted as 
sensitivity drivers of LCA rather than only as reporting items.

Life Cycle Impact Assessment (LCIA)
The findings indicate that RAC LCA studies are strongly dominated by midpoint-based 
impact assessment, while endpoint and combined midpoint–endpoint approaches remain 
comparatively underutilized. This pattern is methodologically understandable, as mid -
point methods are generally considered more transparent, scientifically robust, and asso -
ciated with lower uncertainty, whereas endpoint methods provide greater interpretability 
for decision-makers by translating impacts into damage categories such as human health, 
ecosystem quality, and resource depletion [67, 68]. However, limited adoption of endpoint 
modeling may reduce the decision relevance of LCA conclusions, particularly in policy-ori-
ented applications. This is increasingly important because European standards such as EN 
15804:2012+A2:2019 recommend a combined midpoint–endpoint perspective to capture 
both potential environmental pressures and damage to areas of protection [36].
Transparency in LCIA reporting remains a key limitation. Approximately 24% of the 
studies did not report the LCIA method used. This omission is important because the different 
LCIA methods assign different characterization factors and impact units to the same impact 
category. In addition, LCIA methods are developed based on different modeling assumptions, 
regional scope, midpoint or endpoint structure, and level of uncertainty [69]. When the chosen 
LCIA method is not clearly reported or justified, these methodological uncertainties may be 
overlooked, potentially reducing the reliability, transparency, and comparability of the LCA 
outcome. This is particularly relevant because different characterization models can generate 
divergent results for the same inventory data, highlighting the influence of LCIA method 
selection on LCA outcomes [25, 69]. Therefore, cross-study comparison becomes challenging 
unless the LCIA method is explicitly stated and aligned with the study’s goal and scope. Overall, 
limited disclosure of LCIA methods constrains both the interpretation of individual study results 
and the development of consistent methodological benchmarks for RAC LCA research.
Among the reported methods, CML (midpoint-oriented) was the most widely used, fol -
lowed by IPCC, which focuses on climate change using the GWP indicator. Other LCIA 
methods, including TRACI, ReCiPe, and IMPACT 2002+, were adopted less commonly. 
TRACI is exclusively midpoint-oriented, while ReCiPe integrates both midpoint and end -
point frameworks. IMPACT 2002+ adopts a hybrid structure that links midpoint indicators 
to endpoint domains. The prevalence of CML likely reflects its long-standing use, famil -
iarity among LCA practitioners, and broad software compatibility. However, because the 
CML method does not provide endpoint-level interpretation, relying solely on it may limit 
broader damage- or policy-oriented interpretation. Accordingly, LCIA method selection 
should be consistent with the study’s goal and scope and explicitly justified to support the 
credibility and relevance of LCA conclusions [23, 24].
Consequently, the choice of the LCIA method can influence how RAC environmental 
superiority is interpreted. Different methods and indicator sets vary in category coverage, 
regional relevance, characterization model, and midpoint or endpoint perspective. As a 
result, RAC may appear environmentally favorable in categories linked to climate change, 
resource depletion, or avoided use of virgin aggregate, while trade-offs may emerge in cat-
egories affected by additional processing, transport, treatment energy, water use, or toxicity-
related impacts. Therefore, conclusions on RAC superiority should be based on a clearly 
reported and justified LCIA approach and interpreted across relevant impact categories 
rather than relying on a single indicator.

The climate change impact category, commonly quantified using GWP, was consistently 
identified as the most frequently assessed environmental impact category. This reflects the 
well-established contribution of concrete production to greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and 
prioritization in environmental assessments [67]. Other frequently reported midpoint catego-
ries include EP, AP, POCP, ODP, and ADP. Many studies also emphasized energy use as a 
major environmental concern, often employing standalone energy consumption indicators. 
However, reliance on a narrow set of impact categories may limit the ability of RAC LCA 
studies to capture broader environmental trade-offs associated with RCA integration. There-
fore, impact category selection should extend beyond GWP where relevant and be justified 
according to the environmental mechanisms most affected by the RAC system under study.
The LCA software reporting also has implications for methodological transparency and 
reproducibility. Although software choice does not by itself determine the validity of an 
LCA, it influences database access, modeling structure, available LCIA methods, and the 
reproducibility of inventory and impact assessment procedures. The reviewed studies show 
a clear dominance of commercial tools, particularly SimaPro, while open-source and con -
crete-specific tools were used less frequently. This may improve compatibility with estab -
lished databases and widely used LCIA methods, but it can also limit transparency where 
proprietary databases or undocumented modeling assumptions are used.
Overall, these findings indicate that LCIA reporting in RAC LCA studies remains uneven, 
particularly in the explicit declaration and justification of LCIA methods, impact category 
selection, software tools, and databases. This can limit transparency, comparability, and the 
decision relevance of LCA conclusions.
Life Cycle Interpretation
A more comprehensive interpretation phase is needed to move RAC LCA studies beyond 
descriptive hotspot identification toward clearer decision support. Although many studies have 
compared RAC and CC environmental performance, the interpretation phase was often limited 
to reporting which processes contributed most, rather than analyzing the conditions under which 
RAC becomes environmentally preferable. This is important because the conclusions of the 
RAC LCA are strongly influenced by factors such as functional equivalence, system boundary 
selection, allocation and multifunctionality-handling approach, transport distance, RCA process-
ing intensity, and cement content adjustment. Therefore, conclusions on RAC environmental 
sustainability should not be presented as universal outcomes, but as context-dependent find-
ings shaped by the assumptions adopted in each study. For example, studies should clarify and 
clearly report whether observed benefits arise from reduced virgin aggregate extraction, avoided 
waste management, shorter transport distances, material substitution credits, carbonation uptake, 
or specific modeling assumptions. Such an interpretation would reduce overgeneralization and 
enhance the usefulness of RAC LCA outcomes for decision-making in circular construction. 
This would also help distinguish genuine CE benefits from apparent benefits created by narrow 
system boundaries, unclear allocation approaches, or incomplete EOL modeling.
Utilization of Data Quality Analysis (Sensitivity and Uncertainty Analyses)
Sensitivity analysis is particularly important in RAC LCA studies because environmental 
outcomes often depend on parameters that vary across projects and regions. Transportation

was the most frequently examined sensitivity parameter, reflecting its significant 
influence on the environmental performance of RAC systems, particularly where RCA 
and NA supply routes differ [ 56, 70]. RCA proportion was also commonly tested, 
as it can affect both environmental impacts and mechanical performance, especially 
when mix design adjustments are required to maintain functional equivalence [ 40, 55]. 
Energy-related assumptions, including electricity source, fuel type, and energy mix,

energy and decarbonization policy [ 3, 41]. Importantly, reporting parameters that show 
limited sensitivity are also useful, as they improve transparency and help future studies 
identify which variables should be prioritized, validated, or reasonably excluded.
However, uncertainty analysis remains significantly underutilized. This is a significant 
limitation because RAC LCA studies often combine primary data, literature-derived 
assumptions, and secondary database values, each of which may introduce variability 
into LCA modeling. Therefore, wider adoption of uncertainty analysis would improve 
confidence in LCA conclusions and support more robust comparison and decision-making. 
Future studies would therefore benefit from more consistent application of sensitivity and 
uncertainty analyses to key variables, including transport distance, allocation approach, 
RCA processing energy, cement-content adjustment, substitution ratios, carbonation uptake, 
and EOL assumptions.
ISO-Aligned Methodological Recommendations for RAC LCA Practice
Based on the methodological gaps and reporting inconsistencies identified across the 
reviewed studies, this section presents decision-oriented, ISO-aligned recommendations 
to improve the robustness, transparency, and comparability of future RAC LCA 
research. A summary of recommendations for the main LCA components is provided in 
Supplementary Table S10, while the key recommendations are discussed below.
RAC LCA studies can improve the relevance and transparency of their conclusions 
by clearly defining the study goal and scope, intended application, decision context, 
target audience, and whether results are intended to support comparative claims, product 
development, policy guidance, or preliminary screening. Within this context, studies 
should explicitly state and justify the use of an attributional or consequential approach. 
ALCA is appropriate when the study aims to describe or compare the environmental 
performance of RAC systems under current production and market conditions, whereas 
CLCA can be adopted when the research question concerns the consequences of 
decisions likely to affect market conditions, such as large-scale adoption, policy-driven 
demand changes, shifts in recycling flows, transport logistics, or substitution patterns. 
Therefore, the choice between ALCA and CLCA should be guided by the study goal, 
intervention scale, market relevance, and data availability, rather than applying either 
approach by default.
V olumetric FUs can be strengthened by incorporating relevant performance metrics to 
improve comparability and ensure that environmental impacts are interpreted on a function-
ally equivalent basis in line with ISO 14040/44 and EN 15804 guidance. Where RAC and 
CC differ in strength development, durability performance, or expected service life, these 
parameters should be incorporated directly into the FU definition rather than discussed only 
as separate performance indicators.

As many studies adopted cradle-to-gate boundaries, extending system boundaries to cap-
ture service-life effects, carbonation uptake, EOL recovery, and subsequent recycling stages 
may provide a more complete representation of the environmental implications of RCA 
integration into concrete and material circularity. In addition, transparent and consistently 
applied definitions of cradle-to-cradle system boundaries are needed to clarify how EOL 
recycling is modeled, including whether recovered RCA is treated as an output, credited for 
avoided landfill disposal or virgin aggregate substitution, or reintegrated into a subsequent 
product system. Consistent with ISO requirements, studies should also clearly define cut-off 
criteria and report processes excluded from the LCA modeling, as such exclusions can affect 
the comparability and transparency of RAC LCA outcomes.
Furthermore, the allocation and multifunctionality handling should be explicitly reported 
and transparently justified, especially when environmental credits are assigned for waste 
diversion, NA substitution, or avoided landfill. Studies should clearly specify which pro -
cesses were allocated and how impacts were distributed across life cycles and/or co-prod -
ucts. Future studies should distinguish between scenario analysis, which explores alternative 
modeling choices such as allocation approach, system boundary, RCA processing route, or 
EOL pathway, and sensitivity analysis, which examines numerical parameters such as trans-
port distance, RCA replacement ratio, and energy-related assumptions.
The use of primary data for key RAC unit processes can improve the contextual 
relevance of LCA outcomes, particularly where emissions, transport burdens, and 
recycling efficiencies vary across regions and facilities. In addition, foreground inventories 
should clearly report RCA processing steps, transport distances, energy and water inputs, 
emissions, by-products, and waste outputs to improve inventory completeness and allow 
meaningful comparison across studies. When secondary databases are used, their regional 
and technological relevance needs to be clearly justified. Transparent reporting of the 
software tool, inventory data sources, modeling assumptions, and any modifications made 
to default datasets or characterization methods is essential for improving reproducibility 
and comparability across RAC LCA studies. Digital LCA tools and AI-assisted inventory 
development may also help generate more detailed and harmonized datasets for aggregate 
recycling systems.
Midpoint LCIA methods may be complemented by endpoint or hybrid approaches 
where broader interpretation of environmental damage, resource depletion, or pollution-
related effects is required. To improve robustness, RAC LCA studies should clearly state 
the selected LCIA method, justify its relevance to the study context and impact categories 
considered, and, where appropriate, apply more than one impact assessment method or con-
duct sensitivity analysis to examine the influence of LCIA method selection. Studies should 
also consider a comprehensive set of impact categories rather than focusing exclusively 
on GWP to identify broader environmental sustainability trade-offs associated with RCA 
integration. The interpretation phase should also move beyond hotspot identification to 
clarify the conditions under which RAC is environmentally preferable, particularly regard-
ing transport distance, cement-content adjustment, allocation approach, RCA processing 
intensity, carbonation uptake, and EOL modeling assumptions. Since these factors can alter 
comparative LCA outcomes between RAC and CC, sensitivity and uncertainty analyses 
should be treated as essential decision-support tools for testing the robustness of conclu -
sions, identifying influential assumptions, and improving confidence in policy, design, and 
material-selection decisions.

Finally, while environmental assessment remains central, extending RAC sustainability 
evaluation to include economic and social dimensions would support a more complete sus-
tainability appraisal of RAC systems. This is especially relevant where the practical adoption 
of RAC depends not only on environmental sustainability but also on cost competitiveness, 
supply chain feasibility, and stakeholder acceptance. An integrated assessment approach can 
therefore provide a stronger basis for decision-making associated with RCA integration.
Overall, these recommendations provide decision-oriented and ISO-aligned practical guid-
ance for LCA practitioners, construction-materials researchers, policymakers, and standardiza-
tion bodies by supporting more transparent modeling, functionally equivalent RAC comparisons, 
cautious interpretation of CE claims, and clearer reporting guidance for future RAC LCA studies.
Conclusions
This study conducted an ISO-informed methodological audit of contemporary life cycle 
assessment (LCA) practices applied to recycled aggregate concrete (RAC), using a 
PRISMA-guided systematic review. The review synthesized evidence from 37 peer-
reviewed studies published between January 2015 and May 2025 and critically evaluated 
the prevailing methodological challenges and inconsistencies in LCA practices, including 
how these limitations may influence the LCA outcomes. By linking the identified limitations 
to ISO-aligned recommendations, this review provides practical guidance for improving the 
robustness, comparability, transparency, and credibility of future RAC LCA research and 
circularity claims. The key conclusions from the review are summarized as follows.
 ● Limited transparency in system approach selection affects interpretability: None of the 
reviewed studies explicitly stated whether an attributional or consequential LCA ap -
proach was adopted, reducing methodological clarity and decision relevance.
 ● Over-reliance on volumetric FUs limits functional equivalence: Frequent use of purely 
volumetric FUs (1 m 3 of concrete) does not fully reflect performance equivalence be -
tween RAC and CC and may reduce the comparability of LCA conclusions.
 ● Predominance of cradle-to-gate system boundary limits CE interpretation: Reliance on cradle-
to-gate boundaries restricts the assessment of long-term sustainability trade-offs, including ser-
vice life effects, carbonation uptake, EOL management, and subsequent recycling stages, and 
the extent to which RAC systems genuinely support CE outcomes beyond material substitution.
 ● Limited reporting and justification of allocation and multifunctionality handling weaken 
robustness: Frequent absence of transparent and well-justified treatment of allocation 
and multifunctionality handling weakens confidence in LCA conclusions, particularly 
in studies intended to inform policy or CE decisions. Such omissions may also overstate 
the apparent environmental superiority of RAC when recycling burdens, avoided use 
of virgin material, landfill diversion, or EOL credits are not consistently accounted for.
 ● Inventory data limitations reduce contextual reliability : Continued reliance on litera -
ture-derived and proprietary datasets without sufficient regional or process-specific jus-
tification can reduce the representativeness of RAC LCA outcomes.
 ● Limited LCIA justification affects decision relevance: Incomplete reporting or justifica-
tion of LCIA methods and impact categories can restrict comparability and overlook 
broader environmental trade-offs associated with RCA integration.
1 3

 ● Sensitivity and uncertainty analyses remain underused: Limited testing of key assump-
tions, such as transport distance, energy mix, cement adjustment, RCA processing, al -
location, carbonation, and EOL modeling, can reduce confidence in RAC LCA conclu-
sions.
Collectively, these findings highlight that inconsistent FUs, narrow system boundaries, 
unclear allocation and multifunctionality-handling approaches, limitations in data quality, 
limited LCIA justification, and insufficient sensitivity and uncertainty analyses can lead 
to divergent interpretations of RAC environmental performance. The ISO-aligned recom -
mendations proposed in this review provide a practical basis for improving methodologi -
cal transparency, reliability, reproducibility, and decision relevance in future RAC LCA 
research. They also help distinguish genuine CE benefits from outcomes primarily driven 
by narrow boundaries, unreported allocation assumptions, or incomplete EOL modeling.
This study has some limitations. The review was based on 37 peer-reviewed English-lan-
guage journal articles identified primarily through Scopus and complemented by backward 
and forward snowballing. Therefore, some relevant studies that are only indexed in other 
databases, published in other languages, or in grey literature may not have been captured. 
Nevertheless, the selected studies provide a strong basis for identifying key methodological 
trends, reporting gaps, and opportunities to improve ISO-aligned RAC LCA practice.
