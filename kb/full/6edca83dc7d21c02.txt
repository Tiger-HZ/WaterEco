Understanding how disaster-affected populations experience and interpret unmet needs is essential for designing effective early recovery interventions. This study examines how perceived unmet needs following the 2024 flash flood in the Valencian Community (Spain) shaped survivors’ feelings of abandonment and helplessness, with particular attention to essential household utilities. Drawing on Conservation of Resources theory and Rapid Needs Assessment frameworks, we employed a mixed-methods design using survey data from 300 affected individuals collected seven months after the event. Perceived shortages were assessed across four domains—basic relief, practical support, emotional and social support, and household utilities—at nine time points from 24 h to three months post-disaster. Unmet needs were most acute during the first week, especially in basic relief and household utilities. Shortages of food, shelter, electricity, and health assistance significantly predicted abandonment within the first 72 h, whereas deficits in legal and administrative support became salient predictors in subsequent months. Utility disruptions—particularly electricity and telecommunications—were experienced not only as infrastructural failures but also as psychologically salient indicators of vulnerability. Qualitative evidence highlights a strong sense of institutional abandonment, amplified by perceived delays, misinformation, and slow reconstruction, alongside a contrasting narrative of gratitude driven by widespread volunteer mobilization. Findings support expanding post-disaster needs taxonomies to include household utilities and prioritizing their rapid restoration as a psychosocial intervention, alongside improved communication, coordination, and integration of volunteer networks to reduce secondary stressors and prevent a “second disaster.”
