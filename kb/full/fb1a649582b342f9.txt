Influence of potassium and magnesium sulphate levels on
soil properties and nutrient status under maize (Zea
Mays L.) in Southern Transition Zone of Karnataka
Jonnalagadda Abhishikth1, Jayaprakash SM2, Niranjana KS3, Naveen NE4,
Basavarajappa Bhogi5
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/2618060X.2026.v9.i10b.6762
A BSTRACT
A field experiment entitled “Influence of Potassium and Magnesium Sulphate Levels on Productivity of
Maize (Zea mays L.)” was conducted during Kharif 2025 at the College of Agriculture, Navile,
Shivamogga, under the Southern Transition Zone (Agro-Climatic Zone VII) of Karnataka. The experi-
ment was laid out in a Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) with seven treatments and three
replications, comprising an absolute control, package of practices (PoP: 100:50:25 kg N:P2O5:K2O
ha−1), PoP + magnesium sulphate at 10 and 20 kg ha−1, an elevated potassium level (100 kg N, 50 kg
P2O5, 50 kg K2O ha−1) and its combination with magnesium sulphate at 10 and 20 kg ha−1. Post-har-
vest soil analysis revealed that soil pH, electrical conductivity, organic carbon and DTPA-extractable
Zn, Cu, Fe and Mn were not significantly influenced by the treatments, whereas exchangeable magne-
sium and available sulphur were significantly improved by magnesium sulphate application, particu-
larly at 20 kg ha−1 combined with the higher potassium level (T7). Available N, P and K2O were highest
under the package of practices and elevated-potassium treatments, while exchangeable calcium re-
mained statistically unaffected despite showing a numerically declining trend with increasing potassium
and magnesium supply. Available soil K2O declined progressively from 30 DAS to harvest across all
treatments, reflecting crop uptake, with T5, T6 and T7 maintaining significantly higher residual K status
than the control. The results indicate that combined potassium and magnesium sulphate application, es-
pecially at higher magnesium levels, improves soil secondary-nutrient status and sustains potassium fer-
tility without adversely affecting the major physico-chemical properties of soil.
Keywords: Maize, Potassium, Magnesium sulphate, Soil properties, Exchangeable magnesium,
Available sulphur, Soil fertility
Introduction
Soil fertility management is central to sustaining the productivity of maize (Zea mays L.), a crop known for
its high biomass production and exhaustive nutrient uptake. Among the major and secondary nutrients,
potassium (K) and magnesium (Mg) occupy a distinctive position because of their close physiological and
geochemical interaction. Potassium is the most abundant cation in the soil solution available for plant uptake
and plays a central role in soil cation exchange dynamics, while magnesium, though required in smaller
quantities, is essential for chlorophyll synthesis and enzyme activation and is particularly prone to depletion
in light-textured, high-rainfall soils.
The relationship between potassium and magnesium in soil is governed largely by cationic competition for
adsorption sites on soil colloids. Excessive or unbalanced potassium fertilization can suppress magnesium
availability, while magnesium sulphate application, in addition to correcting Mg deficiency, supplies sulphur
and can influence the broader nutrient equilibrium of the soil. Despite the agronomic importance of this in-
teraction, information on how combined potassium and magnesium sulphate application influences soil
physico-chemical properties, macro- and micronutrient status under maize in the Southern Transition Zone
of Karnataka remains limited.
The present study was therefore undertaken to assess the influence of potassium and magnesium sulphate
levels on soil properties and nutrient status - pH, electrical conductivity, organic carbon, available N, P, K,
exchangeable Ca and Mg, available S, and DTPA-extractable micronutrients — under maize cultivation.
Materials and Methods
The field experiment was conducted during Kharif 2025 at the College of Agriculture, Navile, Shivamogga,
KSNUAHS, situated in the Southern Transition Zone (Agro-Climatic Zone VII) of Karnataka (13°97′ N,
75°57′ E; altitude 667.5 m MSL). The experimental soil was sandy loam in texture, acidic in reaction
(pH 5.63), with an electrical conductivity of 0.13 dS m−1 and organic carbon content of 4.70 g kg−1. Initial

The experiment was laid out in RCBD with seven treatments and
three replications: T1 – absolute control; T2 – PoP (100:50:25 kg
N:P2O5:K2O ha−1); T3 – T2 + MgSO4 10 kg ha−1; T4 – T2 + MgSO4
20 kg ha−1; T5 – 100 kg N, 50 kg P2O5, 50 kg K2O ha−1; T6 – T5 +
MgSO4 10 kg ha−1; T7 – T5 + MgSO4 20 kg ha−1. FYM was applied
at 10 t ha−1, common to T2–T7. Maize hybrid GH-150125 was sown
at 60 × 30 cm spacing. Soil samples were collected at 30, 60, 90 DAS
and at harvest, and analyzed for pH and EC by
potentiometry/conductometry (Jackson, 1973) [1], organic carbon by
the wet oxidation method (Walkley et al., 1934) [2], available N by
the alkaline permanganate method (Subbiah et al., 1956) [3], available
P by Bray’s method (Jackson, 1973) [1], available K by flame pho-
tometry (Jackson, 1973) [1], exchangeable Ca and Mg by versenate
titration (Jackson, 1973) [1], available S by the turbidimetric method
(Black, 1965) [4] and DTPA-extractable micronutrients by atomic ab-
sorption spectrophotometry (Lindsay et al., 1978) [5]. Data were ana-
lyzed following the ANOVA procedure of Gomez and Gomez (1984)
[6] with significance tested at 5 per cent.
Results and Discussion
Soil pH, electrical conductivity and organic carbon
Post-harvest soil pH (5.55–5.63), EC (0.11–0.18 dS m−1) and organic
carbon (4.59–4.71 g kg−1) were not significantly influenced by potas-
sium and magnesium sulphate levels (Table 1). The narrow numerical
range, with slightly higher pH and OC under T7, suggests that the ap-
plied doses of K and MgSO4, though biologically active, were insuffi-
cient over a single season to measurably alter these buffered soil
properties. The findings indicate that potassium and magnesium sul-
phate application at the tested levels had no adverse effect on soil re-
action, electrical conductivity and organic carbon under the experi-
mental conditions. Similar findings were reported by Tisdale et al.
(1993) [7] and Brady and Weil (2017) [8].
Available nitrogen and phosphorus
Available N and P2O5 were significantly influenced by the treatments
(Table 2), with the package of practices (T2: 256.57 kg N ha−1;
49.54 kg P2O5 ha−1) and the elevated-K treatments recording higher
residual values than the absolute control (T ₁ : 198.03 and 35.98 kg
ha ⁻ ¹, respectively). The comparatively lower residual N and P in the
MgSO4-treated plots (T3, T4, T6, T7) relative to their respective base
treatments (T2, T5) is attributable to greater crop uptake associated
with the improved growth and biomass these treatments supported,
rather than to any adverse effect of magnesium sulphate on N or P
availability. Similar observations were reported by Marschner (2012)
[9]and Havlin et al. (2014) [10].

Available potassium at critical growth stages
Available K2O declined progressively across all treatments from 30
DAS to harvest, reflecting continuous crop removal (Table 3). At ev-
ery stage, T5, T6 and T7 (elevated-K treatments) recorded signifi-
cantly higher available K2O than T2, T3 and T4, which in turn were
significantly superior to the control. At harvest, T5 (296.16 kg ha−1),
T6 (291.18 kg ha−1) and T7 (289.24 kg ha−1) remained statistically on
par, indicating that raising the base K level maintained a more robust
residual K pool than magnesium sulphate addition alone. The mar-
ginal decline in K2O with increasing MgSO4 dose within a K level
(e.g., T5 > T6 > T7) is consistent with K–Mg competitive uptake at
root exchange sites, whereby improved Mg availability enhances
plant K uptake efficiency and translocation, drawing down soil K
slightly faster. These findings are in accordance with those reported
by Patil and Basavaraja (2017) [11], Marschner (2012) [9] and Havlin
et al. (2014) [10], who reported that higher potassium application in-
creases exchangeable soil potassium, whereas balanced nutrition and
greater crop productivity lead to higher potassium removal, thereby
reducing residual potassium in the soil after harvest.

Exchangeable calcium and magnesium
Exchangeable Ca did not differ significantly among treatments at any
growth stage, though it showed a numerically declining trend with
higher K and Mg supply (T7 lowest at harvest: 1.14 cmol(p+) kg−1 vs.
T2 highest: 1.38), consistent with cationic competition suppressing Ca
retention on exchange sites under intensive K–Mg fertilization.
Exchangeable Mg, in contrast, was significantly and consistently im-
proved by magnesium sulphate application at all stages (Table 3). At
harvest, T7 recorded the highest exchangeable Mg (0.85 cmol(p+)
kg−1), on par with T4 (0.83) and T6 (0.78), and significantly superior
to T3, T5, T2 and the control (T1: 0.59). This confirms the direct and
sustained contribution of applied MgSO4 to the soil-exchangeable Mg
pool, with the effect scaling with dose. These findings corroborate
those of Ding et al. (2006) [12] and Mengel and Kirkby (2001) [13],
who reported that magnesium fertilization increases exchangeable soil
magnesium, while crop uptake causes a gradual decline during the
growing season.

Available sulphur
Available S was significantly influenced throughout the crop period,
declining from 30 DAS to harvest in all treatments (Table 5). T7
recorded the highest available S at every stage (20.52, 20.14, 19.71
and 19.68 mg kg−1), on par with T4, T6 and T3, and significantly su-
perior to T2 and T5 (the non-MgSO4 treatments), which remained on
par with each other. This directly reflects the S contribution of mag-
nesium sulphate as a fertilizer source, independent of the K level ap-
plied. Treatments receiving magnesium sulphate maintained higher
residual sulphur throughout the crop growth period, indicating sus-
tained sulphur availability following fertilizer application. Similar
findings were reported by Tiwari (2002) [14] regarding increased soil
available sulphur following magnesium sulphate application and its
gradual depletion due to crop uptake.

Micronutrient status
DTPA-extractable Zn, Cu, Fe and Mn in post-harvest soil were not
significantly affected by treatment (Table 6), though all four showed a
consistent numerical peak under T4 and T7 — the MgSO4-supple-
mented treatments — and the lowest values under the control. This
marginal enrichment is plausibly linked to the sulphur supplied
through MgSO4, which can favour micronutrient availability by
maintaining a more reduced soil chemical environment, though the
effect did not reach statistical significance within this trial. Similar
observations were reported by Havlin et al. (2014) [10] and Alloway
(2008) [15].

Conclusion
Application of potassium and magnesium sulphate did not signifi-
cantly alter soil pH, EC, organic carbon or DTPA-extractable mi-
cronutrients, indicating that these treatments are compatible with the
inherent physico-chemical buffering of the soil. However, magnesium
sulphate application, particularly at 20 kg ha−1 combined with the ele-
vated potassium level (T7), significantly improved exchangeable
magnesium and available sulphur status, while elevated potassium
levels (T5–T7) sustained higher residual available K2O than the rec-
ommended package of practices. These findings support the com-
bined application of potassium and magnesium sulphate — specifi-
cally 100 kg N, 50 kg P2O5, 50 kg K2O ha−1 with MgSO4 @ 20 kg
ha−1 — as a nutrient management strategy that improves soil sec-
ondary-nutrient status without compromising major soil properties
under maize in the Southern Transition Zone of Karnataka.
