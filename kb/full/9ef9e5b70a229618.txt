Abstract
Burning of fossil fuels in the form of coal or gasoline in thermal power plants, industries, and automobiles is a prime source 
of nitrogen dioxide (NO2), a major air pollutant causing health problems. In this paper, spatio-temporal unevenness of NO2 
concentrations via both spaceborne Sentinel-5P and ground-based in situ data have been studied for the period of 2017–2021. 
Annual and seasonal distribution of TROPOMI-NO2 depict consistency over the Jharkhand region, highlighting six hotspot 
regions. As compared to 2019, a notable dip of 11% in the spatial annual average TROPOMI-NO 2 was achieved in 2020, 
which were elevated again by 22% in 2021 as the lockdown gradually goes out of the picture. Among eight ground-monitoring 
stations, Tata and Golmuri stations always displayed a higher level of TROPOMI-NO 2 ranges up to 15.2 ×10 15molecules.
cm−2 and 16.9 ×1015molecules.cm−2 respectively, as being located in the highly industrialised district of Jamshedpur. A big 
percentage reduction of up to 30% in TROPOMI-NO2 has been reported in Jharia and Bastacola stations in Dhanbad in the 
lockdown phase of 2020 compared to 2019. Good agreement between TROPOMI-NO2 and surface-NO2 has been achieved 
with R = 0.8 and R = 0.71 during winter and post-monsoon respectively. Among four meteorological parameters, TROPOMI-
NO2 was majorly found to be influenced by precipitation, having R = 0.6–0.8 for almost all stations. More advanced satellite 
algorithms and ground-based data may be used to estimate NO2 in places where monitoring facilities are limited and thus 
can help in air pollution control policy.

Introduction
Pollution is a big threat to human existence, air pollution 
in particular claims around seven million human lives and 
poses a potential threat in long-term irreversible health risks 
(WHO, 2020). The South Asian countries such as China 
and India account for 36.5% of the world’s total population, 
which is at risk (UN, 2015). Ever increasing population cou-
pled with the fast-growing economy, rapid and unplanned 
steps towards urbanisation, socioeconomic development, and 
transportation has resulted in a remarkable rise in air pollu-
tion, especially in Northern India that are highly industrial -
ised and populated area.
Industrialisation, vehicular movement, and thermal power 
plants are identified as the significant anthropogenic con -
tributor to NOx emissions by numerous studies such as V. 
Singh et al. (2020) and Hilboll et al. (2017). Other sources of

 NOx emission studied by various authors are forest fires (Hil-
boll et al., 2017), lightning (IPCC, 2007), soil (Ghude et al., 
2010), solid biomass burning for heating and cooking, and 
stubble burning by farmers, to name a few. Nowadays, the 
study of oxides of nitrogen NOx (=NO+NO2) has fascinated 
the researchers owing to its role as a precursor in the genesis 
of tropospheric ozone (Monks et al., 2015). The presence 
of NOx in the troposphere has resulted in adverse health 
effects (World Health Organization, 2003; Schraufnagel 
et al., 2019) due to climate change (Lin et al., 2015), acid 
rain formation, and secondary aerosol formation as well. The 
variation in tropospheric NOx concentration is dependent 
upon the variation in seasons, climatic conditions, social 
status, and energy consumption (Zhou et al., 2012). In the 
Indian context, transportation is the major source of NOx 
emission followed by thermal power plants, industries, and 
biomass burning (Garg et al., 2001).
In December 2019, COVID-19 was declared as a highly 
contagious disease (Chen et al., 2020) and later declared as 
a pandemic in year 2020 by WHO (Zhu et al., 2020; WHO 
2020). The Government of India announced the COVID-
19-induced lockdown at the midnight on 24th March for 21 
days (25th March–14th April 2020) as phase 1, but as the 
situation was still not under control, extending it to May 
31, 2020, continued in four different phases. Owing to the 
COVID-19 lockdown, various restricted anthropogenic 
activities such as vehicular, industrial, and other commercial 
energy-consuming activities (Jain & Sharma, 2020) nega-
tively affected the country’s social and economic growth, 
on the contrary, on pollution front, it positively affected 
the country, by lifting-up the air and water quality, reduc-
ing noise level and land surface temperature too (Mandal 
& Pal, 2020). Some recent studies adopting satellite and 
in situ–based observations have disclosed that a significant 
drop in NO2 concentrations happened during the lockdown 
phase across the globe.
Ground observation data in Bangalore showed a 87% 
drop in the NO2 level (Navinya et al., 2020) and 52.68% in 
New Delhi (Mahato et al., 2020). An identical finding was 
observed by Xu et al. (2020) for three major cities of China 
for NO2 concentrations with a decrease of 35.6% in Febru-
ary and March 2020 analysed against the same period during 
2017–2019, whereas an 18% decline in NO2 concentration 
was all over India (S. Sharma et al., 2020b). The decrease in 
 NO2 concentration during this time was also observed in the 
USA (Berman & Ebisu, 2020) and two major cities of Spain 
(Madrid and Barcelona) (Baldasano, 2020). According to a 
study by Nguyen et al. (2022), a reduction of 75.8, 55.9, 21.4, 
and 60.7% for NO2, PM2.5, O3, and SO2 concentrations respec-
tively was found in Hanoi, Vietnam. Filonchyk and Peterson 
(2020) also identified a drop of 9%, 77%, 31.3%, 60.4%, and 
3% for PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, and CO respectively through 
the lockdown period analysed against the same period in 2019.
Satellite observation has also played a crucial role in 
assessing tropospheric NO2 concentration as tropospheric 
vertical column density (TVCD). Cao and Han (2022), 
using OMI-NO2 data, observed that owing to the COVID-
19 epidemic, a momentary disruption in socio-economic 
affairs is observed in China. While comparing the findings 
for the whole of India (S. Sharma et al., 2020b) deploying 
OMI tropospheric-NO2 (Biswal et al., 2020), the results are 
comparative. The decreasing trend in NO2 concentration 
was observed along with SO2, PM2.5, and PM10 in seven 
cities of Rajasthan because of the lockdown (Sharma et al., 
2020a, b). Biswal et al. (2021) observed a NO2 reduc-
tion of approx. 20–40% in urban and approx. 15–25% in 
rural regions of India using satellite data for the period 25 
March–3 May 2020.
Filonchyk et al. (2021) also identified a stark change in 
Poland’s air quality during lockdown as analysed against the 
same period in 2018 and 2019. Whereas, Hoang et al. (2021) 
came up with a detailed analysis of NO2 concentrations cov-
ering most part of the world and the impact of behavioural 
change among people owing through the lockdown. On the 
other hand, Kumar et al. (2022) applied interpolation tech-
niques of ArcGIS for air quality mapping in Mumbai, India. 
In contrast to the decreasing trend in NO2 concentration 
worldwide, the continued trend in NO2 level in the plain of 
China was reported by Wang et al. (2020) and three cities 
(Johannesburg, Durban, and Cape Town) of South Africa 
(Matandirotya & Burger, 2022) which deny a linear correla-
tion between emission and pollution level.
Most of the recent studies have either been investigated 
for a few weeks or considered the few months of lockdown, 
pre-lockdown phases of the years 2019 and 2020. Authors 
have not come across any literature on understanding the 
 NO2 variability and its behaviour in post COVID-19 lock -
down year (2021) and their comparison with the COVID-
19-induced lockdown year (2020) and pre-COVID-19 lock-
down year (2019).
Considering its sources and deadly consequences on 
health and the environment as mentioned above, an attempt 
has been made to carry out exploratory, descriptive, and 
explanatory analysis towards our interested domain in a 
better way which is yet less explored, despite it being the 
3rd largest industrialised state of India. In this research, 
Jharkhand is explored in detail and is reported as it is and 
tried to explain further why things happened or why the situ-
ation exists with the support of studies that have been done.
Staying focussed on our target, research has been 
designed to analyse and interpret to present a clear picture 
of the spatial distribution of NO2 and to identify exact infor-
mation for its temporal variability in concentration, in the 
light of before pandemic–induced lockdown, during the 
lockdown, and after lockdown scenarios based on satellite 
observations and ground-based measurements as well. A key

Materials and methods
Study area
Jharkhand state (Fig.  1) was notified 28th state of India on 
15 November 2000, after being split from Bihar. It is the 
hub of industrial and mining activities, stretching vast from 
21°59′N to 25°18′N latitude and 82°52 ′E to 87°54′E longi-
tude in the Northern part of India. The state is spread in a 
total area of 79,714 km2. Being the resort of many miner -
als and the powerhouse for mining activities, Jharkhand is 
positioned as the 3rd largest industrialised state of India. 
It is landlocked by the other Indian states of West Bengal 
(east), Uttar Pradesh (north-west), Chhattisgarh (west), 
Bihar (north), and Odisha (south) (source: https:// www. 
jhark hand. gov. in/).
Data used
Three types of data from different agencies have been taken 
into account for this research to be carried out viz., 3 years 
(2019–2021) Sentinel-5p TROPOMI-NO2 data, surface-NO2 
data (2017–2021) and meteorological data (2019–2021). 
Explanations supporting the selection of the mentioned 
variables for our present discussion are the following. NO2 
is defined as a criteria pollutant and Jharkhand is loaded 
with all the active major sources that emit NO2, so, being 
an aware and responsible citizen, it is required to track its 
presence regularly for the betterment of our environment. 
Still, Jharkhand is lacking an adequate number of monitoring 
stations, so to compensate for that, the application of satel -
lite observation as TROPOMI-NO2 is carried out to monitor 
its concentration.
As the prevailing meteorological environment put its 
remarkable influence on the excellence of ambient air, which 
decides its lifetime in the atmosphere, it is regarded as one 
of the supreme drivers for deciding the goodness of air 
quality. As such, high winds dilute the pollutant towards the 
downwind direction whereas calm winds can stagnate pol -
lutants in a smaller area. Temperature and relative humidity 
are favourable meteorological parameters in the formation 
of secondary pollutants such as ozone and nitrate aerosols 
along with solar radiation and other chemicals present in the 
atmosphere. The longer lifetime of NO2 in the atmosphere is 
determined by lower temperatures which restrict the produc-
tion of OH and RO radicals whereas precipitation helps in 
lowering the pollution level through scavenging. Much more 
details of the data and its sources have been discussed below.
Satellite data
As of having advanced algorithms and improved sensitivity 
of Sentinel-5p regarding NO2 detection at the lower atmos-
phere, its NO2-tropospheric vertical column density data 
(TVCD) have been utilised for analysing and monitoring. On 
October 13, 2017, European Space Agency (ESA) launched 
Sentinel-5 Precursor, a satellite to track worldwide air pol -
lution using its onboard sensor named TROPOMI (tropo -
spheric monitoring instrument) (Potts et al., 2021). TRO-
POMI, a hyperspectral spectrometer, has the capabilities to 
sense ranging from ultraviolet to shortwave infrared por -
tions of electromagnetic spectrum (Veefkind et al., 2012). It 
measures the column concentrations of various atmospheric 
gaseous and particulate pollutants, e.g. CO, and NO2 (Zheng 
et al., 2019; Omrani et al., 2020) that encompass the full 
depth of the atmosphere.
The total columnar NO2 is retrieved using the TROPO -
MI’s spectrometer backscattered solar radiation which is the 
ratio of slant column density of NO2 and total air mass. The 
retrieval of the total NO2 column density was done using 
Level-1b radiance and irradiance spectra, measured by TRO-
POMI using the differential optical absorption spectroscopy 
(DOAS) approach (Platt & Stutz, 2008). Stratospheric and 
tropospheric NO2 column density is separated using data 
assimilation system information based on the TM5-MP 
chemical transport model.
It is followed by the conversion of the tropospheric and 
stratospheric slant column densities into vertical column 
densities. The conversion is carried out by multiplying it by 
an appropriate air mass factor which depends on altitude and 
data on the vertical distribution of NO2 from the TM5-MP 
chemical transport model. Owing to global coverage with 
temporal resolution for 1 day, and spatial resolution of 0.01° 
(Cooper et al., 2022), Prakash et al., 2021, this data helped in 
interpreting major pollutants over large Indian cities through 
the COVID-19 lockdown and found a decrease in concentra-
tions specifically for NO2 and SO2. For a better performance 
and bigger span of study than the near real-time (NRTI) 
assets, offline (OFFL) Sentinel-5P TROPOMI-NO 2 prod-
ucts have been retrieved for the present analysis (Soleimany 
et al., 2021). Subsequently, to identify its variation on three 
different time scales, annual, seasonal and monthly TRO -
POMI-NO2 products were extracted and downloaded using 
an open-source cloud-based platform Google Earth Engine

In situ NO2 data
Assessment and analysis of NO2 variation were carried out 
with the aid of Surface-NO 2 data for 8 ground-monitoring 
stations (Table S1) has been accessed from the Jharkhand 
State Pollution Control Board (JSPCB) office for the period 
of 2017 to 2021. Of these eight selected ground-monitoring 
stations, 5 are in Dhanbad, 2 in Jamshedpur, and 1 in Ranchi, 
which are highly urbanised and industrialised districts and 
are represented separately in their respective shapefiles in 
(Fig. 1) for a visual depiction of this distribution. Ground-
based data was collected from Jharkhand State Pollution 
Control Board (JSPCB); a total of 104 samples were col -
lected in a year by the agency, on a 24-h basis, twice a week. 
Among eight monitoring stations (of JSPCB), Golmuri [J], 
Kusunda [D], Bastacola [D], Jharia [D], and Sindri [D] 
belong to the industrial area whereas Albert Ekka [R] repre-
sents a blend of commercial and residential locales, Tata [J] 
again is a combination of commercial and residential areas 
whereas City Plaza [D] is a residential area only.
Meteorological data
Temperature(°C), wind speed (m/s), relative humidity (%), 
and precipitation (mm/day) were considered meteorological 
parameters for the period of 2019–2021 on a mean monthly 
basis from the open-source NASA website (source: https:// 
power. larc. nasa. gov/ data- access- viewer/), to perform an 
interpretation regarding its effect on TROPOMI-NO2 behav-
iour for eight NO2 ground-monitoring stations during the 
study period.
Data‑pre‑processing method
The steps involved in data acquiring and processing are sum-
marised in a simple flow diagram (Fig.  2). Starting with 
annual, seasonal, and monthly mean TROPOMI-NO 2 data, 
they were downloaded and processed for the period of 2019 
to 2021 using GEE (Gorelick et al., 2017). The researchers 
find the mean value of the desired parameter for the required 
time zone and region of study, process data using the code 
editor tab on the GEE website, and finally export and display 
them in that order. For this purpose, we used the GEE web-
site, where code has been written in the code editor tab by 
the researchers for data processing, which sets out finding 
the mean value of the desired parameter for the required 
time zone and region studied, and finally for displaying and 
exporting them accordingly.
For the post GEE processing of downloaded images 
such as reprojection into WGS 1984, unit conversion and 
clipping, ArcGIS software (ver. 10.4) was used and finally 
mapped into the presentable mode for further analysis. 
Thereafter, the collected mean surface-NO2 and mean mete-
orological data for their respective stations were processed 
and arranged accordingly to get it into the desired form for 
spatial and temporal collocation with mean TROPOMI-NO2 
data. At the end, GPS locations of all 8 monitoring stations 
were used to extract the TROPOMI-NO 2 value for those 
stations out of processed and finalised maps for carrying out 
further analysis and interpretation using a statistical tool for 
a graphical representation.

The assessment of spatiotemporal analysis of NO2 concen-

Month‑wise spatial distribution of TROPOMI‑NO2 
over Jharkhand
To assess the monthly pattern in TROPOMI-NO 2 level, 
monthly mean satellite data were taken into consideration for 
the year 2019 to 2021. The COVID-induced lockdown period 
(2020) exhibited lower spatial monthly mean TROPOMI-NO2 
throughout 12 months as compared with the same period 
for the other two preceding and successive years (2019 and 
2021). As discussed earlier, this decline in TROPOMI-NO2 
during the COVID-induced lockdown period (2020) is mainly 
contributed by social and travel constraints which catalysed 
the closing down of all the businesses that take in industries, 
mining, restaurants, transport, markets, tourism, construction, 
etc., which effectively contributed to reduced NO2 emission, 
predefined essential services being exempted.
The monthly variation of spatial mean TROPOMI-NO 2 
for the whole Jharkhand is represented in Fig. S2, which pro-
nounced the presence of two peaks during the study period: 
one in June (2019) and the other in March (2021) having 2.88 
×1015molecules.cm−2 and 3.56 ×1015molecules.cm−2 TRO-
POMI-NO2 levels respectively. Whereas no such peak has 
been captured during the COVID-19 year. These two peaks 
contribute 11.09% and 12.60% of the total in the correspond-
ing years 2019 and 2020 as represented in Fig. S3. It could be 
assigned to the cumulative effect of the meteorological con-
dition and an increased traffic movement post summer vaca-
tion. Variation in columnar NO2 level due to meteorology is 
reported by Goldberg et al. ( 2020) which has been studied 
in the spring of 2019 and 2020 for the USA. The reasons for 
the existence of the peak in March 2020 was the holiday for 
the festival Holi and after the gradual end of a long period of 
COVID-19-induced lockdown, the over-excited public started 
their movement to their workplace and other locations.
Monthly NO2 changes (Fig. S2) highlight that spatial aver-
age TROPOMI-NO2 are always found to be high all through 
the months of the post-COVID-induced lockdown year (2021) 
except June as compared to the same months of the other two 
considered years 2019 and 2020. It could be one of the reasons 
that the public was busy coping with COVID-19, as the situa-
tion was alarming, and hence, they got off the track to reduce 
air pollution. The heat map of monthly averaged spatial mean 
TROPOMI-NO2 for the whole of Jharkhand during 2019–2021 
is represented in Fig. S4, which is compared and examined 
using three colour scales. The red-coloured cells show high 
TROPOMI-NO2, which are most of the months of winter, pre-
monsoon, and post-monsoon, white-coloured cells represent 
moderate value whereas green-coloured cells depict low TRO-
POMI-NO2 which mainly captures the monsoon months of all 
the considered years.

Season‑wise spatial distribution of TROPOMI‑NO2 
over Jharkhand
Winter (Jan–Feb), pre-monsoon (Mar–May), monsoon 
(Jun–Sep), and post-monsoon (Oct–Dec) are the four sea-
sons classified by the Indian meteorological department 
(IMD) (source: https:// mausam. imd. go
v. in/
). TROPOMI-
NO2 maps averaged over the period 2019–2021 figure out 
the considerable seasonal characteristics of its spatio-tem -
poral spread, which are presented in Fig. S5a–d. Almost 
similar spatial patterns were observed in all the three seasons 
whereas a little lower TROPOMI-NO 2 was observed dur -
ing monsoon. Nowadays, air pollution is not only a winter 
event in the country. The monitored seasonality of TRO-
POMI-NO2 is found to be affiliated with the meteorological 
changes and seasonal cycles of anthropogenic emissions 
(Ghude et al., 2008). Among the four seasons, winter expe-
riences the highest spatial mean TROPOMI-NO 2 2.57 ± 
1.14 × 1015molecules.cm−2, followed by the pre-monsoon 
2.48 ± 0.81 × 1015 molecules.cm–2, post-monsoon 2.21 ± 
0.99 × 1015molecules.cm −2, and monsoon 1.83 ± 0.47 × 
 1015molecules. cm−2 shown in (Fig. S6).
Meteorological states in winter are additionally unfavour-
able for pollutant dispersion and dilution as compared to 
the other three seasons (Yang et al., 2019). An increase in 
adverse meteorology such as having a shallower planetary 
boundary layer (PBL), less UV radiations, and stable wind 
contribute towards a surge in the pollution level during win-
ter. Besides that, during winter, NO2 enhancement is also 
enriched by high biomass fuel consumption for heating 
homes (Ul-Haq et al., 2015), where OMI-tropospheric NO2 
over the South Asia regions were analysed during the past 
decade 2004–2015.
As per the Census of India 2011, 33 million is the popu-
lation of Jharkhand, out of which 76% of the people are 
living in rural areas and the remaining 24% in urban areas, 
whereas the literacy rate in Jharkhand state is stated around 
56–67.63% (Census, 2011). Hence, it helps to clarify that not 
many more people were aware of the harmful effects of bio-
mass burning for domestic heating. Relatively high values in 
TROPOMI-NO2 during pre-monsoon and post-monsoon are 
mainly accompanied by the release of NO2 from significant 
biomass and large-scale open-field burning of crop residue 
through the wheat-rice rotation period (Ul-Haq et al., 2015).
Agriculture is the foremost economic activity in 
Jharkhand by which about 63% of the rural population of 
the state is employed (International Institute For Population 
Sciences, 2016). During pre-monsoon, elevated TROPOMI-
NO2 are also partially added by intensified soil emissions 
owing to fertilisers being used in rice fields (Ul-Haq et al., 
2015)(Ghude et al., 2008). Low TROPOMI-NO2 during 
monsoon season is predominantly associated with excessive 
rainfall, strong winds, and elevated humidity. Prior studies 
have examined and found a remarkable negative correlation 
between NO2 and rainfall (Martin, 1984). A 3% decrease is 
noted during pre-monsoon in TROPOMI-NO2 as compared 
to their last winter season (Table S3). Similarly, a reduc-
tion of 26% and an increment of 21% have been observed 
during monsoon and post-monsoon respectively against 
their preceding seasons. The seasonal minimum and maxi-
mum spatial average TROPOMI-NO 2 level over the whole 
of Jharkhand averaged from 2019–2021 is summarised in 
Fig. S7. It has been observed that the winter exhibits the 
highest level of seasonal maximum TROPOMI-NO 2 (17.2 
×1015molecules.cm −2), and pre-monsoon shows the high -
est level of seasonal minimum TROPOMI-NO2 value (1.64 
×1015molecules.cm−2), whereas monsoon exhibits the low-
est level of both seasonal minimum and maximum TRO-
POMI-NO2 values having 1.05 ×10 15molecules.cm −2 and 
7.28 ×1015molecules.cm−2 respectively.
In India, the maximum and minimum NO2 level is 
reported during winter to pre-monsoon and monsoon 
respectively (Biswal et al., 2021). For the four seasons, 
a range of TROPOMI-NO 2 follows the order winter 
(15.8 × 1015molecules.cm −2)> post-monsoon (15.4 × 
 1015molecules.cm−2) > pre-monsoon (10.6 × 1015molecules.
cm−2) > monsoon (6.23 ×1015molecules.cm−2) (Table S3). 
The frequency distribution of pixels that are loaded with 
seasonal mean TROPOMI-NO2 for the four seasons winter, 
pre-monsoon, monsoon, and post-monsoon averaged from 
2019 to 2021 in Jharkhand is depicted in Fig. S8 as the histo-
gram plot. For all the four seasons, TROPOMI-NO2 appears 
to be well-distributed and unimodal and is also found to 
be positively skewed. The overall count of pixels having 
low TROPOMI-NO 2 value is always observed to be large 
in number as compared to the count of pixels having high 
TROPOMI-NO2 value, which clearly suggests that the whole 
of Jharkhand is mainly covered with the low TROPOMI-
NO2 pixels, and the burden of high TROPOMI-NO 2 is in 
some specific regions, which are captured and surrounded by 
the major industrialised and TPP locations such as Dhanbad 
and Jamshedpur (Bandyopadhyay et al., 2021). NO2 is acidic 
and a highly reactive gas, so its lifetime is perceived to be 
short. That is why TROPOMI-NO2 maps tell us only where 
does the NO2 emission occur and not much about where

Evaluation of Relative difference in TROPOMI‑NO2

The study is further extended by adding analysis and obser-

that the COVID-19-induced lockdown not only contributed 
positively to reducing the TROPOMI-NO 2 level in major 
urban or industrial districts but also blessed other rural set -
tlements too. It is attributed to the strict enforcement of con-
trol measures in Jharkhand in 2020 following the COVID-19

spreading of the COVID-19 virus.
A drop of 20–40% in NO2 level was observed in all major

Analysis of  NO2 concentrations at point stations 
with the aid of a satellite approach
Month-on-month differences in TROPOMI-NO2 level at all 
eight ground-monitoring stations in Jharkhand are illustrated 
in Fig. S10. In order to achieve this target, to start with, 
the GPS location of all eight considered JSPCB monitoring

dataset. Thereafter, with the aid of identify tool in ArcGIS 
software (ver. 10.4), the TROPOMI-NO 2 value is gathered 
for that particular GPS location which lies on their respec -
tive pixel of the TROPOMI-NO 2 raster dataset. However, 
the magnitude of temporal variation in NO2 concentration 
is found to be different at all eight monitoring stations. The 
station-wise temporal variation of TROPOMI-NO2 suggests 
that two monitoring stations Golmuri and Tata situated in 
the Jamshedpur district always exhibit a higher TROPOMI-
NO2 level as compared to the other six stations in Jharkhand. 
Both the stations follow almost the same pattern of varia -
tion, even though having a difference in the environment of 
adjoining areas. In the same way, the rest of the five stations 
Kusunda, Bastacola, Jharia, City Plaza, and Sindri located 
in Dhanbad display a nearly identical pattern of variation, 
despite having dissimilar territorial compositions.
These two districts are packed with all major industries, 
thermal power plants, and mining activities (Bandyopad -
hyay et al., 2021), which emit NO2 into the atmosphere on 
a larger scale. Among all stations, Albert Ekka always dis -
played lower TROPOMI-NO2 throughout the study period. 
This station-wise variation in NO2 level may also be influ -
enced by the level of effectiveness of pollution control 
measures enforced across local stations in Jharkhand. On 
one hand, during the concurrent COVID-19 year (2020), 
the TROPOMI-NO2 levels observed were 4.94, 5.06, 5.20, 
4.52, 4.62, 6.35, 9.14, and 1.84 (×1015molecules.cm−2) dur-
ing March month and 3.91, 4.07, 4.09, 3.55, 4.30, 4.17, 5.86, 
and 1.86 (×1015molecules.cm−2) during April month at City 
Plaza, Bastacola, Kusunda, Jharia, Sindri, Tata, Golmuri, 
and Albert Ekka respectively. On the other hand, in the pre-
COVID year (2019), the TROPOMI-NO2 level was found to 
be 6.02, 6.17, 5.77, 5.33, 6.29, 8.03, 10.8, 2.43 (×10 15mol-
ecules.cm−2) during March month and 5.42, 5.50, 5.54, 4.96, 
5.27, 5.27, 6.92, and 2.51 (×10 15molecules.cm −2) during

Whereas, in the post-COVID year (2021), the TROPOMI-

and 2.37 (×1015molecules.cm−2) during April month at the 
above-mentioned stations respectively.

Analysis of  NO2 concentrations at point stations via in situ 
measurement
For the confirmation of these satellite observations as 
described in the earlier section (Analysis of NO2 concen -
trations at point stations with the aid of satellite approach), 
to quantify the observation near the surface as well, ground-
based measurements of trace gas NO2 are also tracked. 
Based on data availability, month-wise variability of ground-
NO2 profiles at three different monitoring stations Tata, 
Golmurim and Albert Ekka are shown in Fig. S12 for con-
sidered years 2017–2020. The monthly mean surface-NO 2 
data have been generated by averaging the available daily 
mean surface-NO2 data for their respective monitoring sta-
tion. Ground observation also highlighted the same scenario 
of variation for stations Golmuri and Tata that are located 
in heavily industrialised district of Jamshedpur, despite the 
dissimilar surrounding environment.
Ground-NO 2 concentrations are observed to be in the 
range of 45–53 μg/m3 during most of the time at these two 
stations, whereas the occurrence of the higher value of 
ground-NO2 in Albert Ekka is very rare, and it reaches up to 
46.7 μg/m3 only once in the month of October 2019, during 
the study period 2017–2020. At this station (Albert Ekka), 
most of the time ground-NO2 concentrations are noted to be 
in the range of 35–38 μg/m 3. As highlighted in Figs.  3 and 
S5, two stations Golmuri and Tata are always found to be in 
the hotspot (higher concentration) region represented as red 
colour in the southern region of Jharkhand, whereas Albert 
Ekka station does not lie in the hotspot area. One major find-
ing is noticed here that a sharp decline of ground-NO2 at two 
monitoring stations based out of Jamshedpur occurred soon 
after the implementation of total lockdown as we compare it 
with the same time period of the preceding (2019) year. As 
viewed from the graphical representation, the reduction is 
seen from 48.18 to 22.41 μg/m3 and from 47.34 to 22.03 μg/
m3 for Tata and Golmuri respectively from March to April 
of the COVID-induced lockdown year (2020). A reduction 
of such magnitude happens to be lower than the NO2 level in 
the rainy seasons of all three studied years. It shows that Tata 
and Golmuri stations witnessed a drastic decline in surface-
NO2, which may be called a historic event, not seen in the 
last two–three decades.
It may give us a sense that the COVID period acted as a 
short-term blessing for all from an air pollution perspective. 
As the COVID-19-induced lockdown had to come to an end, 
so does the improvement in air quality had to disappear. 
This huge difference in satellite- and ground-based meas -
urement of NO2 can be attributed to the fact that elevated 
 NO2 concentration were found to be present in and around 
the sources of emissions such as thermal regions and urban 
and industrial centres, whereas less NO2 is seen near the 
surface in a rural region and more in the upward atmosphere 
because of the gradual dilution and transportation due to 
meteorological factors. The observation made using both the 
approaches, i.e. ground-based monitoring and satellite-based 
interpretation, justifies the reduction in NO2 concentration 
during lockdown and thus strengthens the findings of this 
study.
Meteorological variation along with their 
relationship with TROPOMI‑NO2 and detection 
of relative difference
The analyses of the pollution level of ambient air are found to 
be complicated due to meteorological influences. It plays an 
important part in air pollution formation, as well as its trans-
portation, accumulation, and transformation too. An unfavour-
able meteorological situation could accentuate extreme pollu-
tion events despite a drop in total emission (Wang et al., 2019). 
Hence, its role is significant while determining air pollution 
levels. This section examines dependencies and the relation of 
monthly average TROPOMI-NO2 with the four monthly aver-
age meteorological parameters, precipitation, temperature, rela-
tive humidity, and wind speed, which were carried out for all 
eight monitoring stations for the studied period 2019–2021. The 
captured correlation was found to be remarkable having moder-
ate to strong relation in multiple cases (Table 1). Among all the 
four parameters, precipitation was found to be influenced more 
by having a strong negative correlation with TROPOMI-NO2 
(R= 0.6–0.8), which depicts that as the precipitation decreases, 
TROPOMI-NO2 level increases, and vice-versa.
Along with the considered meteorological parameters, 
the time series analysis of TROPOMI-NO2 at eight stations 
is graphically depicted in Fig.  4a–h. In the time series plot, 
when the observed meteorological parameters and TRO -
POMI-NO2 level during the COVID-19-induced lockdown 
period were analysed against the pre- and post-COVID-
19-induced lockdown period, it would be observed that 
both kept on changing and fluctuating according to their 
relationship seen in Table  1. As such, at Golmuri station, 
among all the four considered meteorological factors, pre-
cipitation appeared to be strongly negatively correlated with 
TROPOMI-NO 2 having R = 0.8. The time series analysis 
at Golmuri station clearly showed that in March month of 
2020, precipitation was in the range of 2.22 mm/day, which 
is the less optimal condition for the dispersion of air pol-
lutants, and also, TROPOMI-NO 2 was elevated (Fig.  4b). 
However, soon after the lockdown (24th March 2020), in 
April month, precipitation was in the range of 3.49 mm/day, 
which are a comparatively more favourable environment for 
a much better dispersion of air pollutants in the atmosphere.

As we all know, 7 months in 2020 have gone through 
the pre-lockdown (1st January 2020–23rd March 2020), 
lockdown (24th March 2020–31st May), and unlock 
phases (1st June–31st July), which have been considered 
here for performing relative difference analysis by consid -
ering the same period in 2019. A clear view of TROPOMI-
NO2 levels during all the considered time frames for 2019 
and 2020 and the existing relative difference over eight 
monitoring stations are summarised in Fig. S13a–h. The 
interpretation demonstrated that there exists a massive 
percentage drop in TROPOMI-NO 2 at all eight consid -
ered stations in 2020 compared to 2019. These percentage 
declines are different and varying for all stations, but the 
maximum decline seen is up to 28–30% and 26–30% found 
in the Jharia and Bastacola stations of Dhanbad respec -
tively, which is a heavily mined and industrialised city. 
During the unlock period in 2020 as weighed against the 
same period of 2019, a drop in TROPOMI-NO 2 was also 
found to be in the higher range of up to 45% and 46% in 
Jharia and Bastacola respectively.
These huge percentage declines during the unlock 
period are mainly attributed to the dominant influence of 
the rain washout as having a strong negative correlation up 
to 0.7–0.8 between TROPOMI-NO 2 and two meteorologi -
cal factor precipitation and R.H. These relations reveal that 
these two meteorological parameters have influenced sig -
nificantly in deciding the level of TROPOMI-NO 2 during 
unlock period in 2020.
The time series analysis presented in Fig. 4g clearly high-
lights that precipitation was in the range of 3.29 mm/day and 
7.91 mm/day whereas R.H was in the range of 54.88% and 
75.88% during the June month of 2019 and 2020 respec -
tively in Jharia. On the contrary, for April, precipitation was 
in the range of 1.55 mm/day and 2.26 mm/day whereas R.H 
was in the range of 39.62% and 48.62% in 2019 and 2020 
respectively at Jharia. In a similar way, for the May month, 
precipitation was in the range of 2.68 mm/day and 3.98 mm/
day whereas R.H was in the range of 37.75% and 54.5% in 
2019 and 2020 respectively in Jharia. It clearly indicates 
that not much differences exist in meteorological parameters 
during the lockdown period. But there are vast differences in 
meteorological parameters while having unlock period. With 
that note, it is inferred that during the assessment period 
of complete lockdown, the observed percentage decline 
in TROPOMI-NO 2 is found to be directly related to and 
affected by the reduced human activities and industrial pro-
duction. The present outcome is found to be consistent with 
the changes highlighted by Biswal et al. (2020), where they 
noticed a decrease of 20.9% in Jharkhand and in the range of 
up to 6.9 to 65.9% in other 27 states while also observed an 
increment of up to 3.7 to 39.1% in the remaining seven states 
of India, as compared with TROPOMI-NO 2 concentration 
during 2020 and pre-lockdown period (2019).
Assessment of TROPOMI‑NO2
TROPOMI-NO 2 is the columnar data that represents the 
density of NO2 from the surface of the earth to the top of 
the troposphere or to the tropopause. To find out the consist-
ency between satellite observations and surface observation 
of NO2, research was carried out through the assessment and 
evaluation of Sentinel-5p. Further process was conducted by 
sorting the monthly mean surface-NO2 data and then deriv-
ing seasonal data for the considered monitoring stations. 
The pronounced relationship between surface-NO 2 and 
TROPOMI-NO2 is depicted graphically in Fig. 5a–d, which 
revealed that the model performance was found to be better 
during winter and post-monsoon compared to pre-monsoon 
and monsoon. In winter and post-monsoon seasons, good 
agreement is obtained between them with an R= 0.8, R2 = 
0.71 ( p<0.01), and R= 0.71, R2 = 0.50 ( p<0.01) respec -
tively, but huge discrepancies are seen between them dur -
ing the pre-monsoon and monsoon, with the assessment of 
correlation R= 0.45, R2 = 0.20 (p<0.01), and R= 0.16, R2 = 
0.03 (p>0.05) respectively. This is done to appraise whether 
the seasonal variability in the column emulates variability 
in the surface concentrations. The reason for not having an 
appreciable correlation during monsoon and pre-monsoon 
could be because of a reduction in the signal-to-noise ratio, 
possibly found to be associated with the drop in NO2 con-
centration due to scavenging in monsoon (Pawar et al., 2017) 
and due to formation of secondary pollutants during pre-
monsoon season (ul-Haq et al., 2018; Pawar et al., 2017).
Present outcomes are in tandem with similar work using 
the Sentinel-5p columnar tropospheric NO2 and surface-NO2 
with the good and strong correlation (R = 0.7) between them 
(Bassani et al., 2021; Biswal et al., 2021) where they con -
sidered the lockdown phase for both years 2019 and 2020 
for analysis in Rome and India respectively. Line plot and 
comparison highlight that surface-NO2 shows trends identi-
cal to TROPOMI-NO 2 collected from the same location, 
during the winter season, and average for before and after 
the monsoon, whereas poor in monsoon Fig. S14a–d. Thus, 
it gives much confidence that TROPOMI-NO2 can be a use-
ful tool for monitoring surface-NO2 in these two seasons.

Conclusions
One of the consequences of air pollution happened to be 
a global health crisis, which needs to be resolved at the 
earliest. The present research aimed at studying the impact 
of COVID-19-induced lockdown in terms of spatial and 
temporal variation of TROPOMI-NO2 and ground NO2 con-
centration in Jharkhand. It is concluded that a pronounced 
decline of 11% in TROPOMI-NO 2 has been identified 
in Jharkhand during the lockdown period. Among eight 
ground-monitoring stations, Jharia and Bastacola in Dhan -
bad experienced an enormous drop of up to 30% in TRO -
POMI-NO2 during lockdown phase 2020 compared to 2019. 
This study demonstrated very well the role of understanding 
the contribution of meteorological conditions when design-
ing emission control strategies. As in this case, it is found 
to be inversely proportional throughout the considered sta -
tions having R= 0.4–0.7, 0.4–0.7, 0.2–0.7, and 0.6–0.8 for 
temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, and precipita -
tion respectively.
Assessment of satellite observations against ground-based 
appraisal is noted to be in harmony with R = 0.8 and R = 
0.71 during winter and post-monsoon respectively. Analyses 
found a tremendous fall in surface-NO2 at Tata and Golmuri 
stations from 48.18 to 22.41 μg/m3 and from 47.34 to 22.03 
μg/m3 respectively from March to April month of lockdown 
year (2020), which is always abundant with highly polluted 
air. At the district level, reduction ranges from 4 to 16% in 
2020 compared to 2019 and staging a revert, ranging from 
10 to 34% in 2021 as compared to 2020. Overall examina -
tion conducted via both satellite and ground observations 
highlighted that Jharkhand definitely has gone through the 
experience of remarkable improvement in NO2 level during 
the COVID-19-led lockdown period.
The improved algorithm and more precise study with 
more ground-monitoring points will help in establishing 
a strong relationship between satellite data and ground-
level NO2 which will help in estimating NO2 concentration 
in remote locations more precisely. The present outcomes 
could also aid in planning and implementing better air 
pollutant reduction strategies and upgrading all required 
air quality measurement and prediction modelling for the 
betterment of the whole universe. The use of advanced 
technologies in industries especially thermal power plants 
and vehicles will reduce the NO2 emissions. The change 
in domestic fuels from biomass to cleaner fuel will further 
improve the overall air quality.
As we have gone through many more analysis and discus-
sions, owing to limitations, we could not broaden our span of 
study, parameters, and tools. Meteorological behaviour and 
its role on the level of TROPOMI-NO2 fluctuation have been 
analysed on sites located in the Central and North-eastern 
plateau and South-eastern plateau whereas similar analyses 
have not been done for the Western plateau. In upcoming 
research, interpretation and analysis may include a few more 
parameters such as boundary layer height or may apply some 
other approaches such as machine learning in order to extract 
profound insights about the studied domain. All the high -
lights may be targeted for future research to deal with the 
current situation of Jharkhand which is burdened with many 
industries and TPPs and a lot more in the pipeline.
