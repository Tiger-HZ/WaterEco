Abstract Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are persistent environmental contaminants widely detected in food and human serum, raising concerns about dominant exposure pathways in the general population. This study evaluated the relative contributions of major dietary and drinking-water pathways to PFAS exposure in the U.S. population using two complementary approaches. An intake-based model was developed by combining representative PFAS concentrations from published literature with dietary intake data from NHANES, and results were compared with regression-based estimates of contributions to variability in serum PFAS concentrations. The intake-based model estimated a total PFAS intake of approximately 24.7 ng/day in 2015–2020. Seafood was identified as the dominant exposure pathway, with shellfish and finfish contributing 30.2% and 19.2% of total intake, respectively. Drinking water accounted for approximately 24.4% of intake, while plant foods and dairy products contributed 11.0% and 7.9%, respectively. Meat and eggs contributed minimally. Regression-based analyses similarly identified seafood as a major contributor to variability in serum PFAS concentrations, although differences were observed for other pathways. Drinking water contributed relatively little to variability despite its measurable contribution to total intake, whereas plant foods contributed disproportionately to variability relative to their intake contribution. These findings highlight that pathways contributing to total exposure do not necessarily explain variability in serum PFAS concentrations and demonstrate the value of integrating intake-based and biomonitoring approaches. The consistent identification of seafood as a dominant pathway across both approaches provides increased confidence in its importance as a major source of PFAS exposure in the U.S. population.
