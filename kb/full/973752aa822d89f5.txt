Abstract
Wireless Body Area Networks (WBANs) must provide reliable, low-latency, and 
energy-efficient communication despite rapid changes in physiological urgency, 
body-induced channel variation, and heterogeneous sensor quality. Existing adaptive-
rate and QoS-aware schemes often optimize only a subset of these factors and 
may induce unstable rate switching when link or physiological states fluctuate. This 
study proposes HALA-DR, a health- and link-aware adaptive data-rate mechanism 
that jointly integrates physiological urgency, recent link quality, residual energy, 
and node quality. A four-level controller with an emergency override and hysteresis 
maps the fused state to low, medium, high, or emergency transmission rates. The 
per-interval time complexity is O(NM), which reduces to O(N) when the number 
of monitored physiological variables M is fixed. A reproducible MATLAB evaluation 
was conducted using an IEEE 802.15.6-compatible star topology, identical baseline 
settings, 30 independent runs, confidence intervals, and paired statistical tests. In the 
15-node scenario, HALA-DR achieved 236 kbps throughput, a 95.1% packet delivery 
ratio, 45 ms end-to-end latency, 0.94 mJ per successfully delivered packet, and 118 h 
network lifetime. Scalability, mobility, interference, and ablation analyses show that 
the link-quality term contributes most strongly to reliability and latency, whereas the 
energy term contributes most strongly to lifetime. These results indicate that HALA-
DR provides a reproducible and balanced improvement in WBAN communication 
efficiency and QoS.

 1 Introduction
Wireless Body Area Networks integrate wearable or implanted sensors with a body 
coordinator to support continuous physiological monitoring, remote healthcare, reha -
bilitation, and context-aware assistance [ 1]. Unlike conventional wireless sensor net -
works, a WBAN operates close to or inside the human body and must satisfy stringent

requirements for low power consumption, bounded latency, reliability, and coexistence 
[2, 3]. IEEE 802.15.6–2026 explicitly recognizes heterogeneous traffic, quality-of-ser -
vice requirements, extremely low-power operation, multiple data rates, body-induced 
antenna effects, and user motion as central design considerations [ 1]. These characteris-
tics make static transmission settings unsuitable for sustained operation under variable 
physiological and radio conditions.
A fixed-rate sensor transmits at the same rate during both stable and abnormal physi -
ological periods. A high fixed rate can reduce response time but wastes energy and 
channel capacity when signals are stable, whereas a low fixed rate conserves energy but 
may delay urgent information. Adaptive-rate control is therefore attractive, but the deci-
sion is challenging because physiological urgency, link quality, residual battery energy, 
and sensor reliability evolve on different time scales. A controller that reacts too strongly 
to a single noisy indicator may also oscillate between rates, increase control traffic, and 
shorten network lifetime.
Recent real-time WBAN monitoring has emphasized selective signal restoration and 
physiological trend prediction in resource-constrained health-monitoring systems [ 4]. 
Communication-focused research spans discrete utility-based rate adaptation [ 5], meta-
heuristic transmission-rate optimization [ 6], route-stability-aware communication [ 7], 
multi-path QoS routing [8], and learning-based or multi-objective routing [ 9, 10]. These 
studies show that adaptive communication can improve reliability or energy efficiency, 
but they operate at different decision layers and usually optimize only a subset of physi -
ological urgency, channel state, residual energy, sensor quality, and switching stability. 
A lightweight controller that directly couples these factors for sensor data-rate selection 
remains comparatively underexplored.
The research question is therefore: how can a body coordinator select a transmission 
rate that prioritizes urgent physiological data, remains robust to channel and mobility 
variation, protects battery life, and avoids unstable switching on resource-constrained 
WBAN devices? To address this question, we propose HALA-DR, a health- and link-
aware adaptive data-rate controller.
The main contributions are:
 • A normalized physiological-urgency function with explicit normal and emergency 
bounds, together with an emergency override that elevates critical traffic without 
requiring a computationally expensive classifier.
 • A transparent composite state score that jointly considers urgency, link quality, 
residual energy, and node quality, followed by four-level rate mapping and a 
hysteresis/dwell-time constraint that suppresses unnecessary switching.
 • A reproducible and fair evaluation protocol in which fixed-rate, DDRA, GABAT, 
and EPRS baselines are reconstructed under the same topology, channel, traffic, run 
duration, random seeds, and metrics; newer learning-based and link-adaptation 
methods are additionally positioned qualitatively because they optimize different 
decision layers.
 • A broader evaluation using standard WBAN indicators, repeated-run confidence 
intervals, scalability, mobility, interference, ablation, decision-time, communication-
overhead, and complexity analyses.

2 Related work
2.1 Adaptive transmission-rate control
Rate adaptation addresses the mismatch between time-varying network capacity and 
heterogeneous sensor streams. Szkaliczki [ 5] formulates discrete data-rate selection as 
a multiple-choice multidimensional knapsack problem and shows that a greedy strategy 
can approximate the optimum with short execution time. This treatment is useful when 
each stream has a finite set of quality levels, but physiological urgency and node health 
are not primary decision variables. Goyal et al. [ 6] combine genetic and BAT optimi -
zation to adjust transmission rates under QoS and posture-dependent link constraints; 
the approach improves optimization quality but requires iterative search and parameter 
tuning.
2.2 QoS-, route-, and energy-aware WBAN communication
QoS-aware routing complements rate control by selecting reliable forwarding paths. 
EPRS estimates route stability and residual energy to improve packet delivery and net -
work lifetime [ 7]. Mehmood et al. [ 8] distinguish normal and emergency traffic and 
reserve the best path for critical data. IM-QRP [9] focuses on QoS-aware next-hop selec-
tion, while Aryai et al. [ 10] combine metaheuristic optimization with machine learning 
for adaptive real-time routing. Cooperative fault-tolerant communication [ 11], lifetime-
oriented routing [ 12], ZITA [ 13], cooperative RF energy-harvesting transmission [ 14], 
relay-placement optimization [15], two-tier lightweight forwarding [ 16], multi-objective 
routing [ 17], and link-quality-aware clustering [ 18] similarly seek reliable and energy-
conscious delivery. These protocols establish the importance of traffic priority, link 
condition, and residual energy, but routing decisions are not equivalent to selecting a 
sensor’s transmission rate, and they do not directly address rate oscillation.
Recent WBAN research also addresses edge collection [ 19], compressed sensing and 
security [20, 21], encryption [ 22, 23], antennas [24], interference management [25], and 
congestion control [26]. Broader WBAN reviews and energy-efficient healthcare frame -
works [27, 28], together with Markov decision process-based inter-WBAN transmission 
schemes [ 29], further illustrate the diversity of communication objectives and deploy -
ment settings. More recent work extends adaptation toward learning and lower-layer 
control: Chen [ 30] shifts model-driven transmission-power control and link adapta -
tion to the hub, Arafat et al. [ 31] use Q-learning for QoS-aware routing, and Chen [ 32] 
applies Gaussian Thompson sampling to energy-efficient MCS selection under non-sta -
tionary channels. These methods strengthen the recent comparison landscape, but they 
optimize routing, transmit power, or PHY/MCS selection rather than application-level 
health-aware sensor data rates. Table 1 therefore distinguishes direct data-rate control 
from these adjacent decision layers. Only methods that can be reconstructed under the 
same topology, traffic, and metric definitions are used as quantitative baselines; newer

2.3 Research gap and positioning
Table 1 summarizes the closest methods. The remaining gap is narrower than the general 
problem of adaptive WBAN communication: application-facing sensor data-rate control 
still lacks a transparent, low-complexity mechanism that (i) treats physiological urgency 
as an explicit decision variable, (ii) combines urgency with measured link, energy, and 
sensor-health states, (iii) supports a fail-safe emergency override, (iv) suppresses ping-
pong switching through hysteresis and dwell time, and (v) is evaluated with repeated-
run statistical evidence under identical conditions. HALA-DR is designed to address this 
specific and testable gap rather than to replace routing or PHY-level link adaptation.
3 Proposed HALA-DR method
3.1 System model and assumptions
The WBAN contains N sensor nodes and one body coordinator. Each node measures 
one or more physiological variables and periodically reports data to the coordinator 
through an IEEE 802.15.6-compatible wireless link. The coordinator maintains recent 
link statistics, residual-energy reports, node-quality status, and configured urgency 
thresholds, and it selects one of four transmission rates for each node. Routine traffic 
uses the low or medium rate, elevated traffic uses the high rate when resources permit, 
and emergency traffic receives the maximum rate and priority access. Figure 1 shows the 
architecture and the location of the adaptive controller. Because the topology contains 
a single coordinator, the coordinator index j is fixed; after the distance definition below, 
the controller notation omits j and uses Li and Si for clarity.
The design assumes that the coordinator can obtain short-window RSSI, SNR, and 
packet-delivery statistics from acknowledgments and can receive periodic residual-
energy and sensor-health reports. Physiological thresholds are application-specific

3.5 Algorithm workflow and packet scheduling
Figure 2 summarizes the complete workflow. At each decision interval, the coordina -
tor acquires physiological, link, energy, and node-quality states; normalizes the required 
inputs; computes Ui, Li, Ei, and Qi; forms Si; selects a candidate rate; applies emergency, 
capacity, energy, hysteresis, and dwell-time constraints; schedules packets; and updates 
the short-window statistics after acknowledgments or timeouts. Emergency packets 
enter a strict-priority queue, whereas routine classes use weighted round-robin schedul -
ing to prevent starvation.

3.6 Computational complexity and communication overhead
Urgency estimation requires O(M) operations per node, whereas link, energy, quality, 
and rate mapping require constant time. The overall per-interval decision complexity is 
therefore O(NM), which becomes O(N) when the physiological feature set M is fixed. 
The coordinator stores a short state window for each node, giving O(NW) memory for 
history length W. The proposed packet format adds a 4-byte state report and a 1-byte 
rate command per decision interval. In the 15-node scenario, this corresponds to an 
estimated 1.8% control overhead for HALA-DR, lower than the multi-path signaling 
of EPRS but higher than that of a fixed-rate system. The overhead is reported explicitly 
rather than being absorbed into payload throughput.

3.7 Practical safety and security considerations
HALA-DR controls communication resources; it does not diagnose disease or replace 
medical alarms. Clinical thresholds must be configured and validated for the intended 
application. Rate-control messages should be authenticated to prevent false emergency 
escalation, battery-drain attacks, or forced rate suppression. Sensor failures are repre -
sented through Q i and timeout rules, but secure key management, privacy protection, 
and formal fail-safe behavior remain deployment requirements [20, 22].
4 Experimental methodology
4.1 Simulation environment and network configuration
The evaluation protocol was specified to support reproducibility. All methods use the 
same MATLAB release, topology, channel, traffic, energy model, simulation time, and 
random seeds. The body coordinator is located near the waist, and nodes are placed at 
anatomically plausible chest, arm, wrist, waist, finger, and ankle positions with small 
random perturbations. The main comparison uses 15 nodes, whereas scalability tests 
vary the network from 5 to 25 nodes. A 100 s warm-up precedes the 1200 s evaluation 
period and populates the 10 s state-history window before reported metrics are accumu-
lated. Table 3 lists the complete configuration.
4.2 Baselines and fairness controls
Four quantitative baselines are considered: (i) a fixed-rate controller using the medium 
rate; (ii) DDRA, reconstructed from the discrete utility-based adaptation strategy in [ 5]; 
(iii) GABAT, reconstructed from the QoS-supported optimized transmission-rate design 
in [ 6]; and (iv) EPRS, reconstructed as a route-stability-aware QoS baseline from [ 7].

4.3 Evaluation metrics
The primary metrics are defined as follows:
 • Throughput: successfully delivered payload bits divided by active evaluation time.
 • Packet delivery ratio (PDR): successfully delivered packets divided by generated 
packets.
 • Packet loss ratio (PLR): 1 − PDR.
 • End-to-end latency: time from packet generation to successful coordinator reception.
 • Energy per successfully delivered packet: total communication and decision energy 
divided by delivered packets.
 • Network lifetime: time until the first node exhausts its usable energy budget.
 • Control overhead: control bytes divided by total transmitted bytes.
 • Average decision time: coordinator execution time per decision interval.
The primary evaluation avoids custom composite indices such as Data Rate Adjustment 
Efficiency, Energy Consumption Efficiency, and a Robustness Index. Their intended 
concepts are represented more transparently by rate-switch behavior, energy per

4.4 Statistical analysis
For each scenario, the manuscript reports the mean and standard deviation across 30 
independent runs, and the figures show 95% confidence intervals. HALA-DR is com -
pared with the strongest baseline using a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test because 
normality cannot be assumed for bounded PDR and delay distributions. P values 
are adjusted with Holm’s procedure across the four primary metrics, and Hedges’ g is 
reported as an effect-size estimate. All statistical values were computed from the com -
pleted run-level simulation outputs.
5 Results and discussion
This section reports results from 30 independent simulation runs. Values are consistent 
across Figs. 3, 4 and 5; Tables 4, 5 and 6, and the confidence intervals, significance tests, 
and effect sizes were calculated from the corresponding run-level outputs.
5.1 Overall performance
HALA-DR achieves the highest throughput (236 kbps) and PDR (95.1%), while reducing 
mean latency to 45 ms and energy per successfully delivered packet to 0.94  mJ. Rela -
tive to EPRS, the strongest baseline, throughput increases by 7.3%, PDR increases by 
1.5% points, latency decreases by 13.5%, and energy per successfully delivered packet 
decreases by 8.7%. The magnitude of the gain is consistent with the fact that EPRS 
already incorporates route-stability and energy information.

5.2 Scalability, mobility, and interference robustness
All methods degrade as the network grows because contention, collisions, and queueing 
increase. At 25 nodes, HALA-DR retains a PDR of 91.8% and latency of 70 ms, com -

5.3 Ablation, network lifetime, and decision cost
Removing the link-quality term causes the largest degradation in reliability and latency: 
PDR falls from 95.1% to 90.8%, and latency rises from 45 to 57 ms. This result supports 
the claim that rate decisions should be constrained by measured channel conditions 
rather than by physiological urgency alone. Removing physiological urgency reduces 
PDR to 92.6% and weakens prioritization of critical packets, although the average deci -
sion time decreases because one feature group is omitted.
Removing the energy term has little effect on short-term PDR and latency but reduces 
network lifetime from 118 h to 96 h. This separation is important because a component 
can be essential for long-horizon sustainability even when its immediate packet-level 
effect is small. Removing hysteresis lowers decision time to 1.5 ms but also decreases 
PDR and increases latency because rapid rate switching generates transient queue -
ing and control costs. The full model therefore provides the best overall balance, with a 
modest 0.8 ms decision-time increase over fixed-rate operation.
5.4 Practical interpretation
The results support a cross-layer interpretation. Physiological urgency determines which 
information requires rapid delivery; link quality constrains what the channel can sustain; 
residual energy prevents short-term QoS gains from prematurely exhausting a sensor; 
node quality suppresses unreliable measurements; and hysteresis converts noisy state 
changes into stable control actions. The controller therefore does not maximize a single 
metric; it balances reliability, latency, energy use, and signaling overhead while preserv -
ing emergency escalation.
A practical implementation can place the decision logic at the body coordinator, 
avoiding continuous optimization on each sensor. Nodes need only report a compact 
state vector and accept rate commands. The measured 1.9 ms decision time is small rela-
tive to the 1 s decision interval, but embedded-hardware measurements are still required 
because MATLAB workstation timing may underestimate microcontroller execution 
and communication overhead.
5.5 Limitations and threats to validity
First, the evaluation is simulation-based; body shadowing, antenna detuning, coex -
istence, and hardware energy costs may differ from the assumed models. Second, the 
physiological thresholds are scenario configurations rather than clinically validated deci-
sion rules. Third, only a single-WBAN star topology is evaluated; inter-WBAN inter -
ference, relay operation, and implanted sensors require separate validation. Fourth, the 
weights and thresholds are fixed and may therefore be scenario-dependent; a systematic

sensitivity study across broader channel and physiological settings remains future work. 
Fifth, the comparison reconstructs baselines from published descriptions, and code-
level differences may remain when source implementations are unavailable.
Future work should therefore include an IEEE 802.15.6 hardware testbed, measure -
ments during controlled postures and motion, multi-WBAN coexistence, authenticated 
rate commands, sensor-failure injection, prospective calibration of urgency thresholds, a 
systematic weight/threshold sensitivity study, and public release of code, parameter files, 
random seeds, and run-level results. A constrained learning method may also adapt the 
fusion weights online, provided that interpretability and fail-safe emergency behavior 
are preserved.
6 Conclusion
This paper formulates adaptive WBAN communication as a joint health-, link-, energy-, 
and node-quality decision problem. HALA-DR uses a transparent fusion score, a four-
level rate set, an emergency override, and hysteresis to balance reliability, latency, energy 
use, and switching overhead. The methodology explicitly defines the controller variables, 
normalization rules, thresholds, complexity, packet overhead, baseline-fairness condi -
tions, simulation parameters, repeated-run protocol, confidence intervals, and statistical 
tests needed for reproducibility. The experimental results show consistent and statis -
tically supported improvements over fixed-rate, DDRA, GABAT, and EPRS baselines, 
with stronger benefits under larger networks, mobility, and interference. Ablation results 
attribute packet-level robustness mainly to link awareness and long-term lifetime mainly 
to energy awareness. Future work will extend the evaluation to embedded hardware, 
multi-WBAN coexistence, systematic parameter sensitivity, and clinically informed 
deployment scenarios.
