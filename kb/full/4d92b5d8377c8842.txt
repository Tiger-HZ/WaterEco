The characteristics of wastewater evolve according to changes in human lifestyles, with the presence of some compounds diminishing and that of others increasing; accordingly, the processes of wastewater treatment must be adaptable. The diversity of pollutants present in wastewaters and water bodies has increased significantly in recent years, with many compounds present in wastewater not yet widely reported, mainly because their low concentration prohibits quantification. Such compounds have started to appear in ecosystems, and in some cases, effects on aquatic flora and fauna have been observed. These pollutants are referred to as emerging pollutants (EPs) and include medicines (such as antibiotics, analgesics, antipyretics, hormones, and other drugs), personal care products, industrial chemical products, pesticides, etc.; the effects of many of these EPs on ecosystems are still unknown. This problem is exacerbated because many EPs cannot be removed or degraded via standard wastewater treatment methods (based on the use of lagoons that are either aerobic, anaerobic, or facultative), necessitating the addition of new steps to the treatment process focused on the elimination of such pollutants. Recent proposals include technologies using bacteria, fungi, plants, and, most recently, microalgae and cyanobacteria. Nevertheless, the implementation of any given technology is not easy because of the diversity of EPs. In this chapter, we analyze the current global status of EP pollution, briefly review the physicochemical elimination processes, and comprehensively review the biological options for EP removal, highlighting the microbial groups, the types of contaminants, and the removal efficiencies.
