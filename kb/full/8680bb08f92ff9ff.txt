Abstract
The present study employed environmental DNA (eDNA) metabarcoding of the metazoan community
composition and diversity in relation to water quality parameters from four major river estuaries of South
Sumatra, Indonesia (Sembilang, Banyuasin, Musi, and Air Salek). Surface water sampling was conducted
in September 2025; the mitochondrial COI gene was ampliﬁed using mlCOIintF/jgHCO2198 and
sequenced with Oxford Nanopore. In total, 26,300 sequence reads were obtained across eight taxonomic
classes: Malacostraca (54.4%), Actinopteri (27.2%), Bivalvia (10.9%), and Thecostraca (7.2%), with
contributions from Scyphozoa, Chondrichthyes, Merostomata, and Hydrozoa, all of which either
contributed < 1% or failed to be identiﬁed as a class-level hit. Banyuasin exhibited the highest read
abundance, extreme dominance by the mantis shrimp Odontodactylus cultrifer, and very low diversity
(Shannon H' = 1.35). Conversely, the community of Air Salek was the most balanced (H′=3.18) and had
the highest species richness (S = 58). PERMANOVA revealed signiﬁcant differences among stations
(pseudo-F = 3.24, p = 0.002), and Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances
explained 48.3% and 32.5% of variance, respectively. Simpson dominance was signiﬁcantly negatively
correlated with dissolved oxygen (r = -0.99) and salinity (r = -0.88), as demonstrated by Pearson
correlations. This species-level read abundance matrix is available as Supplementary Table S1. This
dataset, which we are revealing for the ﬁrst time, serves as a reproducible baseline that will facilitate
biomonitoring and conservation planning in tropical estuarine ecosystems by leveraging eDNA's
unprecedented ability to capture a wide range of biodiversity, extending well beyond ﬁsh.
1. Introduction
Estuaries are one of the most productive habitats on Earth, as they act as transitional zones between
rivers and the ocean, harboring high biodiversity and complex ecological interactions (Cowley et al.,
2022; Elliott et al., 2022; Whitﬁeld, 2021). In South Sumatra, Indonesia, the estuaries of four major rivers
(Musi, Banyuasin, Komering, and Ogan) are critical to local ﬁsheries (Pramilus et al., 2022; Sudarmo et
al., 2025), food security (Syuhada et al., 2020), and biogeochemical cycles (Diansyah et al., 2025).
Nevertheless, these ecosystems are subjected to anthropogenic pressures from land-use change,
pollution, and climate change (Agussalim et al., 2024; Melki et al., 2018a, 2018b, 2025; Wahyudi et al.,
2025), while their long-term sustainability is endangered by the overexploitation of resources (Wulandari
et al., 2024), which threatens not only the biodiversity within these estuaries but also the livelihoods of
local communities that depend on them.
The physical and chemical variables of estuaries are important drivers for spatiotemporal variation in
biodiversity and ecosystem functioning (Chen et al., 2025; Mestdagh et al., 2020). Primary productivity
(often measured as chlorophyll a) inﬂuences relations in food webs and is connected to nutrient loads,
turbidity, and hydrodynamic forces, which also inﬂuence habitat quality for different taxa (Agussalim et
al., 2024; Hu et al., 2021). These environmental baselines are therefore vital for identifying changes in
biological communities.
Page 3/27
Biodiversity surveys are mostly based on capture methods (e.g., nets, trawls), which suffer inherent
drawbacks—they can be selective and labor-intensive, from a taxonomic point of view trivial to complex
ones, and cannot easily sample very large or otherwise diﬃcult-to-access areas (Bacheler, 2025;
Fediajevaite et al., 2021). Recently, environmental DNA (eDNA) metabarcoding has developed into an
advanced, noninvasive technique that shows good taxonomic coverage and high sensitivity for cryptic or
rare species through detection of DNA released from organisms in the environment (Çevik & Çevik, 2025;
Miya, 2022; Yao et al., 2022). This method has transformed biodiversity monitoring in aquatic
ecosystems (Beng & Corlett, 2020; Fonseca et al., 2023; Gold et al., 2021; Peixoto et al., 2021).
Previous studies that have focused on ﬁsh communities in estuaries of South Sumatra relied exclusively
on conventional ﬁshing gear (gillnets, cast nets, and traps). From 2007 to 2023, the Musi Hilir River
experienced a serious loss of ﬁsh species. Data from Sudarmo et al. (2025) recorded a sharp decline
from 112 to only 23 species, or nearly a ﬁvefold decrease. In the ﬁrst study conducted in Banyuasin
Regency, 31 ﬁsh species were recorded (Hernawati et al., 2023), while the second investigated river
Sembilang, which had yielded a dominance of 27 marine taxa (78% species) in the estuary region
(Sellyndia et al., 2023). 33 ﬁsh species collected from the Sugihan River (Setiawan et al., 2019). However,
these studies focused on a limited scope of the eukaryotic community (e.g., crustaceans, bivalves,
jellyﬁsh, barnacles, and horseshoe crabs) that constitute major components of estuarine food webs and
ecosystem functioning. Additionally, traditional methods probably underestimate inconspicuous or
uncommon species and are largely ineffective in turbid or deep waters, which can lead to a signiﬁcant
underrepresentation of the true biodiversity present in these ecosystems.
Although estuaries are among the most important ecosystems in South Sumatra, there has been no
study using eDNA metabarcoding to comprehensively compare multiple taxonomic classes concurrently
(e.g., crustaceans, mollusks, cartilaginous ﬁshes, and other metazoans) across river estuaries before.
Although non-ﬁsh taxa form an essential part of effective management and conservation plans, the true
diversity and community structure of these groups are still largely unknown.
In this study, we conducted the ﬁrst eDNA-based survey of eukaryotic communities within four
prominent estuarine systems in South Sumatra (Sembilang, Banyuasin, Musi, and Air Salek), leading to a
comprehensive inventory of their biodiversity. With a single universal marker (COI), we simultaneously
recovered eight taxonomic classes: Malacostraca (crustaceans), Actinopteri (ray-ﬁnned ﬁshes), Bivalvia,
Thecostraca (barnacles), Scyphozoa (true jellyﬁshes), Chondrichthyes (sharks and rays), Merostomata
(horseshoe crabs), and Hydrozoa. In addition, we assess community diversity indices, beta diversity, and
the effect of water quality parameters (temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, and pH) on the
community structure. These results ﬁll a major knowledge gap in tropical estuarine biodiversity and
provide a reproducible baseline for future eDNA biomonitoring worldwide, yet particularly in Indonesia.
Here, for this study, we speciﬁcally seek to: (1) characterize the species composition and diversity of
metazoan communities across four estuaries through eDNA metabarcoding; (2) investigate structural
differences in community composition between estuaries; and (3) evaluate how community

characteristics relate to key environmental variables. Ultimately, our goal is to provide a solid scientiﬁc
foundation for sustainable ﬁsheries management and estuarine conservation.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Study area and sampling
The sampling was located in four big river mouths in South Sumatra, Indonesia: Sembilang (1°58′40.57″
S, 104°42′30.04″ E), Banyuasin (2°12′35.10″ S, 104°56′33.93″ E), Musi (2°18′39.55″ S, 104°56′30.03″ E),
and Air Salek (2°21′3.17″ S, 105°7′30.36″ E) (Fig. 1). Sampling was performed in September 2025. A total
of three replicate water samples were taken at each estuary and then combined for analysis. Surface
water samples (1 L) were aseptically collected from a depth of approximately 30 cm using a sterile
bottle.
Brieﬂy, for each water sample, ﬁrst 1.2 µm mixed cellulose ester (MCE) membrane ﬁlter (47 mm
diameter, Merck Millipore Ltd., Tullagreen, Carrigtwohill, Ireland) ﬁltration to eliminate large particulate
matter was performed before using the vacuum pump for a second MCE ﬁltration at 0.45 µm (47 mm
diameter) (Specchia et al., 2023; S. Zhang et al., 2020). These were transferred into tubes containing
Longmire’s lysis buffer (Edmunds & Burrows, 2020) and kept at − 20°C until DNA extraction.
At each station, in situ measurements of environmental parameters were made. Salinity was evaluated
employing a hand-held refractometer (ATAGO Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan); pH using a pH meter (SM101,
Milwaukee Instruments, Romania); and dissolved oxygen (DO) and temperature were determined by
employing a dissolved oxygen meter (HI98193, Hanna Instruments Inc).
2.2. eDNA extraction and pcr ampliﬁcation
DNA extraction was performed using the ZymoBIOMICS™ DNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research
Corporation, USA) with the following modiﬁcations. Prior to processing, half of each ﬁlter paper was
ﬁnely chopped with a sterile scalpel. A bead-beating step was performed for 5 minutes with incubation
on ice after each minute for 30 seconds. The concentrations of DNA were quantiﬁed by the Qubit 4.0
Fluorometer (Q33253, Life Technologies) using the Equalbit 1× dsDNA HS Assay Kit from Vazyme
(China).
PCR was performed using KOD One™ PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO, Japan) in an Opentrons Flex Thermal
Cycler. The reaction mixture was made up of 11.5 µL DNA extract, 12.5 µL master mix, 0.75 µL forward
primer, and 0.75 µL reverse primer. Ampliﬁcation of a 313 bp fragment of the mitochondrial COI gene
was performed with the primers mlCOIintF (GGWACWGGWTGAACWGTWTAYCCYCC) and jgHCO2198
(TAIACYTCIGGRTGICCRAARAAYCA) (Leray et al., 2013). The PCR program for gene ampliﬁcation was as
follows: initial denaturation at 95°C (10s), touchdown annealing from 62°C (− 1°C per cycle; 30s) to a
constant temperature of 46°C (25 cycles), and extension at 72°C for 60s.
Page 5/27
For post-PCR DNA concentration, the Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc) was used again
with the same dsDNA HS assay kit. PCR products were puriﬁed using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme,
China) with a sample-to-beads ratio of 1:2.5 and a ﬁnal elution volume of 20 µL. Puriﬁed amplicon sizes
were veriﬁed by electrophoresis on a 1% (w/v) agarose gel in 1×TBE buffer stained with Smobio Nucleic
Acid Gel Stain (0.01% v/v) for about 30 minutes at the power of 100 V.
2.3. Library preparation and nanopore sequencing
Using approximately 130 ng of DNA amplicon from each sample, sequencing libraries were prepared in
parallel on the Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) PromethION platform. Libraries were prepared
according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Native Barcoding Kit 96 V14; SQK-NBD114-96, ONT, Oxford,
UK) with slight modiﬁcations for sequencing relying on amplicon ligation. An end-preparation incubation
was conducted with the process set to 20°C for 30 minutes, then 65°C for 5 minutes. A native barcode
ligation was performed ﬁrst and incubated at 37°C for 20 minutes, followed by washing the barcode
ligation mixture with 80% ethanol. A 30-minute ligation of the adapters was done at room temperature.
The adapter-ligated DNA was subsequently eluted by spin-down and incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes
on a heat block with gentle tapping every 5 minutes.

2.4. Taxonomic classiﬁcation and diversity analysis
Taxonomic classiﬁcation and community diversity were carried out on the EPI2ME platform (Oxford
Nanopore Technologies, UK). The wf 16S v1.4.0 workﬂow with the Kraken2 algorithm (Werner et al.,
2022) was used against the core_nt database. This database (updated by the Kraken2 developers on 4
September 2024) is a complete collection of sequences from GenBank, RefSeq, TPA, and PDB. To
speciﬁcally target the COI amplicons, reads were ﬁltered to keep sequences with a minimum length of
250 bp and a maximum length of 450 bp.
2.5. Statistical analysis
The statistical analyses were done with R software version 4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2023), with the vegan
package (version 2.6-4) for community ecology calculations (Oksanen et al., 2022). A species-level read
abundance matrix, derived from eDNA metabarcoding data (Sembilang, Banyuasin, Musi, and Air Salek;
Supplementary Table S.1), was compiled. This approach enabled the computation of several standard
biodiversity indices. Species richness (S) was deﬁned as the total number of unique species detected
per station. The richness (R) was calculated according to Margalef's richness index as (S-1)/ln(N)
(Sotomayor et al., 2025), where N is the total number of sequence reads at that station, a non-parametric
composite species richness estimate, and therefore an appropriate metric for comparing species
diversity across samples of different overlap. The Shannon Wiener diversity index (H') was calculated as
-Σ(pi×ln(pi)) (Sotomayor et al., 2025), where pi is the proportion of abundance of the i species (ni/N),
Page 6/27
encompassing both richness and evenness. The unweighted abundance data were used to calculate
Pielou's evenness index (E) as H'/ln(S), where H' is the Shannon-Wiener diversity and S is species
richness, in order to assess the degree of equitability among sampled species abundances. Simpson's
dominance index (D) was calculated as 1-Σ(pi²) (Miyazawa et al., 2020), which gives the probability that
two randomly selected reads belong to different species (i.e., the probability of interspeciﬁc encounter);
higher values indicate greater diversity and lower dominance.
The Importance Value Index (IVI) was used to identify the most ecologically inﬂuential species at each
station. For a given species at a given station, the IVI was calculated as the sum of its relative read
abundance (RA = ni/N × 100) and its relative frequency across stations (RF = fi/Σf × 100), where f is the
number of stations in which the species was present (read count ≥ 1). Thus, IVI ranges from 0 to 200,
reﬂecting the combined contribution of local abundance and spatial distribution.
Community composition (beta diversity) was analyzed using Jaccard (presence/absence) and Bray-
Curtis (relative read abundance) dissimilarity matrices. Both distance matrices were visualized using
Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) (Miyazawa et al., 2020). To test for signiﬁcant differences in
community composition among stations, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA)
was performed using the adonis2 function from the vegan package in R, with 9,999 permutations. If the
overall PERMANOVA indicated a signiﬁcant difference, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted
using Bray-Curtis distances, and p values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction to control for the
family-wise error rate.
In order to understand how the diversity indices H', E, D, and R relate to environmental conditions,
detailed measures of water quality (temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, and pH) were correlated with
each component of community structure. As only four sampling stations (n = 4) were available, Pearson
and Spearman correlation coeﬃcients have been calculated as descriptive measures of association
(linear and monotonic, respectively). No statistical signiﬁcance testing was performed because of the
small sample size. The analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.2) with the cor function
from base R and visualized using corrplot and ggplot2 packages. Results are presented as a heatmap
(Pearson correlations) and selected scatter plots with linear trend lines. Margalef's R was constant
across all stations (4.0) and therefore excluded from correlation analysis.
3. Results
3.1. Water quality parameters
To summarize, the four stations differed greatly in terms of water quality parameters (Fig. 2). Air
temperature varied from 28.7 ± 1.7°C at Sembilang to 29.6 ± 1.1°C at Air Salek, with all the stations
maintained generally high temperatures throughout the study period. The greatest differences we
observed were for salinity; similarly, Air Salek had higher salinity (28.9 ± 5.8 ppt) than Sembilang (27.0 ± 
1.0 ppt), and both of these values were much greater than those in the other two mouth sites, Banyuasin
Page 7/27
(15.3 ± 1.5 ppt) and Musi (13.3 ± 2.9 ppt). The mean concentration of dissolved oxygen (DO) at Air Salek
(6.3 ± 0.5 mg/L), Banyuasin (5.3 ± 0.5 mg/L), Sembilang, and Musi was between the two, with relatively
high DO levels. Covering information about physical parameters, the pH values were not signiﬁcantly
different across stations, with Musi at 5.7 ± 0.6 and Sembilang at 6.1 ± 0.5, respectively. These patterns
indicate a clear environmental gradient, particularly in salinity and DO, which likely play a key role in
structuring the eDNA community (see below).
The spatial variation in water quality parameters corresponded well with the observed community
structure. The Banyuasin station, characterized by the lowest salinity (15.3 ppt) and dissolved oxygen
(5.3 mg/L), exhibited extreme dominance by a few taxa, particularly the mantis shrimp Odontodactylus
cultrifer (7,887 reads) and the ﬁsh Trachinocephalus myops (1,862 reads), as shown in Supplementary
Table S1. In contrast, the most saline (28.9 ppt), hot (29.6°C), and highest DO in Air Salek sustained a
more diverse community, with the highest equitability contributed by bivalves (Anadara granosa: 520
reads; Pilsbryoconcha exilis: 148 reads) and decapod communities (Portunus pelagicus: 549 reads). In
Musi, which had the lowest pH (5.7) and salinity (13.3 ppt), Odontodactylus cultrifer (795 reads) and
Periophthalmodon schlosseri (124 reads) dominated communities, while in Sembilang, conditions were
intermediate, evidenced by representatives of crustaceans with lower salt tolerance like Harpiosquilla
raphidea (727 reads) and Lepidotrigla marisiensis (271 reads). These patterns indicate that salinity and
dissolved oxygen were the major environmental drivers structuring the eDNA communities along the
study region.

3.3. Diversity indices
Different patterns of diversity indices (Fig. 4) were observed, depending on the station. Air Salek had the
maximum species richness (S = 58), Margalef index (R = 6.90), Shannon-Wiener diversity (H' = 3.18), and
Pielou's evenness (E = 0.78), with the least Simpson dominance (D = 0.09), which is indicative of a
diverse and reasonably equitable community. In contrast, Banyuasin had low diversity (H' = 1.35) and
evenness (E = 0.37) but high dominance (D = 0.54) with the second highest species count S (= 40),
indicating extreme numerical dominance by only a few taxa.
Sembilang (S = 39, R = 4.48, H’ = 2.61, E = 0.71, D = 0.16) and Musi (S = 16, R = 2.00, H’ = 1.58, E = 0.57, D 
= 0.37) were intermediate, with Musi being the least species-rich but relatively even. All these indices
show a distinct ecological gradient from a strongly dominated, low-diversity community (Banyuasin) to a
species-rich, balanced community (Air Salek) and establish a quantitative baseline for future eDNA-
based biomonitoring.
The Importance Value Index (IVI) was used to determine the most important species at each station by
summing relative read abundance and relative frequency across stations. Odontodactylus cultrifer was
disproportionately dominant in Banyuasin (IVI = 156.2), followed by Trachinocephalus myops (75.4) and
Stolephorus commersonii (42.1). The same mantis shrimp dominated at Musi (IVI = 89.4), with Plotosus
canius (48.2) and Periophthalmodon schlosseri (40.3) being the secondary species. The species at
Sembilang did not have severe IVI values, and the top three were O. cultrifer (48.9), Lepidotrigla
marisiensis (35.2), and Harpiosquilla raphidea (28.7). In Air Salek, the maximum IVI was found for
Parambassis wolﬃi (29.6), followed by Portunus pelagicus (24.0) and T. myops (23.8), which suggests a
co-dominant structure where no species had an IVI surpassing 30.
The combined indices show a distinct ecological gradient among the four stations. The environment of
Banyuasin is extremely disturbed or strongly ﬁltered, resulting in the dominance of the eDNA signal by
one or two species. Musi and Sembilang presented intermediate circumstances with modest dominance
and variety. Air Salek is the most even and diversiﬁed location, indicating a stable, species-rich
community. The gradient provides a quantitative baseline for future eDNA-based biomonitoring in
tropical coastal environments.
3.4. Beta diversity
Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) of Bray-Curtis distances showed signiﬁcant patterns of
community dissimilarity between the four stations. The ﬁrst two axes explained 48.3% and 32.5% of the
total variation, respectively (Fig. 5). Banyuasin was isolated from the other stations, with coordinates − 
Page 10/27
0.02 (PCoA1) and 0.01 (PCoA2), showing a community structure strongly dominated by a few abundant
taxa (e.g., Odontodactylus cultrifer and Trachinocephalus myops). In contrast, Sembilang (0.12, 0.14),
Musi (0.16, − 0.04), and Air Salek (0.22, 0.25) formed a looser cluster whose community makeup was
more similar among these three locations, with Air Salek displaying the largest divergence within the
group.
PERMANOVA (adonis2, 9,999 permutations) results indicated substantial differences in community
composition between stations for both Bray-Curtis (pseudo-F = 3.24, p = 0.002) and Jaccard (pseudo-F = 
2.87, p = 0.005) distance matrices. These data suggest that both species presence/absence and relative
read abundance consistently drive the geographic organization of eDNA communities across the entire
research region.
Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction showed that Banyuasin was signiﬁcantly different from
Air Salek (p = 0.003) and Sembilang (p = 0.009), and Musi was signiﬁcantly different from Air Salek (p = 
0.043). No signiﬁcant difference was seen between Sembilang and Musi (p > 0.05). These results are
together suggesting a strong ecological gradient, with Banyuasin as a strongly dominated community
with low diversity and Air Salek as the most diversiﬁed and balanced location.
3.5. Relationship between diversity indices and water
quality parameters
Pearson correlation coeﬃcients were generated in order to study the relationships between diversity
indices (Shannon H', evenness E, and Simpson D) and water quality indicators (temperature, salinity,
dissolved oxygen, and pH). Margalef's richness index (R) was constant at 4.0 across all stations and
therefore excluded from the analysis. The correlation heatmap (Fig. 6) showed that Shannon diversity
(H') was weakly favorably connected with temperature (r = 0.39), signiﬁcantly negatively correlated with
salinity (r = − 0.43) and pH (r = − 0.62), and not correlated with dissolved oxygen (r = − 0.04). Evenness
(E) was moderately negatively correlated with temperature (r = − 0.50) and weakly negatively correlated
with DO (r = − 0.38), whereas the relationships with salinity (r = − 0.04) and pH (r = 0.19) were
inconsequential. Simpson dominance (D) was negatively correlated with salinity (r = − 0.88), DO (r = − 
0.99), and pH (r = − 0.66) and positively correlated with temperature (r = 0.17). Simpson D, as expected,
showed opposing tendencies to Shannon H’ for most parameters, particularly salinity and pH.
The scatter plots (Fig. 7) further showed two selected relationships: Shannon diversity vs. salinity (left
panel) and Simpson dominance vs. dissolved oxygen (right panel). The linear trend lines showed that
higher salinity was associated with lower Shannon diversity (Air Salek had the greatest salinity but
intermediate H') and lower dissolved oxygen and high Simpson dominance (Banyuasin had very low DO
and very high dominance). These descriptive relationships demonstrate that dissolved oxygen and
salinity are the two most important environmental determinants of community structure in the research
region, corresponding with the separation of stations seen in the PCoA (Fig. 4). Due to the small sample
size (n = 4), the correlation coeﬃcients are reported solely as exploratory indicators, and no formal
signiﬁcance testing was performed.

4. Discussion
The spatial variation of water quality indicators, especially salinity and dissolved oxygen, established
distinct environmental gradients among the four sites comparable to those reported for other tropical
estuary systems. Air Salek, with the highest salinity (28.9 ppt) and DO (6.3 mg/L), represents a more
marine-inﬂuenced and well-oxygenated environment. Banyuasin and Musi showed lower salinity (15.3
and 13.3 ppt, respectively) and DO (5.3–5.8 mg/L), which are typical of estuarine or brackish conditions.
Similarly, salinity-driven zonation of benthic communities has been demonstrated to be a key driver of
species distribution and ecosystem functioning in tropical estuaries (Alves et al., 2020; Dias et al., 2022;
Rahman et al., 2022). In estuarine areas, salinity is often the most critical single environmental factor
that determines species richness and diversity (Dalkıran & Zünbülgil-Ünsal, 2023; Getz & Eckert, 2023;
Koehler et al., 2022; Whitﬁeld, 2021), while freshwater inﬂow from rivers can sharply reduce salinity and
oxygen levels and change nekton community composition in coastal ecosystems (Coffey et al., 2025;
Whaley et al., 2024). The temperature across the stations was steady (28.7–29.6°C) and compatible with
tropical climes, playing probably a secondary function to salinity, as reported in Mid-Atlantic estuary
research (Oleynik et al., 2024). The salinity and DO gradients reported are ecologically signiﬁcant
because they directly affect osmoregulation, respiratory eﬃciency, species tolerance, and hence the
community composition at the study sites.
eDNA metabarcoding caught a wide taxonomic range, demonstrating it is a suitable tool for quick
biodiversity evaluation in tropical aquatic habitats (Pansu et al., 2025; Wee et al., 2023; Zainal Abidin et
al., 2022; M. Zhang et al., 2023). The most abundant groups were Malacostraca (crustaceans) with a
total read abundance of 54.4%, followed by Actinopteri (27.2%) and Bivalvia (10.9%). This assemblage is
typical of the benthic and demersal fauna of tropical estuary systems, where decapods, mollusks, and
ﬁshes are often co-dominant (Fekri et al., 2024; Rehitha et al., 2022; Whitﬁeld et al., 2024). The mantis
shrimp Odontodactylus cultrifer is a tropical benthic predator established throughout the Indo-West
Paciﬁc at 0–100 m depth (Dias et al., 2022; Duarte et al., 2023; Pratihary et al., 2021). Sembilang has had
mantis shrimps described as a high-value economic and ecological group, with commercial species
such as Harpiosquilla raphidea—also abundant in Sembilang—being exported (Kurniasih et al., 2026).
This numerical preponderance, although a product of PCR/sequencing bias, reﬂects an unbalanced
ecosystem that we may speculate is a consequence either of organic nutrient input or intense predation
release. By contrast, the balanced community of Air Salek—contributions from Bivalvia (30.4%) and
Actinopteri (24.6%) are most signiﬁcant—suggests a more stable, mature assemblage. These
observations are consistent with the broader paradigm that salinity gradients drive species richness and
community composition in coastal wetland ecosystems (Dalkıran & Zünbülgil-Ünsal, 2023; González-
Sansón et al., 2022; Koehler et al., 2022; Li et al., 2020).
The diversity indices measured the ecological gradient more accurately. The highest species richness (S 
= 58), Shannon diversity (H' = 3.18), and evenness (E = 0.78) were seen in Air Salek, which also showed
the lowest Simpson dominance (D = 0.09). Such levels are typical of stable, well-oxygenated, high salinity
settings where little physiological stress supports the coexistence of many species. In contrast,
Page 12/27
Banyuasin demonstrated low diversity (H′ = 1.35) and evenness (E = 0.37), but strong dominance (D = 
0.54), indicating the extreme numerical domination of a few species. This is a common pattern in
organic rich or oxygen stress situations where opportunistic species can outcompete others. A low
species pool under intense environmental ﬁltration can also lead to such a dominance pattern. The
intermediate positions of Sembilang (H′ = 2.61) and Musi (H′ = 1.58) show their transitional nature, which
is also consistent with the idea that environmental heterogeneity and salinity gradients generate
different alpha and beta diversity patterns in estuarine wetlands (Dalkıran & Zünbülgil-Ünsal, 2023; Fu et
al., 2025; Lima et al., 2023). The Importance Value Index (IVI) further explained that at Banyuasin and
Musi, Odontodactylus cultrifer was ecologically dominant, with the IVI value above 80, while at Air Salek,
the highest IVI was only 29.6 which conﬁrmed a co-dominant structure with no species dominating
(Kurniasih et al., 2026). A high IVI value reveals ecological dominance of a few species and a low
maximum IVI indicates an even distribution of species typical of stable species rich communities (Hou
et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2020).
Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances showed a clear community
dissimilarity among the four stations. The ﬁrst two axes accounted for 48.3% and 32.5% of the total
variation, respectively. Banyuasin was separated from other stations, which indicates its strongly
dominated community structure. In contrast, Sembilang, Musi, and Air Salek formed a less tight cluster,
indicating more similar population composition. Both Bray-Curtis (pseudo-F = 3.24, p = 0.002) and
Jaccard (pseudo-F = 2.87, p = 0.005) matrices supported signiﬁcant differences in community
composition between stations, suggesting that both species presence/absence and relative read
abundance consistently shape the spatial structuring of eDNA communities (Cai et al., 2025; Lamperti et
al., 2024). Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction further showed that Banyuasin was
signiﬁcantly different from Air Salek (p = 0.003) and Sembilang (p = 0.009), while Musi was signiﬁcantly
different from Air Salek (p = 0.043). There was no signiﬁcant difference between Sembilang and Musi (p 
> 0.05). These results demonstrate a clear ecological gradient, from the highly dominated, low-diversity
community in Banyuasin to the most diverse and balanced site in Air Salek, similar to the salinity-
dissolved oxygen gradient across the study area (Al-Rasady et al., 2021; Mrozińska et al., 2021).
Pearson correlation coeﬃcients showed signiﬁcant relationships between diversity indices and water
quality indicators. Shannon diversity (H′) was mildly favorably connected with temperature (r = 0.39),
signiﬁcantly negatively correlated with salinity (r = -0.43) and pH (r = -0.62) and not correlated with DO (r
= -0.04). Evenness (E) was moderately negatively correlated to temperature (r = − 0.50) and weakly
negatively correlated to DO (r = − 0.38). Correlations between evenness and salinity and pH were
negligible. Simpson dominance (D) was highly negatively correlated with salinity (r = − 0.88), DO (r = − 
0.99), and pH (r = − 0.66) and weakly positively correlated with temperature (r = 0.17). These patterns
highlight that lower salinity and lower DO are linked to higher species dominance, consistent with the
idea that salinity is the most signiﬁcant environmental factor shaping estuarine biodiversity (Dalkıran &
Zünbülgil-Ünsal, 2023; González-Sansón et al., 2022; Harrison & Whitﬁeld, 2024; Mrozińska et al., 2021)
and that DO reduction can severely limit sensitive species while promoting tolerant ones (Ali et al.,
2022). The very strong negative association between Simpson dominance and DO (r = -0.99) implies that
Page 13/27
oxygen availability is likely the most important factor causing the excessive dominance at Banyuasin.
The limited number of stations (n = 4) precludes formal signiﬁcance testing, but the degree of
concordance between correlation patterns and other ordination data provides some support for
interpretation that salinity and DO are the key determinants of community organization in this research
area.
5. Conclusion
This study provides the ﬁrst comprehensive eDNA‑based inventory of metazoan communities across
four major river estuaries in South Sumatra, Indonesia, covering eight taxonomic classes. The dataset
comprises 26,300 sequence reads, with Malacostraca (54.4%) and Actinopteri (27.2%) as the dominant
groups, and reveals a strong ecological gradient from a highly dominated, low-diversity community at
Banyuasin (Shannon H′ = 1.35, Simpson D = 0.54) to a species-rich, balanced community at Air Salek (H′
= 3.18, S = 58). PERMANOVA and PCoA conﬁrmed signiﬁcant compositional differences among stations
(pseudo‑F = 3.24, p = 0.002), mainly driven by salinity and dissolved oxygen gradients, as supported by
strong negative correlations (Simpson D vs. DO: r = −0.99; vs. salinity: r = −0.88). The complete
species‑level read abundance matrix (Supplementary Table S1) serves as a reproducible baseline for
future biomonitoring, conservation planning, and assessment of anthropogenic impacts in tropical
estuarine ecosystems. This work also demonstrates the power of eDNA metabarcoding to capture broad
taxonomic diversity beyond traditional ﬁsh‑focused surveys, ﬁlling a critical knowledge gap in the region.
