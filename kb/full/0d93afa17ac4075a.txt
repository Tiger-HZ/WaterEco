Abstract
Litterfall and decomposition are pivotal processes governing nutrient cycling and 
ecosystem functioning, yet they remain poorly studied in India’s sacred forests. This 
study quantified litterfall production, standing litter biomass, decomposition, and 
nutrient return in four tropical dry deciduous sacred forests of Western Odisha, India, 
over one year (January-December 2023). The study sites comprised Shorea robusta 
-dominated forests at Andhari, Dedungri, and Medha, and a Cleistanthus collinus 
-dominated forest at Papanga. Litterfall was collected using 1 m² litter traps, and 
decomposition was assessed using 20 g litterbags with 1 mm mesh. Litter fractions 
were analyzed for carbon (C), nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). Annual 
litterfall ranged from 8.59 ± 0.79 Mg ha ⁻¹ yr⁻¹ at Andhari to 11.32 ± 1.12 Mg ha ⁻¹ yr⁻¹ 
at Medha, with leaves contributing 80.65–83.35% of total litterfall. Standing litter 
biomass ranged from 2.39 ± 1.27 Mg ha ⁻¹ at Andhari to 2.68 ± 1.42 Mg ha ⁻¹ at Medha. 
Litter turnover rates (K ₗ) ranged from 3.59 to 4.22 yr ⁻¹, corresponding to residence 
times of 87–102 days. Decomposition was rapid, with approximately 95% mass loss 
within six months; decay rate constants (k) ranged from 5.75 to 6.65 yr ⁻¹, with half-
lives (t₅₀) of 38–44 days and 99% decomposition (t₉₉) occurring within 274–318 days. 
Nutrient release followed the order K > N > P , with Papanga exhibiting the highest 
nutrient loss (96.57%) after six months. Initial litter N and P concentrations were 
significantly correlated with decomposition rates, while K declined rapidly due to 
leaching. These findings highlight the critical role of sacred forests in nutrient cycling 
and ecosystem functioning and provide valuable insights for their conservation 
under increasing anthropogenic pressures.

 1 Introduction
Sacred groves are traditionally protected forest patches conserved by local communities 
through religious beliefs and cultural practices, representing one of the oldest forms of 
in situ biodiversity conservation in India. These ecologically sensitive landscapes support 
rich biodiversity and contribute to microclimate regulation, soil moisture conservation,

and nutrient cycling. In forest ecosystems, leaf litter forms a crucial link between vegeta-
tion and soil processes, serving as a major source of organic matter and nutrients [ 1]. In 
sacred groves, where anthropogenic disturbances are generally limited due to restric -
tions on timber and firewood extraction, NTFP removal, grazing, and other resource-
use activities, litterfall and its subsequent decomposition are important components of 
ecosystem functioning and may contribute significantly to soil fertility and ecological 
resilience [2, 3]. Understanding litter dynamics in these culturally protected ecosystems 
can therefore provide valuable insights into their role in sustaining essential ecosystem 
processes and maintaining environmental health [4].
Litterfall is a fundamental ecological process that links primary production with 
nutrient cycling in terrestrial ecosystems. It represents a major pathway through which 
organic matter and nutrients are transferred from vegetation to the soil, where litter 
decomposition regulates nutrient availability for plant growth [ 5, 6]. The dynamics of 
litterfall and its subsequent decomposition are key determinants of soil fertility, carbon 
storage, and forest productivity, with important implications for climate change miti -
gation. In tropical dry forests, nutrient cycling is strongly influenced by seasonal water 
availability and nutrient status, both of which regulate litter production and decomposi -
tion rates [ 7–10]. Seasonal variation in rainfall and plant phenology can alter litterfall 
patterns, while plant functional traits, including leaf quality and structural characteris -
tics, influence litter decomposability and nutrient release [ 11–14]. The decomposition 
process is further regulated by soil microbial communities, whose activity and composi -
tion respond to moisture conditions and litter quality, thereby influencing nutrient avail-
ability, carbon dynamics, and overall ecosystem functioning [15–17].
Despite their ecological significance, research on litterfall and decomposition in India’s 
tropical dry deciduous forests remains limited, particularly in sacred forests. These for -
ests are traditionally conserved patches of climax vegetation, protected through reli -
gious beliefs and cultural practices. They function as biodiversity hotspots and provide 
essential ecosystem services, including carbon sequestration, water regulation, and soil 
conservation, which are especially important in human-dominated landscapes [ 18–
20]. Although their biodiversity and conservation values have been widely recognized, 
their role in ecosystem processes such as litter decomposition and nutrient cycling has 
received comparatively little attention [ 21–23]. A better understanding of litter dynam -
ics and associated nutrient release in sacred forests is essential for elucidating their con -
tribution to ecosystem functioning, nutrient cycling, and ecological resilience under 
increasing anthropogenic pressures.
Tropical dry deciduous forests of Eastern India, including those of Western Odisha, 
exhibit pronounced seasonality in litterfall, primarily driven by dry-season water stress 
that induces leaf abscission [24–26]. Variations in soil water availability and plant water-
use strategies further influence vegetation structure, productivity, and nutrient cycling, 
underscoring the ecological importance of seasonal water stress in these ecosystems 
[27]. Litter decomposition is regulated by litter biochemical composition, microbial 
activity, and climatic conditions [ 28–32]. Although litter dynamics have been exten -
sively documented in tropical evergreen and semi-evergreen forests [ 33–36], compa -
rable information for sacred forests, particularly tropical dry deciduous sacred forests, 
remains limited. The role of small, culturally protected, and relatively undisturbed sacred 
forests in regulating litter decomposition and nutrient fluxes is still poorly understood,

despite their potential importance as localized reservoirs of organic matter and nutrients 
in human-dominated landscapes [37–41]. This represents a significant gap in the current 
understanding of ecosystem functioning in tropical dry deciduous sacred forests.
The novelty of this study lies in providing region-specific information on seasonal lit -
ter production, decomposition, and nutrient dynamics in the sacred forests of Western 
Odisha, a region for which such ecological information remains limited. Although lit -
terfall and nutrient cycling have been extensively investigated in tropical forests and, to 
a lesser extent, in sacred groves and sacred natural sites in India and elsewhere, com -
parable information on litter decomposition and nutrient fluxes in the sacred forests of 
Western Odisha remains scarce [ 42–46]. By quantifying litterfall production, standing 
litter biomass, decomposition rates, and associated C, N, P , and K fluxes over a one-year 
period (January-December 2023), this study provides baseline information on nutri -
ent cycling in these culturally protected ecosystems. The specific objectives were to: (i) 
quantify litterfall production and standing litter biomass, (ii) assess litter decomposition 
rates and associated nutrient release, and (iii) evaluate nutrient fluxes across sacred for -
ests. The findings provide new insights into the functional role of sacred forests in nutri -
ent cycling and ecosystem functioning and may support evidence-based conservation 
and management strategies for these ecologically and culturally significant ecosystems 
in rapidly changing landscapes [47–51].
2 Methodology
2.1 Study area
The present study was conducted in four sacred forests located in the western region 
of Odisha, India, in a tropical dry deciduous forest ecosystem. These forests are part of 
the unique socio-ecological landscapes in Odisha, where sacred forests are preserved by 
local communities due to cultural and religious significance. The selected sites for this 
study include Andhari, Dedungri, Medha, and Papanga; present in Jharsuguda, Sam -
balpur, Sundargarh and Bargarh district respectably. Each representing distinct sacred 
forest ecosystems that varies in terms of species composition, forest structure, and prox-
imity to human activities. The geographical region lies approximately between20° 40 ′ N 
to 22° 32 ′ N Latitude and 82° 39 ′ E to 85° 22 ′ E Longitude. A Digital Elevation Model 
(DEM) showing the spatial distribution of the sacred forests is presented in Fig.  1, where 
elevation is expressed in meters above mean sea level (AMSL). The elevation of the study 
sites ranges approximately from 180 to 310 m above sea level.
The study area is characterized by a tropical savanna climate, with a distinct seasonal 
pattern of temperature and rainfall (Fig.  2), which plays a significant role in shaping the 
vegetation structure and litter dynamics of the forests. The mean annual temperature 
ranges from 22 °C to 30 °C, with the hottest months being April to June, during which 
maximum temperatures may exceed 45  °C. The monsoon season (June to September) 
contributes to the majority of the annual rainfall, with total annual precipitation ranging 
from 900 mm to 1,400 mm, depending on the specific location (Source: IMD, Annual 
climate summary report 2023).
The presence of these sacred forests within human-dominated landscapes provides a 
unique opportunity to investigate their role in sustaining ecological processes such as 
litterfall production, decomposition, and nutrient cycling despite increasing anthropo -
genic pressures. The vegetation is characteristic of tropical dry deciduous forests and is

2.2 Methodology
2.2.1 Litter sampling and analysis
Litterfall was quantified at monthly intervals over a one-year period. Within each sacred 
forest, fifteen litter collection pits (1 m × 1 m × 0.15 m; length × breadth × depth) were 
randomly established, with a minimum spacing of approximately 100 m between pits to 
capture spatial variation in species composition and litter inputs across the forest. All 
pits were located at least 10  m inside the forest boundary to minimize potential edge 
effects. Each pit was excavated directly into the forest floor, and naturally deposited litter 
within the designated area was collected during each monthly sampling. No suspended 
litter traps, plastic sheets, or other artificial collection materials were used. The collected 
litter was separated into four fractions: leaves, twigs, branches, and other plant parts. 
Each fraction was oven-dried at 80 °C for 48 h, or until a constant weight was attained, 
and the dry biomass was recorded.
2.2.2 Litter decomposition
Leaf litter decomposition was studied using the nylon bag method [ 53]. Fresh leaf litter 
was collected randomly from all four sites during the peak litterfall period (February-
March) and air-dried. Subsamples (20 g) were placed into litterbags (20 cm × 20 cm; 
mesh size 1 mm × 1 mm). Ninety litterbags were prepared for each site and placed on 
the soil surface beneath the litter layer in a completely randomized design. Six litterbags 
were retrieved monthly from each site. Samples were carefully washed to remove soil 
particles, oven-dried at 80 °C for 48 h, weighed, and powdered for chemical analysis.

2.2.4 Chemical analysis
All litter species were analyzed together as mixed-species litter to represent the natural 
litter layer beneath the mixed-species canopy and to reflect site-level litter decomposi -
tion and nutrient cycling conditions. Leaf litter collected from litter traps and decompo -
sition bags was carefully cleaned to remove adhering soil particles, washed with distilled 
water, oven-dried at 80 °C for 48 h, finely ground, and stored for chemical analysis. Ash 
content was determined by igniting 1 g of powdered litter at 550 °C for 6 h in a muf -
fle furnace, and total carbon was estimated as 50% of the ash-free dry mass [ 57]. This 
approach provides an approximate estimate of carbon content and may not fully cap -
ture variation in the carbon concentration among different litter types compared with 
direct elemental analysis. For nitrogen estimation, finely ground samples were digested 
with concentrated H₂SO₄ using Kjeltabs as a catalyst, and total nitrogen was determined 
by the Kjeldahl distillation and titration method, with blanks and standards included 
following the protocol of Allen et al. (1974) [ 58]. Phosphorus concentration was deter -
mined using the molybdenum-blue method [ 58] after digestion with a tri-acid mixture 
(H₂SO₄-HClO₄-HNO₃). Potassium concentration was measured by flame photometry 
[59] following digestion with the same tri-acid mixture. Structural components of litter, 
including lignin and cellulose, were not analyzed in the present study.
2.2.5 Statistical analysis
For litter decomposition, six litterbags were retrieved from each of the four study sites 
at each monthly sampling interval, resulting in 24 litterbag samples per month ( n = 24). 
Each litterbag was treated as an independent experimental unit within each sampling 
period, while sampling month and forest site were considered temporal and spatial fac -
tors, respectively. Data were not transformed prior to analysis. Normality and homoge -
neity of variance were evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively, 
implemented in the stats package in R. When the assumptions of parametric tests 
were not satisfied, the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test was used, without post-hoc pair -
wise comparisons. For datasets meeting the assumptions of parametric analysis, two-
way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the effects of forest site, 
sampling month, and their interaction. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to exam -
ine relationships between monthly litterfall and climatic variables, as well as between 
litter mass loss and selected litter biochemical traits. Correlation analyses were con -
ducted using the corrplot package in R. Statistical significance was assessed at α = 0.05

3 Results
3.1 Dynamics of litterfall production
The monthly litterfall production over the 12-month study period exhibited a dis -
tinct seasonal pattern, with peaks occurring in March across all four sacred forests 
(Fig.  3). Although monthly litter production did not differ significantly among the 
sites ( p = 0.3191), significant variation was observed in the monthly litterfall pattern 
within sites ( p < 2.2e-16). Annual litterfall was highest in Medha (11.32 Mg ha ⁻¹ yr ⁻¹), 
followed by Dedungri (10.28 Mg ha ⁻¹ yr ⁻¹), Papanga (8.94 Mg ha ⁻¹ yr ⁻¹), and lowest in 
Andhari (8.59 Mg ha ⁻¹ yr⁻¹). Leaves constituted the largest fraction of litterfall, ranging 
from 80.65% in Medha to 83.35% in Andhari, followed by twigs, branches, and other 
miscellaneous components. Thus, the order of litterfall fractions across all sites was 
leaves > twigs > branches > others. However, no formal statistical correlation between 
monthly litterfall and climatic variables was conducted in the present study; therefore, 
the observed seasonal association is interpreted descriptively.
3.2 Standing litter and turnover rate
Standing litter exhibited pronounced seasonal fluctuations that closely followed litterfall 
dynamics, with maximum accumulation occurring in March across all study sites and 
minimum values during the rainy and post-rainy months (August-November). Standing 
litter did not differ significantly among forest sites (ANOVA: F = 0.157, p = 0.925; Krus-
kal-Wallis: χ² = 0.836, df = 3, p = 0.841), whereas significant seasonal variation among 
months was observed (ANOVA: F = 101.757, p < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶; Kruskal-Wallis: χ² = 44.625, 
df = 11, p < 0.001) (Table 1). Mean annual standing litter biomass was highest in Medha 
(2.68 Mg ha⁻¹), followed by Dedungri (2.51 Mg ha ⁻¹), Papanga (2.47 Mg ha ⁻¹), and And-
hari (2.39 Mg ha⁻¹). The litter turnover rate (Kₗ) ranged from 3.59 yr⁻¹ in Andhari to 4.22 
yr⁻¹ in Medha, corresponding to turnover times of 0.24–0.28 years. Litter residence time

3.3 Litter decomposition and mass loss
The in-situ litterbag experiment revealed a distinct phased pattern of decomposition. 
Litter mass loss increased progressively over time, although the rate of decomposition 
varied among sampling intervals. Mass loss was highest during the first month, declined 
in the second month, and increased again from the third month onward. Litter mass 
loss did not differ significantly among the four sacred forests (p = 0.9079). Approximately 
95% of the initial litter mass was lost within six months across all study sites (Fig.  4). 
The decay rate coefficient (k) ranged from 5.75 to 6.65 yr ⁻¹ and followed the order: And-
hari = Papanga > Dedungri > Medha. The estimated litter half-life (t₅₀) ranged from 0.10 
to 0.12 years (38–44 days), while the time required for 99% decomposition (t₉₉) ranged 
from 0.75 to 0.87 years (274–318 days). Because the decomposition experiment was con-
ducted for only six months, the t₉₉ values represent model-based extrapolations derived 
from the fitted exponential decay model and should therefore be interpreted as esti -
mated rather than directly observed decomposition times.

3.5 Nutrient return from litter decomposition
Nutrient release patterns closely followed the trends observed for litter mass loss 
(Fig. 6). Overall nutrient release did not differ significantly among the four sacred forests 
(p = 0.996474), whereas significant temporal variation was observed during the decom -
position period (p = 0.000136). After six months of decomposition, Papanga recorded the 
highest total nutrient loss (96.57%), followed by Andhari (95.36%), Dedungri (94.18%), 
and Medha (92.38%). During decomposition, N and P concentrations increased in the 
residual litter, C remained relatively stable in Andhari and Medha but decreased in 
Dedungri and Papanga, whereas K declined markedly across all study sites (Fig. 7).
Nutrient-specific release patterns indicated that K exhibited the greatest release across 
all forests, followed by N and P . Potassium release was highest in Andhari (99.20%), fol -
lowed by Papanga (99.09%), Medha (98.84%), and Dedungri (98.72%). Nitrogen loss fol -
lowed the order Papanga (95.08%) > Andhari (95.06%) > Dedungri (93.58%) > Medha 
(92.26%), whereas phosphorus showed the lowest release, ranging from 95.34% in 
Papanga to 80.54% in Medha. No significant differences among forests were detected in 
the release of C (p = 0.9836), N (p = 0.9778), P (p = 0.7221), or K (p = 0.9615).

4 Discussion
Plant litter production is a major pathway linking vegetation productivity with soil 
organic matter formation and nutrient cycling [60–62]. In tropical dry deciduous sacred 
forests, the pronounced seasonal climate, particularly dry-season water stress, is likely 
to be a primary driver of litterfall dynamics by inducing leaf senescence and abscission 
as adaptive responses to seasonal moisture limitation [ 31]. The observed peak in litter -
fall during the dry season and the predominance of leaf litter (80–83% of total litter -
fall) are consistent with the deciduous phenology of tropical dry forests and indicate that 
leaf shedding is the principal pathway of litter input to the forest floor [ 23, 51, 63–67]. 
Although monthly litter production did not differ significantly among the four sacred 
forests, the pronounced seasonal variation suggests that temporal climatic and pheno -
logical factors exert a stronger influence on litterfall dynamics than site-level differences 
within these ecosystems. The dominance of leaf litter further indicates that seasonal 
nutrient transfer from vegetation to soil is primarily mediated through leaf turnover, 
with subsequent decomposition contributing substantially to nutrient release and soil 
fertility. Collectively, these findings emphasize the importance of seasonal litterfall 
dynamics in regulating nutrient cycling and ecosystem functioning in tropical dry decid-
uous sacred forests.
The magnitude of litterfall observed in these sacred forests falls within the range 
reported for tropical forest ecosystems globally [ 24]. However, annual litter production 
was higher than values previously reported for several tropical dry deciduous forests in 
India [29, 68]. This difference may be associated with variation in disturbance intensity, 
species composition, canopy structure, stand age, or site-specific environmental condi -
tions, although these factors were not directly evaluated in the present study [27]. Future 
studies should explicitly examine the environmental and vegetation-related drivers of 
litter production across tropical dry deciduous sacred forests. In contrast, standing lit -
ter biomass was lower than that reported for several other tropical forest ecosystems in 
India [51, 69, 70]. Litter turnover rates were comparatively higher than those reported 
for tropical semi-deciduous [ 71, 72], tropical dry evergreen [ 51], and tropical evergreen 
forests [64], indicating relatively rapid litter turnover in these sacred forests. Such rapid 
turnover may facilitate faster nutrient recycling and organic matter processing within 
these ecosystems. The turnover rates, turnover times, and residence times reported in 
this study were derived from observed litterfall and standing litter measurements; how -
ever, confidence intervals or other uncertainty estimates were not calculated for these 
derived parameters. Therefore, these values should be interpreted as site-level estimates, 
and future studies incorporating uncertainty propagation and longer-term monitoring 
would provide a more robust assessment of variability in litter turnover and residence 
time.
Litter decomposition patterns further support the rapid turnover observed in these 
sacred forests. Although decomposition rates did not differ significantly among the four 
study sites (p = 0.9923), nearly 95% of the initial litter mass was lost within six months, 
a rate that is relatively high compared with values reported for several tropical forest 
ecosystems [73–78]. Similar patterns have been documented under tropical dry decidu -
ous conditions, where more than 85% litter mass loss was observed within six months 
for home garden tree species [ 79–81]. The rapid decomposition observed in the pres -
ent study is likely associated with favorable temperature and moisture conditions during

matter breakdown [82, 83]. In addition, variation in litter quality and forest disturbance 
may influence decomposition dynamics and nutrient cycling processes in tropical dry 
ecosystems [84]. Collectively, these results suggest that rapid litter decomposition may 
contribute substantially to nutrient recycling and organic matter turnover in tropical dry 
deciduous sacred forests, although the specific microbial and biochemical mechanisms 
were not directly examined in the present study.
Correlation analyses revealed strong associations between litter chemistry and decom-
position dynamics. In addition to nutrient concentrations, structural components such 
as lignin and cellulose are widely recognized as important regulators of litter decom -
position because they influence the recalcitrance of organic matter and its accessibility 
to decomposer microorganisms. High lignin concentrations generally slow decomposi -
tion due to their resistance to microbial degradation, whereas cellulose is more read -
ily decomposed and contributes substantially to early-stage mass loss. Although lignin 
and cellulose were not quantified in the present study, variation in decomposition rates 
and nutrient release among sites may partially reflect differences in litter chemical qual -
ity and structural composition. Previous studies have shown that interactions between 
nutrient concentrations (particularly N and P) and structural compounds such as lignin, 
cellulose, and hemicellulose strongly influence decomposition dynamics in tropical eco -
systems. Consequently, the rapid decomposition observed in these sacred forests may 
be associated with the combined effects of favorable climatic conditions, litter nutrient 
concentrations, and potentially lower lignin-to-nitrogen ratios in mixed-species litter, 
although this mechanism could not be directly evaluated in the present study.
Nitrogen and phosphorus concentrations were negatively correlated with litter mass 
remaining, whereas C, K, C, C, and N ratios showed positive correlations. The progres -
sive increase in N and P concentrations in residual litter likely reflects microbial immo -
bilization and nutrient accumulation during decomposition, a pattern widely reported 
in tropical forest ecosystems [ 7, 34, 85–88]. In contrast, K declined rapidly throughout 
decomposition, most likely due to its high solubility and susceptibility to leaching losses 
[87, 88]. The observed order of nutrient release (K > N > P) is consistent with patterns 
reported for other tropical forest ecosystems [ 72, 89, 90]. The rapid release of K reflects 
its high mobility, whereas N and P are more strongly regulated by microbial immobiliza-
tion and mineralization processes. Overall, the results indicate that the sacred forests of 
Western Odisha are characterized by high litter production, rapid decomposition, and 
substantial nutrient turnover, highlighting their important role in nutrient cycling and 
ecosystem functioning in tropical dry deciduous landscapes.
The present study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the 
absence of non-sacred forest control sites limits direct comparison of litterfall, decom -
position, and nutrient fluxes between sacred and surrounding non-sacred forests. 
Because the study was conducted exclusively within sacred forests that differ in vegeta -
tion structure, species composition, and traditional management practices, a single non-
sacred control site would not have provided an ecologically comparable baseline and 
could have introduced additional confounding factors. Consequently, the study focuses 
on seasonal and site-level variation within sacred forest systems rather than the specific 
effects of sacred-forest protection. Second, lignin and cellulose concentrations were not 
measured, although these structural components are important determinants of litter

decomposition rates and nutrient release patterns. Third, repeated temporal observa -
tions from the same forest sites were not explicitly analyzed using repeated-measures 
or mixed-effects models; therefore, temporal dependence among observations may 
not have been fully accounted for, and the correlation analyses should be interpreted 
as indicating associations rather than causal relationships. Future studies incorporat -
ing carefully matched sacred and non-sacred forest sites, lignin and cellulose analyses, 
mixed-effects or regression-based decomposition models, and longer-term monitoring 
would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the drivers of litter dynamics 
and nutrient cycling in sacred forests.
5 Conclusion
This study demonstrates that the sacred forests of Western Odisha are characterized by 
substantial litter production, rapid litter decomposition, and considerable nutrient turn -
over, highlighting their important role in nutrient cycling and ecosystem functioning in 
tropical dry deciduous landscapes. Annual litterfall ranged from 8.59 to 11.32 Mg ha ⁻¹ 
yr⁻¹, with leaves contributing the dominant fraction of total litterfall. Decomposition 
was rapid, with decay rate constants (k = 5.75–6.65 yr ⁻¹) and approximately 95% litter 
mass loss within six months, indicating relatively fast organic matter turnover. Nutri -
ent release followed the order K > N > P , with initial litter nitrogen and phosphorus con-
centrations significantly associated with decomposition dynamics, while potassium loss 
was primarily influenced by leaching. These findings indicate that sacred forests contrib-
ute substantially to nutrient turnover and soil nutrient replenishment despite varying 
degrees of anthropogenic influence. The study was limited by the absence of non-sacred 
forest control sites, lignin and cellulose analyses, and statistical models that explicitly 
accounted for temporal dependence among repeated observations. Future studies inte -
grating litter biochemical quality, microbial community dynamics, environmental driv -
ers, and long-term monitoring would provide a more comprehensive understanding of 
decomposition and nutrient cycling in sacred forests. Overall, this study provides an 
important ecological baseline for the sacred forests of Western Odisha and underscores 
their significance as functionally important ecosystems for nutrient cycling, biodiver -
sity conservation, and the long-term ecological sustainability of tropical dry deciduous 
landscapes.
