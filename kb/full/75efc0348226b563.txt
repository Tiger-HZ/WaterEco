Boosting water evaporation with clever 
surface design
Shuai Guo, Zhen Yu & Swee Ching Tan
Efficient evaporation is crucial for 
solar-powered water purification, resource 
recovery and energy utilization, yet boosting 
evaporation without using more energy 
remains challenging. Here, a smart surface on 
which water binds weakly, thereby enabling 
rapid solar evaporation, makes clean, 
sustainable water production more efficient.
Escalating global freshwater scarcity has intensified the search for 
zero-energy and eco-friendly methods for clean water production1. 
Solar-driven interfacial evaporation systems, which localize heat at 
the water–air interface, instead of transferring heat to the bulk water, 
for rapid evaporation, offer a promising solution2. Over the past dec-
ade, solar-driven evaporation has been widely explored for various 
applications ranging from clean water production and mineral recov-
ery to electricity and fuel generation3–5. Yet a central and long-lasting 
challenge remains: how evaporation can be accelerated under a fixed 
energy input. Progress in this area can be broadly understood through 
two complementary levels: thermodynamics and kinetics. Thermo-
dynamic strategies focus on methods such as reducing the energy 
demand for evaporation, harvesting ambient heat and recycling 
condensation latent heat. These methods have pushed evaporation 
rates beyond the thermodynamic evaporation limit6. Recent efforts 
have shifted towards kinetic approaches to accelerate evaporation, 
but remain mainly focused on micro- or nanoscale water and vapour 
regulation7,8. Earlier work has sped up evaporation by managing thin 
water layers, designing channels that guide water flow and using a 
switchable water supply, while preventing salt from building up7. 
Now, writing in Nature Sustainability, Chu and colleagues9 push this 
kinetic manipulation to the molecular scale. Their central idea is that 
if water interacts weakly with the evaporation surface, the molecules 
can detach more easily. This lowers the energy needed for evapora-
tion and allows water to escape as vapour more quickly (Fig. 1). As a 
proof of concept, they construct a heterogeneous structure made of 
two phases: graphene oxide and a liquid-metal alloy called eutectic 
gallium indium. The boundary between these materials, formed by 
oxygen-containing groups in graphene oxide and oxidized gallium 
species on the liquid-metal surface, reduces how strongly water mol-
ecules attach to the material. In device form, the resulting evapora-
tor with a thin architecture demonstrates a high evaporation rate of 
3.64 kg m−2 h−1 while maintaining solar-to-vapour efficiencies above 
95%. This performance value is comparable to that of many reported 
advanced solar evaporators. Importantly, strategies that enhance 
evaporation rates through molecular-level interface design have 
rarely been reported.
Another notable aspect of this work is that the authors combined 
computer simulations with real-time (in situ) experiments to under -
stand how the material accelerates evaporation. Computer models 
were used to examine how easily water molecules detach from the 
surface under different interface designs. The simulations suggest 
that when two different materials are joined together at the surface, the 
interaction between nearby water molecules becomes weaker, making 
it easier for them to escape as vapour. To test this idea experimentally, 
the authors used in situ infrared spectroscopy under simulated sunlight 
to monitor how the water structure on the surface changes during evap-
oration. Consistent with the simulations, the graphene oxide–eutectic 
gallium indium surface reached a dry state faster than conventional 
materials. It is worth noting that the combined use of simulations and 
in situ characterization has been widely adopted in previous studies; 
thus, this work does not introduce fundamental breakthroughs in 
computational methods or characterization techniques. Looking 
ahead, integrating artificial intelligence-assisted molecular dynam-
ics simulations into the materials screening process could enable the 
rapid identification of high-performance material candidates. When 
coupled with experimental validation via in situ characterization, 
this approach is expected to substantially shorten the trial-and-error 
cycle and accelerate the development of next-generation interfacial 
solar evaporators.
The broader implications of this work lie in prompting a re- 
examination of the evaporation process itself. Previously, the main-
stream explanation for evaporation rates exceeding the so-called 
‘thermodynamic evaporation limit’ in two-dimensional evapora -
tion systems typically relied on the ‘reduced apparent enthalpy’

due to the lack of direct experimental evidence to demonstrate its 
validity10,11. Furthermore, traditional explanations often focus on 
thermodynamic possibilities while largely neglecting the crucial role 
of kinetic factors in the actual evaporation process. By contrast, Chu 
and colleagues suggest that weak interactions between water and 
the evaporation surface offer an alternative explanation for rapid 
evaporation. It should be noted that they used the reduced apparent 
enthalpy to evaluate the solar-to-vapour efficiencies, but they have 
not yet obtained direct thermodynamic evidence for the enthalpy 
decrease. Because evaporation in porous materials can be viewed 
as a continuous adsorption–desorption cycle of water molecules, 
future studies could benefit from examining the process across mul-
tiple scales, from molecular to macroscopic. Integrating large-scale 
simulations with advanced experimental characterization may help to 
connect molecular-level interactions at the interface with the overall 
evaporation performance of the material. Despite these limitations, 
Chu and colleagues propose a promising design approach to adjust the 
evaporation process by regulating how easily water molecules detach 
from surfaces. This design concept is expected to provide inspiration 
for further research and development directions in thermal manage-
ment, catalytic reaction engineering, micro- and nanoscale sensing 
and aerosol science.
