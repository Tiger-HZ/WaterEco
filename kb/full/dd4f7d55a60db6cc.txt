Abstract
The telluric field frequency selection method (TFFSM) has been proposed for more 
than 40 years and has achieved good exploration results in groundwater exploration. 
The cause of its anomaly formation has always been one of the issues that people 
are concerned about. This article mainly explains the causes of TFFSM anomalies 
through experimental detection near a known water well, and demonstrates its 
relevant issues in application. Firstly, the triple-frequency observation was carried out 
along the survey line to quickly determine the location of groundwater. Secondly, 
the synchronous observations of three type of frequency selectors in the case of 
electrode separation and electrode sharing were adopted respectively. Thirdly, the 
variation of TFFSM exploration results with electrode spacing ( MN) was studied. 
Finally, audio-magnetotelluric method (AMT) measurements were taken at two 
points on the survey line. Through the above experiments, we obtained the potential 
difference (ΔV) profile curves and their pseudo-sections of TFFSM under different 
conditions, as well as the power spectral density curves of electric and magnetic 
field of AMT, and corresponding apparent resistivity curves. According to the analysis 
of the experimental results, it can be concluded that the existence of groundwater 
will cause static effects in the detection results of TFFSM. TFFSM has relatively loosen 
grounding requirements for electrodes, but the size of MN has a certain influence on 
exploration depth. From the comparison between TFFSM and AMT, the former has 
stronger anti-interference ability and site adaptability, and TFFSM can be applied to 
urban geological exploration in the future.
Highlights
 • Through the telluric field frequency selection method (TEFSM) detection near a 
known water well, it is verified that the cause of the anomaly is static effect.
 • In TEFSM exploration, the grounding mode of the electrode has little effect on 
the exploration results.
 • In the practical application of the TEFSM, there is a certain correlation between 
the exploration depth and the electrode spacing ( MN). The smaller MN, the 
smaller the observation value and the shallower the exploration depth.
 • Compared with the AMT method, the TEFSM instrument is lighter, more resistant 
to interference and more adaptable to the site, and can achieve satisfactory 
results in some areas where AMT cannot work.

 1 Introduction
The telluric field frequency selection method (TFFSM) is commonly referred to as the 
frequency selection method (FSM) and has a history spanning nearly five decades [ 24]. 
In prior literature, it has also been designated the telluric electric field frequency selec -
tion method (TEFSM) [30]. Developed in China, this method emulates the observational 
paradigm of magnetotellurics (MT), focusing exclusively on measurements of the hori -
zontal electric field components at the Earth’s surface [ 7, 13]. Today, TFFSM has been 
widely adopted in groundwater exploration. For instance, the frequency selector devel -
oped by the Hunan Puqi Geological Exploration Equipment Research Institute (Hunan 
Province, China) has been deployed in over 180 countries worldwide [ 28]. Nevertheless, 
certain geophysical experts remain skeptical about this method—primarily due to a lack 
of theoretical research on this method in the academic community.
Although both TFFSM and MT are grounded in the theory of natural electromagnetic 
(EM) wave propagation, their field signal acquisition strategies differ fundamentally. 
TFFSM employs hardware-based frequency selection: predefined detection frequencies 
are set in advance to target specific subsurface depths, enabling targeted suppression of 
noise. In contrast, MT records time-domain signals, from which electric and magnetic 
field components at various frequencies are subsequently extracted via spectral analy -
sis [ 34]. TFFSM typically presents raw horizontal electric field measurements directly 
as its output, requiring minimal post-acquisition data processing, MT, by comparison, 
relies on observed electric and magnetic fields to compute transfer functions—including 
apparent resistivity, phase, and other derived parameters. Moreover, while MT entails 
higher operational costs and greater logistical complexity, it offers significantly deeper 
penetration, enabling investigation of the deep crust [ 19]. The operating frequencies of 
TFFSM typically range from 10 to 5000 Hz, with the lowest measurable frequency for 
certain instruments reaching as low as 2  Hz. TFFSM is primarily employed for near-
surface groundwater exploration within depths of up to 500 m [ 22, 29], and has yielded 
satisfactory geological outcomes in numerous field applications [ 1, 9, 14–16]. Follow-
ing years of practical deployment and systematic research, Yang et al. [ 24, 26] identified 
static shift—commonly associated with the natural electromagnetic (EM method—as 
the predominant cause of TFFSM anomalies,although weak radar-like reflection signals 
may occasionally be present [ 6, 28]. Concurrently, they introduced the concept of the 
telluric magnetic field frequency selection method (TMFSM) [30].
Audio-magnetotellurics (AMT), first proposed by Berdichevski et al. [ 2] as an exten -
sion of MT method, shares identical observational principles and measurement param -
eters with MT but operates over a higher-frequency band (> 1 Hz). Consequently, AMT 
is generally suited for investigations targeting depths of up to approximately 2 km [5, 18]. 
To date, AMT has matured both theoretically and technologically and is widely applied 
in groundwater, mineral, and engineering geophysical surveys [ 8, 21, 33]. Nevertheless, 
compared with TFFSM, both MT and AMT are designed for deeper targets, thus, static

In this study, three distinct frequency-selection instruments for TFFSM and a con -

experiment at a potable water well site. Through comprehensive analysis of the acquired 
field data, the applicability and effectiveness of these two geophysical approaches under 
culturally disturbed conditions are critically evaluated. Furthermore, the results substan-
tiate that TFFSM anomalies predominantly arise from static effects. The influence of 
electrode spacing on TFFSM survey outcomes is also systematically examined.
2 Experiment site
The experimental site is located at the geographical coordinates 27° 54 ′ 9.860 ″ N and 
112° 55 ′ 7.658 ″ E—specifically, the plum garden of Hunan University of Science and 
Technology (HNUST), Xiangtan, China (Fig.  1). A potable water well is situated at the 
corner of a road intersection (Fig.  1), which was identified and drilled in 1988 by fac -
ulty and students from the Department of Geology at HNUST. At that time, geophysi -

source of domestic water supply for the southern campus of HNUST. In recent years, 
however, it has been retained solely as an emergency backup, following substantial 
upgrades to the municipal tap-water supply infrastructure.

approximation—where displacement current is negligible—EM wave propagation in

larger skin depths and thus greater potential penetration, whereas higher frequencies 
correspond to shallower investigation depths—as directly implied by Eq. (3).
3.2 TFFSM
TFFSM primarily exploits the static effects—also termed static shifts—of natural elec -
tromagnetic (EM) methods to detect shallow electrical anomalies [ 26]. In layered Earth 
media, EM fields exhibit predominantly horizontal distribution, and induced currents 
flow preferentially in the horizontal plane. When a near-surface electrically heteroge -
neous body is present, it perturbs the ambient EM field, generating localized regions 
of intensified or attenuated field distortion. This distortion disrupts the uniformity of

primary electric field via a multiplicative factor—termed the static shift factor. From the 
perspective of electric flux, the fundamental cause of static shifts is the localized distor -
tion of electric flux due to near-surface charge accumulation (i.e., a non-uniform source 
term), rather than the electromotive force induced by time-varying magnetic flux. At 
sufficiently low frequencies and for heterogeneities whose size is small compared to the 
EM wavelength, the magnetic field remains unaffected by interfacial charge accumula -
tion [23]. As only the electric field is modified, the apparent resistivity derived from fre -
quency-domain EM measurements undergoes a frequency-independent, multiplicative 
shift in logarithmic space.
This theoretical framework indicates that the static offset in frequency-domain EM 
arises from distortion of the horizontal potential difference between measurement 
electrodes—induced either by near-electrode surface conductors or by shallow sub -
surface heterogeneities. Although static offset is conventionally treated as an unavoid -
able source of interference—particularly when targeting deeper structures [ 3]—TFFSM 
deliberately leverages this effect. Theoretical modeling demonstrates robust correlations 
between static shift characteristics and the geometric, electrical, and geological proper -
ties of shallow heterogeneities [26]. By quantitatively analyzing the spatial and amplitude 
variations of static offsets, TFFSM facilitates the inference of shallow target geometry,

near-surface electrical anomalies.
4 Instruments and parameters
Three frequency selection instruments—namely, the PQWT-TC150, PQWT-TC300, 
and PQWT-TC1200—were employed in the TFFSM experiments. Their respective 
maximum detection depths are approximately 150 m, 300 m, and 1200 m. The PQWT-
TC150 operates across 33 discrete frequencies within the range of 170–5000  Hz; the 
PQWT-TC300 covers a broader band of 12–5000 Hz using 40 discrete frequencies; and 
the PQWT-TC1200 supports three configurable maximum detection depths—600  m, 
900  m, and 1200  m. For this study, the 900-m configuration was selected, utilizing 48 
discrete frequencies spanning 8–5000 Hz. All instruments exhibit a measurement res -
olution of 0.001  mV and are rated for operational use within an ambient temperature 
range of −20 to +50 °C.
The experimental procedure comprised the following steps:
(1) A triple-frequency (25  Hz, 67  Hz, and 170  Hz) potential difference survey was 
conducted along the designated survey line to preliminarily identify zones exhibiting 
relatively low apparent resistivity anomalies—indicative of potential subsurface 
groundwater presence. Data acquisition was performed using the PQWT-TC150, 
with a station interval and electrode spacing (MN) of 1 m and 10 m, respectively.
(2) Three frequency selectors simultaneously acquire data at the same measurement 
point using their respective electrode pairs, and then collectively advance along the 
survey line (Fig. 4a, c).
(3) Three frequency selectors share a single pair of electrodes to simultaneously acquire 
data at the same measurement point, and then collectively advance along the survey 
line (Fig. 4b, c).
(4) Repeated measurements are conducted along the survey line using three distinct 
electrode spacings ( MN = 2, 6, and 10 m). Data acquisition is performed using the 
PQWT-TC300 frequency selector. The shielding receiving cable supplied by the

5 Results and discussion
5.1 TFFSM
A triple-frequency acquisition mode (170 Hz, 67 Hz, and 25 Hz) was initially adopted 
to record the potential difference (ΔV) along the survey line and to identify approximate 
anomaly locations (Fig.  6). At each measuring station, the typical acquisition time was 
approximately 3 s—corresponding to rapid, preliminary reconnaissance in practical field 
surveys.
The three operating frequencies of the frequency selector—25  Hz, 67  Hz, and 
170 Hz—are pre-set by the manufacturer. These frequencies were selected for the follow-
ing reasons [13]: first, 25 Hz corresponds to one of the Schumann resonance frequencies 
generated by electric field resonance between the Earth's surface and the ionosphere; 
second, 67 Hz and 170 Hz are associated with natural magnetotelluric field fluctuations 
caused by distant lightning and other atmospheric activities; third, these frequencies 
avoid odd multiples of 50  Hz, thereby minimizing interference from power-line noise 
commonly present in industrial environments.
According to the exploration results obtained using the TFFSM triple-frequency mode, 
an anomalous zone is identified at 8–9 m along the survey line, characterized by a rela -
tively pronounced low-potential anomaly (Fig.  6). To investigate this anomaly in greater 
detail, additional multi-frequency surveys were conducted. The sampling frequency 
range spans 8–5000 Hz. Figures  7, 8 and 9 present the simultaneously acquired data 
from three instruments, each employing its designated electrode configuration (Fig.  4a).

At the 9-m position along the survey line, a distinct low-potential-difference anomaly 
is consistently observed across all three profile curves (Figs. 7a, 8a and 9a), thereby cor-
roborating the reliability of the initial triple-frequency detection results (Fig. 6). Notably, 
the temporal synchrony of curve fluctuations is superior for the TC150 profile (Fig.  7a) 
compared with those of TC300 and TC1200 (Figs. 8a and 9a). This difference arises from 
the distinct frequency bands employed by each instrument: asynchronous behavior is 
predominantly observed in the lower-frequency components. The dotted–solid lines in 
Figs. 7a, 8a, and 9a denote the detection results corresponding to the lowest operational 
frequency of each instrument. Specifically, the minimum frequency of the TC150 fre -
quency selector exceeds 100 Hz, whereas those of TC300 and TC1200 are approximately 
12 Hz and 8 Hz, respectively. As established in prior studies [ 12], high-frequency elec -
tromagnetic (EM) signals exhibit shallow penetration depths, whereas low-frequency 
signals achieve greater depth of investigation. However, deep-penetrating low-frequency 
signals are inherently weaker and more susceptible to cultural noise. Consequently, the 
observed asynchrony in the profile curves (Figs. 8a and 9a) is likely attributable to envi -
ronmental interference.
According to the EM wave skin depth formula and the empirically derived exploration 
depth coefficient [1], the corresponding pseudo-sections of ΔV were generated (Figs. 7b, 
8b and 9b). A “noodle-like” anomaly is observed between the 8th and 11th meters along 
the survey line in Figs. 7b, 8b, and 9b—this feature reflects the static shift effect. Con -
currently, it also indicates the presence of shallow, low-resistivity, water-saturated geo -
logical bodies. Although the acquisition frequencies differ across the three datasets, the 
manifestation of the static effect varies in detail; notably, the effect is most pronounced 
in Fig. 7b. In Fig. 9b, the larger exploration depth necessitates lower frequency sampling, 
resulting in a comparatively weaker expression of the static effect. Taking the 3-mV con-
tour as an example, its length along the downward extension direction in the pseudo-
sections of TC150 (Fig.  7b), TC300 (Fig.  8b), and TC1200 (Fig.  9b) exceeds 150  m, is 
approximately 100 m, and is less than 100 m, respectively.

Next, we examine the influence of electrode spacing ( MN) on the observation results 
of the TFFSM. In all preceding experiments, MN was fixed at the commonly adopted 
value of 10 m; therefore, only the detection results for MN = 6 m and MN = 2 m are pre-

decline markedly: the peak ΔV values in Figs. 8, 10, 13 and 14 are 29.979 mV, 30.428 mV,

11a, 13a and 14a), reflecting increasingly refined contour-line resolution (Fig. 14b).

the pseudo-sections—similar to those shown in Figs. 11b and 12b—is unreliable and arises 
from an inherent limitation of the method. Specifically, as exploration depth increases, the 
measured signal attenuates and the operating frequency decreases; consequently, in prac-
tical applications, it becomes difficult to unambiguously interpret the deepest low-poten-
tial anomaly as a true low-resistivity feature.

When MN = 2 m, the static shift effect induced by shallow groundwater (as observed in 
Fig. 14b) vanishes. Correspondingly, the observed potential difference values decrease, and 
the entire pseudo-section is predominantly characterized by low-values (blue) anomalies 
(Fig. 14b). This observation indicates that TEFSM results are sensitive to electrode spac -
ing (MN). To detect deeper targets using TFFSM, a larger MN spacing may be required. 
The underlying cause likely lies in the burial depth of the water-bearing cavities—exceed-
ing 40 m—combined with an excessively small MN spacing of only 2 m. Under these con-
ditions, the potential difference induced by the deep cavities falls below the detectable 
threshold within the 2-m electrode interval. Moreover, reducing MN exacerbates instabil-
ity—particularly in the low-frequency portion—of the acquired data. A careful compari -
son of the profile curves for MN = 10 m (Fig.  11a), MN = 6 m (Fig.  13a), and MN = 2 m 
(Fig. 14a) reveals that decreasing MN leads to an increasing number of profile curves con-
verging toward the horizontal axis (0 mV), and these curves behavior become increasingly 
erratic. Further examination confirms that the frequencies associated with these anoma-
lous profile curves are generally below ~20 Hz. Based on these experimental findings, it is 
unreasonable for frequency selector manufacturers to prescribe a fixed MN spacing (e.g., 
10 m or 20 m) across all applications. Instead, users should adaptively select MN according 
to the expected burial depth of the target during practical surveys.
In the application of TFFSM, the natural EM field exhibits diurnal variation; however, 
its overall amplitude remains relatively stable between 08:00 and 18:00 daily [ 32]. More-
over, TFFSM enables rapid data acquisition—in practice, the observation time for a single 
survey line is typically short. Consequently, diurnal variations are routinely neglected in 
routine processing. Thus, spatial variations in the electric field components along the sur-
vey line direction directly reflect lateral and vertical changes in subsurface electrical prop-
erties. Based on over four decades of practical application experience with TFFSM, the 
influence of diurnal variation on exploration results is negligible and can be entirely disre-
garded [28].
5.2 AMT
To further investigate the influence of the surrounding environment on natural-source EM 
methods, AMT field experiments were conducted at the test site (Fig.  5). A vector mea -
surement configuration was employed, simultaneously recording orthogonal electric field 
components (Ex and Ey) and orthogonal magnetic field components ( Hx and Hy). Non-
polarizing electrodes were used to measure the electric field components. Due to spatial 
constraints at the site, electric field measurements were restricted to two positions along 
the survey line—x = 10 m and x = 15 m—while the magnetic field sensors remained fixed 
at their initially determined locations throughout the observation period.
For direct comparison with the TFFSM results, only the power spectral density (PSD) 
curves of the electric field component Ex and the PSD curves of the magnetic field com -
ponent Hy are presented for the AMT dataset (Fig. 15). Both the PSD (PSDEx and PSDHy) 
curves exhibit pronounced fluctuations and discontinuities across frequency, indicating 
significant contamination from ambient EM interference. The experimental site is located 
within a university campus, where multiple concurrent interference sources are present—
including vehicular and pedestrian traffic on adjacent roads, nearby high-voltage power 
transmission lines, and various electrical appliances and infrastructure. In practice, such 
a highly disturbed EM environment renders AMT unsuitable for routine geophysical

exploration. In contrast, TFFSM demonstrates robust performance near high-voltage 
transmission lines: when the survey line is oriented parallel to the transmission lines, high-
quality subsurface imaging can be achieved [27].
The variation trends of the PSD Ex curves at the measurement stations located at x = 
10 m and x = 15 m are generally consistent (Fig.  15a); the observed differences are likely 
attributable primarily to geological heterogeneity between the two sites. In contrast, the 
PSDHy curves for the two stations overlap almost perfectly (Fig.  15b)—this is due to the 
fixed position of the magnetic probe. Minor discrepancies between the two curves arise 
mainly from variations in ambient noise levels during data acquisition and diurnal geo -
magnetic fluctuations.
The AMT measurements of the electric and magnetic field components exhibit poor 
reliability (Fig. 15); consequently, the derived apparent resistivities fail to accurately rep -
resent the true subsurface electrical structure at the survey stations (Fig.  16). Figure  16 
presents the apparent resistivity (ρxy) curves computed from Ex and Hy, where the black 
dot–solid line and red triangle–dashed line corresponds to x = 10 m and x = 15 m, respec-
tively. These ρxy curves display pronounced fluctuations and are severely contaminated by 
strong noise—particularly at frequencies below 10 Hz (Fig. 16).

We now revisit the detection results obtained using the PQWT-TC300 frequency selec-
tor at x = 10 m and x = 15 m, as shown in Fig.  8. Figure 17 plots the frequency-depen -
dent variations of the TFFSM Ex component at the two survey stations. Unlike the highly 
erratic behavior observed in Figs. 15 and 16, the curves in Fig. 17 exhibit relatively smooth 
and gradual changes. This suggests that the frequency selector is significantly less suscep-
tible to external electromagnetic interference. Its superior anti-interference performance 
is likely attributable to its unique signal acquisition methodology. As introduced previ -
ously, unlike AMT—which acquires data in the time domain—the TFFSM instrument 
determines its sampling frequencies through dedicated hardware configurations. More -
over, noise suppression is implemented directly at the hardware level, which contributes 
to the stability of the acquired signals and enables rapid data acquisition. Therefore, in the 
future development of AMT instrumentation, we propose adopting a frequency-selective 
data acquisition approach—namely, sequentially sampling signals at discrete frequencies 
across the frequency domain—thereby enabling the pre-configuration of filters to more 
effectively suppress ambient electromagnetic noise. Furthermore, in contrast to Fig. 16, the 
detectable frequency range in Fig. 17 extends exclusively above 10 Hz, thereby effectively 
avoiding the dominant low-frequency interference (< 10 Hz) that severely degrades AMT 
data quality.
6 Conclusions
Field experiments employing both TFFSM and AMT were conducted in close proximity 
to a water well within a university campus—a site characterized by intense anthropogenic 
electromagnetic interference. Based on rigorous analysis of the acquired data, the follow-
ing conclusions are drawn:
(1) At locations coinciding with water-bearing geological structures, TFFSM yields 
distinct low-potential-difference (Δ V) anomalies. Pronounced static shifts are 
consistently observed in both Δ V profile curves and pseudo-sections. Among the 
three frequency selectors tested, the TC150 exhibits the most severe static effect.

(2) Whether each frequency selector employs its dedicated electrode pair or shares a 
single common electrode pair, the resulting TFFSM measurements remain statistically 
indistinguishable—indicating negligible influence of electrode configuration on 
detection fidelity.
(3) With MN = 10  m, site-specific interference significantly degrades TFFSM performance 
below approximately 20  Hz. Moreover, reducing MN further exacerbates low-
frequency instability in TFFSM responses. In contrast, AMT measurements are 
compromised across the entire frequency band, rendering them unreliable under 
these conditions.
(4) Decreasing MN leads to a systematic reduction in both the magnitude of observed 
ΔV and the effective exploration depth of TFFSM. Notably, when MN = 2 m, static 
effects are nearly eliminated from the recorded signals. This suggests that MN must 
be carefully optimized according to target depth requirements. Presently, several 
commercial frequency selectors feature fixed 10-m measurement cables—an 
inflexible design potentially unsuitable for deep targets (~ 1000 m).
(5) TFFSM demonstrates exceptional site adaptability: the instrumentation is 
lightweight and portable, enabling deployment in confined spaces. Owing to its 
frequency-domain signal acquisition strategy, this method demonstrates superior 
performance in mitigating environmental electromagnetic interference compared 
with conventional MT and AMT techniques. Consequently, TFFSM constitutes a 
viable and practical technique for urban geophysical prospecting.
