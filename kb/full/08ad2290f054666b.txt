Purpose: This paper analyses the trends and drivers of floods and droughts that frequently occur in Rwanda. Design/Methodology/Approach: The 44-year CHIRPS and ERA5 datasets were partitioned into 3 epochs to account for climatic oscillations like El Niño. The analysis was done through climatic water balance calculation, drought and flood monitoring indices, agricultural seasons analysis, and statistical analysis like trend detection, drought anatomy, and Extreme Value analysis. Research Limitation: The study is limited by technical constraints of the CHIRPS and ERA5 datasets, which may underestimate high-intensity peaks or miss low-level moisture in high-elevation regions. Findings: Temperature has increased since 1981, but precipitation shows a dip-and-recovery trend. Storms are becoming more explosive and less frequent. The agricultural season B is shorter than before. Droughts are caused primarily by increased evapotranspiration. Practical Implication: The results suggest a revision of infrastructure design standards, specifically drainage systems and IDF curves, to accommodate the "new normal" of explosive storms. More research is needed to improve rainwater harvesting. Social Implication: Increasing climate volatility threatens food security and rural livelihoods dependent on rain-fed agriculture. Originality/ Value: This paper provides a data-driven framework that integrates remote sensing and multidimensional indices to address research gaps in dual-hazard analysis in topographically complex, data-scarce regions.
