If water can make its way in and out of the sovereign imaginary and its primitive technology, if it can wet the map&s;s function as the most abstract construct in political geography, could it be that it carries the agency to dampen conditions of (post-)colonial life too? Could we conceive a material consistency in technologies that curtail access to air for the Black migrant, an element to disrupt the human lung&s;s primary function to inhale oxygen? As I was reading Gurnah&s;s 1980s novels, Pilgrims Way (1988) and Dottie (1990) , I felt a very subtle change in my breathing pattern that I had not noticed before, almost as imperceptible as breathing itself, but I had a hard time placing it in the order of an empathic response. It was not that I was choking for Gurnah&s;s characters, or that I had identified with the migrant, drowning once in water and once in the socio-bureaucratic antagonisms long after arriving on land; it felt more like the reading body was attuning itself to the gasping for air in the confinement of possibilities of flourishing after the ship docks.
