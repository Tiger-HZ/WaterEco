The transition to renewable energy has positioned biomass as a critical component of the United Kingdom's decarbonisation strategy. This study investigates the viability of wood and timber processing for energy production, employing Scotland as a case study. Utilising a mixed-methods approach combining semi-structured interviews with four wood recycling companies and biomass plant managers in Scotland, secondary analysis of industry data (2015-2024), and evidence from RWE's Markinch CHP facility, the research evaluates four dimensions: current processing infrastructure, biomass energy contributions, government policy effectiveness, and environmental sustainability outcomes. Findings reveal that the UK processes 4.31 million tonnes of waste wood annually, with Scotland possessing an estimated 10.7 million metric tonnes of softwood availability over the next 25 years. Softwood constitutes 92% of UK timber usage, while hardwood comprises 84% of wood fuel. Biomass contributes 12.9% of UK electricity supply, sufficient to power over 840,000 households. Government policies—particularly the Renewable Obligation Certificates (ROCs) and Renewable Heat Incentive (RHI)—have transformed the sector, reducing landfill disposal from 50% historically to minimal current levels through landfill taxes exceeding £100 per tonne. Life Cycle Assessment demonstrates that untreated waste wood achieves up to 91% greenhouse gas reduction compared to fossil fuels. However, challenges include the grandfathering time limits on renewable schemes (20-year periods), seasonal operational variability, and contaminant management. The study concludes that wood-to-energy processing is viable and environmentally sustainable, recommending increased infrastructure investment, policy renewal mechanisms, strategic integration with housing development for district heating, and enhanced monitoring of transportation emissions.
