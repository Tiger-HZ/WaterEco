Abstract
Sustainable management of tropical soils is critical for maintaining ecosystem services, agricultural productivity, and 
environmental sustainability. This study develops a Remote Sensing (RS) and Geographic Information System (GIS)-
based Digital Soil Mapping (DSM) framework to quantitatively assess soil health and environmental vulnerability in the 
Bhavani River Basin, South India. Multi-source geospatial datasets, including satellite-derived spectral indices, terrain 
derivatives, land use/land cover (LULC) information, and SoilGrids environmental variables, were integrated to char -
acterize key soil properties such as soil organic carbon (SOC), soil organic carbon density (OCD), soil organic carbon 
stock (OCS), nitrogen, pH, soil texture, cation exchange capacity (CEC), electrical conductivity (EC), bulk density (BD), 
and available water-holding capacity (AWHC). Advanced machine-learning algorithms and spatial analytical techniques 
were employed to generate high-resolution digital soil maps and evaluate the spatial variability of soil properties across 
the basin. The results reveal pronounced spatial heterogeneity in soil characteristics and environmental conditions. The 
southwestern and western regions exhibited high SOC (451), OCD (180), OCS (93), and nitrogen (4197) values, indicating 
strong carbon–nutrient coupling and enhanced soil fertility. These regions store approximately 2–4 times more carbon and 
nitrogen than the eastern basin, highlighting their significance as major carbon sequestration and nutrient reservoirs. In 
contrast, intensively cultivated and rapidly urbanizing lowland areas displayed lower carbon stocks, reduced soil quality, 
and higher degradation risk. Elevated CEC values (up to 373 cmol( +)/kg) in parts of the eastern basin suggest substantial 
nutrient-retention capacity despite relatively low organic carbon content. Environmental Vulnerability Index (EVI) and 
hotspot analyses identified critical zones vulnerable to degradation and requiring immediate conservation measures. The 
study provides a robust scientific framework for precision land management, watershed conservation, and climate-resilient 
soil management strategies, supporting long-term ecosystem sustainability and resilience in tropical river basins.

Introduction
Terrestrial ecosystems depend on healthy soils for local to 
global ecosystem functions (Ponyane et al., 2025; Wank -
müller et al., 2024). To address global environmental issues 
like erosion, land degradation, biodiversity loss, and climate 
change, spatially variable soil characteristics and functional 
processes are essential (Kaur et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023; 
Adeniyi et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026). Developing region-
specific soil function plans requires high-resolution, harmo-
nized soil data (Son et al., 2026). Remote sensing, Digital 
Soil Mapping (DSM), and geospatial data integration have 
improved agricultural planning, environmental monitoring, 
conservation, and predictive modelling by producing spa -
tially explicit soil information from global soil databases 
and satellite observations (Stoorvogel et al., 2017; Han et 
al., 2019; Shi et al., 2026).
Protecting soil biodiversity, ensuring long-term food 
security, and preventing soil erosion through land reha -
bilitation and sustainable land-use planning are urgent 
priorities (Soo et al., 2026). Since geologic time, soil has 
supported terrestrial biodiversity and regulates biodiversity, 
carbon storage, and water and nutrient cycles for food and 
water security (Centeno et al., 2020). The need for spatially 
detailed soil databases for sustainable land management is 
growing (Arun et al., 2020).
Soils produce biomass, recycle carbon, and store it, 
affecting food security, climate regulation, and ecosystem 
resilience (Poggio et al., 2021; Asongwe et al., 2025). Seven 
SDGs are linked to soil resources in the UN 2030 Sustain -
able Development Agenda. However, agricultural intensi -
fication and fast land-use changes have upset carbon and 
nutrient flows. Accelerated erosion and soil organic carbon 
oxidation—the Earth's greatest near-surface carbon pool—
have exacerbated greenhouse gas emissions and ecologi -
cal deterioration. Human security is linked to soil security, 
which supports food production, carbon sequestration, 
water filtration, and nutrient cycling. Agriculture is crucial 
to livelihoods in emerging nations like India (Das et al., 
2022; Morton et al., 2023). Accelerated agricultural growth, 
urbanization, and climate unpredictability have diminished 
soil fertility and biodiversity in southern India's Bhavani 
River Basin, threatening ecological productivity and social 
stability.
Several Tamil Nadu studies have addressed land-use 
change, climate impacts, and soil erosion (Samuel et al., 
2025; Vijayaraghavalu et al., 2025; Bharath et al., 2026; 
Kaliraj et al., 2026), however soil biodiversity monitoring 
and spatial soil security evaluations are lacking. Combin -
ing biological indicators with geographic models enhances 
degradation hotspot identification and conservation plan-
ning (Perović et al., 2023). Recent study shows that remote 
sensing, DSM, and molecular technologies can reveal soil 
dynamics and ecosystem resilience. Biophysical, biological, 
and spatial datasets can help understand soil dynamics and 
human–environment risks, according to studies from China, 
Ethiopia, Karnataka, and Rajasthan.
Nutrient loss, erosion, and industrial pressures degrade 
soil in the Bhavani River Basin, which grows sugarcane, 
paddy, banana, and jasmine. Local food and livelihood 
security depend on its dominant Fluvisols, Entisols, and 
Alfisols. This study uses soil biodiversity indicators, soil 
property databases, and DSM to create a geospatial soil 
security framework for sustainable soil management. Global 
datasets like SoilGrids250m (Hengl et al., 2017), region-
specific environmental variables (Reuter & Hengl, 2016; 
Nussbaum et al., 2018; Arrouays et al., 2020; Bao et al., 
2024), and 250-m grid modelling are used to identify ero -
sion and biodiversity loss hotspots using advanced machine 
learning and geostatistical methods (Rossiter et al., 2022). 
This study aims to develop an integrated Remote Sensing 
and GIS-based Digital Soil Mapping (DSM) framework for 
the quantitative assessment of soil health and environmen -
tal vulnerability in the Bhavani River Basin, South India. 
By utilizing multi-source geospatial datasets, advanced 
machine learning algorithms, and spatial modeling tech -
niques, the research seeks to characterize and predict the 
spatial distribution of critical soil properties, including soil 
organic carbon (SOC), pH, texture, cation exchange capac -
ity (CEC), electrical conductivity (EC), bulk density (BD), 
and available water-holding capacity (AWHC). The study 
further aims to identify patterns of soil degradation, envi -
ronmental vulnerability hotspots, and areas of ecological 
resilience across the basin. Through the integration of soil 
quality indicators and environmental variables, the research 
intends to provide a robust scientific framework for soil 
health assessment, environmental monitoring, sustainable 
land management, and climate-resilient watershed conser -
vation planning.

A major hydrological sub-basin of the Cauvery River sys -
tem is the Bhavani River Basin in Tamil Nadu and Kerala 
(Fig. 1). The river travels southeast from the biodiverse Nil-
giri Hills of the Western Ghats to join the Cauvery in Trichy, 
supporting vital ecological and social services. The 7,500 
km2 basin includes rugged hills, undulating pediments, lush 
alluvial plains, and growing metropolitan areas. The earthen 
dam Bhavani Sagar Reservoir manages irrigation, drinking

Data Sources and Collection
Remote sensing, socioeconomic variables, and global soil 
property datasets were integrated to assess soil and human 
security in the Bhavani River Basin. Key datasets and spa -
tial resolutions are summarized in Table 1. The primary 
soil information source was ISRIC’s World Soil Informa -
tion Service (WoSIS), which provides harmonized, quality-
controlled global soil profile data. ISRIC’s SoilGrids250m, 
a machine-learning-based product, supplied predictions of 
pH, organic carbon, bulk density, cation exchange capac -
ity, texture fractions, and nutrients at six standard depths 
(0–200 cm), using environmental covariates related to 
climate, terrain, and vegetation (Hengl et al., 2017; Pog -
gio et al., 2021). These 250-m maps improve regional soil 
assessment in data-poor areas of South India. To verify local 
accuracy, SoilGrids outputs were compared with national 
datasets (e.g., NBSS&LUP) using predicted means and 
median quantiles. SoilGrids 2.0 further enhances spatial soil 
variability detection, supporting soil classification, ecosys-
tem evaluation, and sustainable land-use planning (Rossiter 
et al., 2022).

Data Integration and Processing
Soil property datasets were extracted and analyzed from the 
ISRIC SoilGrids database through the Google Earth Engine 
(GEE) platform. The selected soil parameters included bulk 
density (BD), cation exchange capacity (CEC), coarse frag-
ments, clay, sand and silt fractions, total nitrogen (TN), 
soil pH (H₂O), soil organic carbon (SOC), and soil organic 
carbon stock. These datasets represent spatially modelled 
soil characteristics at different standard depths (0–5 cm, 
5–15 cm, 15–30 cm, and other depth intervals) with a spa -
tial resolution of 250 m. The SoilGrids layers were used to 
evaluate the spatial variability of soil properties and their 
influence on soil health conditions across the Bhavani River 
Basin. The Bhavani River Basin boundary was delineated 
using hydrological analysis based on the Shuttle Radar 
Topography Mission (SRTM) Digital Elevation Model 
(DEM). The extracted basin boundary shapefile was used 
as the Area of Interest (AOI) for clipping and processing all 
soil datasets. Google Earth Engine (GEE) was employed for 
cloud-based data acquisition, preprocessing, visualization, 
and export of soil property layers. JavaScript-based GEE 
scripts were developed to select the required soil bands, clip 
the datasets according to the basin boundary, and gener -
ate spatial distribution maps of individual soil properties. 
The same processing workflow was applied to all selected 
soil parameters to produce basin-scale soil property maps. 
The processed raster datasets were exported and integrated 
within a GIS environment for further analysis, including 
Digital Soil Mapping (DSM), soil health assessment, spatial 
modeling, and environmental vulnerability evaluation. This 
approach enabled efficient handling of large-scale soil data-
sets and provided a consistent framework for analyzing spa-
tial patterns of soil characteristics across the Bhavani Basin.
Geospatial Analysis and Validation
After GEE extraction and visualization, the layers were 
exported in GeoTIFF format and examined in ArcGIS Pro 
10.8 for further spatial analysis. The methodology involved 
spatially overlaying soil data with LULC and topography to 
investigate variability patterns. Calculating administrative 
and ecological zone statistics. Integrating biodiversity and 
socioeconomic variables to assess soil quality and human 
vulnerability.

Result and Discussion
Soil Physical Characteristics
Topographic, vegetation, and land-use gradients create 
strong physical and functional soil variability across the 
Bhavani River Basin. SoilGrids data and field observations 
show that the forested upper basin contains Humic Cambi -
sols and Ferralsols, while the cultivated lowlands are domi-
nated by Arenosols and Fluvisols. This variability influences 
carbon storage, nutrient retention, and water regulation.
Forested Nilgiri uplands exhibit high organic carbon 
(~ 3.0%), moderate bulk density, and stable aggregates, 
reflecting minimal disturbance and continuous litter inputs. 
These conditions enhance humification, microbial biomass, 
and soil respiration, supported by near-neutral pH (6.0–
7.5), which promotes nutrient availability and biodiversity 
(Fig. 2).
In contrast, intensive agriculture and excessive chemical 
fertilizer use in the plains have reduced soil organic carbon 
to ~1.5%, weakening infiltration, cation exchange capacity, 
and structural stability. Poor drainage and prolonged irriga-
tion have led to salinity stress, with EC values >4.0 dS/m, 
exceeding crop tolerance limits (Fao, 2018) and contribut -
ing to crusting, erosion, and yield loss. Forest and plantation 
soils remain comparatively resilient, with low salinity (~ 1.0 
dS/m) and neutral pH. However, long-term fertilizer use 
and declining liming have acidified croplands (pH ~5.8), 
disrupting microbial processes, nutrient cycling, and eco -
system resilience.
Chemical Properties and Carbon Storage of the 
Bhavani Basin
In the Bhavani River Basin, natural and human activities 
interact to alter soil chemistry and carbon cycles. Defores -
tation, intensive agriculture, pollution, and water shortages 
have disrupted the basin's soil chemical equilibrium, jeopar-
dizing productivity and ecology.
Basin soil and ecosystems need carbon, the most basic ele-
ment in biogeochemical cycles. Photosynthesis turns atmo-
spheric carbon into biomass, litter, and soil organic matter in 
vegetation, the main carbon sink. This mechanism accumu-
lates organic carbon in soils for long-term storage, reduc -
ing urban and industrial CO 2 emissions. Basin surface soil 
chemical variability (05 cm) and distribution were assessed 
using digital soil mapping and remote sensing. The maps 
(Fig. 3) illustrate that land-use type affects soil carbon reten-
tion and storage. Organic carbon concentrations in upstream 
wooded regions (> 3.0%) and downstream agricultural areas 
(< 1.5%) suggest high vegetation density and low anthro -
pogenic disturbance. Mono cropping, excessive fertilizer 
usage, and nutrient degradation in downstream agricultural 
areas (3.0%), however, are unsustainable. Upper catchment 
agricultural land-use and logging have fragmented habitat, 
changed hydrological seasons, and increased soil erosion. 
Silt deposition reduces downstream reservoir and floodplain 
water storage and increases monsoon flood risk.
Comparing land-use and land-cover (LULC) assess -
ment (2018–2023), forest cover declined 12%, largely due 
to agricultural land and urban expansion. Transitions have 
sequestered soil and water carbon. Soil erosion and limited 
aquifer recharge result from inadequate infiltration and sur-
face runoff on deforested slopes. Additionally, urban soil 
development and sealing in downstream areas has reduced 
the basin's water- and carbon-storage capacity.
Spatial modelling shows geographic differences in soil 
carbon dynamics in the Bhavani Basin due to land-use inten-
sity, plant cover, and urbanization gradients. The northwest 
retains the most soil carbon due to high vegetation density, 
little anthropogenic stress, and good soil texture. Industrial, 
residential, and transit emissions create large urban and 
peri-urban carbon fluxes.
Static and dynamic soil chemistry variations affect basin 
carbon balance, study finds. Organic matter buildup in ripar-
ian environments with 420–565 dg/kg fixed carbon is opti-
mized by sediment deposition. Digital soil mapping (DSM) 
utilizing SoilGrids datasets has quantified and regionalized 
basin soil carbon stores (Fig. 3).
This spatially explicit atlas was created to examine soil 
organic carbon (SOC) and total organic carbon (TOC) tem-
poral and spatial variations for the global carbon budget. 
Urban topsoil (0–5 cm) TOC contents vary greatly with 
land-use intensity, from 1.3 to 21 mg/kg by location. Vege -
tated and forested fields had greater TOCs than highly popu-
lated areas, demonstrating the relevance of soil management 
and vegetation cover in carbon stock maintenance.

Biodiversity and Land Use
The Western Ghats' Bhavani River Basin, one of the world's 
most biodiverse, affects southern India's ecology and eco-
nomics. Flora and wildlife cover 35% of the basin, including 
endangered tigers, leopards, and Asian elephants. Wildlife 
and soil biodiversity are protected by these woods. The high 
organic carbon and microbiological content of forest soils 
increase nutrient cycling, soil structure, and ecological sta -
bility. Because of little human interference, these soils can 
keep their fertility and ecological integrity. Instead, 45% of 
the basin is intensively farmed with rice, sugarcane, and mil-
lets. Monocropping and chemical fertilizers and pesticides 
have deteriorated soils, increased salinity, and depleted 
nutrients, hurting aquatic life. Overuse of agrochemicals 
diminishes soil microbial populations, organic matter, fer -
tility, and resilience. Due to population and development, 
the remaining 20% of the Bhavani Basin is fast urbaniz -
ing. Urban growth fragments ecosystems, seals soils, and 
reduces pervious surfaces, reducing infiltration capacity and 
hydrological imbalance. These changes stress the environ -
ment, reduce ecosystem services, and endanger the soil-bio-
diversity nexus for ecological and human health.
Bhavani Basin's biological balance requires conservation 
and development. Due to their high organic carbon content 
and low human involvement, forest soils are natural soil 
health reservoirs, while agricultural regions must utilize a 
sustainable strategy to restore fertility and prevent further 
deterioration. Integrated nutrition management, organic 
inputs, minimal tillage, and biological pest control reduce 
intensive production. Reforestation, anti-deforestation, and 
ecosystem restoration increase soil biodiversity and basin 
production.
Climate Vulnerability and Socio-Ecological Implications
Climate change poses a significant threat to the hydrologi-
cal and ecological stability of the Bhavani Basin, which is 
highly dependent on monsoon rainfall (Bheeman & Ranga-
rajan, 2025; Rashiq et al., 2024). Variations in the timing, 
intensity, and distribution of monsoon precipitation directly 
influence agricultural productivity in the downstream 
plains, where the Bhavani River and its tributaries provide 
irrigation for major crops. Increasing climate variability has 
led to more frequent droughts, flash floods, and unpredict-
able rainfall patterns, adversely affecting soil quality, water 
availability, and agricultural sustainability (Raheem et al., 
2025; Rahmati et al., 2024).
Agriculture remains the primary livelihood for most 
inhabitants of the basin. However, land inequality and a 
heavy reliance on rain-fed farming increase the vulnerabil -
ity of rural communities to climatic and economic stresses. 
Strengthening rainwater harvesting systems, constructing 
check dams, and promoting micro-irrigation technologies 
can reduce vulnerability and enhance resilience to climate-
related risks (BenYishay et al., 2024; Mekuria et al., 2025; 
Musa et al., 2026).
Ensuring the long-term sustainability of soil and water 
resources in the basin requires climate-adaptive management

Local Climate Zone (LCZ)
Cities are becoming the epicentre of 21st-century environ -
mental and health challenges due to growing urbanization. 
Cities are especially vulnerable to climate change's heat 
stress, air pollution, and heat waves, which impair health 
(Founda & Santamouris, 2017). To research and manage 
urban meteorological phenomena, Stewart and Oke ( 2012) 
suggested the hierarchical Local Climate Zone (LCZ).

comparability (Table 2). These classifications vary in sur-
face, structure, materials, and human activity. Metropolitan 
Heat Island (UHI) study increasingly uses the LCZ-system 
to share data and compare metropolitan climates internation-
ally. Recent geospatial analytic breakthroughs have enabled

PCA and Overlay Analysis and Drought Index of 
Bhavani Basin
Geospatial approaches are needed to investigate soil prop-
erty, land degradation, and drought dynamics in the geo -
morphologically and climatologically diverse Bhavani 
River Basin. PCA and Overlay Analysis help identify 
geographical trends and prioritize sustainable manage -
ment. Geospatial and environmental research employ 
PCA to reduce dimensionality and discover trends. Google 
Earth Engine (GEE) and ArcGIS 10.8.2 were used to 
identify Bhavani Basin soil fertility and quality indica -
tors using PCA (Fig. 7). Remote sensing data and ground-
based parameters such soil pH, organic carbon, nitrogen 
(N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), and soil texture were 
examined to identify trends and reduce repetition. The 
first few important factors (PC1 and PC2) explained most 
of the variance, demonstrating how soil organic carbon, 
moisture, and nutrient availability are connected. This ana-
lytical technique showed soil fertility and degradation in 
space. Using multispectral image processing (particularly 
reflectance bands and vegetation indices NDVI, SAVI, 
and NDWI), GEE-based PCA revealed basin-wide surface 
moisture and soil organic content variability. To supple -
ment PCA results, ArcGIS (version 10.8.2) thematic maps 
of land use/land cover (LULC), slope, elevation, soil pH, 
nutrient concentrations, and erosion risk were overlayed. 
The weighted overlay model weighted parameters by soil 
fertility and land productivity. The slope and texture were 
modestly valued for run-off and erosion, whereas nitrogen, 
phosphorus, and potassium affected agricultural produc-
tion and were valued more.
The Soil Suitability and Vulnerability Map, created using 
this overlay technique, divided the Bhavani Basin into man-
agement zones from very suitable agricultural land to highly 
vulnerable to deterioration and pollution. PCA and overlay 
mapping helped basin agricultural, land use, and soil man -
agement decisions.
Agriculture and water security in the Bhavani Basin are 
threatened by drought. GEE based Drought Index Models 
measure drought patterns using 2018–2023 remote sensing 
data. The model assessed drought severity using the Nor -
malized Difference Drought Index (NDDI), which energizes 
NDVI and NDWI.
This measure identifies vegetative stress and moisture 
loss for time- and location-based drought monitoring. Using 
MODIS, Sentinel-2, and Landsat 8 data, cloud-based GEE 
computation created monthly Bhavani Basin drought maps. 
Due to soil moisture, upstream forest-covered areas were 
resilient, but downstream agricultural areas had mild to 
severe droughts. Results match monsoon variability and 
groundwater depletion.

Conclusion
This study developed a Remote Sensing (RS), Geographic 
Information System (GIS), and Digital Soil Mapping 
(DSM)-based framework to evaluate soil health, environ -
mental vulnerability, and ecosystem sustainability in the 
Bhavani River Basin, South India. By integrating SoilGrids 
datasets, satellite-derived environmental variables, terrain 
attributes, land-use/land-cover information, and geospatial 
analytical techniques, the study generated high-resolution 
maps of key soil properties, including soil organic carbon 
(SOC), soil organic carbon density (OCD), soil organic car-
bon stock (OCS), nitrogen, pH, soil texture, cation exchange 
capacity (CEC), electrical conductivity (EC), bulk density 
(BD), and available water-holding capacity (AWHC).
The results revealed substantial spatial heterogeneity in 
soil quality and environmental conditions across the basin. 
The western and southwestern regions exhibited high SOC 
(451), OCD (180), OCS (93), and nitrogen (4197) values, 
indicating strong carbon sequestration potential, enhanced 
nutrient availability, and improved soil fertility. These areas 
function as major carbon and nutrient reservoirs and play a 
crucial role in maintaining ecosystem stability. In contrast, 
intensively cultivated agricultural lands and rapidly urban -
izing lowland regions showed lower soil quality, reduced 
organic matter content, and greater susceptibility to degra -
dation. Although parts of the eastern basin displayed rela -
tively high CEC values (up to 373 cmol( +)/kg), suggesting 
favourable nutrient-retention capacity, the overall lower 
carbon stocks indicate the need for improved soil manage -
ment practices.
The Soil Quality Index (SQI), Soil Overlay Analysis, 
Environmental Vulnerability Index (EVI), and Drought 
Index further highlighted critical spatial patterns of environ-
mental stress. Areas characterized by vegetation loss, land-
use change, and intensive anthropogenic activities exhibited 
higher vulnerability and degradation risk, whereas the for -
ested upper catchments of the Nilgiri Hills demonstrated 
better ecological integrity and resilience. Hotspot analysis 
identified priority zones requiring immediate conservation 
interventions, particularly in regions vulnerable to drought, 
soil degradation, and declining ecosystem services.
Overall, the study determines the effectiveness of inte-
grating DSM, machine learning, Remote Sensing, and 
GIS techniques for comprehensive soil and environmen -
tal assessment in tropical river basins. The findings pro-
vide valuable scientific support for precision agriculture, 
watershed management, soil restoration, biodiversity con -
servation, and climate-resilient land-use planning. Future 
research should focus on long-term monitoring of soil 
health, climate change impacts, and socio-economic driv -
ers of land-use transformation to strengthen sustainable 
resource management and enhance ecosystem resilience in 
the Bhavani River Basin.
