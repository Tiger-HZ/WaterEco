A Cyanobacteria-Mediated TiO₂ Nanoparticle and Propolis Cream 
Enhances Antifungal Activity and Wound Healing Against Candida 
albicans in Mice

1Department of Biology, SR.C., Islamic Azad University, Tehran, Iran.
2Department of Microbiology, TeMS.C., Islamic Azad University, Tehran, Iran

Abstract
Nanoformulations of natural drugs derived from cyanobacteria can be 
designed using various nanoparticles, thereby acquiring suitable properties 
for antimicrobial, cytotoxic, and antioxidant activities. In this study, we aimed 
to use a combination of natural nanoparticles and propolis to produce a 
wound healing cream and evaluate its antifungal, cytotoxic, and antioxidant 
activities through various tests. Four types of creams were prepared: a base 
cream, a cream containing nanoparticles, a cream containing propolis, and a 
cream containing both propolis and nanoparticles.
The nanoparticles were polyvinyl alcohol/titanium dioxide nanoparticles 
(PVA/ TiO2 NCs) extracted from the cyanobacterium Neowestiellopsis persica. 
The results of measuring the diameter of inhibition zones, minimum 
inhibitory concentration (MIC), and minimum fungicidal concentration (MFC) 
of the prepared creams showed that Candida albicans is much more resistant 
than Trichophyton rubrum. Histological examination of wound samples on 
day 14 revealed the highest epithelialization and the lowest levels of 
neutrophils and macrophages in mice treated with the cream containing both 
propolis and nanoparticles (group 4). Overall, the cream prepared with 
nanoparticles and propolis demonstrated significant improvement in wound 
healing compared to the control group. This study is the first to show the 
effectiveness of a therapeutic cream based on natural ingredients in 
promoting wound healing in animals.

Introduction
Wounds are defined as a disruption in tissue integrity. Wounds are classified 
in various ways and may be categorized as superficial, partial, or full-
thickness based on the number of skin layers and the affected area. Wounds 
can be clean or infected, acute or chronic [1].
Acute wounds typically heal completely within an expected timeframe, 
usually 8 to 12 weeks, with minimal scarring, while chronic wounds heal 
slowly over longer periods exceeding 12 weeks and often recur. Such wounds 
fail to heal due to repeated tissue damage or other physiological conditions 
such as diabetes and malignancies, persistent infections, inadequate initial 
treatment, and other patient-related factors. Chronic wounds include 
pressure ulcers and venous leg ulcers. Some chronic wounds also originate 
from acute wounds. They are often referred to as non-healing wounds and 
require specialized care [2].
Since 2.45 billion years ago, algae have developed extensive diversity for 
their survival in competitive ecological niches. This adaptation has led to the 
production of a wide range of diverse biochemical compounds. 
Polysaccharides, cell wall structures, mucopolysaccharides, 
exopolysaccharides, and storage polysaccharides in algae are recognized as 
important biomaterials for medical and pharmaceutical applications. These 
polymers, composed of simple sugars (monosaccharides) linked by glycosidic 
bonds, provide highly diverse structures and thus serve as an exceptional 
source for drug discovery [3].
The most commonly used wound-healing polysaccharides are extracted from 
marine algae, particularly macroalgae, which contain 4 to 76% 
polysaccharides per unit dry weight. Cell wall polysaccharides mainly consist 
of cellulose, hemicellulose, and neutral polysaccharides. They have numerous 
commercial applications in products such as stabilizers, thickeners, 
emulsifiers, food, feed, beverages, and more. Green algae contain sulfated

Hydrophilic polysaccharides have the ability to absorb large amounts of 
water, salts, or physiological solutions, which promotes moist wound healing. 
Additionally, due to their bioactivity, these products may play a significant 
role in the wound healing process [4].
Nanoformulations of natural drugs derived from cyanobacteria can be 
designed using various nanoparticles to achieve suitable properties for 
antimicrobial, cytotoxic, and antioxidant activities [5]. For example, 
nanoparticles can be constructed from materials of diverse origins, inorganic 
(such as metals, carbon, silica, and their respective oxides) and organic (such 
as polymers and lipids). Moreover, nanoparticle size can be manipulated to 
range from a few nanometers to over one millimeter, their shape can be 
smooth or edged, their form can be rigid or soft, and their surface can be 
functionalized with various molecules. The greatest advantage of carrier 
nanoparticles for target anticancer agents is that these particles can enhance 
drug efficacy through several remarkable mechanisms, including increased 
solubility for better drug delivery, extended drug half-life in circulation, 
increased drug accumulation in target cancerous tissues or cells, sustained 
and steady drug release, and reduced drug resistance [6]. All these factors 
have led to increasing access to bioactive anticancer compounds based on 
microalgae and commercialized microalgal drugs. In the near future, 
biocompatible methods using cyanobacterial extracts will be developed as 
potential sources of reducing and stabilizing agents for green nanoparticles. 
It is hoped that in the near future, more clean, biocompatible, safe, and cost-
effective synthesis methods will also be utilized. Additionally, nanoparticle 
stabilization in various matrices should follow precise and judicious methods 
that lead to sustained release of the antibacterial agent. The optimal solution

should ensure localized activity of the antibacterial agent with minimal 
release into the environment. Furthermore, other researchers have 
demonstrated in a study that biogenic silver nanoparticles are more effective 
antibacterial agents compared to chemical silver nanoparticles. Secondly, 
antibacterial nanoparticles may bring fundamental changes to the 
pharmaceutical industry. Using nanoparticles, the solubility of poorly water-
soluble antibiotics will be improved [7].
Thirdly, the cellular toxicity of antibacterial nanoparticles toward bacteria as 
well as humans must be considered, as most heavy metal ions (Ag+ and Cu+2) 
are toxic. Silver ions are toxic to humans at very high doses. In most cases, 
silver ions exhibit effective antibacterial activity at low concentrations of 
approximately 0.001 to 0.05 ppm [8]. It is expected that in the near future, 
antibacterial nanoparticles based on environmentally safe materials such as 
alkali metals (magnesium and calcium) will be developed. Additionally, TiO 2 
is a material that, despite being safe, has high potential (used globally as an 
excipient in pharmaceuticals and as a colorant in the food industry) and is 
light-sensitive. Future research should focus on reducing the activation 
energy of TiO 2 nanoparticles to exhibit catalytic activity in the visible light 
range rather than UV. However, it should be noted that while bulk TiO 2, 
which has widespread applications, is highly biocompatible, nano-scale TiO 2 
has been reported as toxic in some studies [9]. Furthermore, toxicity of TiO 2 
nanoparticles has also been demonstrated in in vivo studies relative to mouse 
models. Accordingly, the potential impact of TiO 2 nanoparticles on living 
organisms should be investigated due to their small particle size and high 
specific surface area. Moreover, other researchers have shown in a study that 
TiO2 nanoparticles without photoactivation had no effect up to 100 µg/mL, 
and UV light irradiation led to a significant increase in toxicity induced by the 
nanoparticles [10]. As observed, although silver nanoparticles are effective 
antibacterial agents, their cellular toxicity must always be considered, 
whereas nanoparticles based on alkali metals are non-toxic and a suitable 
replacement for them. It is predicted that new antibacterial nanoparticles will

be based on biocompatible and safe materials possessing physicochemical 
properties and nanoscale features [11]. The widespread use of nanoparticles 
in everyday consumer products may pose a threat to the environment. Other 
researchers have reported the detection of silver sulfide nanoparticles in 
sewage sludge at the final stage of urban wastewater treatment plants. They 
stated that the presence of silver nanoparticles in consumer products leads 
to increased silver ions in wastewater, which, in the presence of reduced 
sulfur ions and anaerobic conditions, results in the formation of silver sulfide

environment is likely a threat to a wide range of living organisms [12].
Propolis, or bee glue, is the world's strongest natural antibiotic without side 
effects and possesses numerous therapeutic properties. Among the most 
important therapeutic properties of propolis are its antimicrobial and 
antitumor effects, immune system enhancement, suitability for skin and 
teeth, benefits for the gut and uterus, improved lung function, wound healing 
and cataract treatment, assistance in treating urinary tract infections, and its 
biological properties. Propolis contains polyphenols such as flavonoids, which 
are a type of antioxidant considered among its most important compounds. 
Flavonoids are plant compounds that exhibit high levels of antioxidant 
activity [13].
Phenolics are compounds produced by plants that protect them against 
oxidative stress. Both flavonoids and phenolics are found in most fruits and 
vegetables and have been attributed to many health benefits in plant-based 
diets. Another important component of propolis is the enzymes it contains, 
which are a combination of salivary secretions and enzymatic materials. 
These compounds accelerate various enzymatic reactions, enhance cell 
metabolism, improve blood circulation, and promote collagen fiber formation 
[14].
Although numerous studies have focused on the development of microalgal 
nanoformulations and their awareness in medical sectors to date, no 
formulations have been developed for preparing wound-healing compounds

Materials and Methods
Cultivation of Neowestiellopsis persica Strain
The N. persica cyanobacterium strain was isolated and cultured from the 
Cyanobacteria Culture Collection (CCC) at the Science and Research Branch 
of Islamic Azad University. Purification and axenization of cultures were 
performed in 400 mL of liquid Z8 medium in a growth chamber at 28°C with 
continuous fluorescent lighting at an intensity of 300 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹ 
for 10 to 15 days [15] (Figure 1).
Figure 1: Cultivation of N. persica strain in liquid Z8 medium.
Synthesis of TiO2 NPs
After centrifugation of the culture media, the resulting biomass was dried in 
an oven at 25°C. 1 g of dry weight was boiled in 10 mL of double-distilled 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
water for 15 minutes at 100°C in an Erlenmeyer flask. After boiling, the 
mixture was cooled and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 minutes. The 
supernatant was collected and stored at 4°C. Cell-free extracts of the 
cyanobacterial strains were used for TiO 2 NPs. 40 mL of 25 mM titanium 
hydroxide solution was added to the mixture and maintained at 37°C with 
constant shaking for 24 hours. The mixture was then left at room temperature 
for 8 hours; formation of a white precipitate indicated TiO 2 NPs production. 
The precipitate was separated from the medium by centrifugation at 3820 × 
g for 15 minutes. Finally, it was washed with water and subjected to 
spectrophotometric analyses [16] (Figure 2).
Figure 2: Synthesis of TiO2 NPs. a: dried biomass, b: addition of distilled water, c: boiling, 
d: addition of 25 mM titanium hydroxide solution, e: shaking of the obtained solution, f: 
formation of white precipitate indicating TiO2 NPs production.
Preparation of PVA/ TiO2 NCs
Initially, a 10% (w/w) aqueous solution of PVA was prepared. Various 
concentrations of TiO2 NPs (0, 0.5, 1, and 2%) were added to the solution and 
stirred at room temperature for 60 minutes to ensure uniformity. 1.5% (w/w) 
glycerol was added as a plasticizer. Finally, the obtained solution was poured 
into Petri dishes and kept at room temperature for 48 hours for evaporation 
and film formation. After separating the films from the plates, they were 
placed in an oven at 50°C for 5 hours to ensure complete drying [17] (Figure 
3).

Characterization of the Wound Healing Cream
The spreadability of the wound healing cream, indicating its ability to be 
uniformly distributed over the skin surface, was evaluated visually [18]. 
Viscosity was measured at 4 °C using a Brookfield viscometer equipped with 
spindle No. 64 at 100 rpm [19]. The pH of the formulations was determined 
at 25 ± 1 °C using a calibrated pH meter [20].
Homogeneity was assessed by visual inspection of the cream after placement 
in a container, ensuring uniform appearance without lumps or phase 
separation [21].
For stability evaluation, the wound healing cream was stored for 4 weeks at 
4, 25, 37, and 40 °C. Stability was determined by monitoring changes in color, 
phase homogeneity, and overall appearance [22].
In Vivo Wound Healing Cream Evaluation
Preparation of Wound Healing Cream
The cream was prepared from liquid and solid phases. The liquid phase 
consisted of 3 g of coconut oil, 10 g of liquid paraffin, and 5 g of glycerin. The solid 
phase consisted of 5 g vaseline, 4 g almond oil, 5 g beeswax, 5 g stearic acid, 
and 3 g glyceryl monostearate. 0.1 g of 2% PVA NPs powder was added per 
100 g of cream. The films were pulverized by pounding. The components were 
combined with 60 mL of water and 2 g of propolis (Figure 4) [23].

Artificial Infection of Mice with Tested C. albicans
Adult mice (strain: C57BL/6), aged 8–12 weeks, were used. Both sexes were 
included in the experiments. Mice were divided into four groups of three. 
Each mouse was anesthetized with 100 mg/kg ketamine and 5 mg/kg xylazine 
injection. The hair on their back skin was shaved using a sterile razor. The 
incision area was sterilized with 70% ethanol, and then a circular lesion 
approximately 20 mm in diameter was created on the disinfected skin. Mice 
were subcutaneously injected with 1 mL of fungal suspension (10 6 CFU/mL) 
into the wounded area. To confirm infection occurrence, scales were taken 
from the lesion. They were then cultured on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA; 
Merck, Germany) medium with olive oil, leading to the growth of new C. 
albicans colonies [24] (Figure 5).

Cream Assays
Antifungal Activity of Wound Healing Cream by Disk Diffusion Method 
and Determination of Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) and 
Minimum Fungicidal Concentration (MFC)
The tested pathogenic fungi, C. albicans (PTCC 5199) and T. rubrum (PTCC 
5143), were obtained from the Iranian Industrial Microorganisms Collection 
Center. To determine the antifungal activity of the nanoparticle-containing 
wound healing cream against various human pathogenic fungi and measure 
the inhibition zone diameter, each fungus was first inoculated into 0.5 mL of 
sterile Sabouraud's Dextrose Agar at approximately 10 6 cells per mL for C. 
albicans and T. rubrum. Before solidification, they were allowed to cool. Each 
disk was then aseptically filled with 100 µL of each extract at 5% 
concentration prepared with different solvents. Clotrimazole cream (1% w/w, 
commercial formulation, Canesten®, Bayer, Germany) was used as the 
positive control under the same experimental conditions as the tested 
formulations.
T. rubrum and C. albicans plates were incubated at 37°C for 15 days and 
28°C for 3 days, respectively. After incubation, the inhibition zone diameter 
was measured [24].
This assay was performed for all 4 creams and both fungi. After inoculating 
C. albicans and T. rubrum with a 10 6 cells/mL suspension, MIC was 
determined by microdilution method. For the experiment, the first group 
consisted of 9 test tubes; 1 mL of Sabouraud dextrose broth was added to

each, and 1 mL of cream solution diluted with Ringer's was added to the first 
tube. Then, 1 mL from the first tube was transferred to the second, continuing 
to the ninth tube, and finally 1 mL from the last tube was discarded to 
equalize volumes. Subsequently, 100 µL of 0.5 McFarland was added to each 
of the 9 tubes, and the same was done for all 4 groups along with clotrimazole, 
followed by incubation at 25°C for 3 to 5 days. Three replicates were used for 
each culture medium and concentration to minimize experimental error, and 
the antimicrobial effects of different dilutions were examined [25].
Finally, tube concentrations were prepared at 50, 25, 12.5, 6.25, 3.125, 1.56,

completed in all tubes except the negative control tube, which was inoculated 
with 100 µL of the extraction solvent. MIC was recorded as the lowest extract 
concentration preventing pathogenic fungal growth. MFC was recorded as 
the lowest concentration causing fungal death.
Examination of Nanoparticle-Containing Wound Healing Cream by 
Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)
For TEM analysis, nanoparticle- and propolis-containing cream samples were 
collected by centrifugation and treated with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M 
phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 4°C for 2 hours. Then, 1% osmium tetroxide was 
used as a post-fixative (4°C, 1.5 hours). Samples were dehydrated with 
graded ethanol series (50, 70, 90, 95, and four times 100%, each for 30 
minutes) followed by acetone dehydration for 30 minutes. Finally, epoxy resin 
was used for embedding fixed samples. Ultramicrotome was used for cutting 
ultra-thin and semi-thin sections. Semi-thin sections (1 µm to 850 nm) were 
stained with 1% toluidine blue and observed under a light microscope using 
Olympus BX61. Ultra-thin sections of 70 to 90 nm thickness were cut, stained 
with 2.5% uranyl acetate as primary stain and lead citrate as counterstain. 
Finally, ultra-thin sections were examined using JEM-1400 Plus TEM [26].

Cytotoxicity Assay
The MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) 
assay was performed on HFFC human foreskin fibroblast cell lines to evaluate 
the inhibitory effects of materials on cell proliferation. The HFFC cell line was 
purchased from the National Cell Bank of Iran (NCBI), Pasteur Institute of 
Iran. Cell lines were incubated in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal 
bovine serum. Incubation occurred at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ incubator with 100 
units/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Cell lines were seeded in a 
96-well plate at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated at 37°C with 
5% CO₂ for 48 hours. After incubation, cells were treated with serial dilutions 
of four different wound healing cream formulations (control cream, 
nanoparticle cream, propolis cream, and nanoparticle–propolis cream) 
prepared in RPMI-1640 medium. The tested concentrations (0–200 ppm; 0, 
0.195, 0.39, 0.78, 1.56, 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 ppm) represent 
dilutions of the complete cream formulations (w/v) rather than individual 
components. After 24 hours of treatment, 20 µL of 5 mg/mL MTT solution was 
added and incubated for 4 hours. To dissolve the produced purple formazan, 
100 µL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well. The assay was 
measured and recorded at 570 nm absorbance using a plate reader [28] [28].

Light Microscopy Histological Examination
For histological evaluation, healthy mouse skin served as the positive control, 
and infected and treated samples from four experimental groups were 
examined: group 1, C. albicans -infected skin treated with base cream without 
propolis and nanoparticles (therapeutic negative control); group 2, infected 
skin treated with propolis-containing cream without nanoparticles; group 3, 
infected skin treated with nanoparticle-containing cream without propolis; 
and group 4, infected skin treated with cream containing nanoparticles and 
propolis. This grouping aimed to compare infected and healthy skin as well 
as evaluate the efficacy and topical safety of nanoformulations.
On day 14 after infection and treatment initiation, full-thickness skin samples 
were collected under sterile conditions (Figure 6) and immediately fixed in 
10% buffered formalin for 24–48 hours. Routine tissue processing steps 
followed, including washing, dehydration in graded increasing alcohol 
concentrations, clearing in xylene, infiltration, and embedding in molten 
paraffin at approximately 50–56°C, ultimately forming paraffin blocks. From 
the blocks, 5 µm thick sections were prepared using a microtome, transferred 
to glass slides, and dried [24].

Data Analysis Methods and Tools:
All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results reported as mean ± 
standard deviation. Experimental data were compared using one-way 
analysis of variance (One-way ANOVA), and significant statistical differences 
between means (when overall treatment effect was significant) determined 
using Duncan's multiple range test. Statistical test results were obtained 
using SPSS version 26 software.

Characterization of the Wound Healing Cream
The spreadability of the wound healing cream was found to be satisfactory 
throughout the 30-day storage period, indicating good applicability and 
consistency of the formulations.
The viscosity of all formulations showed a slight increase over the 8-week 
storage period. Overall, no significant phase instability or abrupt changes 
were observed in any formulation. The results indicate that incorporation of 
different concentrations of TiO₂ nanoparticles had no adverse effect on the 
rheological stability of the cream.
The pH values of all formulations remained close to neutral throughout the 
30-day period, ranging approximately from 7.07 to 6.97. A slight gradual 
decrease in pH was observed over time; however, all formulations remained 
within an acceptable range for topical application. No significant 
destabilizing effect of TiO₂ nanoparticle concentration was observed.
The homogeneity results indicated that incorporation of different 
concentrations of TiO₂ nanoparticles did not affect the physical uniformity of 
the wound healing cream. All formulations remained homogeneous 
throughout the 30-day storage period, with no visible phase separation, 
aggregation, or lump formation observed.

Antifungal Activity of the Wound Healing Cream
Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA showed significant differences in 
inhibition zone diameters against C. albicans (PTCC 5199) and T. rubrum 
(PTCC 5143), with the positive control treatment (clotrimazole) exhibiting the 
highest inhibition zone diameter with significant difference in both cases. 
Among treatments against both fungi, the cream containing nanoparticles 
and propolis showed the highest inhibition zone diameter with significant 
difference (p < 0.05). Additionally, the aforementioned treatment exhibited 
the highest inhibition zone diameter against T. rubrum with significant 
difference. The lowest inhibition zone diameter against both fungi was 
reported for the control treatment (Figures 8, 9, and 10).

propolis cream, and nanoparticle cream, with no significant difference 
between the aforementioned creams. Comparison of antifungal activity

significant difference (p < 0.05) (Figure 11).

Discussion
In this study, the use of TiO₂ NPs synthesized by the cyanobacterium N. 
persica in combination with propolis provided a novel approach based on 
natural biomolecules for designing a cream to treat fungus-infected wounds. 
The presence of secondary metabolites, polysaccharides, and pigments from 
cyanobacteria leads to better biocompatibility and colloidal stability, and 
contributes to maintaining nanoparticles in the nanometric range and 
preventing particle aggregation, as observed in TEM results and size 
distribution [29]. These features provide a suitable platform for topical 
application of nanostructures on skin, especially when combined with another 
natural substance like propolis, which itself contains antioxidant and anti-
inflammatory polyphenols and flavonoids. One of the main motivations for 
developing such formulations is the global challenge of drug resistance 
against antibacterial and antifungal agents that has limited the efficacy of 
conventional treatments and highlighted the need for alternative options with 
multiple mechanisms [30]. Metal and oxide nanostructures, including silver, 
gold, ZnO, and Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles, have shown a wide range of antimicrobial 
and antifungal effects in previous studies through cell wall and membrane 
disruption, penetration into cytoplasm, and induction of reactive oxygen 
species production [31].
The TEM data of this research also showed a similar pattern for 
cyanobacterial TiO₂; nanoparticle adhesion to the C. albicans spore wall, 
creation of irregularities and breakage in the wall, cytoplasmic membrane 
separation, cytoplasmic vacuolization, and ultimately partial cell lysis were 
observed. Such changes are consistent with other reports on fungal and 
bacterial cell structure disruption in the presence of extracts or biogenic 
nanoparticles [24]. These observations suggest that nanoparticle interaction 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
with the fungal cell wall may contribute to structural damage and loss of 
membrane integrity.
The results of antifungal assays in this study demonstrated that the 
nanoparticle–propolis cream (T4) produced the largest inhibition zone 
diameter and the lowest MIC and MFC values against C. albicans and T. 
rubrum, whereas the base cream (control) showed no inhibitory effect. These 
findings are consistent with previous reports highlighting the anti-Candidal 
activity of cyanobacterial extracts and pigments, such as phycocyanin and 
methanolic Arthrospira platensis extract, which have shown significant 
inhibition zones and low MIC values against similar fungal strains [24].
The superior performance of the T4 formulation compared to creams 
containing only nanoparticles or only propolis suggests an enhanced 
antifungal effect associated with the combined presence of cyanobacterial-
derived TiO₂ nanoparticles and phenolic compounds present in propolis. This 
improved activity may be attributed to the ability of nanoparticles to disrupt 
fungal cell wall and membrane integrity, thereby increasing cellular 
permeability, while propolis compounds interfere with intracellular metabolic 
pathways and oxidative defense systems. Similar combined or additive effects 
between metal nanoparticles and bioactive plant or microalgal compounds 
have been reported to enhance antifungal efficacy [32].
Clotrimazole was used as a positive control in this study due to its well-
established clinical efficacy as a broad-spectrum antifungal agent against 
dermatophytes and yeasts, including C. albicans and T. rubrum. As expected, 
clotrimazole exhibited stronger antifungal activity compared to the tested 
formulations. However, despite this difference, the developed nanoparticle–
propolis cream offers several important advantages. Unlike conventional 
antifungal drugs, this formulation combines antifungal, antioxidant, and 
wound healing properties within a single system. In addition, the use of 
natural compounds such as propolis may reduce the risk of adverse side 
effects associated with prolonged use of synthetic antifungal agents. 
Therefore, this multifunctional formulation may serve as a complementary or 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
alternative therapeutic option, particularly in the management of infected 
wounds where both antimicrobial activity and tissue regeneration are 
required.
From an antioxidant perspective, the nanoparticle-propolis cream showed the 
lowest IC₅₀ and thus the highest radical scavenging power in the DPPH assay, 
indicating significant enhancement of the formulation's antioxidant capacity 
in the presence of both components. Previous studies have shown that 
cyanobacterial and microalgal extracts, especially species like Nostoc and 
Arthrospira, exhibit strong antioxidant activity and metal chelation ability 
due to high content of polyphenols, flavonoids, carotenoids, and 
exopolysaccharides [33]. Adding propolis, which is rich in flavonoids and 
phenolic acids, logically can lead to strengthening this capacity, and the 
results of this study confirm this hypothesis. Such a combination of 
antioxidant effects is important for wound healing, as reducing oxidative 
stress in the wound bed helps keratinocyte migration, fibroblast proliferation, 
and regulation of inflammatory response; the same process reported in 
studies on phycocyanin-containing creams and herbal healing creams [24].
MTT assay results showed that with increasing cream concentration up to 
200 ppm, fibroblast cell line viability percentage decreases in a dose-
dependent manner, but no significant reduction was observed below about 
0.78 ppm in formulations containing propolis and nanoparticle-propolis, and 
also up to 1.56 ppm in base cream and cream containing only TiO₂ 
nanoparticles. These findings indicate that the cream demonstrates 
acceptable biocompatibility within the dose range relevant to topical 
application, without serious cellular toxicity concerns. At higher 
concentrations, a reduction in fibroblast viability was observed, particularly 
in the combined formulation, which may be related to nanoparticle-induced 
oxidative stress and cellular interactions reported for similar nanostructures 
[34].

ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
Although this study's nanoparticles are TiO₂ not silver, general ROS-based 
mechanisms and direct cellular structure interactions reported for other 
nanoparticles can partly explain the observed proliferation reduction [35].
Clinical-macroscopic and histopathological wound assessments 
complemented laboratory findings. Wound area measurements showed 
significant reduction across all groups over 14 days, but healing speed and 
percentage closure in the nanoparticle-propolis group were significantly 
higher than control, nearing complete healing by day 14 while substantial 
wound area remained open in the base cream. This trend matches studies 
using phycocyanin-containing or plant/algal extract creams to accelerate 
wound healing; those researches also showed faster wound reduction and 
higher healing percentages in algal extract or herbal cream recipient groups 
versus controls [36]. Day-14 H&E and Masson's trichrome staining results 
provided clear tissue evidence of nanoparticle-propolis formulation 
superiority. In the control group and to some extent in groups containing only 
propolis or only nanoparticles, thick scabs, severe neutrophil and 
macrophage infiltration in the hypodermis, incomplete epithelium formation, 
and collagen disorganization were still observed; findings indicating 
prolongation of the inflammation phase and delay in entering the 
reconstruction phase. In contrast, in group 4, inflammation was noticeably 
reduced, epidermis formed with regular rete ridges and higher 
epithelialization score, hair follicles and sebaceous glands reappeared, and 
collagen fiber density and order increased. This pattern is similar to reported 
results for phycocyanin-containing creams and other algal and plant extracts, 
where reduced inflammatory cells, better collagen formation, and return of 
skin appendages were reported as optimal healing indicators [24]. Therefore, 
it can be concluded that the cyanobacterial TiO₂-propolis combination not 
only inhibits fungal growth but also enhances wound healing quality by 
reducing inflammation, improving oxidative balance, and stimulating tissue 
regeneration.

ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
Overall, this research shows that the cream containing TiO₂ nanoparticles 
from cyanobacterium N. persica combined with propolis is a multifunctional 
formulation with strong antifungal properties, high antioxidant capacity, 
acceptable cellular toxicity at therapeutic doses, and clear ability to 
accelerate and improve the quality of C. albicans -infected wound healing. 
Alignment of the obtained results with previous reports on antifungal, 
antioxidant, and healing activities of cyanobacterial and algal compounds 
strengthens this formulation's position among novel natural resource-based 
options for managing infected wounds [37]. However, for translating this 
product from laboratory to clinical application, further studies including long-
term skin safety tests, more detailed examination of molecular mechanisms 
at gene and protein levels, and ultimately human clinical trials are necessary. 
A limitation of the present study is the absence of additional physicochemical 
characterization techniques such as dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta 
potential analysis. Future studies should include these analyses to further 
elucidate nanoparticle stability and surface charge properties.
Conclusion
This study demonstrated that the nanocream containing TiO₂ nanoparticles 
derived from cyanobacteria combined with propolis is a promising 
formulation for the treatment of fungus-infected wounds. TEM analysis 
showed that nanoparticles exhibited quasi-spherical morphology with a size 
distribution in the range of ~20–100 nm, with most particles in the 40–50 nm 
range. These nanoparticles were observed to interact with C. albicans cell 
walls and may contribute to structural damage and membrane disruption. 
Antifungal assay results showed that the nanoparticle–propolis cream had the 
highest inhibition zone diameter and lowest MIC and MFC against both C. 
albicans and T. rubrum , while the control cream showed no significant 
antifungal activity. Antioxidant activity (DPPH) indicated that the 
nanoparticle–propolis formulation exhibited the strongest radical scavenging 
capacity among all treatments, suggesting improved protection against 
oxidative stress in the wound environment. MTT assay results demonstrated 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
a dose-dependent reduction in fibroblast viability at higher concentrations; 
however, no significant cytotoxicity was observed at low concentrations 
relevant to topical application (below ~0.78 ppm), and the nanoparticle–
propolis formulation showed an acceptable biocompatibility profile compared 
to single-component formulations. Macroscopic wound healing results 
showed that healing rate and wound closure percentage in the nanoparticle–
propolis group were significantly higher than in the control group, 
approaching near-complete healing by day 14. Histopathological analysis 
(H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining) confirmed these findings, showing 
reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, improved epithelialization, increased 
collagen deposition, and regeneration of skin appendages in the treated 
group. Overall, these findings suggest that the cyanobacterial TiO₂ 
nanoparticle–propolis formulation exhibits strong antifungal activity, 
antioxidant potential, and acceptable biocompatibility, and may be a 
promising candidate for further development as a topical agent for infected 
wound management. Future studies are required to investigate molecular 
mechanisms, long-term safety, and clinical applicability.
