Abstract 
Groundwater resource protection is a worldwide issue because groundwater plays a crucial role in meeting water supply demands 
for both natural ecosystems and human activities. Effective groundwater management and protection therefore require 
appropriate approaches for assessing contamination risks and identifying ar eas that are particularly vulnerable to pollution. In 
this study, a GIS -based DRASTIC model was applied to assess groundwater vulnerability in Lai City, southern Chad. Seven 
hydrogeological parameters were considered, namely depth to water, net recharge, aquifer media, soil media, topography, impact 
of the vadose zone, and hydraulic conductivity. A weighted overlay analysis based on the DRASTIC Index (DI) was subsequently 
used to generate the groundwater vulnerability map of the study area. The results show that the DRASTIC Index values range 
from 119 to 175, enabling the study area to be categorized into three groundwater vulnerability classes: low, moderate, and high. 
The high-vulnerability class covers approximately 35% of the study area, highlighting are as that require particular attention for 
groundwater protection and management. The average and low class of vulnerability covers portions of the study area 31% and 
34%, respectively. The northern and south -western portion of the catchment, namely Lai, Gab ri Mbassa, Sategui, and Django, 
possesses high groundwater vulnerability. GIS -based Drastic model validation is performed via water quality parameters such 
as Nitrate and Total Dissolved Solids, with the accuracy of 97% and 75% respectively. The resulting groundwater vulnerability 
map provides a valuable tool for groundwater protection and sustainable land-use planning in Lai City and its surrounding areas.

1. Introduction 
Groundwater resources are the most precious source of 
fresh water available from natural resources on the earth [13, 
49]. It is the main source of drinking water supply in many 
countries because of its availability, easy accessibility, and 
low vulnerability to pollution compared to surface water re-
sources [29]. According to data from Global Water Partner-
ship (GWP), approxim ately 20% of chad's groundwater 
withdrawals are allocated to human consumption, while the

201 
remaining 80% are used for agricultural purposes [27]. De-
spite their importance in human life in most parts of the 
world, aquifers have been highly exposed to polluti on in re-
cent years, and this situation limits the potential of ground-
water resources for use [20, 33, 62] . Groundwater contami-
nant sources could be natural (e.g., natural deposits of gyp-
sum, minerals, salts, seawater intrusion) or anthropogenic 
(untreated livestock waste, nitrates, pesticides.). Among the 
various sources of groundwater pollution, anthropogenic 
pollution represents one of the most significant threats to 
groundwater resources and is increasingly recognized as a 
major contributor to groundwate r quality degradation, par-
ticularly in urban environments [32, 66, 71]. Groundwater 
quality in the southern part of the Lake Chad basin, which 
includes the town of Lai, has shown that anthropogenic ac-
tivities contribute to the degradation of groundwater qu ality 
[12, 64]. The protection and restoration of groundwater re-
sources have become increasingly important in recent years, 
highlighting the need to assess groundwater contamination 
in areas that may be potentially affected. However, the 
groundwater vulner ability assessment is needed in this con-
text for taking remedial steps. 
Aquifer vulnerability concept is introduced first by Margat 
(1968) [37]. This concept refers to the capacity of contami-
nants to diffuse and infiltrate from the soil surface into the un-
derlying aquifer system. The approach has been successfully 
applied under a wide range of climatic conditions worldwide, 
including arid and semi-arid environments [10, 39, 73], tropi-
cal and subtropical regions [26, 59, 63] , and temperate cli-
mates [36, 41]. Furthermore, it is implemented across a wide 
range of hydrogeological settings, including coastal regions 
[21, 43], Hard rock aquifers [51, 60], Karst aquifers [11, 45, 
70], Alluvial aquifers [6, 24]. Vrba and Zaporozec (1994) [72] 
described groundwater vulnerability as an intrinsic property 
of an aquifer system that reflects its susceptibility to natural 
and anthropogenic impacts. This vulnerability is governed by 
various intrinsic characteristics of the aquifer and its surround-
ing environment, including soil properties such as structure 
and texture, as well as hydrological and topographic factors 
such as drainage density, runoff volume, and slope [2, 28, 54]. 
Therefore, a groundwater vulnerability assessment approach 
in a given aquifer system is put in p lace to delineating areas 
likely to be impacted by contaminants derived from anthropo-
genic activities. 
In recent years, aquifer vulnerability assessment has 
emerged as an essential tool for preventing groundwater pol-
lution. However, several approaches can be used to assess 
groundwater vulnerability such as: process -based model [46, 
74], DRASTIC [7], GOD [23], AVI [69], SINTACS [16], 
EPIK [19], SIGA [72], PCA technique [53], Decision random 
forest [34], tree-based data mining [77], Fuzzy Clustering [31], 
Boosted regression tree [44]. The selection of a suitable 
groundwater vulnerability model depends largely on the type 
of aquifer, the availability of relevant data, and the specific 
objectives and scope of the study [13, 20]. Among the various 
groundwater vul nerability assessment methods, the DRAS-
TIC model —based on seven hydrogeological parameters, 
namely depth to groundwater (D), net recharge (R), aquifer 
media (A), soil media (S), topography (T), impact of the va-
dose zone (I), and hydraulic conductivity (C) —is one of the 
most widely used approach. Its applicability has been demon-
strated in numerous studies [4, 5, 13, 20, 42, 61 , 63, 67]. The 
DRASTIC method is based on four fundamental assumptions 
[7, 20] : contaminants are introduced at the ground surface; 
precipitation transports them toward the groundwater; their 
mobility is assumed to be equivalent to that of water; and the 
assessment area is generally expected to cover at least 0.4 km2. 
The GIS -based DRASTIC model, which integrates the 
DRASTIC framewor k with GIS -based spatial analysis and 
mapping techniques, has been widely applied to delineate and 
map groundwater vulnerability zones [13, 20]. This integrated 
approach offers a cost -effective and time -efficient means of 
assessing and mapping groundwater vulnerability. 
Groundwater represents the main resource for domestic 
needs in Lai city (Figure 1). The problem we are currently fac-
ing stems from the fact that the study area is a floodplain 
where agriculture is the main activity of the population. Agri-
cultural activities are degrading the groundwater quality of Lai 
city due to the uncontrolled use of fertilizers and pesticides. 
Furthermore, field observations indicate that the city is facing 
rapid urbanization, accompanied by inadequate sanitation in-
frastructure, as evidenced by the widespread use of open la-
trines and the lack of adequate wastewater and sewage dis-
posal facilities. It appears essential to conduct a study to high-
light the parameters and conditions that control the pollution 
of the groundwater in the Lai city. However, few studies exist 
to date regarding groundwater pollution and the assessment of 
groundwater vulnerability through GIS-based DRASTIC vul-
nerability model in the Lai area although research on this issue 
has been conducted at the sca le of the Lake Chad Basin [14, 
40, 47], of which the study area is a part. In this context, 
groundwater vulnerability assessment of Lai city is needed in 
order to carefully predict pollution that faced by the city and 
its surroundings. The ultimate objecti ve of this study is to 
evaluate the groundwater vulnerability of Lai City by integrat-
ing the DRASTIC model into GIS and also discuss the spatial 
distribution of nitrate concentration in the groundwater. Ulti-
mately, this study provides a useful basis for pl anners and lo-
cal authorities to develop an effective groundwater manage-
ment and protection plan for Lai City. This research was car-
ried out in N'Djamena, Chad, throughout the period of March 
2024 to January 2025.

2.2. Groundwater Vulnerability Assessment 
Using the DRASTIC Model 
The DRASTIC model, originally developed by the U.S. En-
vironmental Protection Agency (EPA), is among the most 
widely applied methods for assessing aquifer vulnerability to 
contamination. The model evaluates groundwater susceptibil-
ity by integrating seven hydrogeological parameters through a 
weighted-index approach [7]: depth to water (D), net recharge 
(R), aquifer media (A), soil media (S), topography (T), impact 
of the vadose zone (I), and hydraulic conductivity (C). The 
Drastic approach is divided into three significant parts [7]: 
weights, ranges and rating. According to Aller et al. (1987) [7], 
Journal of Water Resources and Ocean Science http://www.sciencepg.com/journal/wros 
 
203 
each DRASTIC parameter is assigned a relative weight rang-
ing from 1 to 5, reflecting its relative significance in control-
ling groundwater flow and contaminant transport ( Table 1 ). 
The weights of Drastic parameter are constant and may not be 
changed. The most significant parameter has weight of 5 
(Depth to water level, vadose media) and least significant has 
a weight of 1 (topography). The parameter ratings associated 
to each Drastic factors are variable and u ser can define and 
calibrate values to suit a given region [18]. Note that, the pa-
rameter D, R, S, T, and C have been assigned one value per 
range. However, A and I have a “typical” and variable rating. 
The Delphi technique [7] has been used to establish numerical 
weight and rating. This method is well - known and used 
worldwide [9, 13, 52, 55, 61]. In this technique, practical and 
research experiences of professionals in the area of interest are 
used to assess levels of risk [17, 52]. The DRASTIC index is 
determined by multiplying the rating assigned to each hydro-
geological parameter by its corresponding weight, as pre-
sented in Table 2. Based on these data, thematic maps were 
generated for each hydrogeological parameter using a spatial 
resolution of 30 m × 30 m and subsequently reclassified ac-
cording to their respective ratings and weights. The final 
groundwater vulnerability map was subsequently produced 
through a weighted-sum overlay analysis based on the DRAS-
TIC index, following the methodological workflo w shown in 
Figure 2.

2.3. Data Collection and Analysis 
To apply the DRASTIC model previously described, seven 
parameters are necessary. These parameters are: depth to water 
level, net recharge, aquifer media, soil media, topography, im-
pact of vadose zone, and hydraulic conductivity. The data relat-
ing to the depth of the water level were collected from a field 
survey conducted in April 2024. A total of 50 well locations 
have been selected in the study area for measuring depth to wa-
ter level and main groundwater quality parameters, including 
Nitrate and TDS. Groundwater samples were analyzed for se-
lected quality parameters, including nitrate and total dissolved 
solids (TDS), at the LHGR Laboratory o f the University of 
N’Djamena. The analyses were conducted in accordance with 
the standard methods recommended by the American Public 
Health Association [8]. The spatial distribution map of Depth to 
water level, TDS, and Nitrate was prepared using the standard 
kriging tool available in ArcGIS 10.8.1. Rainfall data has been 
collected from the National Agency of Meteorology. The an-
nual rainfall values were used to calculate the recharge using a 
simple water balance model [22]. The data relating to aquifer 
media are obtained from well log data available in the CDIG 
(Geographical Information Centre of the Water Ministry of 
CHAD) archives. The soil media map is obtained by digitaliza-
tion of the pedological map produced by Pias (1970) [50] in the 
study area. ASTER DEM (30 m spatial resolution) has been 
used for generating the topography of the study area using the 
surface tools of ArcGIS 10.8.1. The data relating to the impact 
of the vadose zone and hydraulic conductivity were obtained 
based on the data collected from CDIG. 
2.4. Validation of Groundwater Vulnerability 
Map 
Validation of the vulnerability map derived from the Dras-
tic model is needed before the use of its results. For this reason, 
a validation procedure was adopted to evaluate the accuracy 
of the Drastic model. Contaminant data sets collected from 
wells throughout the study area can provide an effective basis 
for validating the groundwater vulnerability map [20]. Nitrate 
and total dissolved solids (TDS), which are common indica-
tors of groundwater contamination, were selected as valida-
tion parameters for the groundwater vulnerability assessment, 
consistent with the methodology proposed by Bera et al. 
(2021)[13]. Along the same lines, Elmeknassi et al. (2021)[20] 
used groundwater contaminant parameters such as nitrate con-
centrations and electrical conductivity measured in the study 
site to validate the vulnerability map. Firstly, a superimposi-
tion of nitrate and TDS data on the vulnerability map was car-
ried out to obtain a qualitative validation of th e model. High 
values of the studied chemical compounds (nitrate and TDS) 
should be found in the high vulnerability zone, and conversely, 
low concentrations in the low vulnerability zone. This valida-
tion procedure was adopted by several authors [13, 20 , 76]. 
Secondly, the relationship between the DRASTIC Index (DI) 
and nitrate and TDS concentrations was further assessed using 
the Pearson correlation coefficient (r). This statistical measure 
is commonly used to quantify the strength and direction of the 
linear association between two continuous variables (Singh et 
al., 2015). The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was calcu-
lated using the following equation [13]: 
𝑟 =
𝑛(∑ 𝑥𝑦)−(∑ 𝑥)(∑ 𝑦)
√[𝑛 ∑ 𝑥2−(∑ 𝑥)2][𝑛 ∑ 𝑦2−(∑ 𝑦)2] (2) 
where r is the Pearson correlation coefficient, n represents the 
number of pairs, and x and y denote the two variables consid-
ered. 
At the end, the maximum nitrate and TDS concentrations 
were determined for each groundwater vulnerability class and 
compared with the corresponding DRASTIC Index (DI) val-
ues. A similar procedure was adopted by El -Meknassi et al. 
(2021) [20]. 
3. Result and discussion 
3.1. Depth to Water Level 
The first DRASTIC parameter, depth to water (D) level, is 
defined as the vertical distance between the ground surface 
and the water table [76]. This hydrogeological parameter is a 
key component of the DRASTIC model because the unsatu-
rated zone between the ground surface and the water table pro-
vides an important pathway for contaminant attenuation be-
fore pollutants reach the groundwater. In g eneral, greater 
depth to the water table provides a longer travel distance and 
greater opportunity for contaminant attenuation, thereby re-
ducing groundwater vulnerability to contamination [7]. Water 
level measurements are carried out in April 2024 in order to 
perform a depth to water level map. A total of 50 water points, 
comprising 15 wells and 35 boreholes, were surveyed. These 
wells and boreholes are located in the study area (Lai City) 
and its surroundings and used for domestic needs. The loca-
tions and measured water levels of the 50 wells and boreholes 
were used to interpolate the depth to the water table using the 
ordinary kriging method available in the Spatial Analyst ex-
tension of ArcGIS 10.8.1. The depth to the water table across 
the study area ranges from 1.5 to 9.0 m. Based on the DRAS-
TIC classification scheme, these values were divided into two 
classes: 1.5-4.5 m and 4.5-9.0 m, which were assigned ratings 
of 45 and 35, respectively ( Table 1 ). The southwestern and 
northwestern parts of the study ar ea, including Lai, Sategui, 
and Gabri Mbassa, are characterized by relatively shallow 
groundwater depths ranging from 1.5 to 4.5 m. These areas are 
therefore considered more vulnerable to groundwater contam-
ination. In contrast, the southeastern and northeastern sectors, 
including Djoun, Totalie, and Madjang, exhibit greater depths

paratively lower groundwater vulnerability to contamination.

3.3. Aquifer Media 
Aquifer media represent the porous and/or fractured geo-
logical formations that provide pathways for groundwater 
flow. The characteristics of the aquifer medium strongly in-
fluence contaminant transport and attenuation, as various 
physicochemical processes, including sorption, cation ex-
change, filtration, and dissolution, may occur during ground-
water movement. Therefore, aquifer media represents an im-
portant hydrogeological parameter in groundwater vulnerabil-
ity and quality assessment. Acco rding to Lathamani et al. 
(2015) [35], larger grain sizes and a greater abundance of frac-
tures, joints, or other openings enhance aquifer permeability, 
facilitating the migration of contaminants and consequently 
increasing groundwater vulnerability to cont amination. The 
DRASTIC parameter A (aquifer media) was characterized us-
ing lithological data obtained from well logs available in the 
archives of the CDIG (Geographical Information Centre of the

3.4. Soil Media 
Soil media corresponds to the uppermost part of the vadose 
zone, where biological activity is particularly active [13]. The 
Drastic Factor S (soil media) plays an important role in the 
control of recharge processes and the attenuation and removal 
of contaminants during their migration through the soil profile. 
The soil media map of the study site (Figure 6) was developed 
by digitizing the pedological map produced by Pias (1970) 
[50]. Three main soil types were identified within the study 
area: tropical ferruginous soil, halomorphic soil, and hydro-
morphic soil. Based on their relative influence on groundwater 
vulnerability, DRASTIC ratings were assigned to the different 
soil media as follows ( Table 1): tropical ferruginous soil was 
assigned a rating of 4, halomorphic soil a rating of 8, and hy-
dromorphic soil a rating of 16. Halomorphic soils are predom-
inantly distributed in the southern part of the study area, par-
ticularly around Gabri -Bassa and Djoun, whereas hydromor-
phic soils occur mainly in the northern sector, particularly 
around Sategui. The presence of h ydromorphic soils in the 
northern part of the study area may favor enhanced infiltration 
and contaminant transport, potentially increasing groundwater 
vulnerability in this sector. In the Figure 6 below, the photo of 
the representative soils types in the s tudy site (hydromorphic 
soil (a'), tropical ferruginous soil (c'), halomorphic soil (d')) 
has been illustrated in order to provide valuable visual images 
and improve interpretation of soil characteristics in place at 
the site studied.

3.5. Topography 
Topography, represented by slope, is an important parame-
ter that impact surface runoff and contaminant infiltration [7]. 
Steep slopes generally promote rapid surface runoff, thereby 
reducing the opportunity for contaminants to infiltrate into the 
subsurface. Conversely, gentle slopes favor water retention 
and infiltration, increasing the potential for contaminants to 
reach the groundwater system. The slope map of the study area 
(Figure 7 ) was derived from an Digital Elevation Model

210 
(DEM) with a spatial reso lution of 30 m. The DEM was pro-
cessed using the 3D Analyst extension of ArcGIS 10.8.1 to 
generate the slope layer. Slope values across the study area 
range from 0 to 9% and were classified into three categories: 
0-2%, 2 -4%, and 4 -9%. Each slope class was a ssigned a 
DRASTIC rating according to its relative influence on 
groundwater vulnerability, following the classification pro-
posed by Aller et al. (1987) [7] (Table 1). The resulting slope 
map indicates that the study area is predominantly flat, with 
most of the area characterized by slopes ranging from 0 to 2%. 
Such low-gradient conditions favor infiltration and limit sur-
face runoff, thereby increasing the potential for contaminants 
to migrate toward the groundwater. Consequently, the pre-
dominantly gentle to pography contributes to the relatively 
high groundwater vulnerability of the study area.

3.6. Impact of Vadose Zone 
The unsaturated zone, referred to as the vadose zone, is the 
subsurface layer extending from the ground surface to the wa-
ter table and overlying the saturated zone. This critical zone 
plays a role in controlling the downward transport and perco-
lation of contaminant -laden water toward the groundwater 
system [75]. During contaminant migration through the va-
dose zone, several physicochemical and biological processes, 
including mechanical filtration, biodegradation, dispersion, 
and volatilization, may occur. These processes contribute to 
the attenuation an d transformation of contaminants before 
they reach the water table [7]. The vadose zone acts as a nat-
ural protective layer for groundwater, with its capacity to at-
tenuate contaminants largely depending on its lithological 
characteristics. Data on vadose zo ne media were obtained 
from well logs available in the CDIG archives, using the same 
approach as that applied to characterize the aquifer media. The 
results indicate that the vadose zone in the study area consists

3.7. Hydraulic Conductivity 
Hydraulic conductivity is an important hydrogeological pa-
rameter that controls the rate and extent of contaminant 
transport within the groundwater system. Higher hydraulic 
conductivity values generally facilitate more rapid groundwa-
ter flow and contaminant migration, thereby increasing the po-
tential for groundwater contamination [52]. It is more chal-
lenging to achieve an accurate estimation of hydraulic conduc-
tivity, and it is also considered a limitation of the DRASTIC 
approach [38, 65]. The h ydraulic conductivity map of the 
study area (Figure 9) was developed based on hydraulic con-
ductivity values associated with the main rock types, as re-
ported by Gilbert (1982) [25]. Based on the reference values 
provided in Gilbert (1982) [25], conductivity values were as-
signed to each aquifer media (compact clay: 0.2 m/day; later-
itic clay: 11.15 m/day; sandy clay: 24.11 m/day; fine sandy 
clay: 38.97 m/day; medium sand: 43.2 m/day). The weighted 
scores assigned to the different hydraulic conductivity values 
corresponding to the aquifer media in the studied zone are pre-
sented as follows: 3 for compact clay, 9 for lateric clay, 15 for 
sandy clay, 21 for fine sandy clay, and 24 for medium sand.

3.8. DRASTIC Index 
The groundwater vulnerability map of Lai City (Figure 10) 
was generated by calculating the DRASTIC Index (DI) using 
Eq. (1) through a weighted overlay analysis of seven thematic 
layers, each represen ting one of the DRASTIC parameters. 
The resulting DI values range from 119 to 175 across the study 
area. Based on the classification proposed by Aller et al. 
(1987)[7], three groundwater vulnerability classes were iden-
tified. Based on the DRASTIC Index (DI) classification, areas 
with DI values below 119 were categorized as having low 
groundwater vulnerability, whereas DI values ranging from 
119 to 155 and 155 to 175 corresponded to moderate and high 
vulnerability classes, respectively. The spatial extent of each 
vulnerability class is presented in Table 3. The northern and 
southwestern parts of the study area exhibit high groundwater 
vulnerability to contamination, covering approximately 35% 
of the total study area ( Table 3 ). These highly vulnerable 
zones include Lai, Gabri Mbassa, Sategui, and Djongo. Field 
observations and available soil data indicate that these areas 
are predominantly characterized by hydromorphic soils ( Fig-
ure 6). In addition, the depth to the water table is relatively 
shallow, ranging from 1.5 to 4.5 m. The combination of shal-
low groundwater levels and hydromorphic soil conditions 
contributes to the elevated vulnerability of these areas by fa-
voring infiltration and facilitating the potential transport of 
contaminants toward the groundwater system. In contrast, the 
southeastern part of the study area is characterized predomi-
nantly by low groundwater vulnerability, accounting for ap-
proximately 33% of the total area (Table 3). The greater depth 
to the water table and other hydrogeological characteristics of 
this sector likely contribute to its relatively lower susceptibil-
ity to contamination. This low -vulnerability sector includes 
the localities of Djoun, Totalie, and Madjang. Field observa-
tions indicate that these areas are predominantly characterized 
by halomorphic soils and relatively deep groundwater levels, 
ranging from 4.5 to 9.0 m. These hydrogeological conditions 
are associated with relatively low groundwater vulnerability 
in this sector, primarily due to the greater depth to the water 
table. Bera et al. (2021) [13] noted that contaminants migrat-
ing through the vadose zone generally require more time to

213 
reach the groundwater table when groundwater is deeper, al-
lowing greater opportunity for natural attenuation. In contrast, 
the northeastern and central-eastern sectors of the study area, 
particularly around Tchindai and Totalie, display low to mod-
erate groundwater vulnerability. This spatial heterogeneity re-
sults from the combined effects of groundwater depth and the 
other hydrogeological parameters integrated into the DRAS-
TIC model.

3.10. Discussion 
This study represents the first comprehensive effort to as-
sess groundwater contamination and apply a GIS -based 
DRASTIC vulnera bility assessment in southern Chad, with 
particular emphasis on Lai City. As already mentioned, 
groundwater vulnerability assessment of Lai city is needed in 
order to carefully predict pollution that is faced by the city and 
its surroundings. Based on the nitrate and TDS results pre-
sented above, groundwater quality in Lai City is degraded in 
certain areas, which correspond closely to zones characterized 
by high groundwater vulnerability. 
In this study, the original DRASTIC model developed by 
Aller et al. (1 987) [7] was applied to assess the intrinsic vul-
nerability of groundwater in Lai City. The resulting DRAS-
TIC Index (DI) values indicated a spatial distribution ranging 
from low to high vulnerability. The groundwater vulnerability 
map revealed that areas characterized by shallow groundwater 
levels (1.5 -4.0 m), particularly Lai, Gabri Mbassa, Sategui, 
and Djongo, exhibit the highest degree of vulnerability. This 
finding is consistent with previous studies by Bojó rquez -
Tapia et al. (2009) [15], Bera et al. (20 21) [13], and El-Mek-
nassi et al. (2021)[20], which identified shallow groundwater 
levels as a key factor contributing to increased groundwater 
vulnerability. The high vulnerability observed in these areas 
can be attributed primarily to the shallow groundwa ter table 
and gentle slopes (0 -2%), which favor infiltration and facili-
tate the downward migration of potential contaminants. In ad-
dition, agricultural activities in parts of the study area, partic-
ularly around Gabri Mbassa and Sategui, may further increase 
the potential for contaminant infiltration into the groundwater 
system. These findings are consistent with recent studies by 
Said et al. (2025) [56] and Oyda et al. (2025) [48], which re-
ported similar groundwater vulnerability patterns using the 
DRASTIC approach in comparable hydrogeological settings. 
The accuracy of GIS -based Drastic model have been eval-
uated through nitrate and TDS parameters. As mentioned ear-
lier, validation of the Drastic model from observed contami-
nants datasets throughout the study area, such as nitrate and/or 
TDS is commonly applied by different authors [13, 20, 48, 56]. 
We found that elevated nitrate levels (> 45 mg/l) align with 
areas of high vulnerability, a correlation consistent with other 
studies [13, 20, 48, 56]. The primary contamination sources 
are agricultural inputs (e.g., fertilizers) because in the areas of 
high vulnerability (Lai and Gabri Mbassa) located along the 
Logone River, intensive irrigated agriculture is practiced. 
Conversely, the south eastern sides of the study area where 
Djoun, Totalie, Madjang areas are located, show low concen-
trations of TDS and nitrate. This is attributed to minimal agri-
cultural activity, resulting from poor soils (halomorphic soil) 
unsuitable for farming. This finding is consist ent with those 
obtained by Oyda et al. (2025) in similar areas. 
4. Conclusion 
The present study assessed groundwater vulnerability in Lai 
City, located in the Tandjile -East Department of southern 
Chad, using the GIS -based DRASTIC model and seven hy-
drogeological factors are considered. The resulting DRASTIC 
Index (DI) values ranged from 119 to 175, allowing the study 
area to be organized into three groundwater vulnerability clas-
ses: low, moderate, and high. The high-vulnerability class ac-
counted for approximately 35% of the study area, while the 
moderate- and low -vulnerability classes covered approxi-
mately 31% and 34%, respectively. The reliability of the GIS-
based DRASTIC assessment was further evaluated using ni-
trate and Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) concentrations. Higher 
nitrate and TDS concentrations were generally associated with 
areas classified as highly vulnerable, whereas lower concen-
trations occurred predominantly in areas of low vulnerability. 
The Pearson correlation coefficients between g roundwater 
vulnerability and the maximum concentrations of nitrate and 
TDS were r = 0.97 and r = 0.75, respectively, indicating strong 
positive relationships. These results provide supporting evi-
dence for the reliability of the GIS -based DRASTIC vulnera-
bility assessment. The northern and southwestern parts of the 
Journal of Water Resources and Ocean Science http://www.sciencepg.com/journal/wros 
 
217 
study area were identified as zones of high groundwater vul-
nerability. These areas are characterized by shallow ground-
water levels and predominantly hydromorphic soils, condi-
tions that favor infiltration and the downward transport of po-
tential contaminants. The most vulnerable areas, including Lai, 
Gabri Mbassa, Sategui, and Djongo, are located along the Lo-
gone River, where intensive irrigated agricultural activities 
may further contribute to the ri sk of groundwater contamina-
tion. Overall, the groundwater vulnerability map developed in 
this study provides a useful tool for groundwater resource pro-
tection, contamination risk management, and land -use plan-
ning in Lai City and its surrounding areas.
