In Gurnah, something irreversible happens to the migrant in going across waters, something in the register of a navigational confusion caused by the untranslatability of experiences between the two worlds. The bug in the existential compass is just as vague as it sounds. Let&s;s put it this way: the migrant knows that they are a stranger, but with some stretch and struggle, they might in time conjure up a somewhat functional, although fragile, affinity with the host society. All the while, they wonder if they have become irrelevant at ‘home’; and who wants to be treated like a foreigner by their kin? Belonging, after all, is a survival instinct, and if we are once beaten by one form of social alienation, it is only natural to be twice shy about throwing ourselves in the bosom of another community, even if it is the one that looks and sounds most like us. This vulnerability must be one of the reasons why out of Gurnah&s;s many characters who leave Zanzibar for English shores only one makes a return home.
