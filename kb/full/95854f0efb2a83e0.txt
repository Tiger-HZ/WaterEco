Abstract
Hybrid molecules integrating different pharmacophoric motifs have emerged as a promising strategy in anticancer drug 
discovery. In this study, we report for the first time the biological effects of a sulfonamide-chalcone hybrid in the PC-3 
prostate cancer cell line under standard culture conditions and nutrient deprivation. The molecular design of SSC185 
(µ-(2,5-dichloro-N-{4-[(3E)-4-(3-nitrophenyl)buta-1,3-dien-2-yl]phenyl}benzenesulfonamide) incorporates an α,β-
unsaturated system conjugated to the chalcone scaffold together with a sulfonamide moiety, structural features frequently 
associated with modulation of cancer-related signaling pathways. Results demonstrated that SSC185 reduced the MTT 
signal of PC-3 cells in a concentration- and time-dependent manner, with IC₅₀ values of 24.69 µM at 24 h and 18.9 µM at 
72 h, and exhibited enhanced activity under serum-deprived conditions, with an IC₅₀ of 4.20 µM. Complementary studies 
employing pharmacological modulators of autophagy, apoptosis, redox status, and proteostasis were conducted to further 
characterize the compound’s activity. SSC185 increased SQSTM1/p62 and LC3B-I levels without detectable changes in 
LC3B-II levels or increased acidic vesicle formation, accompanied by reduced AKT/RPS6 signaling and mitochondrial 
dysfunction. These molecular alterations were associated with the cellular response to SSC185, while nutrient deprivation 
markedly enhanced its activity in PC-3 cells, indicating that the cellular nutritional context influences the response to the 
compound.

Introduction
 Identifying relationships between the structural charac -
teristics of small molecules and their biological effects is 
a cornerstone of modern medicinal chemistry [ 1]. There -
fore, understanding chemical and physicochemical proper -
ties influences the interaction of compounds with cellular 
targets, guiding the development of molecules with greater 
efficacy and biological selectivity [ 2]. Among the promis -
ing approaches, molecular hybridization has proven to be an 
established medicinal chemistry strategy that aims to com -
bine two molecules into a new and unique chemical entity, 
capable of acting through different modes of action and tar-
geting multiple targets simultaneously, resulting in greater 
therapeutic efficacy and a lower propensity for resistance 
[3, 4]. In this context, sulfonamide-chalcone hybrids have 
emerged as attractive scaffolds owing to the combination 
of complementary pharmacophoric features [5–9]. The α,β-
unsaturated system of chalcones can act as an electrophilic 
center capable of interacting with nucleophilic residues in 
proteins [10–12], whereas the sulfonamide moiety contrib -
utes physicochemical and interaction properties favorable to 
molecular recognition by biological targets [13–15].
In cancer cells, adaptation to unfavorable conditions 
involves coordinated regulation of growth signaling, pro -
teostasis, and cellular stress responses, which contribute 
to survival under conditions such as limited nutrient avail -
ability and hypoxia [ 16–18]. These processes are particu -
larly relevant in advanced prostate cancer, in which tumor 
cells can maintain proliferation and survival despite adverse 
environmental conditions [19, 20]. Among the signaling net-
works involved in these responses, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR 
pathway integrates growth and nutrient-derived signals and 
regulates downstream effectors, including RPS6, involved 
in protein synthesis and cellular growth [ 21–23]. Beyond 
growth-related signaling, cellular adaptation to stress also 
requires mechanisms that maintain protein homeostasis 
through coordinated protein synthesis, turnover, and degra-
dation [24, 25]. Accordingly, cellular proteostasis involves 
stress-response mechanisms, including autophagy-related 
processes in which LC3 and the multifunctional adaptor 
SQSTM1/p62 participate [ 26, 27]. Changes in these pro -
cesses may also involve vesicular compartments, providing 
complementary information on cellular responses to stress 
[28]. In addition, nutrient availability can influence redox 
balance and mitochondrial function, thereby affecting tumor 
cell responses to pharmacological treatment [29, 30]. Thus, 
evaluating SSC185 under standard and nutrient-deprived 
conditions may help determine how the nutritional context 
influences its cellular effects.
SSC185 (µ-(2,5-dichloro-N-{4-[(3E)−4-(3-nitrophenyl)
buta-1,3-dien-2-yl]phenyl}benzenesulfonamide) is a 
sulfonamide-chalcone hybrid synthesized via Claisen-
Schmidt condensation and contains an α,β-unsaturated 
system with potential electrophilic reactivity (Fig. 1a). 
Previous studies have demonstrated biological activity of 
SSC185 in different tumor models, including alterations in 
cell-cycle progression and concentration-dependent apopto-
sis or necroptosis in SW-620 metastatic lymph node-derived 
colorectal cancer cells [ 31], as well as inhibiting adhesion 
and migration in B16-F10 murine melanoma metastatic 
cells [ 32]. These findings prompted further investigation 
of the cellular effects of SSC185 in other aggressive can -
cer models. PC-3 cells represent an androgen-independent 
prostate cancer model characterized by aggressive behavior 
and alterations in survival signaling pathways, including 
the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, providing a suitable system for 
further characterization of the cellular responses associated 
with SSC185.
Initial morphological observations of SSC185-treated 
PC-3 cells revealed cytoplasmic vacuolization, which 
prompted further investigation of acidic vesicular com -
partments and autophagy-related proteins, including LC3B 
and SQSTM1/p62. Based on these observations and on the 
cellular processes involved in stress adaptation described 
above, the present study characterized the effects of SSC185 
in PC-3 cells under standard culture conditions and nutri -
ent deprivation. Complementary analyses included cell-
cycle distribution, clonogenic capacity, cellular integrity, 
autophagy-related markers and acidic vesicular compart -
ments, AKT/RPS6 signaling, mitochondrial membrane 
potential, and responses to pharmacological modulators. 
This approach allowed the cellular effects of SSC185 to be 
examined across processes related to proliferative signaling, 
proteostasis, vesicular organization, mitochondrial function, 
and nutrient availability.

MTT assay
Cell viability was assessed indirectly using the MTT reduc-
tion assay [ 33]. PC-3 cells were seeded in 96-well plates 
at a density of 5 × 10³ cells/well and allowed to adhere for 
24 h. The cells were then treated with increasing concentra-
tions ranging from 0.02 to 100 µM of SSC185 as an ini -
tial screening to determine IC 50 values at 72 h following 
[34]. After determining the IC50 value at 72 h, the 24 h time 
point was evaluated, with concentrations ranging from 1.6 
to 100 µM, to assess the effects of compound exposure over 
time under standard culture conditions (with FBS) or serum 
deprivation, in which cells were deprived of FBS for 24 h 
before treatment. For the inhibition studies, cells were pre -
treated for 4 h with CQ, Q-VD-OPh, CHX, or NAC. After 
pretreatment, the inhibitors were removed, and the cells 
were exposed to the compound for 24 h, both under standard 
conditions and under serum deprivation conditions. Control 
groups treated only with inhibitors were included. At the 
end of exposure to the compound, MTT (0.5 mg/mL) was 
added, and the cells were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. Forma-
zan crystals were solubilized with DMSO, and absorbance 
reading was performed in a microplate reader (Spectramax 
190, Molecular devices, CA, USA) at 595 nm.
Intermittent exposure assay
For intermittent SSC185 exposure, the MTT assay was 
used. The assay was conducted as described previously 
[35]. PC-3 cells (5 × 10³ cells/well) were seeded in a 96-well 
plate. Cells were exposed to SSC185 (1.6 to 100 µM) for 
3, 6, 12, or 24 h. After each predetermined time point, the 
medium containing SSC185 was removed, and the wells 
were washed with PBS, followed by incubation in com -
pound-free media for 72 h. The MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) 
was added to each well and incubated for 3 h. The MTT-
formazan product dissolved in DMSO was quantified by 
measuring absorbance at 595 nm using a microplate reader 
Spectramax 190 (Molecular devices, CA, USA).
Colony formation assay
The clonogenic assay was conducted as described previously 
[34]. 500 cells per well of the PC-3 cell line were seeded in 
sterile 6-well plates. Concentrations of 4.8, 9.5, 19, 38, and 
76 µM of SSC185 were added to the corresponding wells 
for 6 and 24 h. After the designated time, the cells were 
washed with PBS, and the medium was promptly replaced 
with compound-free RPMI for 8 days. The cells were then 
washed with PBS and stained with a solution of methanol 
and 1% crystal violet for 5 min. Images were acquired using 
the G: BOX Chemi XRQ (Syngene, Cambridge, UK) and 
analyzed using the “ColonyArea” plugin in ImageJ software 
(National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).
Cell morphology analysis
Cell morphology was assessed by optical microscopy. PC-3 
cells were seeded at a density of 0.5 × 10 5 cells per well 
in 24-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. The cells 
were then treated with SSC185 at concentrations of 19 
and 38 µM, using CQ (100 µM) as a control. After 6 h of 
exposure, the cells were either examined directly by opti -
cal microscopy or stained with the Panoptic rapid kit [ 9] 
or Neutral Red (40 µg/mL) [ 36], as previously described, 
to visualize morphological changes and intracellular vacu -
olization. Images were acquired using a Zeiss Primovert 
inverted microscope (Oberkochen, BW, DE) equipped with 
a 200× total magnification objective.
Cell cycle distribution and plasma membrane 
integrity analysis
Cell cycle and plasma membrane integrity analysis was per-
formed by flow cytometry with propidium iodide (PI) DNA 
labeling following [37]. PC-3 cells were seeded in 12-well 
plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well. Cells were 
exposed to SSC185 (19 µM and 38 µM) under standard 
conditions for 6 and 24 h or (4 µM) under starvation condi-
tions for 24 h. The pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of PBS.

unexposed to the compound, was incubated with 200 µL

the distribution of DNA content. For the analysis of plasma 
membrane integrity, PC-3 cells were collected, washed with 
PBS, and incubated with PI at a final concentration of 25 µg/
mL for 10 min at room temperature, protected from light. 
After incubation, the samples were analyzed immediately. 
Cells were considered PI-negative when they exhibited 
preserved plasma membrane integrity. Analyses were per -
formed using a CytoFLEX cytometer (Beckman Coulter,

Mitochondrial depolarization analysis
Mitochondrial depolarization analysis was performed

were incubated for 15 min in the cold, protected from light. 
After incubation, the cells were washed with cold 1× PBS 
and centrifuged (2,000 rpm/5 minutes) to remove excess

(Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), and the data were ana-
lyzed using the FlowJo software v.X.0.7 (Treestar, Inc., San

Acidic vesicular organelles (AVOs) stained by 
acridine orange
The assessment of acidic vesicular organelles was per -

After staining, cells were analyzed using GFP and RFP

scope (Agilent BioTek Instruments Inc., Santa Clara, CA,

Western blotting
Protein expression was analyzed by Western blotting, as 
described previously [ 40]. Total protein extraction from 
PC-3 prostate cancer cells was performed using a buffer 
containing 100 mM Tris (pH 7.6), 1% Triton X-100, 2 mM 
PMSF, 10 mM Na3VO4, 100 mM NaF, 10 mM Na4P2O7, 
and 4 mM EDTA, from cells with or without exposure 
to SSC185 (9, 5, 19, and 38 µM) for 24 h. The extracted 
proteins were quantified using the Bradford method. After 
sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis 
(SDS-PAGE) (30 µg), the proteins were transferred to nitro-
cellulose membranes (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) 
and detected by incubating the membranes with specific 
primary antibodies and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated 
secondary antibodies. Protein bands were detected using the 
SuperSignalWest Dura Extended Duration substrate system 
(Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) and a G: BOX Chemi XX6 
gel documentation system (Syngene). Protein bands were 
normalized relative to tubulin or GAPDH.

Results
SSC185 reduces the MTT signal and clonogenic 
capacity of PC-3 cells in a time- and concentration-
dependent manner
To investigate the biological effects of SSC185 in androgen-
independent PC-3 prostate cancer cells, we first evaluated 
its effects using the MTT reduction assay. SSC185 reduced 
the MTT signal with an IC₅₀ of 19 µM after 72 h of continu-
ous exposure (Fig. 1b). When PC-3 cells were treated for 
24 h, SSC185 reduced the MTT signal in a concentration-
dependent manner, with an IC₅₀ of 24.69 µM (Fig. 6a), indi-
cating that the compound exerts a relevant effect in the first 
24 h of exposure.
To further characterize the temporal dependence of 
SSC185 activity, an intermittent exposure assay was per -
formed in which PC-3 cells were exposed to SSC185 for 
3, 6, 12, or 24 h. The medium containing the compound 
was then replaced with compound-free medium until a 
total incubation time of 72 h was reached. The MTT signal 
was then quantified. Short exposure intervals of 3 and 6 h 
required markedly higher concentrations of SSC185 (IC₅₀ 
> 50 µM) to reduce the MTT signal (Fig. 1c, d). In con -
trast, exposures of 12 h and 24 h resulted in IC₅₀ values of 
35.68 µM and 22.31 µM, respectively (Fig. 1c, d). These 
results indicate that the effect of SSC185 on MTT reduction 
depends on both exposure time and concentration, with lon-
ger exposure resulting in lower IC₅₀ values.
The long-term proliferative potential of PC-3 cells after 
transient exposure to SSC185 was evaluated using a clono -
genic assay. Cells were treated with SSC185 (4.8, 9.5, 19, 
38, and 76 µM) for 6–24 h, after which the medium was 
replaced with compound-free medium and colonies were 
allowed to form. The percentage of colony area was then 
quantified. After 6 h of treatment, a significant reduction 
in colony formation was observed only at 38 and 76 µM 
(p < 0.001), whereas no significant reduction was detected at 
4.8, 9.5, or 19 µM (Fig. 1e).
In contrast, after 24 h of exposure, a significant reduction 
in colony formation was observed across a broader concen-
tration range. Even the lowest concentration tested (4.8 µM) 
significantly reduced colony formation ( p < 0.001), while 
complete suppression of colony formation was observed at 
concentrations ≥ 19 µM (p < 0.001; Fig. 1f).
SSC185 promotes morphological changes and 
reduces plasma membrane integrity
Next, the initial effects of SSC185 on the morphology 
and plasma membrane integrity of PC-3 cells were evalu -
ated during the first 6 h of exposure. Plasma membrane 
integrity was assessed based on the proportion of PI− cells, 
which correspond to cells retaining the ability to exclude 
propidium iodide. Neutral red and rapid panoptic staining 
revealed concentration-dependent morphological changes. 
Treatment with SSC185 at 19 µM (IC₅₀) induced cytoplas -
mic vacuolization (Fig. 2a) and reduced the proportion of 
PI-negative (PI−) cells to 52.80% (p < 0.001; Fig. 2b), com-
pared with 89.95% in the control group. At 38 µM (2× IC₅₀), 
cells exhibited retraction and prominent cytoplasmic vacu -
oles with intense basophilia (Fig. 2a), accompanied by a fur-
ther reduction in the PI− population to 31.13% ( p < 0.001; 
Fig. 2b). These findings indicate a concentration-dependent 
reduction in the proportion of cells retaining plasma mem -
brane integrity. Although cytoplasmic vacuolization was 
observed at both 19 and 38 µM, the neutral red staining pat-
tern differed from that observed following chloroquine treat-
ment (CQ, 100 µM), indicating distinct patterns of vacuolar 
staining under these experimental conditions (Fig. 2a).
After 24 h of treatment, the proportion of PI− cells was 
reduced to 57.68% following exposure to 19 µM SSC185 
(p < 0.001) and to 16.27% following exposure to 38 µM 
SSC185 (p < 0.001), compared with 94.47% in the control 
group (Fig. 2c). The marked reduction in the PI− population 
at 38 µM indicates a pronounced loss of plasma membrane 
integrity after prolonged exposure.
SSC185 induces time- and concentration-dependent 
cell cycle alterations
Given that SSC185 impaired viability, morphology, and 
membrane integrity, its impact on cell cycle progression 
was evaluated in PC-3 cells. Flow cytometry analysis dem-
onstrated time- and concentration-dependent alterations 
in cell cycle distribution. After 6 h of treatment, SSC185 
reduced the G0/G1 fraction and increased the proportion of 
cells with 4 C DNA content, consistent with accumulation in 
the G2/M phase. The proportion of cells in G 2/M increased 
to 26.13% ± 1.00% at 19 µM and 23.93% ± 0.94% at 38 
µM ( p < 0.001), compared with 19.85% ± 0.39% in con -
trol cells (Fig. 3a). Correspondingly, the G 0/G1 population 
decreased from 40.27% ± 1.31% in control cells to 31.04% 
± 1.78% and 32.70% ± 1.72% at 19 and 38 µM, respectively 
(p < 0.01; Fig. 3a).
After 24 h, the effects diverged according to concentra -
tion. At 19 µM, the proportion of cells in G 2/M remained 
elevated and further increased to 40.68% ± 2.01% (p ≤ 0.01; 
Fig. 3b). In contrast, at 38 µM, cells shifted predomi -
nantly to G 0/G1 (46.47% ± 3.01%, p ≤ 0.01), accompanied 
by a reduction in the S-phase fraction (17.79% ± 1.64%, 
p ≤ 0.01; Fig. 3b). Notably, the sub-G0/G1 population already 
increased within the first 6 h at 38 µM, from 7.76% ± 0.50% 
in control cells to 11.21% ± 1.08% ( p < 0.05), suggesting

SSC185 exhibits time-dependent thiol reactivity
To further assess the possibility of nonspecific electro -
philic reactivity, a DTNB-based thiol-reactivity assay was 
performed with L-cysteine under physiologically relevant 
conditions. SSC185 induced a mild-to-moderate and time-
dependent reduction in free L-cysteine levels, with 78.50% 
± 2.78 of thiol content remaining after 120 min at 50 µM. 
A weaker effect was observed at 25 µM (87.55% ± 4.25), 
suggesting partial concentration dependence. Importantly, 
the overall profile was comparable to that observed for 
curcumin, the reference electrophilic compound (84.46% ± 
4.53 and 87.28% ± 0.66 at concentrations of 50 µM and 
25 µM, respectively). These findings indicate that SSC185 
exhibits measurable but not rapid or exhaustive thiol reac -
tivity under the tested conditions (Fig. 9).
Discussion
In this study, we investigated the effects of the sulfonamide-
chalcone hybrid SSC185 on androgen-independent PC-3 
prostate cancer cells and observed that its cellular effects 
depend on exposure time, concentration, and nutritional 
conditions. The greater response observed under nutrient 
deprivation highlights the influence of the nutritional con -
text on SSC185 activity and suggests that cellular adapta -
tion to limited nutrient availability may shape the response 
of PC-3 cells to the compound [42, 43].
From a structural standpoint, the sulfonamide moiety 
exhibits distinctive electronic properties, acting simultane -
ously as a hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor, which favors 
stable interactions with diverse biomolecular targets [44]. In 
addition, the presence of two highly electronegative oxygen 
atoms bound to sulfur confers pronounced polarity to this 
fragment, a feature commonly associated with protein and 
enzyme recognition [45, 46]. The chalcone scaffold, in turn, 
contains an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl group recognized as 
a prototypical Michael acceptor [47, 48]. This electrophilic 
center can form covalent adducts with cellular nucleophiles, 
particularly cysteine residues in regulatory proteins and 
metabolic enzymes. Consistent with this structural feature, 
SSC185 displayed measurable and time-dependent thiol 
reactivity in a DTNB-based assay using L-cysteine under 
physiological-like conditions, indicating the ability to inter-
act with sulfhydryl-containing molecules without inducing 
rapid or exhaustive thiol depletion [ 49, 50]. Such covalent 
modifications may alter protein function, stability, or rec -
ognition by protein quality control systems, thereby dis -
rupting cellular homeostasis [ 51, 52]. While electrophilic 
chalcone-containing compounds may potentially interact 
with multiple nucleophilic targets, selectivity in these sys -
tems is often influenced by binding orientation, target-site 
microenvironment, and the balance between reversible and 
irreversible interactions [ 53]. Therefore, additional studies 
will be important to further characterize target engagement 
and molecular selectivity associated with SSC185. In tumor 
cells, the formation of these adducts has been associated 
with dysregulation of redox-sensitive signaling pathways, 
impairment of energy metabolism, and accumulation of 
misfolded or damaged proteins, contributing to proteotoxic 
stress and activation of cell death mechanisms [52, 54, 55].

indicates that not all Michael acceptors behave as nonspe -
cific promiscuous agents, and several clinically relevant 
compounds contain related electrophilic motifs [ 56, 57]. In 
this context, the moderate and progressive thiol reactivity 
profile observed for SSC185, combined with its biological 
effects, contradicts the hypothesis of a mechanism driven 
solely by indiscriminate thiol sequestration. Thus, the 
hybrid structure of SSC185, combining a sulfonamide motif 
commonly linked to enzyme modulation with a conjugated 
aromatic system bearing Michael acceptor properties and 
electronegative substituents that tune electronic and lipo -
philic profiles, suggests the potential for multitarget cellular 
interactions [58–60].
Our comparison of continuous and intermittent exposure 
protocols showed that short treatments were insufficient 
to elicit robust cytotoxicity, whereas prolonged exposures 
resulted in a greater reduction in MTT signal and clonogenic 
capacity. The loss of proliferative potential observed in clo-
nogenic assays, particularly after 24 h of treatment, even 
at lower concentrations, suggests that SSC185 induces irre-
versible cellular damage, likely associated with loss of met-
abolic competence and eventual activation of late cell death 
programs [61, 62]. These findings support the notion that 
SSC185 exerts a time-dependent, cumulative effect, consis-
tent with intracellular accumulation and progressive stress 
induction rather than an immediate cytotoxic insult [ 63, 
64]. Similar behavior has been reported for sulfonamides 
and chalcone derivatives acting as modulators of cellular 
homeostasis [ 65, 66], survival pathways [ 67–71], energy 
metabolism [72–75], and mitochondrial integrity [76–78].
SQSTM1/p62 is a multifunctional adaptor protein that 
links LC3B to ubiquitinated substrates, thereby promoting 
their sequestration into autophagosomes for degradation 
[79, 80]. In many contexts, simultaneous LC3B-I-to-LC3B-
II conversion and p62 accumulation indicate an aborted 
or inefficient autophagic response, often reflecting a late 
blockade of autophagic flux [9, 81–83]. In the present study, 
SSC185 induced a significant increase in SQSTM1/p62 
and LC3B-I levels after 24 h of treatment, without a detect-
able change in LC3B-II or a significant increase in acridine 
orange-stained acidic vesicles. This pattern indicates that 
SSC185 alters the expression profile of autophagy-related 
markers; however, it does not allow definitive conclusions 
regarding autophagic flux. In particular, SQSTM1/p62 
accumulation may result from different cellular processes, 
including changes in its synthesis, degradation, or stress-
responsive regulation, and therefore cannot be interpreted 
solely as evidence of impaired autophagic degradation [ 84, 
85]. Similarly, the absence of detectable LC3B-II accumu -
lation does not exclude alterations in autophagic activity, 
since LC3B-II levels reflect the balance between its forma -
tion and degradation. Thus, the molecular changes observed 
following SSC185 treatment may reflect a cellular stress-
associated response involving autophagy-related proteins, 
although their functional relationship with autophagic flux 
remains to be established. Dynamic approaches, including

In this context, it is important to consider that SQSTM1/
p62 is a multifunctional adapter protein involved in vari -
ous cellular stress response pathways through its multiple 
protein-interaction domains [ 26, 86]. Previous studies 
have demonstrated that p62 expression can be modulated 
in response to oxidative and proteotoxic stress and is func -
tionally linked to stress-responsive pathways, including 
the KEAP1/NRF2 axis [26, 87–90]. Against this backdrop, 
the increase in SQSTM1/p62 levels observed following 
SSC185 treatment may represent a stress-associated cel -
lular response. The morphological changes observed after 
exposure to SSC185, including cytoplasmic vacuolization 
and loss of plasma membrane integrity, corroborate the 
occurrence of cellular injury but do not establish a direct 
link to p62 accumulation [ 91–93]. Thus, current data do 
not determine whether p62 accumulation contributes func -
tionally to SSC185-induced cytotoxicity or represents a 
secondary response to cellular stress. Furthermore, inhibi -
tion of protein synthesis with cycloheximide did not sub -
stantially alter the response to SSC185 under the conditions 
evaluated, suggesting that ongoing protein synthesis is not 
required for the observed effect [ 94, 95]. Consequently, 
these findings indicate that SSC185 treatment is associated 
with alterations in markers related to stress and proteostasis; 
however, the functional contribution of SQSTM1/p62 and 
the specific mechanisms underlying these changes remain 
to be determined.
Data from the literature highlight the importance of the 
PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in many cellular processes, 
contributing especially to the growth and survival of vari -
ous tumors [ 96–98], and this pathway is frequently hyper -
activated in prostate cancer [ 99–102]; Notably, 4E-BP1 is 
frequently hyperphosphorylated in CRPC, allowing for high 
cap-dependent translational activity via eIF4E [ 103, 104]. 
In the exploratory immunoblot analysis, SSC185 treatment 
was associated with reduced AKT and total 4E-BP1 lev -
els, whereas phosphorylated 4E-BP1 remained relatively 
unchanged. Although these observations may indicate dif -
ferential regulation of signaling pathways involved in cell 
growth and protein synthesis, their exploratory nature pre -
cludes quantitative interpretation. In contrast, the reduc -
tion in p-RPS6 was consistently observed in subsequent 
independent experiments, supporting an effect of SSC185 
on this downstream signaling component. Likewise, the 
increase in p-ERK1 may be consistent with the activation of 
a compensatory stress-response mechanism [105], although 
its functional significance remains to be established. These 
molecular alterations were accompanied by time-depen -
dent changes in cell-cycle distribution and a progressive 
reduction in clonogenic capacity. At earlier exposure times, 
alterations in cell-cycle distribution were observed with -
out comparable loss of long-term proliferative capacity at 
lower SSC185 concentrations, whereas prolonged exposure 
resulted in a more pronounced and persistent reduction in 
clonogenic potential. Previous studies have shown that inhi-
bition of the PI3K/AKT axis can reduce RPS6 phosphory -
lation and alter cell-cycle progression and the expression 
of cell-cycle regulatory proteins in prostate cancer cells, 
supporting an important role for PI3K/AKT/mTOR signal -
ing in the maintenance of prostate cancer cell proliferation 
[106–108]. Nevertheless, although the signaling alterations 
observed here occurred alongside the progressive cellular 
effects of SSC185, the present data do not establish a direct 
causal relationship between these molecular changes and 
the development of sustained cytotoxic effects.
Evaluation of SSC185 under standard and nutrient-
deprived conditions revealed a marked influence of the 
nutritional context on the cellular response to the compound. 
Under standard culture conditions, pharmacological modu -
lation of autophagy, protein synthesis, apoptosis, or redox 
status did not substantially alter the response to SSC185. In 
contrast, nutrient deprivation markedly potentiated SSC185 
activity, reducing the concentration required to affect the 
MTT signal. Under this condition, pre-treatment with NAC 
partially attenuated the effect of SSC185. Although NAC 
is widely used as an antioxidant and a precursor for intra -
cellular cysteine and glutathione, its protective effects may 
also involve direct interactions with reactive electrophilic 
species [ 109, 110]. Therefore, the partial protection con -
ferred by NAC cannot be interpreted as direct evidence that 
oxidative stress mediates SSC185 activity. Mitochondrial 
membrane potential also decreased during prolonged expo-
sure and under nutrient-deprivation conditions, indicating 
that mitochondrial dysfunction accompanies the cellular 
response to SSC185. Although these findings demonstrate 
that nutrient deprivation increases the sensitivity of PC-3 
cells to SSC185 and that this intensified response is associ -
ated with alterations in redox-sensitive and mitochondrial 
parameters, further studies are needed to directly assess 
cellular metabolism and determine the metabolic processes 
involved.
Conclusion
Taken together, the results of this study demonstrate that 
SSC185 exerts time- and concentration-dependent effects 
on androgen-independent PC-3 prostate cancer cells. These 
effects were accompanied by alterations in cell cycle dis -
tribution, reduced AKT/RPS6 signaling, and increased 
levels of SQSTM1/p62 and LC3B-I without detectable 
1 3
Molecular and Cellular Biochemistry
accumulation of LC3B-II or increased formation of acidic 
vesicles, as well as a delayed compromise of mitochondrial 
membrane potential. Notably, nutrient deprivation markedly 
potentiated the cellular response to SSC185, lowering the 
concentrations required to affect the MTT signal and clono-
genic potential. In summary, the data suggest that SSC185 
activity is influenced by the cellular nutritional context and 
involves coordinated changes in signaling, stress-related 
proteins, and mitochondrial function. Further investiga -
tion into these interconnected cellular responses may help 
elucidate the molecular processes underlying the enhanced 
effects of SSC185 under nutrient-deprived conditions.
Although additional studies will be necessary to further 
characterize target selectivity, pharmacological safety, and 
the molecular mechanisms underlying SSC185 activity, 
including orthogonal approaches to evaluate covalent inter-
actions and protein engagement, the present findings support 
the potential of sulfonamide-chalcone hybrids as promising 
pharmacological scaffolds capable of compromising adap -
tive stress responses and proliferative capacity in aggressive 
prostate cancer cell models.
