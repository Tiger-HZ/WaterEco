Abstract
Freshwater ecosystems, such as Lakes and Reservoirs, have been facing escalating anthropogenic 
activities across the world, leading to the decline of lake water quality. Consequently, monitoring 
and evaluation of Lake water quality conditions are essential to ensure a sustainable water quality 
ecosystem. A comprehensive assessment of the Weighted Arithmetic-Water Quality Index (WA-
WQI), Canadian Council of Ministers Water Quality Index (CCME-WQI), and Nemerow’s 
Pollution Index (NPI) was undertaken to investigate the spatiotemporal alternation in Tappan 
Lake's aquatic wellbeing. Eleven monitoring stations were established across the lake to collect 
key parameters at every 1-meter depth intervals of each stations, including temperature (14.3-27.8 
0C), pH (7.5-9.9), dissolved oxygen (0.45-14.8 mg/L), specific conductivity (~400- 900 µS/cm), 
turbidity (2.7-15.9 NTU), blue-green algae (0.43-6.19 µg/L), chlorophyll-a (4.1-24.7 µg/L). While 
some sections of the Lake exhibited conformity with established water quality standards, others 
displayed significant deviations, particularly in terms of dissolved oxygen concentrations, Specific 
Conductivity, and elevated levels of blue-green algae. Seasonal trends and lake stratification were

WQI consistently reported unfit throughout the entire stations, with mean values rising from

seasonal stratification, upstream turbidity spikes, and recurrent harmful algal blooms. These Water 
Quality indices will provide a unified evaluation outlook by combining various water quality 
parameters into a comprehensive numerical assessment and suggest clear guidelines for the 
stakeholders engaging in the restoration and management of the Tappan Lake.

Introduction
Rise in the global population has created an escalating demand for water across various 
applications, leading to a notable proliferation of pollutant discharge (Reid et al., 2018; Shemer et 
al., 2023; Tella, 2025). Consequently, contemporary waterbodies across the world are 
experiencing substantial degradation in water quality (Akhtar, 2021; Beck, 2005). This global 
environmental challenge of water quality deterioration, fueled by anthropogenic activities (Akhtar, 
2021), has been disrupting ecosystems and threatening public health globally (Herrera, 2019; Reid, 
2018; Shemer et al., 2023). Natural water bodies, particularly lakes, are especially vulnerable due 
to contamination from industrial effluents, agricultural runoff, rapid and unplanned urbanization, 
industrialization, and climate change impacts like altered precipitation patterns and rising

temperatures (Priya, 2023; Saxena, 2025). Emerging contaminations like microplastics and HABs, 
coupled with climate-driven changes, further worsen threat levels (Akhtar, 2021).
In recent years, the USA has been facing significant challenges in safeguarding water quality while 
simultaneously striving to enhance water supply and sanitation (Herrera, 2019). On the other hand, 
there are huge threats to aquatic life and the freshwater ecosystem (Bănăduc, 2022; Reid et al., 
2018). Even though numerous mitigating measures have been implemented to maintain the 
integrity of freshwater ecosystems, the water quality problems in the lakes and reservoirs are 
consistently alarming (US EPA, 2023; N. S. Akhtar, 2021). The situation underscores the urgency 
of extensive monitoring efforts with global policy frameworks.
In response to these threats, scientific research has initiated developing methodologies to assess 
and monitor water quality effectively (Glasgow, 2004; Priya, 2023; Reid et al., 2018). Over the 
last few decades, several approaches and methods have been introduced to assess water quality. 
Yet, each method has its own inherent model limitations and configurations to adequately evaluate 
the comprehensive water quality assessment (Bascaron, 1979; Uslu, 2024). More importantly, 
studies related to comprehensive spatial and temporal assessments (Uslu, 2024) through multiple 
Water Quality Index (WQI) to critically analyze lake water quality are limited.
When addressing water quality concerns in these areas, this study utilizes the WQI as a primary 
tool. WQI interprets complex data by a comprehensive and standard score, reflecting the overall 
condition of water by incorporating key parameters (N. I. Akhtar, 2021; Ebrahimi Sarindizaj, 2026; 
Uddin, 2021) such as temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen (DO), specific conductivity (SPC), 
turbidity, blue-green algae (BGA), chlorophyll-a (Chl-a), and depth. WQI implements the method

of aggregation and assessment of multiple water quality parameters and depicts it with a single 
value index (Bascaron, 1979; Brown, 1970).
This research is intended to monitor high-resolution datasets and conduct a multi-dimensional 
investigation into the present state of water quality using three different globally accepted indices, 
namely WA-WQI, CCME-WQI, and NPI. The WA-WQI is a widely applied method, in which 
weights are assigned based on their relative importance and interpreted in a single score (Bascaron, 
1979; Brown, 1970; Tyagi, 2013). In contrast, the CCME-WQI integrates scope, frequency, and 
amplitude of guideline exceedances, offering flexibility and adaptability across diverse ecosystems 
(CCME, 2001; Haldar, 2016). The NPI integrates the maximum and minimum values of multiple 
parameters (Kowalska, 2018; Su & W, 2022), thereby providing comprehensive impairment levels 
and identifying contamination hotspots. Utilizing these advanced analytical water quality indices 
and interdisciplinary approaches, this study aims to elucidate the fundamental mechanisms behind 
pollutant proliferation and critically evaluate the health of water. Through this endeavor, it is 
intended to generate long-term sustainable solutions to preserve and enhance water quality for 
future generations.
Although several studies have been conducted in the past few decades on freshwater quality, there 
remain some research gaps. Firstly, much of the existing research has focused on limited 
spatiotemporal datasets, which restrict their ability to capture fine-scale variability within lakes 
and reservoirs (N. I. Akhtar, 2021; Uslu, 2024). Many investigations employ very sporadic and 
low-frequency sampling (Mogane, 2023; Uslu, 2024), which may overlook some seasonal shifts, 
water dynamics like lake stratification, storm events, and watershed inputs. Similarly, even though 
numerous studies related to WQI were documented, most of those investigations utilized a single 
index, resulting in an incomplete representation of the lake’s ecological state and missing the

comparative strength of multiple WQI indices (Auddy et al., 2023; Ebrahimi Sarindizaj, 2026). 
Furthermore, studies that incorporate high-resolution, depth-wise temporal datasets (Shi, 2025) 
across multiple lake zones to understand vertical mixing, stratification, and spatial water quality 
degradation patterns are still limited and critical. Addressing the existing gaps, this study 
distinguishes itself by spatially and temporally distributed, depth-resolved high-resolution datasets. 
Along with that, multiple WQI indices were used to assess a more comprehensive and granular 
perspective of the lake’s water quality. To the best of the author’s knowledge, this is a first attempt 
to assess lake water quality using multiple indices within a depth-resolved high-resolution dataset 
and assessment framework. Applying the WQI framework, the study provides insight to assess the 
lake's environmental health, analyze spatial variations, vertical dynamics, and identify factors 
influencing water quality dynamics.
Methodology
Study Area
Tappan Lake, also known as Tappan Reservoir, is situated in Harrison County, Ohio (40.3417° N, 
81.2275° W). This reservoir is an integral component of the Muskingum Watershed Conservancy 
District (MWCD), which oversees water resources management in this region. The Lake was 
created in the late 1930s by damming Little Stillwater Creek. The lake spans an area of 9.5 km 2 
with a watershed area of 184 km2 and has a normal pool elevation of 274.1 m above sea level. The 
maximum depth of this lake is about 10.4 m, and the shoreline extends over 76 km, making it the 
second-largest waterbody managed within the MWCD. This large land-water interface heightens 
the lake’s exposure to watershed influences, which are either forested, dispersed residential, or 
agricultural.

This lake was selected for research owing to its ecological significance and rising threats driven 
by anthropogenic activities, HABs occurring, and episodic hypoxic events. The lake experiences 
summer temperatures around 27.5°C, and winter temperatures as low as -5°C. Annual precipitation 
in the lake averages around 1016 mm. During seasonal stratification, the lake experiences 
depletion of dissolved oxygen recorded as low as 0.45 mg/L, where the Ohio EPA minimum 
standard is 4 mg/L (Ohio EPA, 2010). Turbidity, with a concentration of undissolved particles of 
soil, algae, and other foreign matter, has been detected at dangerously high rates (Zuckerman, 
2025) in the water from Tappan Lake. Likewise, the total dissolved solids (TDS) concentrations 
were found to be 1527 mg/L, exceeding the Ohio EPA limit of 1500 mg/L(Ohio EPA, 2010). These 
exceedances show a great concern for the Village of Cadiz, which relies on Tappan Lake for its 
Water supply, suggesting that the lake water should be rigorously treated before its supply to the 
community.
Data Collection and Sampling
Water quality assessment of Tappan Lake was conducted in a systematic pattern to ensure reliable, 
comprehensive, and accurate data collection to achieve spatiotemporal variations in key water 
quality parameters. Eleven discrete locations were strategically selected throughout the lake, as 
shown in Figure-1, encompassing the areas of inlet zones, outlet zones, mid-lake zones, and the 
most vulnerable locations with pollution. Precise global positioning system (GPS) coordinates 
were documented for each site to ensure consistency in all water quality sampling for two years.

To account for seasonal and temporal variability, data samples were collected biweekly during the 
spring, summer, and fall months. Hence, through meticulous planning of the temporal cadence of 
sampling, the methodology guarantees that the data accurately represent various conditions 
throughout the year. Furthermore, such comprehensive frameworks are vital for reliable WQI 
calculations, as they enable precise interpretation of ecological conditions, identify seasonal 
fluctuations, and detect patterns of spatial water quality deterioration.
Although WQI calculations were aggregated into three representative seasonal snapshots (April, 
June, August) for methodological consistency across indices, the full depth-resolved dataset (1-m 
resolution) was retained and analyzed separately to characterize vertical structure. The seasonal 
compositing approach was chosen because WA-WQI, CCME-WQI, and NPI require uniform 
temporal inputs; however, the underlying high-resolution profiles were still used to interpret 
stratification, hypolimnetic oxygen depletion, and depth-specific biological responses. This 
dual-framework approach preserves the comparability of WQI outputs while still leveraging the 
scientific value of the depth-resolved measurements.
Different WQI Computation Equations
WQI is structured through a systematic four-step sequential stage. Firstly, it initializes the 
identification and selection of important water quality parameters that are essential for assessing 
the health of the water body. Following this, sub-indices for each parameter are derived, which 
convert initial data into a standardized scale. Subsequently, the weightage of each parameter is 
derived to reflect its relative importance precisely. Finally, these sub-indices are aggregated to 
produce an overall WQI, offering a comprehensive assessment of the water quality (Uddin, 2021). 
These indices were eventually combined to derive the overall WQI of the water body, which 
indicated a comprehensive assessment of the lake’s water quality. In this study, WQI was

ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS
calculated using three different water quality indices. The first was the Weighted Arithmetic Water 
Quality Index (WA-WQI) method, which was initially proposed by Horton (1965) and later refined 
by Brown (1970). The second was the Canadian Council Members of the Environment Water 
Quality Index (CCME-WQI) developed by CCME (2001) and the third was Nemerow’s Pollution 
Index (NPI) developed by Nemerow (1974). Using these three indices, WQI was finally computed 
for three distinct sampling periods: early April (spring), mid-June (summer), and late August (fall).
To enhance the accuracy of the WQI, a comprehensive set of parameters and a meticulously 
calibrated weighting system were employed. It ensured that the significance of each parameter was 
represented with greater efficiency. The resulting WQI score range was then classified into distinct 
categories to expedite interpretation, as shown in Table-2. Such a nuanced classification propounds 
the utility of the WQI in the sustainable decision-making process pertaining to water management, 
treatment, and remediation strategies (Brown, 1970).

Weighted Arithmetic-Water Quality Index (WA-WQI)
Normalization of each parameter in the context of WQI is performed to monitor and measure the 
parameters on a common scale, typically ranging from 0-100, to compare the units and magnitude 
meaningfully. To normalize, ideal and poor values were assigned for each parameter based on 
water quality guidelines. Further, a scaling formula was implemented to obtain a standardized 
scale of 1-100, representing the state of water quality.
In this process, measured values (Mi) represent the actual concentration or measured water quality 
parameter data and indicate the current state of the water parameter. Ideal value (Ii) represents the 
optimal value of water quality, suggesting that water is of high quality. On the other hand, poor 
values represent the threshold value at which the water quality is hazardous and reveals poor water 
quality and potential health hazard. Normalization of each parameter was accomplished following 
an understanding of the context and reference values given by the authoritative sources as the 
World Health Organization (WHO) and the Ohio Environmental Protection Agency (Ohio EPA). 
Using a simple arithmetic equation, we calculated the normalized value (Qi), which represented 
the normalized value of a particular water quality parameter (Table-3).

Weighted Arithmetic WQI (WA-WQI)
Typically, the parameters with values that exceeded the standard value had more influence on the 
WA-WQI. In this study, the WA-WQI was found to be highly influenced by blue-green algae 
(BGA), contributing 83.24% of the total weight, substantially higher than that of all other water 
quality parameters. This disproportionate influence is not surprising since HABs are extremely 
sensitive to over-nutrient enrichment and water stagnation (Zhao, 2022). Nonetheless, the least 
contributing was specific conductivity, which accounted for 0.083%, which seemed reasonable 
since precipitation inflow contributes more than that of the groundwater flow and other sources of 
inflow (Summers, 2024). Using the weighted arithmetic method, WQI scores across the locations 
from S1 to S11 indicated that all sites were classified as "Unfit" (WQI > 100) due to elevated 
turbidity, BGA, and chlorophyll-a levels. The average WQI in the spring was 199.33 (Unfit), with 
values ranging from 135.27 to 239.35 (Table-5). During spring (April), the water quality was 
primarily dominated by SPC (~360–700 µS/cm; with many stations showing > 500 µS/cm), above 
the 300 µS/cm standard used in weighting. The reason behind this was due to spring runoff and 
road salt inputs (Ellen Foley, 2023; Kaushal, 2018), which increased ionic concentration in the 
lake and ultimately contributed to early-stage nutrient enrichment.

There also seemed to be a moderate presence of BGA and Chl-a, indicating early-stage biological 
enrichment (Brenckman, 2025; Paerl, 2013). By summer, the water quality further worsened with 
an average value of 497.34 (Unfit), with values ranging from 309.9-757.8 (Table-6). Turbidity 
spiked at the upstream S10-S11 (>14 NTU). The reason behind this is the precipitation-driven 
sediment inflow, which carries substantial sediment, including nitrogen and phosphorus loads from 
the agricultural zone, particularly during Ohio’s fertilizer application season (Ohio State 
University Extension, 2024; Ohio, Purdue, and University of Illinois, 2020). In general, the BGA 
was twice as much as it was in the spring at several stations (e.g., S5, S8–S11 >2 µg/L), and Chl-

summer stratification, which accelerated the internal loading. The anoxic sediment boosts the 
release of bound phosphorus, which may further aid in phytoplankton growth (Nürnberg, 1984). 
By fall, it was substantially increased to 1521.205 (Unfit), with values ranging from 948.57-
1738.07 (Table-7). In the fall, the condition was critical, where the BGA dramatically increased 
with a value of higher than ≥6 µg/L at several stations (~3.3–6.2 µg/L), Chl-a approximately 
doubled (~17–25 µg/L typical maxima) with the elevated range of turbidity. There could be many 
reasons for this, including warm surface waters, sustained summer stratification, and nutrient 
accumulation throughout the summer, which resulted in the proliferation of HABs (Nürnberg, 
1984). Some other reasons for higher index values could be due to intense biological enrichment, 
cyanobacterial proliferation, and sustained phytoplankton biomass (Chl-a) during stratified 
conditions, which produced high index values as suggested by Summers (2024).

CCME-WQI
The CCME-WQI is highly influenced by the number of total tests performed, the number of failed 
parameters, and the magnitude of those failures at each sampling period and station. Consequently, 
the results in spring were 66.23 (Fair), in summer (mid-June) were 81.42 (Marginal), and in fall 
(late August) were 67.89 (Fair). The primary contributors to these low scores could be due to high 
turbidity and BGA concentration at almost every time and location. During the spring months, 
almost all the stations (Table-8) exhibited fair water quality (~60–79) except S11. S11 (Inlet zone) 
showed marginality with an index value of 50.43, where most of the variables failed, among which

SPC, turbidity, and BGA accounted for most failures. This pattern may be attributed to road salt 
input (Ellen Foley, 2023; Kaushal, 2018), early summer bloom, and spring runoff (Newell, 2024; 
Steinman, 2015). There seemed to be multiple minor to moderate deviations (often turbidity and 
BGA), but not consistently with higher amplitude. In contrast, during summer, many stations 
showed improvement and maintained a good status (≈80–86) except in stations S5 and S9. CCME 
is highly influenced by the failure counts and their magnitude across all tests, rather than the single 
highly weighted variable. During the summer, even though there was a rise in BGA (Nürnberg, 
1984) and turbidity (Lettenmaier, 1999), some stations had just a single failing parameter; 
therefore, CCME still ranked them as Good. There seemed to be a “good” water quality status 
because of some localized improvement in the failure counts (Table-9), despite the biological 
increase (Brenckman, 2025; Cihelio, 2021) at many sites in the summer. However, by the fall 
season (Table-10), water quality was fair (≈69–72) at most of the stations and marginal at stations 
S8, S9, and S11 with more failures and large excursions. In contrast, during summer, more 
parameters are failing often (BGA, turbidity, occasionally DO/pH excursions), leading to the score 
dropping to Fair/Marginal. Seasonal dynamics also contributed to these fluctuations (Dugener, 
2023; Jane, 2021; LaBrie, 2024).

Nemerow’s Pollution Index (NPI)
The Nemerow’s Pollution Index (NPI) was applied to evaluate the overall water quality index 
using the maximum and the average values of single-factor pollution indicators of selected water 
quality parameters. Computed values varied from station to station and ranged from 1.41 to 2.56 
in the spring (early April) with an average of 1.94, indicating a moderately polluted status (Table-
11). During the spring months, BGA and SPC had a higher impact, which might be the 
consequence of early-season bloom and spring-runoff-driven road salt input (Ellen Foley, 2023; 
Kaushal, 2018; Kumar, 2008), with max term consequent to high NPI. During summer (mid-June), 
the value ranged 2.72-6.51, where the average was 4.30, indicating a seriously polluted status 
(Table-12). There still seemed to be serious NPI in the summer because of large single-factor ratios 
of BGA and SPC. This occurs due to the impact of nutrient inflow from agricultural runoffs, 
enhanced by nitrogen and phosphorus levels (Ohio State University Extension, 2024; Ohio, Purdue, 
and University of Illinois, 2020). Furthermore, hypoxic conditions could be the reason aids for 
internal loading with the release of sediment-bound phosphorus, which further enhances HABs 
(Nürnberg, 1984). Lately in fall (late August), the value ranged 8.14-14.90 with an average of 
13.04, stating it as seriously polluted (Table-13).

Across the three methods employed, including W-WQI, CCME-WQI, and NPI, the outcome 
indicated that the water quality at Tappan Lake is under significant ecological pressure and threat

The dominance of BGA in the WA-WQI weighting scheme introduces important management 
blind spots. A BGA-driven WA-WQI may overemphasize bloom-related impairment while 
underrepresenting chronic stressors such as conductivity, turbidity, or dissolved oxygen depletion. 
This is particularly relevant for Tappan Lake, where multiple stressors co-occur. To mitigate this 
limitation, we explicitly rely on the CCME-WQI and NPI indices that distribute influence more 
evenly across parameters and incorporate exceedance frequency and worst-case conditions. The 
combined multi-index framework therefore prevents over-interpretation of WA-WQI alone and 
ensures that management decisions are not disproportionately influenced by a single biological 
parameter.

Conclusion
This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of Tappan Lake's water quality using three 
different widely accepted water quality indices: the Weighted Arithmetic- Water Quality Index 
(WA-WQI), Canadian Council Ministers of Environmental Water Quality Index (CCME-WQI), 
and Nemerow’s Pollution Index (NPI). High-resolution, depth-resolved spatiotemporal datasets 
were integrated into those indices. Despite the methodological and framework differences, all 
indices across all seasons and monitoring stations indicated that the water quality of Tappan Lake 
is in significant deterioration. It is primarily driven by strong seasonal stratification, explosive 
toxic blue-green algae, higher turbidity inflows, and specific conductance exceeding the limits.
WA-WQI index consistently classified the lake as persistently polluted across every location and 
season, i.e., with values intensifying from spring to fall as BGA and Chl-a concentrations surged. 
This is strongly related to cyanobacterial proliferation, hypolimnetic hypoxia, and exceedance of 
specific conductivity. While CCME-WQI indicated the lake to be fair to marginal, with multiple 
parameter failures. It reflected that CCME-WQI is sensitive to the frequency and magnitude of 
parameter failures rather than the dominance of any single pollutant. It indicates the probable 
improvement during partial mixing in the lake. In contrast, the NPI consistently indicated serious 
pollution across seasons and stations, with elevated NPI values being driven by peaked turbidity 
and BGA. It emphasized the maximum exceedance among the parameters over the dominance of 
a single stressor. Moreover, the comparative use of WA-WQI, CCME-WQI, and NPI 
demonstrated the value of multi-index evaluation and provided a sustainable solution for making 
strategic planning and decisions.
Depth-wise interpretation showed the presence of seasonal stratification and periodic hypolimnetic 
hypoxic events (< ~0.45 mg/L). This finding suggests that the compounding impact of nutrient

Overall, Tappan Lake’s water quality is facing ecological stress and demands urgent attention for

implementation of effective and sustainable watershed management strategies and continuous

to be helpful in supporting Tappan Lake restoration and sustainable freshwater management.
