1. INTRODUCTION 
Air pollution is one of the most pressing environmental challenges globally, 
with persistent adverse effects on human health, ecosystems, and climate [1–3]. 
Numerous peer-reviewed studies document air pollution levels worldwide, reporting 
decreases in emissions and improvements in air quality [4, 5], as well as significant 
global reductions in particulate matter (PM), nitrogen oxides, sulfur oxides, carbon 
monoxide, volatile organic compounds, and other gaseous pollutants in 2020 [6, 7]. 
Despite these improvements, air pollution remains a major global concern due to 
its well-documented impacts on human health and the environment. Anthropogenic 
air pollutant levels vary due to ch anges in emissions linked to industrial activities, 
traffic patterns, agricultural practices, and energy production, and further vary by time

of day and season in response to meteorological conditions and vegetation cover [8, 9]. 
Linkages betw een air quali ty and climate are multiple and bidirectional [10]. 
Consequently, changes in air pollutant concentrations exert complex impacts on both 
global and regional climate [11]. Anthropogenic and natural pollutant emissions [12], 
together with their atmospheric transformations, govern the ambient concentrations 
of aerosols and trace gases in any given region, a quantificatio n that remains both 
methodologically challenging and critically important for environmental management. 
Ambient pollution levels can further in crease during episodic events driven 
by anthropogenic or natural causes, including seasonal agricultural practice s such 
as biomass burning on the outskirts of cities [13] and long-range transport events, 
most notably Sah aran dust intrusions [14]. Together, these factors underscore that 
air quality management must account for a wide spectrum of emission sources and 
atmospheric processes operating simultaneously across multiple spatial and temporal 
scales. Environmental control strategies aimed at further imp roving air quality 
therefore require tight collaboration among various stakeholders and must be grounded 
in robust scientific evidence. Over the last decade, it has become increasingly clear 
that such strategies need to be tailored to the specific characte ristics of particular 
geographical regions [15]. 
The COVID -19 pandemic and the associated lockdown measures impos ed 
worldwide in 2 020 created an unprecedented, real -world test of rapid emission 
reduction. The sharp reduction of traffic, industrial activity , and human mobility 
produced measurable changes in air pollutant concentrations in cities and regions 
across the globe, offering a rare opportunity to isolate the contribution of spec ific 
emission sectors to ambient pollution levels. Yet the atmospheric r esponse was not 
uniformly one of improvement: intense Saharan dust intrusions and other episodic 
natural events counteracted the anthropogenic emission reductions at certain times 
[16], resulting in 2020 being a year of exceptional variability, spanning fr om some 
of the lowest pollutant concentrations on record to episodic peaks well above 
regulatory thresholds. This full range of variabi lity makes 2020 a particularly 
valuable reference year for understanding the resilience and vulnerability of urban 
atmospheres under contrasting emission and meteorological conditions. Geographic 
variations in urban –suburban–rural air pollutant concentration s represent another 
critical dimension of air quality assessment. While rural areas generally exhibit the 
lowest values of anthropogenic pollution, followed by suburban/peri -urban zones 
and urban cores, this traditional gradient has become considerably more complex in 
recent decades. Rapid urban sprawl, accelerating suburban development, and shifting 
mobility patterns near large urban centers have changed the classical urban-to-rural 
pollution gradient. Despite its clear policy relev ance, comparatively little is known 
about the magnitude and drivers of air quality differences between urban and suburban 
areas [17, 18]. This knowledge gap is particularly significant in Eastern European

metropolitan regions undergoing rapid economic development and urban expansion, 
where suburban zones are growing in both population and industrial activity. 
In Romania, the cities of Bucharest, Braşov, and Iaşi are recurrently identified 
in European Commission records related to infringement procedures concerning air 
quality exceedances, particularly for PM 10 and NOx concentrations [16]. Previous 
studies conducted at city and national scale repo rted significant reductions in NO 2 
(10–40%) and PM10 (19–25%) during the 2020 lockdown period, primarily associated 
with decreased mobility and traffic intensity [16, 19, 29]. More recent analyses 
extending beyond 2020 have further revealed notable temporal evolution in urban–
suburban pollutant gradients: in Bucharest, differences in PM10 and PM2.5 background 
concentrations between urban and suburban sites over 2020–2024 remained modest 
(only a few μg·m–3), whereas the N Ox urban background shifted from a positive 
urban excess (annual mean of 13 –14 μg·m–3 in 2020–2021) to a negative value of 
approximately −31 μg·m–3 in 2024, indicating that suburba n N Ox background 
concentrations have come to exceed th ose in the urban core [18]. Under th e 
broader context of documented climate change in Romania [21–23], a thor ough 
characterization of air pollutant dynamics in these cities during 2020 provides a 
valuable reference for long -term mon itoring efforts and cont ributes to growing 
knowledge on air pollution in regions still under significant anthropogenic pressure 
[24, 25]. 
The present study investigates urban–suburban contrasts in major air pollutant 
concentrations (i.e., PM10, PM2.5, NO, NO2, NOx, CO, SO2, O3) across the metropolitan 
regions of Bucharest, Braşov, and Iaşi during the exceptional year of 2020, using a 
statistical framework that, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously 
applied to this specific context. By combining observational dat a from the national 
ground-based monitoring network with longitudinal analys is, probability density 
functions, and change point analysis, we characterize how pollutant concentrations 
evolved across the p re-lockdown, lockdown, and post -lockdown periods of 2 020, 
and how urban and suburban areas differed in their response. By capturi ng the full 
range of pollutant variability, from the minimum values recorded during strict mobility 
restrictions to the maximum values observed during desert dust intrusion episode s, 
this study provides new insights into the interplay between anthropogenic emission 
reductions and natural atmospheric forcings on urban air quality . The findings 
extend knowledge on air pollution dynam ics at local and regional scales and offer 
scientific support for the design and evaluation of targeted air pollution control 
strategies in Eastern European urban centers. 
The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: Section 2 describes the study 
areas, observational datasets, and statistical method s employed; Section 3 presents 
and comparatively discusses the results; and the Conclusion section summarizes 
the main findings and their implications for air quality management.

2.1. STUDY AREAS 
The locations of the studi ed metropolita n areas are shown on the map of 
Romania in Fig . 1. Only a few characterist ics of these cities are presented here, 
as they are described in detail elsewhere [16], including a comprehensive account 
of the mobility and social restrictions imposed during 2020 to control the spread of 
SARS-CoV-2.

The major pollution sources in these cities can be classified into several 
categories: industrial (including electrothermal power stations, many of which still 
rely primarily on fossil fuels), traffic (characterized by a mixed vehicle fleet ranging 
from non -EURO to EURO 6 standards), construction (with numerous active 
construction sites both within t he cities and in their sur roundings), and residential 
heating. Additionally, scattered collection points where solid domestic waste is stored 
or illegally burned represent a further diffuse emission source. Extensive agricultural 
activities are carried out in the suburban areas of all three cities. All three metropolitan 
regions are characterized by continued economic growth and industrial development, 
with Bucharest leading the other two in both respects. 
2.2. DATA SOURCE AND AIR POLLUTANT DATASETS 
Major air pollutant concentration data for the cities of Bucharest, Braşov, and 
Iaşi were obtained from the National Air Quality Monitoring Network web -based 
platform (www.calitateaer.ro). This platform provides hourly and daily in situ 
measurements of major air pollutants (PM10, PM2.5, CO, SO 2, NO, NO 2, NOx) and 
some minor pollutan ts at national scale. In the present study, data from 17 air 
quality monitoring stations located in the metropolitan areas of the three cities were 
used. For each metropolitan region, u rban and suburban monitoring stations were 
designated as follows: stations B1 through B6 represent the urban area of Bucharest, 
while B7 and B8 represent the suburban area. Stations BV1, BV3, and BV5 were 
selected for the urban area of Braşov, and BV4 for its suburban area. Stations IS1 
through IS3 describe the urban area of Iaşi, while IS4 and IS5 characterize the 
suburban area. The classification of stations as urban or suburban follows the typology 
defined by the national monitoring network and is consistent with the categorization 
applied in [16]. 
2.3. DATA ANALYSIS 
For each station and each pollutant, hourly measurements were compiled and 
aggregated to daily values; all analyses are therefore based on daily mass concentrations. 
To assess the temporal and spatial characteristics of air quality and to evaluate the 
impact of l ockdown regulations across all three metropolitan regions, the 2020 
calendar year was divided into three consecutive periods: January 1 to March 15 
(pre-lockdown, pre -LD), March 16 to May 15 (lockdown, LD), and May 16 to 
December 31 (post-lockdown, post-LD). For each metropolitan region and each time 
interval, standard descriptive statistics were computed. 
Probability density functions (PDFs) were applied to each time series to 
identify changes in mean concentrations and in the overall struct ure of pollutan t 
Article no. 804 Gabriela Iorga et al. 6 
 
distributions across the three periods. Change point analysis [26] was applied to 
identify statistically significant shifts (at the 95% confidence level) in the mean 
level of pollutant concentrations over defined time win dows. These analyses were 
complemented by correlation analysis and by a longitudinal examination of the 
NOx/CO and PM 2.5/PM10 concentration ratios, which serve as indicators of traffic -
related emissions; results from all metrics were interpreted in combination. 
Individual concentration peaks in the time series were further investigated to 
attribute them to specific pollution episode types, following the procedure described 
in [27]. The duration of a pollution event was defined as the time spa n from the 
initial increase in PM 10 mass concentrati ons above the regulatory daily threshold 
of 50 μg ·m–3 to their complete dissipation below this threshold, corroborated by 
analogous behavior in co -occurring gaseous pollutants in the case of events 
associated with temperature inversions.

3.1. SPATIAL AND TEMPORAL DIFFERENCES IN CONCENTRATIONS BETWEEN

Figures 2 –4 show the temporal variations of daily mass concentrations of 
PM10 and PM2.5, followed by NO, NO2, and NOx = NO + NO2, and finally O3, SO2, 
and CO, f or all studied urban and suburban areas throughout 2020. I n Bucharest 
(Fig. 2), a clear and general decrease in pollutant mass concentrations, with the 
exception of O3, is observed from the start of restriction measures (March 15–May 
15, 2020); the lockd own period is delimited by vertical dashed lines. This is 
followed by the summ er period, during which an additional factor contributing to 
pollutant decline is the increase in mixing layer depth associated with rising 
temperatures. Ozone , produced photoche mically via reactions involving nitrogen 
oxides in the presence of solar radiation, responds in the opposite sense, increasing 
when precursor NO x levels decline. Except for the winter months (January –March 
2020), SO2 concentrations remain relatively stable throughout the year, suggesting 
that emissions from residen tial heating and stationary industrial sources were less 
responsive to the lockdown restrictions than traffic -related emissions, particularly 
in suburban areas. 
A pronounced PM 10 peak is clearly v isible in all three Figs. 2–4 during the 
lockdown period. Th is spike co rresponds to the most intense desert dust intrusion 
recorded in Romania in 2020, with the dust source identified in the Aralkum desert 
(the desiccat ed southern Aral Sea basin, Kazakhs tan). The even t pro duced very 
high concentrations of particulate matter across large regions of Romania on 27–28 
March 2020, reaching maximum values of over 170 µg ·m–3 in Bucharest and 
Brașov, and over 90 µg ·m–3 in Iași. This episode has been previously do cumented 
[16, 28].

3.2. POLLUTANT DISTRIBUTION STRUCTURE, CHANGE-POINT ANALYSIS, 
AND SOURCE ATTRIBUTION 
Probability density distribution analysis. Time series of NOx and PM2.5 serve 
as useful indicators of the contribution of traffic to overall pollution levels in a 
given area. However, PM 2.5 measurements were not available for all stations and 
areas across the full year 2020; P M10 concentrations were therefore used a s a 
substitute. PM10 variations are only partially indicative of traffic changes, since this 
fraction also includes contributions from natur al sources ( e.g., wind -blown dust) 
and the coarse fraction PM10–PM2.5 can contain aged aerosol of industrial origin that 
has undergone physical and chemical transformation during atmospheric mixing and 
transport. Combining the probability density functions (PDFs) of N Ox and PM 10 
with change point analysis, longitudinal analysis of the PM 2.5/PM10 and N Ox/CO 
ratios, and c orrelation analysis allows identification of the relative influence of 
traffic on the overall pollution in each area. The PDFs for N Ox and P M10 time 
series in the urban and suburban areas of all three cities, for the three periods in 
2020 (pre-lockdown, lockdown, and post -lockdown), are shown in Fig s. 5 and 6. 
The PDFs highlight changes in the full concentration distribution, not merely 
changes in the mean, and thus provide a richer characterization of air quality shifts. 
The PDFs clearly show that both PM 10 and NOx decreased immediately after the 
start of the lockdown, with a pronounced reduction in daily peak concentrations, 
consistent with a reduction in road traffic and broader socio-economic activity. 
Tropospheric O3 increased during the lockdown (PDF curves not shown), 
in keeping with the O 3 titration effect: lower N Ox, especially NO, reduces O 3 
consumption, resulting in elevated surface ozone concentrations. NOx and PM 10 
concentration levels differ across cities and the rankings are pollutant -specific. For 
NOx, lockdown-period mean concentrations rank from highest to lowest as Brașov, 
Bucharest, and Iași; for PM 10, the corresponding ranking is Iași, Bucharest, and 
Brașov. The regional climate and topography of eac h city contribute to these 
differences, as discussed in Section 3.1. N Ox is considerably more sensitiv e to 
traffic restrictions than PM 10, confirming the dominant role of road transport in 
NOx emissions. The post -lockdown NOx distributions suggest a recove ry toward, 
but not a full return to, pre -pandemic concentration levels, which may reflect 
lasting changes in mobility behavior, remote working patterns, or the influence of 
prevailing meteorological co nditions. Clear di fferences are evident between the 
urban and suburban N Ox PDFs. Both NOx and PM10 distributions show not only a 
reduction in mean concentrat ions during lockdown, but also a narrowing of 
variance and a decrease in extreme events, reflecting the suppression o f local 
emission sources. Because PM 10 has multiple contributing sources – traffic,

Correlation analysis reveals additional source attribution insights. A negative 
correlation between N Ox and tropospheric O 3 is consistently ob served across all 
sites (r = −0.57 and −0.60 for urban and suburban Bucharest ; r = −0.65 and −0.47 
for urban and suburb an Brașov; r = −0.68 and −0.46 for urban and suburban Iași), 
reflecting the well-known photochemical O3–NOx titration relationship. 
In Bucharest, the temporal evolution of SO 2 appears largely decoupled from 
the other pollutants, pointing to industrial and/or residential heating as its dominant 
source. Nevertheless, sulfate contributions to the fine PM fraction are non-negligible 
(r = 0.44 in urban and r = 0.40 in suburban Bucharest for SO 2 vs PM2.5). In urban 
Iași, SO2 is well correlated with NOx (r = 0.77), and contributes more to sulfates in 
the coarse PM10 fraction (r = 0.68) than to the fine fraction ( r = 0.53). In suburban 
Iași, SO 2 shows a weaker correlation with PM 10 (r = 0.26), while the N Ox–PM10 
correlation (r = 0.46) suggests a non -negligible nitra te component, pointing to 
different dominant atmospheric chemistry relative to urban Iași. These results are 
consistent with previous findings on the differing aerosol composition between 
Romanian cities [29]. 
Urban Brașov shows a pattern broadly similar to Bucharest, with SO 2 well 
correlated with both N Ox (r = 0.77) and PM 10 (r = 0.66) and PM 2.5 (r = 0.65). 
In suburban Brașov, however, the SO 2–NOx correlation is considerably weaker 
(r = 0.22), while SO2 retains a moderate correlation with PM10 (r = 0.36). This pattern 
suggests that suburban Brașov PM 10 contains a non-negligible sulfate contribution, 
but that this sulfate does not share a common combustion source with N Ox, 
potentially implying a con tribution from biomass burning or a distinct stationary 
industrial source. 
 
Comparison with pollutant levels in neighboring countries . There is now a 
large body of evidence that nitrogen oxides and other primary pollutants dec lined 
significantly across Euro pe and worldwi de in 2020. However, marked pollution 
inequalities between regions, and particularly between Western and Eastern 
Europe, were also revealed. Nitrogen oxides fell sharply across Europe due to 
reduced traffic, whi le PM 2.5 showed only a limited decline and O 3 trends were 
mixed. The European Environment Agency (EEA) explicitly noted that, despite 
lockdown-driven reductions, central and eastern Europe continued to record the 
highest PM concentrations in the continent, with the use of solid fuels for domestic 
heating and industry among the principal contributing factors [31].

4. CONCLUSIONS 
This study presents a statistical analysis of yearlong measurements of near -
surface major air pollutants – trace gases (NO, NO2, NOx, O3, SO2, CO) and particulate 
matter (PM10 and PM2.5) – in the urban and suburban areas of three major Romanian 
metropolitan centers (Bucharest, Brașov, and Iași) throughout the exceptional year 
of 2020. The combination of strict COVID -19 mobility restrictions and intense 
natural dust episodes made 2020 a year of unusually wide pollutant variab ility, 
spanning from some of the lowest anthropogenic emission levels on record to 
episodic concentration peaks far exceeding regulatory thresholds. This full range of 
conditions makes the 2020 dataset a uniquely informative baseline for understanding 
how urban and subu rban atmospheres in Eastern Europe respond to rapid changes 
in emission patterns and natural forcing. 
The main findings of the study can be summarized as follows. As expected, 
the lockdown periods result ed in significant reductions in traffic-related pollutants 
(i.e., NOx, CO, and PM 10) across all three cities, while O 3 concentrations increased 
due to reduced N Ox titration, and SO 2 remained largely unchanged, reflecting the 
dominance of stationary combustion (residential heating and industry) as its primary

source. The onset of lockdown restrictions produced measurable changes in N Ox 
concentrations within days: in urban Bucharest, NOx dropped from a pre-lockdown 
mean of approximately 77 –67 µg·m–3 to about 27–29 µg·m–3 within 4–5 days of 
lockdown declaration, illustrating the near -immediate sensitivity of atmospheric 
nitrogen oxide levels to traffic suppression. 
Urban and suburban areas responded differently to these changes, and the 
character of the differe nces varied from city to city. Urban at mospheres prov ed 
highly sensitive to direct emission changes, exhibiting sharp concentration drops 
and rebounds that closely tracked restriction timelines. Suburban atmospheres, by 
contrast, showed a more buffered reg ional response, w ith lower N Ox amplitudes, 
more stable background concentrations of CO and SO 2, and a weaker correlation 
between NOx and PM10, consistent with a pollution regime that is more chemistry -
dominated than emission -driven. This urban –suburban con trast in atmosphe ric 
behavior is a central finding of the study and has direct implications for the design 
of air quality monitoring networks and emission control strategies. 
Urban air quality responded to NOx emission reductions on a shorter timescale 
than to PM 10 reductions. This difference r eflects the mu lti-source nature of PM 10: 
while traffic-related PM declined during the lockdown, other sources (e.g., residential 
heating, construction, wind-resuspension, and long-range transported dust) maintained 
their contributions, resulting in a less pronounced and more diffuse PM10 response. 
The PM 2.5/PM10 ratio declined during the lockdown in all cities where data were 
available ( e.g., from 0.59 to 0.37 in urban Bucharest), confirming that coarse -
particle source s became relative ly more important as t raffic-related fine particle 
emissions were suppressed. 
The three cities exhibited distinct pollution fingerprints that reflect their differing 
topography, emission structures, and atmospheric chemistry. Brașov, enclosed within 
the Carpathian Arc, showed the highest suburban PM10 annual mean (36.93 µg·m–3), 
likely due to restricted dispersion in its topographic setting. Iași recorded the highest 
urban PM10 and PM2.5 annual means, and NOx in its urban area was well correlated 
with both SO 2 and CO, pointing t o a greater co ntribution of mixed combustion 
sources compared to Bucharest. The source attribution analysis, combining PDF 
analysis, change point analysis, and longitudinal examination of PM 2.5/PM10 and 
NOx/CO ratios, confirms that traffic and combustion are the dominant common sources 
of both pollutant pairs across the three cities, but that their relative contributions and 
spatial distributions differ. These findings reinforce the conclusion that air pollution 
reduction measures must be adapted to the spe cific emission and meteorological 
characteristics of each city. 
Comparing the results with the broader European context, the pollutant levels 
observed in Bucharest, Brașov, and Iași are broadly consistent with the regional pattern 
for south-eastern Europe, with PM10, PM2.5, SO2, and CO generally ranking in the 
lower half of the European distribution, and NO 2 somewhat higher for Bucharest. 
This is consistent with the ongoing European Environment Agency assessment that

central and eastern Europe continues to experience higher PM concentrations than 
western Europe, driven in part by the persistent use of solid fuels for domestic 
heating and industry [31]. 
Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledg ed. PM2.5 data 
were not available for all stat ions throughou t 2020, constraining the spatial 
coverage of the PM 2.5/PM10 ratio analysis. N Ox/CO data for suburban areas were 
largely unavailable, limiting the longitudinal analysis in suburban zones. Transient 
meteorological events, such as intense precip itation, fog, or storm systems, can 
temporarily lower atmospheric pollutant concentrations through enhanced wet 
scavenging and increased atmospheric mixing; these short -duration meteorological 
effects were not isolated in the present analysis, as they cons titute an intrinsic part 
of the atmospheric background and their influence on the overall statistical results 
is considered marginal. Additionally, while traffic and combustion were identified 
as dominant sources, a firm quantitative conclusion regarding the relative contribution 
of solid fuel use for heating and industry, and the potential benefit of transitioning 
to cleaner energy forms, could not be drawn from the available dataset alone. 
By documenting air p ollution conditions spanning from unusually lo w 
anthropogenic emission levels to episodic natural dust peaks across three Romanian 
metropolitan regions in 2020, this study provides an empirical benchmark for 
attribution studies, chemical transport model ev aluation, and population exposure 
assessment under contrasting emission and meteorological scenarios. The multi-site 
urban–suburban observation framework strengthens understanding of local versus 
regional controls on pollution extremes and offers actionable evidence for air quality 
management. As the broader body o f evidence on Romania's changing climate 
continues to grow , the air quality record documented here serves as a valuable 
reference point for long-term monitoring networks. Future work should extend this 
analysis to subsequent years (2021 –2025) to characterize the longer-term recovery 
trajectories and to assess whether the urban –suburban N Ox gradient inversion 
reflects a structural shift in emission patterns or a transient response to post-pandemic 
mobility changes.
