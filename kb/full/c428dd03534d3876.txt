In Yuncheng, China, some state-protected water-deity temples are gradually losing their ritual function, whereas peripheral villages outside official protection continue active rainmaking practices. We ask how these practices are sustained and reorganized amid limited institutional support and an ageing practitioner population. Drawing on embodied ethnography at six temples and 60 interviews, we develop the concept of Cultural Energy Autonomy (CEA), bringing Ingold’s taskscape into dialogue with Chinese cosmological understandings of qi. Ritual continuity is sustained through three interrelated dimensions: temporal rhythms responsive to drought and flood coordinate collective action; sensory attunement through ritual labour renders qi bodily perceptible; and ritual objects anchor experience within a sacred spatial order. Our findings show that water-deity worship is a geographical process through which environmental dependence is transformed into spatial order and collective action. They also advance geographical debates on sacred–secular relations by showing how local sacredness is spatially organized through the interplay of agrarian conditions, bodily labour and village authority. Heritage conservation should therefore move beyond preserving static buildings to sustaining dynamic, embodied social and environmental practices.
