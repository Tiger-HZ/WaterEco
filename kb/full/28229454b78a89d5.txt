Abstract
Purpose To investigate the clinical application value of multi-parameter imaging with 
dual-layer detector spectral CT (DLCT) for GTH visualization.
Methods A total of 61 patients who underwent abdominal portal venous phase-
enhanced SLCT were retrospectively enrolled. The raw image data were reconstructed 
into 120 kVp conventional images (CI120 kVp) and virtual monoenergetic images 
(VMIs) at 40, 50, 60, and 70 keV (VMI 40−70 keV). Regions of interest (ROIs) were placed 
on the main trunk of GTH and the erector spinae muscle at the corresponding level 
to measure CT values and standard deviations (SD, indicative of image noise). The CT 
values, image noise, signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs), and contrast-to-noise ratios (CNRs) 
of VMI and CI120 kVp were analyzed. The image quality and anatomical information 
of each group were subjectively evaluated by integrating three-dimensional (3D) 
reconstruction technology, iodine density map, and effective atomic number (Zeff ) 
map.
Results For GTH visualized on VMI40 keV, the CT value (530.56 ± 82.33 HU), SNR 
(25.34 ± 5.63), and CNR (57.85 ± 12.43) was significantly higher than those of the 
remaining groups (all P < 0.001). Subjective scoring indicated that VMI40 keV achieved 
the optimal image quality and vascular visualization performance. The iodine density 
map demonstrates relatively high density in vascular regions, the Zeff map shows 
distinct coloration between vascular regions and non-vascular areas.
Conclusion VMI 40 keV using DLCT provides better GTH image quality compared with 
CI120 kVp, which may offer valuable support for clinical practice and preoperative surgical 
planning.
Keywords Dual-layer detector spectral CT (DLCT), Gastrocolic trunk of Henle, Virtual 
monoenergetic image
1 Introduction
GTH typically arises from the confluence of multiple tributaries, including the right gas -
troepiploic vein (RGEV), right colic vein (RCV), and anterior superior pancreaticoduo -
denal vein (ASPDV), occasionally, the superior right colic vein (SRCV) and middle colic 
Page 2 of 9
He et al. Discover Imaging (2026) 3:10 
vein (MCV) may also be incorporated. Characterized by a high rate of anatomical varia -
tion and a predisposition to intraoperative bleeding, the GTH serves as a critical con -
trol point in procedures such as laparoscopic right hemicolectomy [ 1]. Thus, accurate 
characterization and diagnosis of GTH variations are crucial for preventing iatrogenic 
vascular injury and ensuring radical tumor resection. To date, most GTH-related stud -
ies have relied on intraoperative observations and cadaveric dissections [ 2–4]. CT, one 
of the most widely used diagnostic modalities in clinical practice, enables clear, accu -
rate, and non-invasive visualization of human vascular anatomy [ 5], thereby facilitating 
large-scale in vivo studies of the GTH. However, the dilution of intravascular contrast 
agent concentration during systemic circulation impairs the clear visualization of small 
branches (e.g., ASPDV) [ 6, 7], limiting the anatomical assessment of the GTH via CT 
venography. DLCT can optimize the contrast between vessels and surrounding tissues 
by extracting VMI, while enhancing the CT values of target tissues to achieve optimal 
image quality. While this technique has been applied in arterial imaging [ 8, 9], its appli-
cation in GTH imaging remains unreported. In this study, we evaluated the imaging 
quality of the GTH using multi-parameter DLCT images combined with 3D reconstruc -
tion technology, with the aim of identifying the optimal VMI for GTH visualization. In 
summary, this is a novel imaging method that significantly improves the image quality of 
GTH. Our findings are expected to provide a theoretical basis and reference for clinical 
practice and precision surgery in abdominal surgery.
2 Materials and methods
2.1 Patients
A total of 94 patients who underwent abdominal enhanced spectral CT scans between 
August 2025 and September 2025 were retrospectively reviewed, with 61 patients ulti -
mately included after meeting the study eligibility criteria. Among the included patients, 
there were 35 males and 26 females, aged 36–81 years, with a mean age of (60.91 ± 12.82) 
years. Inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) Visualization of the GTH in the por -
tal venous phase of abdominal enhanced CT; (2) No prior gastrointestinal surgery before 
CT examination; (3) Without respiratory artifacts. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Severe 
CT image artifacts precluding diagnostic adequacy; (2) History of abdominal or vascular 
surgery; (3) Obscured GTH visualization due to adjacent tumors; (4) Severe hepatic or 
renal dysfunction (Fig. 1).
2.2 Computed tomography protocols and scanner
All patients underwent enhanced abdominal CT scan with SLCT (Spectral CT 7500, 
Philips Healthcare, the Netherlands). The patients were positioned in the supine posi -
tion for scanning. The scanning range spanned from the hepatic dome level to the ischial 
tuberosity level. Bolus Tracking technology was employed for contrast agent monitor -
ing, with the tracking plane set at the descending aorta and a trigger threshold of 110 
HU. Arterial phase and portal venous phase scans were acquired at 10 s and 36 s post-
threshold triggering, respectively. Scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage: 
120 kV, tube current: adaptive, pitch: 0.85, gantry rotation time: 0.75 s, matrix: 512 × 512, 
slice thickness: 1 mm, slice interval: 1 mm. A non-ionic iodinated contrast agent, iopam-
idol (370 mgI/mL, Beijing Northland Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.), was administered via 
the antecubital vein using a high-pressure injector (MEDRAD® Centargo CT Injection

System, Bayer AG, Germany) at an infusion rate of 3.0 mL/s, with a total dose ranging 
from 1.5 to 2.0 mL/kg of body weight. A spectral-based imaging (SBI) dataset was auto -
matically generated post-acquisition.
2.3 Image reconstruction and analysis
The SBI data were transferred to the Philips IntelliSpace Portal workstation, CI 120 kVp, 
VMI40−70 keV, iodine density maps, and Zeff maps were reconstructed. Subsequently, vol -
ume rendering (VR) images were generated based on the CI120 kVp and VMI40−70 keV. ROIs 
were placed on the main trunk of GTH and the left erector spinae muscle at the same 
axial level. The CT values and SD were measured, followed by the calculation of SNR 
and CNR. To ensure consistency, the diameter of each ROI was set to two-thirds of the 
GTH lumen diameter, and vascular walls were carefully avoided. All measurements were 
performed independently by two senior radiologists, and the mean values were adopted 
for subsequent analyses. A schematic illustration of the measurement protocol is pro -
vided in Fig. 2.
The calculation formulas for SNR and CNR are as follows:
 SNR = CT value(GTH)/SD(GTH)
 
 CNR = [CT value(GTH) − CT value(erector spinae muscle)] /SD(erector spinae muscle)

Subjective evaluation of VR images were independently performed by two senior radiol -
ogists, with the scoring criteria defined as follows: 5 points: excellent vessel signal inten -
sity with very sharp edge of vessels; 4 points: good signal intensity with sharp edge of 
vessels; 3 points: fair signal intensity with fairly sharp edge of vessels; 2 points: bad signal 
intensity with unclear vessel edge; 1 points: worse signal intensity with blurred edge of 
vessels [10].
2.4 Statistical analysis
The SPSS 22.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. 
All parameter values were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance. Data 
conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. 
Inter-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), 
followed by Tukey’s test for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. For data deviating from a 
normal distribution, values were expressed as median (first quartile, third quartile) 
[M (Q1, Q3)], with inter-group comparisons conducted via the Kruskal-Wallis H test 
and Bonferroni correction applied for pairwise comparisons. The inter-rater reliability 
of subjective image quality scores between the two radiologists was assessed using the 
Kappa test. P < 0.05 was set as the statistical significance threshold.
3 Results
The CT values: No statistically significant differences were observed between the 
CI120 kVp and VMI70 keV groups (P > 0.05), whereas statistically significant differences were 
noted among all other groups ( P < 0.001). The CT value of VMI 40 keV was the highest, 
with increasing energy levels, the CT values of the vessels gradually decreased.
Image noise: No statistically significant differences were observed between the 
CI120 kVp and VMI70 keV groups (P > 0.05), whereas statistically significant differences were 
noted among all other groups (P < 0.001). The VMI40 keV group demonstrated the highest 
image noise, with increasing energy levels, the image noises gradually decreased across 
all groups.
SNR: No statistically significant differences were observed among the VMI 60 keV , 
VMI70 keV, and CI 120 kVp groups ( P > 0.05), whereas statistically significant differences 
were noted among all other groups ( P < 0.001), with increasing energy levels, the SNR 
of each group gradually decreased, and the VMI40 keV group exhibited the highest SNR.
CNR: No statistically significant differences were observed between the VMI 70 keV and 
CI120 kVp groups (P > 0.05), whereas statistically significant differences were noted among 
all other groups (P < 0.001), with increasing energy levels, the CNR of each group gradu -
ally decreased, and the VMI40 keV group exhibited the highest CNR.

landmark for delineating the surgical plane, guiding vascular manipulation, and assess -
ing the radicality of resection. Owing to its deep anatomical location, variable tribu -
tary patterns, and short, robust main trunk, iatrogenic injury during surgery frequently 
results in refractory venous hemorrhage. In recent years, advances in three-dimensional 
(3D) imaging have facilitated the maturation of anatomical insights, preoperative evalua-
tion protocols, and surgical management strategies for the GTH [12].
Conventional CT exhibits limitations in visualizing small venous vessels, primarily 
attributed to the reduced contrast agent concentration within the portal vein. DLCT 
employs a stacked dual-detector configuration: the upper detector layer absorbs low-
energy X-ray photons, while the lower layer captures high-energy photons. This design 
enables simultaneous dual-energy data acquisition and decoupling in a single scan, elim-
inating the need for repeated scanning or voltage switching. Retrospective generation of 
multimodal parameters, including VMI 40–200 keV, iodine density map, and Zeff map—is 
feasible without additional radiation exposure [13, 14].
In this study, the CT values of GTH were compared across VMI 40−70 keV and CI 120 kVp 
group. The results demonstrated that, with the exception of VMI 70 keV, the CT values

of GTH in all other VMI groups were significantly higher than those in the CI 120 kVp 
group—indicating that spectral CT can enhance vascular enhancement. Among these 
groups, the VMI 40 keV yielded the most pronounced elevation in CT value of GTH, 
which is consistent with related vascular studies [ 15, 16]. Low-energy VMI (40–60 keV) 
approximates the K-edge of iodine (33.2  keV), markedly amplifying the photoelectric 
effect, this leads to a significant increase in the CT values of iodine-containing vessels 
and facilitates the visualization of distal small vessels [17].
Image noise exhibited a downward trend with increasing energy levels. Among the 
groups, VMI40 keV showed the highest noise, while CI 120 kVp had the lowest noise—con -
sistent with prior reports [ 18]. This phenomenon can be attributed to two key factors: 
first, low-energy X-rays have limited penetration, leading to insufficient photon flux 
detected by the detector and thus marked amplification of quantum noise; second, 
VMI40 keV yield the highest venous CT values, which increases the degree of inhomoge -
neity and further contributes to elevated noise levels [ 19, 20]. Studies focusing on lower 
extremity veins have reported that the image noise of VMI 40 keV is lower than that of 
CI120kVp [21], which is inconsistent with this study. This discrepancy can be attributed to 
the relatively small caliber of GTH, which renders it more susceptible to partial volume 
effects and uneven contrast agent filling, consequently, the image noise of GTH is higher 
than that of large-caliber vessels such as the portal vein, aorta, and lower extremity ves -
sels [ 22–24]. Nonetheless, the noise level of VMI 40 keV remains less than twice that of 
CI120 kVp, this outcome is facilitated by the anti-correlation noise suppression technology 
integrated into SLCT, which not only mitigates noise but also fully preserves the high-
contrast advantage of VMI40 keV. In contrast, no significant difference in image noise was 
observed between VMI 70 keV and CI 120 kVp. This indicates that VMI 70 keV benefits from a 
higher photon flux and more uniform enhancement, leading to inherently reduced noise 
and greater similarity to CI 120 kVp. Despite exhibiting the highest noise level, VMI 40 keV 
achieved the optimal SNR and CNR, with statistically significant differences compared 
to VMI 50–70 keV and CI 120 kVp. Additionally, VMI 40 keV received the highest subjective 
image quality scores, confirming it as the optimal imaging approach for visualizing the 
GTH. Prior studies have established that low-energy VMI enhances the imaging quality 
of the common hepatic artery, portal vein, and hepatic vein [ 25], further, VMI40 keV has 
been shown to deliver the highest diagnostic performance for characterizing portal vein 
thrombosis and to provide the most favorable visualization of the adrenal vein [ 26, 27], 
which is consistent with this study. Regarding clinical workflow, Yoshida et al. compared 
VMI40eV of the portal vein with CI in the late venous phase [ 27], which is more refined. 
However, it is impractical to intentionally scan the late venous phase in enhanced 
abdominal CT. Furthermore, adrenal veins are tributaries of the inferior vena cava, while 
GTH is a tributary of the portal vein, so they are not comparable. Some studies have 
placed the ROI on the psoas major muscle as background noise [ 28, 29], yet using the 
erector spinae muscle as background noise is more common in academic research [ 15, 
22, 30]. Notably, neither approach has been shown to exert a significant impact on the 
study outcomes.
In iodine density map, iodine-free voxels are calibrated to 0 mg/ml (displayed as black), 
while iodine-containing tissues are visualized in grayscale corresponding to their iodine 
concentrations [31]. Iodine density map [mg/ml] hold the potential to quantify iodine 
enhancement and improve the visualization of iodine within contrast-enhanced tissues,

thereby enhancing the detectability of microvessels. Zeff map facilitate more promi -
nent vessel visualization through pseudocolor staining of distinct substances. However, 
iodine density map and Zeff map cannot be subjected to 3D reconstruction, precluding 
stereoscopic assessment of vessels. As such, they can serve as a robust complement to 
VMI40 keV.
This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective single-center study, it is sus -
ceptible to selection bias, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, 
the anatomical classification of GTH based on VMI40 keV requires validation with a larger 
sample size to enhance the robustness of the results. Third, further investigations are 
warranted to explore whether optimizing contrast agent parameters (e.g., concentration, 
dose, and injection rate) can further improve the imaging quality of GTH, as well as to 
determine whether low-voltage, low-radiation-dose scanning protocols can serve as a 
viable alternative to CI120 kVp.
In conclusion, VMI40 keV with DLCT exhibits the optimal vascular optimization effect 
for the GTH, significantly enhancing vascular imaging quality. When combined with 
iodine density map and Zeff map, the branching patterns of the GTH can be visualized 
more comprehensively. With the continuous advancement of CT and scanning technol -
ogies, as well as the deepening of research on the GTH, it is believed that DLCT venous 
imaging technology will be increasingly widely applied in clinical practice in the future.
