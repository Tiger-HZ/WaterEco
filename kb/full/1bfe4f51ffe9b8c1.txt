Abstract
Riverine ecosystems face interacting environmental, climatic and human pressures that threaten
freshwater sustainability. This study assessed the socio-ecological drivers, causal interactions
and governance dynamics shaping freshwater vulnerability in Malawi ’s Bua River Basin using
integrated Drivers –Pressures–State–Impact–Response (DPSIR) and Socio-Ecological Systems
(SES) frameworks. Physicochemical water-quality measurements across spatial and seasonal
gradients were combined with qualitative field observations. Factorial ANOVA revealed sig-
nificant seasonal and physicochemical variability (F ₂,₅₂ = 21.84, p < 0.0001), with season –
parameter interactions showing that hydrological variability affected indicators differently.
Weak spatial interactions suggested catchment-wide influences. Key changes included elevated
turbidity, riparian vegetation loss, exposed riverbanks and sediment mobilisation, driven by
agricultural expansion, domestic activities and hydrological variability. The integrated analysis
identified reinforcing feedbacks among livelihood dependence, hydropower-related problems,
weak institutional coordination and declining freshwater resilience. Trade-offs emerged where
intensified agriculture and freshwater-resource utilisation supported short-term livelihoods
while accelerating ecological degradation, whereas riparian protection and local stewardship
practices demonstrated potential resilience-supporting synergies. Overall, the findings show that
freshwater degradation within the Bua River Basin is driven by interacting hydrological,
ecological and socio-economic pressures, highlighting the value of integrated DPSIR and SES
approaches for understanding freshwater vulnerability and guiding adaptive governance in
tropical river systems.
Impact statement
Freshwater systems in the tropics are increasingly threatened by interacting environmental and
socio-economic pressures that weaken ecosystem resilience, water security and rural livelihoods.
Using insights from Malawi’s Bua River Basin, this study demonstrates how water quality degrad-
ation in the broader Zambezi Basin is shaped by b iophysical changes, and interconnected socio-
ecological drivers including agricultural expansion, riparian degradation, climate variability and
governance limitations. By employing an integ rated socio-ecological systems (SES) and DPSIR
framework, supported by statistical and field- based evidence, the study shows how short-term
livelihood benefits may generate long-term ecological trade-offs that progressively erode freshwater
resilience. The findings demonstrate that adaptivegovernance, riparian restoration and community
stewardship can create synergistic pathways that strengthen both ecosystem sustainability and local
livelihood resilience. By linking quantitative environmental variability with broader socio-ecological
dynamics, the study provides a transferable framework for understanding resilience erosion and
governance challenges in similar tropical river basins facing multiple stressors. The research
contributes to broader discussions on integrated catchment management, climate-resilient fresh-
water governance and ecosystem restoration in de veloping regions. Its findings are relevant to
policymakers, researchers and practitioners working towards sustainable and resilience-oriented
freshwater management across the Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and comparable SES globally.

Introduction
Freshwater degradation and socio-ecological vulnerability in
Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA)
Globally, freshwater ecosystems have been recognised to be among
the most vulnerable ecological systems due to interacting pressures
associated with climatic variability, land-use transformation, bio-
diversity decline and growing anthropogenic disturbance (Dudgeon
et al., 2006; Vörösmarty et al., 2010;R e i de ta l . ,2019). Tropical river
systems in developing regions are particularly susceptible because
ecological degradation frequently coincides with high livelihood
dependency, limited governance capacity and increasing socio-
economic vulnerability. These interacting pressures often gener-
ate cumulative socio-ecological feedbacks capable of progressively
weakening freshwater-system re silience and long-term ecosystem
sustainability. Across the SSA, both lotic and lentic water bodies
are undergoing rapid ecological transformation due to commonly
intensifying anthropogenic activities, climatic variability and
increasing pressure from river-dependent livelihoods such as
fishing, river-sand mining and sewing products from riparian
vegetation.
Malawi’s river systems are also not spared from experiencing
multiple interacting stressors including agricultural intensification,
deforestation, urbanisation, sedimentation, nutrient enrichment
and hydrological instability, all of which threaten ecosystem func-
tioning and water security (Vörösmarty et al., 2010). These pres-
sures are expected to be severe if not addressed, given that the
increasing population, institutional limitations, poverty and high
natural-resource dependence constrain adaptive capacity and long-
term environmental management, which are all recipes for degrad-
ation of nature ’s ecosystem services provision (Biggs et al., 2012;
2015). Freshwater degradation can be recognised as a socio-
ecological challenge rather than solely a biophysical problem
because ecological changes directly influence social wellbeing, live-
lihood sustainability and governance systems (Folke, 2006). River-
dependent communities often rely simultaneously on freshwater
systems for agriculture, fisheries, domestic consumption and local
economic activities, creating tightly coupled relationships between
human wellbeing and ecosystem stability. Consequently, ecological
degradation may trigger cascading impacts across food systems,
public health, economic security and social resilience.
Resilience thinking and the socio-ecological systems
perspective
The SES framework has become increasingly important for under-
standing interactions between ecological processes and human
systems within complex environmental contexts. The SES theory
conceptualises ecosystems and human societies as interconnected
adaptive systems characterised by feedbacks, thresholds, nonlinear-
ity and dynamic change (Ostrom, 2009). Within freshwater envir-
onments, resilience refers to the capacity of ecological and social
systems to absorb disturbances while maintaining essential struc-
ture, function and adaptive potential (Walker et al., 2004). In river
basins experiencing multiple stressors, resilience dynamics are
often shaped by trade-offs and synergies among ecological, social
and economic objectives. For example, agricultural expansion may
improve short-term household income and food production while
simultaneously increasing erosion, nutrient loading and aquatic
habitat degradation. Such causative trade-offs generate reinforcing
pressures that gradually reduce ecological resilience and adaptive
capacity.
Conversely, synergistic interactions may emerge where ecosystem
conservation practices support both livelihood sustainability and
ecological recovery through improved riparian stability, enhanced
water quality and reduced sediment transport. Understanding these
interactions is essential because river degradation frequently results
from cumulative and interconnected pressures rather than isolated
environmental disturbances. Systems-oriented approaches therefore
provide greater explanatory power for diagnosing resilience decline
and identifying adaptive governance opportunities within vulnerable
freshwater landscapes. Resilience thinking for freshwater SES
extends beyond ecological resistance alone and includes the capacity
of coupled human –environment systems to absorb disturbance,
reorganise and maintain essential ecosystem functions under chan-
ging environmental conditions (Holling, 1973; Carpenter et al.,
2001). Recent resilience scholarly work further emphasises the
importance of adaptive governance, institutional flexibility and par-
ticipatory management in sustaining freshwater ecosystem services
under increasing climatic and anthropogenic stress (Folke et al.,
2005;B i g g se ta l . ,2012; Hein et al., 2021).
DPSIR framework and integrated environmental assessment
The Drivers –Pressures–State–Impact–Response (DPSIR) frame-
work provides a structured mechanism for analysing causal rela-
tionships between human activities and environmental change.
Originally developed for integrated environmental assessment,
the DPSIR has been widely applied in freshwater management to
evaluate how socio-economic drivers generate environmental pres-
sures that alter ecosystem states and produce ecological or societal
impacts requiring policy or management responses (Svarstad et al.,
2008; Elliott et al., 2017; Rouillard et al., 2018). Within river
systems, drivers such as population growth, agricultural intensifi-
cation and livelihood dependency generate pressures including
land degradation and pollution discharge. These pressures alter
the ecological state of aquatic systems through declining water
quality, habitat deterioration and reduced ecological integrity.
The resulting impacts may include biodiversity decline, reduced
fisheries productivity, increased public-health risks and weakened
livelihood resilience. Responses may then emerge through govern-
ance interventions, community adaptation strategies or conserva-
tion initiatives.
Here, we advance environmental assessment methodology in
the Zambezi ’s Bua Basin by merging the DPSIR with socio-
ecological resilience thinking. While standalone DPSIR models or
the Water-Energy-Food (WEF) nexus have been relatively utilised
in regions like the Bua Basin, their coupled application with another
framework to tropical river systems represents a novel academic
approach. Combining the DPSIR with SES perspectives provides
opportunities to better understand how ecological degradation
interacts with governance structures, community vulnerability
and adaptive responses under conditions of environmental uncer-
tainty.

domestic water use and rural livelihoods across multiple commu-
nities. However, increasing anthropogenic pressure within the

Methods
Study area
The study was conducted along selected sections of the Bua River in
central and northern Malawi, which is in the Zambezi River Basin
(see Figure 1 ). The Bua River constitutes an important freshwater
system supporting domestic water use, fisheries, small-scale agricul-
ture, livestock activities and riparian livelihoods across surrounding
rural communities. The Bua River catchment (see Figure 2)e x p e r i -
ences strong seasonal hydrological variability characterised by wet
and dry climatic periods that influence river discharge, sediment
transport and water quality dynamics.
Land-use activities within the Bua Catchment include subsist-
ence farming, riverbank cultivation, livestock grazing, vegetation
clearing and settlement expansion, all of which exert varying
degrees of ecological pressure on the river system. Increasing
dependence on riverine resources has intensified concerns regard-
ing habitat disturbance, declining water quality and reduced eco-
logical resilience within several sections of the river corridor. For
our present study, sampling stations were strategically selected to
capture upstream, midstream and downstream environmental gra-
dients as well as varying levels of anthropogenic influence. Site
selection considered accessibility, dominant surrounding land uses,
hydrological variability and observed human activity intensity. The
broader ecological and socio-economic characteristics of the catch-
ment allowed the river system to be examined as an interconnected
socio-ecological landscape rather than solely a hydrological unit.
Research design and conceptual framework
This study adopted an integrated SES and DPSIR analytical frame-
work to evaluate causal relationships between environmental deg-
radation, human activities and adaptive responses within the Bua
River system. The framework enabled the interpretation of water
quality degradation as a multidimensional process shaped by eco-
logical, institutional and livelihood interactions rather than isolated
physicochemical changes. The SES perspective was used to examine
feedback relationships between ecological conditions, resource
dependency and adaptive community responses, while the DPSIR
framework provided a structured mechanism for identifying causal
pathways linking anthropogenic drivers to environmental impacts
on freshwater and governance responses (Dudgeon et al., 2006;
Svarstad et al., 2008; Ostrom, 2009).
Emphasis was placed on identifying causative trade-offs and
synergistic interactions influencing ecosystem resilience. Trade-
offs were conceptualised as processes where short-term livelihood
or economic benefits generated negative ecological consequences,
whereas synergies referred to interactions where adaptive or
conservation-oriented practices simultaneously improved eco-
logical stability and social wellbeing. The analytical structure
therefore allowed interpretation of reinforcing and balancing
feedback mechanisms within the riv erine socio-ecological system.
The combined application of SES and DPSIR frameworks aligns
with growing interdisciplinary freshwater-governance approaches
emphasising systems-oriented u nderstanding of environmental
change and resilience dynamics (Pahl-Wostl, 2015). DPSIR was
particularly valuable for structu ring causal relationships among
environmental drivers, ecological pressures and adaptive govern-
ance responses, while SES analysis supported interpretation of
coupled human –environment feedback mechanisms shaping
freshwater vulnerability and resilience.

by APHA (2017). Measured parameters included temperature, pH,
dissolved oxygen (DO), electrical conductivity (EC), turbidity and
total dissolved solids (TDS), which are widely used indicators of

Socio-ecological observations and qualitative assessments
Qualitative field observations were conducted to complement phy-
sicochemical measurements and support interpretation of socio-
ecological processes influencing freshwater degradation within the
Bua River Basin. Observational assessments focused on riparian
vegetation condition, erosion intensity and visible anthropogenic
activities including agricultural cultivation, livestock access,
vegetation clearing, waste disposal and riverbank disturbance.
Additional contextual information was obtained through infor-
mal interactions with local residents and direct observations of
livelihood activities occurring along the river corridor. Focus
group discussions also formed a b asis for information synthesis.
These observations were used as supporting evidence for identi-
fying ecological pressures, resou rce-use patterns, adaptive prac-

Observed environmental conditions and human activities
were subsequently interpreted within the DPSIR framework to
support systems-oriented analysi s of freshwater vulnerability.
For example, agricultural expansion and increasing resource
dependence were interpreted as socio-economic drivers, while
riparian degradation, sediment mobilisation and hydrological
disturbance represented environmental pressures contributing
to ecological-state changes within the river system.
DPSIR and resilience analysis in the Bua River Basin
The DPSIR framework was applied to organise and interpret inter-
actions among socio-economic drivers, environmental pressures,
ecological state changes, resulting impacts and adaptive responses
observed within the Bua River system. Drivers included livelihood
dependence, population-related pressure and agricultural intensi-
fication, while identified pressures included riverbank cultivation,
erosion, sedimentation and riparian vegetation loss. Ecological
state changes were interpreted through observed variability in water
quality parameters and physical habitat conditions. Impacts were
evaluated in relation to ecosystem degradation, declining water
quality, vulnerability of river-dependent livelihoods and potential
long-term reductions in ecological resilience. Community adapta-
tion measures, conservation awareness and local environmental
management efforts were interpreted as response mechanisms
within the socio-ecological system.

sures and environmental conditions within the catchment. Quali-

Statistical analysis
Quantitative water-quality data were analysed using a factorial three-
way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate the effects of sampling
site, season and measured physicochemical water-quality parameters,
together with their interactions, on observed water-quality conditions
within the Bua River system. The analysis examined differences
among upstream, midstream and downstream river sections while
simultaneously assessing temporal variability associated with seasonal
hydrological conditions. The statistical approach was selected because
it allowed simultaneous evaluation of interacting environmental gra-
dients influencing freshwater conditions within the socio-ecological
system.
Specifically, the factorial ANOVA simultaneously evaluated
the main effects of sampling site (location) , season and water-
quality parameter , together with the two-way interaction effects
(site × season, site × parameter and season × parameter). This
analytical framework enabled as sessment of how environmental
conditions varied across spatial and seasonal gradients while
accounting for differences among the measured physicochemical
indicators.

physicochemical indicators while simultaneously evaluating spatial
and seasonal influences, each water-quality parameter was analysed
using its original measured values. No standardisation or normal-

confirmed through assessment of normality and homogeneity of
variance before fitting the factorial ANOVA model.
The null hypothesis (H 0) assumed no significant differences in
mean water-quality conditions among sampling locations and
seasons, whereas the alternative hypothesis (H 1) proposed that at
least one environmental condition differed significantly across the
river system. Where significant differences were detected, Tukey –

differences among spatial and seasonal group means (Tukey,1949).
The post hoc procedure enabled more detailed interpretation of
environmental variability and hydrological disturbance patterns
across the catchment. Importantly, the statistical analysis was not
used solely to identify physicochemical variability, but also to
support interpretation of socio-ecological instability and resilience
dynamics within the Bua River system.
Significant spatial and seasonal interactions were interpreted as
quantitative indicators of ecological sensitivity to interacting
anthropogenic and hydrological pressures operating across the
catchment. To complement quantitative analyses, qualitative field
observations and stakeholder-derived environmental narratives
were analysed thematically using content-analysis approaches.
Thematic categories focused on agricultural expansion, riparian
degradation, sediment mobilisation, livelihood dependency, gov-
ernance limitations and adaptive environmental responses.
The factorial analytical approach additionally enabled interpret-
ation of interacting environmental gradients and resilience-related
ecological variability within the river system. Such approaches are
increasingly recognised as important for understanding complex
freshwater-system responses to several interacting stressors oper-
ating across spatial and temporal scales (Beevers et al., 2021). The
integration of statistical and qualitative evidence enabled oper-
ational application of the SES and DPSIR frameworks by linking
measurable ecological variability with observed socio-environmental
pressures and resilience-related system responses.
Results and discussion
Spatial and seasonal variability in physicochemical water quality
Physicochemical parameters measured across the Bua River
revealed substantial spatial and seasonal variability associated with
differences in hydrological conditions, surrounding land use and
intensity of anthropogenic activities. Turbidity levels were generally
elevated during the wet season, particularly at sampling locations
characterised by active riverbank cultivation, exposed soils and
reduced riparian vegetation cover. Increased surface runoff during
rainfall events likely intensified sediment mobilisation into the river
system, contributing to declining water clarity and elevated sus-
pended particulate loads. Similar relationships between catchment
disturbance and turbidity dynamics have been reported in tropical
freshwater systems experiencing agricultural pressure (Allan,2004).
Electrical conductivity (EC) and total dissolved solids (TDS)
also exhibited spatial variability across the study sites, suggesting
localised influences of human activities and catchment-level dis-
turbances. Downstream sections exposed to intensified human
utilisation and settlement activities generally showed relatively
higher conductivity values compared to upstream locations with
comparatively lower visible disturbance. Such patterns may reflect
cumulative nutrient inputs, dissolved materials and altered runoff
pathways associated with expanding land-use activities within the
catchment.
Dissolved oxygen concentrations varied seasonally and spa-
tially, with lower values frequently observed in slower-flowing
sections and areas characterised by increased organic disturbance
or elevated turbidity. Reduced dissolved oxygen conditions may
indicate declining habitat quality for aquatic organisms and
reduced ecological resilience under intensified environmental
stress. Variability in pH and temperature remained within ranges
generally typical for tropical freshwater systems but nevertheless
reflected changing hydrological and environmental conditions
across sampling periods. The observed physicochemical variability
(see Supplementary Figure S1 ) shows that water quality degrad-
ation within the Bua River is spatially heterogeneous and strongly
influenced by interacting environmental and anthropogenic pro-
cesses rather than a single isolated pressure source.
Statistical evidence of spatial and seasonal socio-ecological
variability
Factorial ANOVA revealed statistically significant spatial and sea-
sonal variability in physicochemical water-quality conditions
across the Bua River system, indicating that ecological conditions
within the catchment are strongly influenced by interacting hydro-
logical and anthropogenic pressures ( Table 1).
Factorial ANOVA demonstrated that seasonal hydrological
variability and interacting environmental pressures signifi-
cantly influenced ecological-state conditions within the Bua
River socio-ecological system. Highly significant seasonal
effects ( F2,52 = 21.84, p < 0.0001) provided quantitative evidence
that climatic and hydrologica l fluctuations strongly shape
freshwater-system dynamics across the catchment.

gen variability observed during wetter periods suggest that seasonal
runoff processes intensify the transport of sediments and environ-
mentally disruptive materials into the river network. Within the

Socio-ecological drivers and environmental pressures
Agricultural expansion and riverbank disturbance
Field observations identified agricultural expansion along riparian
zones as a major socio-ecological pressure contributing to envir-
onmental degradation within the Bua River system. Cultivation
activities extending close to riverbanks reduced vegetation stability
and increased soil exposure to erosive runoff processes during
seasonal rainfall events, contributing to elevated sediment trans-
port, turbidity and ecological disturbance in affected sections of
the river. At the same time, rain-fed agriculture remains an
important livelihood and food-security strategy for surrounding
communities, illustrating a soc io-ecological trade-off between
short-term subsistence benefits and long-term freshwater ecosys-
tem sustainability.
Within the SES–DPSIR framework, agricultural expansion there-
fore functions simultaneously as a livelihood-supporting socio-
economic driver and an environmental pressure contributing to
riparian degradation and declining freshwater resilience within the
catchment. Similar interactions between rain-fed agricultural intensi-
fication and freshwater ecosystem decline have been documented
across multiple developing tropical catchments (Foley et al.,2005).
Livelihood dependency and resource-use pressure
The Bua River supports multiple livelihood functions including
domestic water access, fishing, irrigation-related activities and live-
stock utilisation. Observed dependence on riverine resources
reflects the central role of freshwater systems within rural socio-
economic structures in Malawi. However, increasing resource
dependency may simultaneously intensify ecological pressure
where governance capacity and alternative livelihood opportunities
remain limited. In several locations, direct livestock access to
riverbanks contributed to trampling, bank destabilisation and loca-
lised sediment disturbance.
Informal domestic utilisation and waste-related activities fur-
ther illustrated how cumulative small-scale pressures may collect-
ively influence ecological conditions within freshwater systems.
These findings support broader socio-ecological theory suggesting
that environmental degradation often emerges from cumulative

Riparian vegetation loss and habitat destabilisation
Loss of riparian vegetation was widely observed across several
sampling locations and appeared closely associated with cultivation
activities, vegetation clearing and settlement expansion. Riparian
zones play essential ecological roles in stabilising riverbanks, regu-
lating runoff, filtering sediments and maintaining habitat integrity
within freshwater systems (Naiman and Décamps, 1997; Trimmel
et al., 2018; Timm et al., 2021; Miralha et al., 2024; Stackhouse et al.,
2024). Areas characterised by reduced vegetation cover frequently
exhibited visible erosion features, unstable banks and increased
sediment deposition within adjacent river sections. The degraded
Bua River’s riparian buffers (see Figure 4) thus function both as a
direct ecological pressure and as a mechanism accelerating add-
itional environmental deterioration. The weakening of riparian
ecosystem functions also reduced the natural buffering capacity
of the river system against climatic and hydrological variability,
thereby increasing vulnerability of both ecological processes and
dependent human communities.

Hydrological variability, ecological instability and resilience
thresholds
The strong seasonal effects identified through factorial ANOVA
suggest that periods of intensified rainfall and runoff substantially
increase ecological disturbance within already pressured sections of
the river system. Elevated sediment mobilisation, turbidity vari-
ability and riverbank destabilisation observed during wetter periods
indicate that hydrological processes amplify existing environmental
pressures associated with land-use disturbance and riparian deg-
radation. Such interactions are consistent with broader freshwater-
system studies demonstrating that climatic variability frequently
intensifies the ecological consequences of anthropogenic landscape
modification (McCartney et al., 2012).
Importantly, the observed ecological variability may also indi-
cate gradual movement towards reduced resilience conditions
within portions of the catchment. Resilience theory suggests that
ecosystems exposed to repeated disturbance may progressively lose
buffering capacity, eventually becoming more vulnerable to abrupt
ecological transitions or functional degradation (Carpenter et al.,
2001; Folke, 2006). Although the present study did not directly
quantify ecological tipping points, the convergence of hydrological
instability, riparian degradation and statistically significant envir-
onmental variability suggests increasing stress on ecological regu-
latory mechanisms within the freshwater system.
The findings additionally suggest that ecological instability
within the river basin may intensify under future climatic uncer-
tainty. Projected increases in rainfall variability, extreme hydro-
logical events and land-use pressure across many tropical African
regions may further weaken freshwater resilience where catchment
degradation and socio-economic vulnerability are already present
(Steffen et al., 2015). Under such conditions, river systems char-
acterised by reduced riparian integrity and declining ecological
buffering capacity may become increasingly sensitive to both cli-
matic and anthropogenic disturbances.
However, resilience erosion should not necessarily be interpreted
as irreversible ecological collapse. Resilience-oriented freshwater
management approaches emphasise that adaptive interventions
implemented before critical ecological thresholds are exceeded may
still support recovery, reorganisation and long-term system stability
(Walker et al., 2004). The present findings therefore reinforce the
importance of early integrated management interventions capable of
reducing cumulative ecological stress while strengthening adaptive
socio-ecological capacity across the catchment.

Strengths, limitations and future research directions
A major strength of this study lies in its integrated analytical
approach combining quantitative water-quality assessment, factor-
ial ANOVA and socio-ecological interpretation within the SES and
DPSIR frameworks. The integration of statistical environmental
evidence with field-based qualitative observations strengthened
interpretation of interacting hydrological, ecological and livelihood-
related pressures influencing freshwater resilience within the Bua
River Basin. However, several limitations should be acknowledged.
The study focused primarily on physicochemical indicators and
visible socio-ecological pressures, while other important dimensions
such as aquatic biodiversity, habitat connectivity and long-term
ecosystem-function responses were not directly assessed.
In addition, although the study identified evidence of resilience
erosion and increasing ecological vulnerability, specific ecological
thresholds and tipping points were not explicitly quantified. Gov-
ernance dynamics and livelihood adaptation processes may also
require longer-term investigation beyond the temporal scope of the
present study. Even though the study focused specifically on the
Bua River Basin, the broader resilience dynamics identified may
hold relevance for other tropical freshwater systems experiencing
interacting pressures associated with climatic variability, land-use
change and increasing socio-economic dependence on riverine
ecosystems. However, broader transferability should be interpreted
cautiously and within comparable socio-environmental contexts.
Future research should therefore prioritise long-term socio-
ecological monitoring, integrated hydrological –ecological model-

Conclusions
This study demonstrates that freshwater degradation within the
Bua River Basin is shaped by interacting hydrological, ecological
and socio-economic pressures operating within a coupled socio-
ecological system. By integrating factorial water-quality analysis
with SES and DPSIR frameworks, the study showed that seasonal
hydrological variability, agricultural expansion, riparian degrad-
ation and livelihood dependency collectively influence freshwater
vulnerability and resilience dynamics within the Zamebzi ’s Bua
River catchment in Malawi.
The statistically significant seasonal variability identified through
factorial ANOVA confirmed that climatic and hydrological processes
strongly affect ecological-state conditions across the river system. The
convergence between statistical environmental evidence and qualita-
tive socio-ecological observations further revealed important causal
trade-offs in which livelihood-support activities simultaneously con-
tributed to ecological degradation and increasing freshwater vulner-
ability. At the same time, observations of reduced disturbance and
partial riparian protection suggested the presence of adaptive and
potentially synergistic pathways capable of supporting both ecosys-
tem stability and livelihood sustainability.
Our findings additionally emphasise that freshwater-management
approaches focusing solely on physicochemical monitoring may
inadequately address the broader socio-ecological mechanisms
driving environmental decline. Adaptive freshwater governance
within vulnerable tropical catchments will therefore require inte-
grated approaches capable of balan cing ecological sustainability,
livelihood resilience and institutional coordination. Overall, the
study highlights the value of combining statistical environmental
analysis with SES and DPSIR perspectives for improving under-
