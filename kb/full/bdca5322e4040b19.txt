Tooth wear and obesity: investigating the impact of 
BMI, waist circumference, and oral health-related 
quality of life
Fakeha Azhar Syed · Syed Murtaza Raza Kazmi · Rubbeya Shahid · Taimur Khalid
Received: 7 December 2025 / Accepted: 29 July 2026

Abstract
This study evaluated the association between tooth-wear risk and obesity-related measures, and explored its 
impact on the Oral-Health-Related Quality of Life (OHRQoL) among Pakistani adults. This single-centred 
cross-sectional study examined tooth wear using the Basic Erosive Wear Examination (BEWE) in 406 partici -
pants visiting the dental clinics of a tertiary care hospital, aged 18–65 years. The Oral Health Impact Profile-14 
(OHIP-14) questionnaire, available in English and a validated Urdu translation, was completed. Age, sex, 
weight, height, Body-Mass Index (BMI), and waist circumference (WC) were recorded. Data were analysed 
using. SPSS version 23.0. Higher BMI and WC were significantly associated with increased tooth wear severity 
(p < 0.001). Thirty-five (22.3%) and 44 (28.8%) participants in the increased and higher-high risk BMI catego -
ries, respectively, had BEWE scores ≥ 14. Fifty-seven (32.8%) males and 19 (17.3%) females in the excessive 
risk group for WC had BEWE scores ≥ 14. Multinomial logistic regression showed that increasing BMI and 
WC were significantly associated with higher odds of being in the medium- and high-risk tooth wear groups. 
A slight increasing trend in OHIP-14 scores was observed with increasing wear severity, differences across risk 
groups were not statistically significant ( p = 0.956). However, specific functional impacts were observed; par -
ticipants in higher tooth wear risk groups and those reporting frequent GERD symptoms showed significantly 
greater impairment in taste perception ( p < 0.05). Higher BMI and waist circumference were associated with 
higher BEWE tooth-wear categories among Pakistani adults. BMI and waist circumference represent general 
and central adiposity, respectively. However, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding cau -
sality or tooth-wear progression. Overall OHRQoL did not differ significantly across tooth-wear categories, 
although an exploratory association with worsening taste perception was observed.

Introduction
Tooth wear is a multifactorial and progressive loss of dental hard tissues, presenting as erosion, attrition, or abra-
sion, either independently or in combination 1,2. Its prevalence is rising globally 3, particularly among the adult 
population, where its severity poses challenges to restorative dental care and compromises both aesthetics and 
function4. Clinically, severity is often assessed using the Basic Erosive Wear Examination (BEWE), a validated 
four-point index that records the most affected surface in each sextant 5. The cumulative score stratifies patients 
by risk level and guides preventive and recall strategies6. While the BEWE was initially conceptualized with an 
emphasis on erosive wear, the index records tooth wear from all aetiologies, including attrition, abrasion, and ero-
sion. It assesses the degree of surface loss regardless of the cause, providing a comprehensive tool for evaluating 
the severity of tooth wear in clinical practice. Traditionally attributed to mechanical and chemical insults7, tooth 
wear is now increasingly linked to systemic conditions such as obesity, diabetes mellitus, and xerostomia, which 
may contribute to its pathogenesis through behavioural, dietary, and physiological risk factors8–10.
Obesity is a chronic metabolic disorder affecting over 650 million adults worldwide and is linked to numer -
ous systemic conditions, including cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and gastroesophageal reflux disease 
(GERD)11,12. Repeated acid exposure to the teeth, characteristic of GERD, makes it a major intrinsic cause of 
dental erosion13. Hampel et al. have shown a strong association between the prevalence of GERD and elevated 
body mass index (BMI)14. While BMI has commonly been used to assess general obesity, there is now an increas-
ing focus on central obesity, defined by excess abdominal fat and measured by waist circumference or waist-hip 
ratio15. Central obesity, being more metabolically active16, is more strongly associated with reflux and oesopha-
geal dysfunction17. This association is likely due to increased abdominal pressure leading to transient relaxation 
of the lower oesophageal sphincter, thereby exposing the oral cavity to gastric content18.
Several studies support the connection between obesity and tooth wear. Kamal et al. in their research on Ameri-
can adults found that those in the overweight and obese groups had 1.72 and 1.61 times, respectively, more worn 
surfaces than those with normal weight 9. Similarly, Jastaniyah et al. revealed a significantly higher prevalence 
of dental erosion among Saudi children and adolescents in the overweight/obese group (8.42%) compared to the 
control group (2.78%)19. These findings suggest that obesity may be a potential risk indicator for dental erosion 
in children and adults. However, obesity-related patterns and their systemic impacts are influenced by regional 
and ethnic differences20. Specifically, the use of BMI and waist circumference cut-offs specific to Asian popula-
tions21 underscores the importance of accounting for regional body composition profiles when examining the 
links between obesity, systemic health, and oral health outcomes.
Beyond physical effects, both obesity and tooth wear are now increasingly recognised as factors contributing 
to reduced Oral Health-Related Quality of Life (OHRQoL) 22,23. Tooth wear may compromise chewing, speech, 
and appearance, causing emotional distress and social discomfort 24,25. This combination of functional and psy -
chological challenges can lead to lower self-esteem and overall dissatisfaction with oral health 25. Mehta et al. 
found that individuals having a BEWE score of ≥ 14 reported substantially higher impacts on daily life26. Given 
the bidirectional relationship between obesity and tooth wear, and their combined effect on OHRQoL, compre -
hensive clinical attention is needed to address these interconnected issues and improve overall well-being of the 
population.
Previous studies have linked obesity with tooth wear, but most have relied mainly on BMI, examined non-
South-Asian populations, or focused on younger age groups.27 Evidence therefore remains limited on the concur-
rent assessment of general and central adiposity in relation to tooth-wear severity among South Asian adults. This 
distinction is important because BMI reflects overall adiposity, whereas waist circumference captures abdominal 
fat distribution. Accordingly, the present study used Asian-specific BMI action points and sex-specific waist-
circumference cut-offs to provide a population-relevant classification of general and central adiposity, rather than 
tooth-wear-specific diagnostic thresholds27,28.

This report is part of a broader research study evaluating the frequency and severity of tooth wear, as well as its 
association with various risk factors. The present analysis explicitly examines the association between anthropo-
metric measures of general and central obesity, namely, BMI and waist circumference (WC) with BEWE-based 
tooth-wear categories among Pakistani adults. Additionally, this study examines the relationship between tooth 
wear severity and participants’ OHRQoL, as measured by OHIP-14 scores.
Methods
This cross-sectional study received ethical approval by the institutional Ethical Review Committee (AKU-ERC) 
under reference number 2023-6721-26979 and was performed in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and 
regulations, including adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human participants.
An anticipated population proportion of 0.40 affected individuals was used, as reported in the reference study 
by Awad et al. 29 to estimate a sample size of 396 participants, as calculated by the World Health Organisation 
(WHO) sample size calculator, with a 95% confidence interval and an absolute precision requirement of ≤ 0.05. 
After inflating the sample size by 10% to compensate for errors, the sample size increased to 406 participants.
Screening and eligibility
The investigators employed a non-probability consecutive sampling technique for this single-centre study. Adults 
aged 18 to 65 years, visiting the dental clinics of a tertiary care hospital for a dental consultation, were screened. 
Eligibility required all six sextants to be recordable for clinical examination, where each sextant contained two 
or more teeth with less than 50% of their surface area covered by restorations. Patients exhibiting any dental 
anomalies, or suffering from dental or maxillofacial trauma, having hearing or speech disorders, suffering from 
neurological conditions, depression, or undergoing orthodontic treatment, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy were 
excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.
Study assessment
A clinical examination was conducted on each participant under the dental unit light using a mouth mirror and 
WHO probe, with gauze used to dry the tooth surfaces to facilitate accurate assessment of tooth wear. Tooth 
wear was assessed according to the guidelines of the Basic Erosive Wear Examination (BEWE) index 5 by a 
single trained and calibrated examiner. BEWE score of ‘0’ indicates no signs of tooth wear; ‘1’ depicts initial 
loss of surface texture, ‘2’ denotes distinct surface defect with < 50% of the surface area involved in wear; and 
‘3’ indicates ≥ 50% of the hard tissue loss of surface area. A cumulative BEWE score (sum of scores from all six 
sextants) ranging from 0 to 18 was calculated and used to categorise participants into low (≤ 6), medium (7–13), 
and high-risk (≥ 14) groups, consistent with thresholds derived from the original BEWE framework proposed by 
Bartlett et al., and commonly applied in epidemiological studies for simplified risk stratification and analysis 30. 
To assess intra-examiner reliability, 40 participants (10% of the sample) were reassessed at recall appointments 
scheduled within three weeks of initial examination, during their ongoing dental treatment. Intra-rater agreement 
was calculated using Cohen’s Kappa.
Data collection
Data were collected over a period of one year, from January 4, 2023, to January 16, 2024, using REDCap Soft -
ware (Research Electronic Data Capture; version 13.7.1, developed by Vanderbilt University). Demographic 
data, including age and sex, weight (in kilograms), and height (in meters), were recorded. Readings of weight 
and height were used to calculate BMI using the formula: weight (in kilograms) / height² (in meters) 31. Waist

circumference (WC) was measured at the level of the iliac crest using a non-stretchable measuring tape, with 
the participant standing upright, the tape held parallel to the floor, and measurements recorded at the end of 
normal expiration. An average of two readings was taken for each participant. This method was selected due to 
its standardization and widespread use in clinical and epidemiological studies, although alternative anatomical 
landmarks have been described in the literature. While waist-to-hip ratio is also considered a complementary 
measure of central obesity, WC was preferred in this study due to its simplicity, clinical applicability, and strong 
association with metabolic risk15. Excessive risk was defined using WC thresholds of 35.4 inches (≥ 90 cm) for 
males and 31.5 inches (≥ 80 cm) for females32,33. All participants completed the OHIP-14 questionnaire, available 
in both the original English version34 and a previously validated Urdu translation35. The questionnaire consists of 
14 items assessing oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL), with responses recorded on a five-point Likert 
scale (0 = Never, 1 = Hardly ever, 2 = Occasionally, 3 = Fairly often, 4 = Very often), resulting in a total possible 
score ranging from 0 to 56. Data on participants’ self-reported frequency of exposure to dietary acids, episodes 
of GERD and related symptoms, and parafunctional habits were collected using a structured interview-based 
questionnaire designed to assess the frequency of exposure to various tooth wear risk factors.29,36.
Statistical analysis
Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY , USA. 
Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of data for cumulative BEWE scores, WC, weight, 
BMI, and OHIP-14 scores. Mann-Whitney U test was used to detect statistical significance in differences of these 
variables among males and females, while Kruskal-Wallis Test was used to detect differences among patients in 
the tooth wear risk groups. Statistical significance in the differences of frequencies (number and percentages) 
was analysed for varying categories of BMI and WC among population groups using Chi-square or Fisher’s 
exact test. Multinominal logistic regression analysis was used to derive relative risk ratio using (Stata Software 
version 18.0) for each unit increase in WC, weight, BMI, and OHIP-14 sum scores, comparing participants in 
the medium- and high-risk tooth wear groups to those in the low-risk group, as classified by cumulative BEWE 
scores keeping the confidence interval at 95% and a statistical significance level of ≤ 0.05.
Results
Of the 406 participants, 263 (64.8%) were males and 143 (35.2%) were females. Signs of tooth surface loss were 
observed in 99% of the population and were categorised into low, medium, and high-risk levels for tooth wear 
progression based on cumulative BEWE scores. Eighty participants (19.7%) were identified as being at low risk 
for tooth wear progression, 233 (57.4%) were at medium risk, and 93 (22.9%) were at high risk. Table 1 presents 
summary statistics for cumulative BEWE scores, waist circumference, BMI, and OHIP-14 scores of the study 
population. Intra-rater reliability for BEWE scoring yielded a Cohen’s Kappa value of 0.963 ( p < 0.001), with a 
percentage agreement of 97.5%.

Age distribution of the study population was categorised into four groups: 18–30 years, 31–40 years, 41–50 
years, and 51–65 years. Two hundred twenty-nine participants (56.4%) fell within the ≤ 40 years category, with 
143 (62.4%) classified as having a medium risk for tooth wear progression (Fig. 1). Table 2 further illustrates 
the distribution of participants across tooth wear risk categories by age group, showing a statistically significant 
association (p < 0.001).
Figure 2 illustrates the distribution trend of participants at risk for tooth wear progression across different BMI 
categories. A statistically significant difference was observed in cumulative BEWE scores across the BMI catego-
ries (p < 0.001, Kruskal–Wallis test), Table 2. Mean rank of BEWE scores increased consistently with higher BMI 
levels, from 105.95 in the underweight group, to 169.68 in the ‘increasing but acceptable risk’ group, 213.43 in 
the ‘increased risk’ group, and 223.70 in the ‘higher high-risk’ BMI category, indicating greater tooth wear sever-
ity among participants with higher BMI. Post-hoc pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction 
(adjusted α = 0.0083) confirmed significantly higher BEWE scores in the ‘increased risk’ and ‘higher high-risk’ 
BMI groups compared to the underweight group (both p < 0.001).
Excessive waist circumference was observed in 174 (66.2%) males (≥ 35.4 inches or 90 cm) and 110 (76.9%) 
females (≥ 31.5 inches or 80 cm), with marked variation across tooth wear risk groups 37. One hundred fifty-one 
(86.8%) males and 95 (86.4%) females in the high-risk category for WC also had cumulative BEWE scores ≥ 7, 
as shown in Fig. 3. Participants with medium or high risk of tooth wear progression had significantly greater 
waist circumference values compared to the low-risk group (p < 0.001). A significant difference was also observed 
between the medium- and high-risk groups (p = 0.003), Table 2.
A slight upward trend in OHIP-14 scores was observed among study participants across low to high tooth wear 
risk groups (Fig. 4), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.956). No significant differences 
in OHIP-14 domain scores were found between males and females across the tooth wear risk groups (Table 3).

significantly associated with increased relative risk ratio of being in the medium- or high-risk tooth wear groups

indicating a positive association with tooth wear risk.

Discussion
This study aimed to explore the relationship between anthropometric measures, specifically BMI and WC, and 
the risk of tooth wear progression, while also evaluating the impact of tooth wear on OHRQoL using OHIP-14 
scores. The statistically significant association between increasing age and higher tooth wear risk observed in 
this study is consistent with previous studies highlighting the cumulative effect of ageing on tooth surface loss 
over time1. However, it is noteworthy that a substantial proportion of younger individuals (n = 143, 62.4% of ≤ 40 
years old population) presented with medium risk. This underscores the importance of early detection and timely 
preventive strategies to mitigate the progression of tooth wear from an early age7,38.
BMI and WC serve as established indicators of general and central adiposity, respectively39. The combined use 
of these measures is widely recommended, particularly in Asian populations where central obesity is prevalent 
even at lower BMI thresholds, to enhance the identification of individuals at increased metabolic and cardiovas-
cular risk40–42. In this study, both parameters were used to investigate their potential role in tooth wear, an emerg-
ing but underexplored association.
An increased WC has been reported to have an adjusted relative risk ratio of 1.264 (95% CI: 1.014–1.582) for 
the severity of GERD43. Abdominal obesity is thought to increase intragastric pressure, alter the gastroesophageal 
pressure gradient, promote transient lower oesophageal sphincter relaxations (TLOSR), and enhance oesopha -
geal acid exposure—all of which can increase the likelihood of acid reflux episodes reaching the oral cavity 44. 
Given the established link between abdominal obesity and GERD44–46, and the role of GERD as a risk factor for 
erosive tooth wear7,10,13, our findings underscore the associated relationship between central adiposity and tooth 
wear progression.

To ensure population-specific relevance, BMI and WC classifications used in this study followed World Health 
Organisation recommendations tailored for Asian populations21,37. While region-specific anthropometric thresh-
olds for the Pakistani population are still lacking, these Asian-specific criteria offer a suitable interim benchmark, 
given the shared ethnic and metabolic risk profiles 21,47. Applying these thresholds enabled the identification of 
statistically meaningful relationships between anthropometric measures and tooth wear risk. However, nationally 
representative studies are warranted to refine these parameters further.
The association between obesity and tooth wear has previously been linked, in part, to dietary behaviours, 
particularly the consumption of sugar-sweetened acidic beverages9. Our results build on this evidence, showing 
that participants with higher BMI and WC values were significantly more likely to present with severe tooth wear 
(Table 4). Specifically, 22.3% of individuals in the ‘increased risk’ BMI category and 28.8% in the ‘higher high 
risk’ category exhibited a cumulative BEWE score of 14 or higher, a threshold indicating advanced tooth wear 
(Fig. 2). These findings further support the emerging understanding that both general and central obesity are 
associated with a greater risk of non-carious tooth surface loss.
Comparison with local studies reinforces the generalizability of our WC findings. The median WC in our 
cohort was 37 inches (IQR 6) (Table 1), comparable to a recent adult survey in Pakistan reporting a mean WC 
of 36.7 ± 3.5 inches 48. Another community-based study from Karachi reported elevated waist circumference 
(≥ 90 cm in men and ≥ 80 cm in women) in 73.9% (95% CI: 71.1 − 76.5) of participants with a mean age of 
42.66 ± 12.18 years49, illustrating a high burden of central adiposity. In our cohort, 32.8% of males and 17.3% of 
females in the ‘excessive risk’ category for WC exhibited BEWE scores of 14 or higher. This suggests a meaning-
ful overlap between central obesity and severe tooth wear, given the rising prevalence of central obesity in urban 
South Asian populations due to sedentary lifestyles and dietary shifts50,51. These findings emphasise the need for 
integrated oral health screening and counselling in systemic disease prevention frameworks.
An additional observation from this study was the statistically significant association between severe tooth 
wear and both self-reported taste changes and frequent GERD symptoms. This aligns with recent regional data 
indicating that GERD symptoms are highly prevalent in Pakistani adults, where up to 68% report mild reflux 
episodes52, and 60–66% report hyperacidity and bitter regurgitation 53. While no direct studies have assessed 
taste perception in patients with tooth wear, it is plausible that chronic acid exposure from GERD could alter oral 
mucosal integrity, salivary pH, and taste receptor function, thereby contributing to dysgeusia13,54,55. Our findings 
support this hypothesis and call for further exploration of sensory implications in patients with acid-induced tooth 
wear.
Although a trend of increasing OHIP-14 scores with higher BEWE risk categories was observed (Figs. 4 and 
5), the differences did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that, within this relatively healthy adult 
cohort, tooth wear had a limited perceived impact on daily functioning or psychosocial well-being. This is in line 
with previous findings indicating that mild to moderate tooth wear does not substantially affect OHRQoL unless 
it is functionally or aesthetically debilitating56.
The non-probability, single-centre sampling limits the external validity and generalizability of the results. 
Additionally, the cross-sectional design restricts our ability to establish causal relationships between obesity and 
tooth wear. The reliance on self-reported data for key exposure variables, such as GERD symptoms and dietary

expand upon these results.
Conclusion
In this single-centre cross-sectional study of Pakistani adults, higher BMI and waist circumference were associ -
ated with higher tooth-wear categories, suggesting that both general and central adiposity may be relevant to 
tooth-wear severity. However, the study design does not establish temporality, causality, or progression, and the 
study findings may be affected by self-reported GERD symptoms and dietary exposures. Overall OHRQoL did 
not differ significantly across tooth-wear categories. Multicentre longitudinal studies including objective assess-
ment of reflux and dietary exposures are required to determine whether general or central adiposity predicts the 
development or progression of tooth wear.
