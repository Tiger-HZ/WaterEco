Karst regions in China have high geological permeability, fragmented river networks, and strong
surface– groundwater interactions, making water quality management challenging. Despite their
ecological and societal importance, long-term patterns of surface water quality remain poorly
understood. Here we provide a nationwide, multi-decadal evaluation of surface water quality in karst
regions using remote sensing and machine learning. The results show that surface water quality
improved markedly, with the proportion of water bodies classi ﬁed as the highest quality increasing
from 22.7% to 35.9%. Attribution analysis indicates that pollution-related factors account for the
dominant contribution across basins (>65%), whereas climate effects are secondary and land-use
contributions are generally small (<5%). The number of people beneﬁting from high-quality water rose
to 4.86 million by 2020, although 1.36 million people remained exposed to polluted water near
expanding settlements. Future scenario projections indicate that these improvements may be
reversed without sustained pollution control. These ﬁndings highlight the importance of protecting
surface water in karst regions for ecosystem and human well-being.
Karst landscapes, characterized by soluble carbonate rock, complex
hydrology, and high ecological sensitivity, occupy approximately 15% of the
global land surface and support hundreds of millions of people1,2.C h i n ah a s
the largest absolute karst surface area,spanning diverse climatic zones with a
variety of natural geological environments3,4. Some ecologically critical and
densely populated provinces, including Yunnan, Guizhou, and Guangxi, are
largely characterized by karst landscapes.
Karst aquifers exhibit a pronounced vulnerability to contamination
due to their rapid, often non-linear recharge dynamics and intricate
hydrological connectivity between surface and subsurface systems 5,6.
Research on karst hydrology has larg ely focused on groundwater, with
extensive studies addressing contamination risks, recharge mechanisms,
and aquifer vulnerability7–9. These studies have advanced the understanding
of groundwater vulnerability, pollution pathways, and resource security. By
comparison, karst surface waters have received less attention, even though
they are central to drinking-water supply, water-quality management, and
ecological conservation.
Although water pollution has been documented in previous studies,
the majority have focused on small catchments and relied on short-term
monitoring efforts10–13. Traditional monitoring approaches rely heavily on
in-situ measurements from ground-based stations, which are often spatially
sparse and unevenly distributed, e specially across the dispersed and
mountainous karst regions. These methods face logistical, ﬁnancial, and
technical constraints, making it dif ﬁcult to achieve continuous, high-
resolution coverage over large spatial and temporal scales14,15. As a result,
critical areas with limited monitoring infrastructure are frequently excluded
from assessments. The lack of national -scale assessments has resulted in
critical knowledge gaps regarding the spatiotemporal dynamics of surface
water quality and their driving forces. The implications of surface water
quality across karst regions at national level for human populations, parti-
cularly in terms of exposure and health risks, are even less understood.
Machine learning, combined with geospatial and remote sensing data,
offers a powerful alternativeto conventional monitoring16,17. By leveraging
environmental predictors and histor ical observations, these models can
generate spatially continuous, temp orally resolved estimates of water
quality, even in unmonitored or hard-to-access regions18–20.T h i sa p p r o a c h
enables retrospective analysis and forward-looking projections, thereby
expanding the reach of water qualit y surveillance and supporting more

aim to: (1) reconstruct high-resolution surface water quality maps for
2000–2023; (2) quantify spatiotemporal trends in water-quality classes and

primarily driven by pollution-related factors. These improvements beneﬁt

Results
Reconstruction of surface water quality across karst regions
in China
In this study, we developed a data-d riven modeling framework to recon-
struct the spatiotemporal dynamics of surface water quality in karst regions
of China from 2000 to 2023. By integrating long-term water quality
observations with a comprehensive suite of environmental, geospatial, cli-
matic, and soil-related variables, we aimed to capture the spatiotemporal
dynamics of surface water quality and its driving factors in these envir-
onmentally sensitive landscapes.
Surface water quality was categorized intoﬁve standard classes (I–V)
following national classi ﬁcation guidelines23. Four supervised machine
learning algorithms, including Ra ndom Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient
Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient-Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and
Logistic Regression, were applied and compared to predict these categories
(Supplementary Fig. 1). Among these models, RF achieved the highest
overall accuracy (0.85), with class-speciﬁc accuracies exceeding 0.70 for all
categories. Ten-fold cross-validation conﬁrmed the robustness of the RF
model, with AUC (Area Under Curve) values consistently above 0.87 across
all folds (Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, XGBoost showed lower overall
performance (accuracy = 0.72) and struggled to accurately distinguish
among the more polluted classes (III –V). LightGBM exhibited mis-
classiﬁcation between classes I and II, indicating potential overlap in feature
distributions for low-pollution water bodies.
We reconstructed annual surface water quality maps for China’s karst
regions from 2000 to 2023 using the trained RF model. Surface water in the
karst regions of China is primarily concentrated in the Yangtze River Basin,
Pearl River Basin, and the Southwest Rivers. In contrast, karst surface waters
in the Inland River Basin, Haihe River Basin, and the Huai River Basin are
more sparsely and discontinuously distributed. To evaluate predictive
uncertainty, we performed Monte Car lo simulations by perturbing con-
tinuous predictors and repeating RF predictions (Supplementary Fig. 3). For
each perturbation level, uncertainty was summarized using Shannon
entropy computed from the Monte Carlo-derived class probabilities for
each grid cell (see Methods), where lower entropy indicates more stable class
assignment. Under the 1% perturbation, over 94% of samples in 2000, 2010,
and 2020 exhibited low entropy (total entropy below one); this proportion
declined to 89% under the 5% perturbation and to 71% under the 10%
perturbation, indicating increasin g ambiguity as input perturbations
intensify. These results support the model ’s robustness for long-term
assessments of surface water quality in data-scarce karst regions.
Water quality trends during 2000– 2023
Surface water area in China’s karst regions exhibited a general increasing
trend, expanding from approximately 13,914 km² in 2000 to a peak of over
25,000 km² in 2017 (Fig.1a). However, a notable decline occurred between
2008 and 2012, which coincided with a period of pronounced drought in
southwestern China24,25. Shifts in water quality composition were also
observed from 2000 to 2023. Speciﬁcally, Class I waters expanded steadily
from 3153 km² in 2000 to over 7800 km² in 2023, with their proportion
rising from 22.7% to 35.9% (Fig. 1b). This growth, especially after 2012,
potentially reﬂecting the policy interventions of water protection and pol-
lution control in the past decade may have yielded tangible environmental
beneﬁts in karst landscapes26,27. Class II waters consistently dominated in
size, reaching over 15,600 km² in 2019 and accounting for more than 50% of
the total surface water area throughoutthe study period, despite their extent
having declined slightly in recent years. Class III waters remained relatively
stable, covering a modest share (4–13%) of total area.
Classes IV and V (representing poor and very-poor water quality)
exhibited divergent trends. Class IV waters exhibited a general decline, with
areas shrinking from a peak of 1366 km² in 2003 to nearly 731 km² in 2023,
and their share of total surface water area dropping from over 9.0% to just
3.4%. Conversely, Class V waters demonstrated a long-term increasing
trend, expanding from only 107 km² (0.8%) in 2000 to a peak of 1466 km²
(5.8%) in 2017. Although this extent slightly declined in subsequent years, it
remained consistently above the levelat the beginning of the 21st century.
These dynamics suggest that while moderate pollution has been alleviated,
severe pollution remained persistent in localized areas. The inherent vul-
nerability of karst landscapes, characterized by rapid inﬁltration and limited
naturalﬁltration, likely exacerbates these conditions by facilitating the swift
spread of contaminants and hindering ecological recovery28,29.
The annual proportion of high-quality waters (Classes I and II) versus
non-high-quality waters (Classes III –V) demonstrates a consistently
improving trend of water quality (Fig.1c). High-quality waters constituted
about 84.5% of the total surface water area in 2000, peaking at over 91.2% in
2014. From 2010 onward, the share of high-quality water consistently
remained above 87%, indicating a re markable level of improvement and
stability. This trend suggests that national and regional water management
policies have had a measurable positiveeffect, particularly after the imple-
mentation of stricter environmental regulations around 2010. However, the
proportion of non-high-quality waters, though declining, still ﬂuctuates
slightly in some years (e.g., 2013 and2021). Given the complex hydrological
and geological character istics of karst regions, even slight increases in
degraded water classes merit close attention. Sustained monitoring and early
warning efforts are therefore critical, especially in areas where Class III–V
waters persist, to safeguard long-term water quality and ecosystem stability
in karst-dominated catchments.
While the overall trend indicates improvement, spatial heterogeneity
persists (Fig.2). For example, the Longdai River system in Anhui Province,
situated within the Huai River Basin and lying in a transitional zone between
mountainous and hilly terrain, has shown signi ﬁcant recovery. Likewise,
smaller water bodies, such as Hongqitang River, Ditang River, and Longxi
River in the Yangtze River Basin, have also experienced consistent
improvements, reﬂecting effective pollution control and localized con-
servation efforts in peri-urban and rural settings30. In contrast, several rivers
in the Pearl River Basin, particula rly in Yunnan Province, continue to
experience persistent water quality challenges. The Huaxi River, Lujiang,
and Dianxi River remained hotspots of degradation.
In addition, we analyzed the proportion of surface water areas across
ﬁve water quality classes in the years 2000, 2010, and 2023 for each province
(Fig. 3). Between 2000 and 2023, most provinces in the karst region
experienced substantial improvements in surface water quality, character-
ized by increasing proportions of Class I and II waters and declining shares
of Classes IV and V. Guizhou, a province dominated by karst topography,

Drivers of surface water quality

in China’s karst regions, we analyzed the relative contributions of three
major factor groups: climate, pollution, and land use (Fig.4). Analysis of the
driving factors across major river basins reveals that anthropogenic pollu-
tion is the dominant contributor to surface water quality variations, with its
inﬂuence exceeding 65% in all basins. The Yangtze River Basin shows the
highest pollution-related contribution (84.27%). This pattern is consistent

These regions are often characterized by strong hydroclimatic variability,
where precipitation and drought patterns signiﬁcantly affect hydrological
processes and pollutant transport34. Land use factors generally exhibit the
smallest relative contribution in the factorial attribution results, with pro-
portions mostly below 5% in the more developed eastern basins (e.g., 0.47%
in the Hai River and 1.73% in the Yangtze River Basin), and slightly higher
values (up to 6.05%) observed in the less urbanized Southwest Basins.
Within the present framework, this outcome reﬂects that land-use variables
change more gradually over time and that their inﬂuences on surface water
quality are often mediated through po llution-related inputs and hydro-
logical processes, rather than acting asstrongly time-varying drivers at the
basin scale.
Analysis of key time points indicates that anthropogenic pollution has
consistently been the dominant dri ver throughout the study period,
accounting for 62.3% of observed changes in 2005 and reaching a peak of
85.3% in 2010. This trend suggests that anthropogenic pollution has played
a major role in shaping observed patterns of surface water quality change. In
contrast, climate-related factors such as precipitation, temperature, and
aridity exhibited a more dynamic pattern, contributing 36.3% in 2005,
dropping sharply to 10.4% in 2010, and gradually recovering to 26.8% by
2020. The sharp decline around 2010 likely re ﬂects the intensiﬁcation of
human-induced pressures during that period, overshadowing the inﬂuence
of climatic variability. The modest but increasing in ﬂuences of climate
factors in later years may be attributed to the compounding effects of climate
change, particularly in hydrologica lly sensitive karst basins. Land use
remained the least inﬂuential driver, consistently contributing less than 5%
to overall changes.
Considerable heterogeneity was observed in the relative importance of
different drivers across water quali ty classes. Climate factors were the
dominant inﬂuence on both Class I and II waters in 2005. However, in
subsequent years, anthropogenic pollution gradually overtook climate as the
primary driver, indicating that even high-quality or well-preserved waters
are increasingly susceptible to human-induced impacts. For Class III, pol-
lution dominated throughout, accounting for over 70% in both 2010 and
2020. The trend is even more pronounced in Classes IV and V, where
pollution accounted for more than 98% of the changes in both 2010 and
2015, indicating severe anthropogenic impacts, while the inﬂuence of cli-
mate and land-based drivers becomes relatively less pronounced under
highly degraded conditions. Interestingly, in Class V waters in 2020, land use
showed a slightly elevated contribut ion (4.3%), potentially signaling
intensifying local pressures such as u rban sprawl or unsustainable land
conversion. Taken together, these ﬁndings highlight the crucial role of
pollution control in water quality in the karst regions of China. While
climate and land use exert measurable in ﬂuence, particularly in less
degraded systems or under changing climatic conditions, the overarching
determinant of water quality remains the intensity of anthropogenic
pollution.
Exposed and beneﬁting populations
High-quality surface water representspristine conditions that are suitable
for direct human consumption and essential ecological functions. We dif-
ferentiated between beneﬁted and exposed populations based on the pro-
portion of high-quality and polluted waters, respectively (Fig. 5). Despite
broad improvements in surface water quality across China’s karst regions,
the relationship between water quality and population exposure remains
complex and regionally varied. In 2000, approximately 3.99 million people
beneﬁted from high-quality water, while 937,675 people were exposed to
polluted waters (Class IV–V). These populations were distributed across
multiple river basins, reﬂecting a mixed spatial pattern of water quality and
human settlement. By 2010, the beneﬁted population declined slightly to
2.51 million, while the exposed population increased to 1.18 million. This
trend likely results from localized degradation in certain areas, such as parts
of Yunnan and Shandong, where rising proportions of Class IV and V water
were observed. The increase in exposed population, despite overall
improvement in national water quality, indicates that degradation was
occurring in areas with growing or densely settled populations, thus
amplifying its human impact.
By 2020, the bene ﬁted population had risen sharply to 4.86 million,
while the exposed population also increased to 1.36 million. The population
exposure rose despite a national-leveldecline in the proportion of polluted
waters, reﬂecting two interrelated phenomena. First, population migration
and urban expansion played a criti cal role. As economic development
accelerated, increasing numbers of people settled near surface water bodies,
particularly in ecologically sensiti ve karst regions. Although the overall
extent of polluted water declined, the concentration of population around
the remaining degraded water bodies increased. This pattern was especially
evident in Yunnan Province, where theproportion of Class II water dropped
substantially; However, Classes IV and V waters expanded, resulting in
Yunnan accounting for a substantial share of the exposed population in
2020. Second, a spatial mismatch developed between regions that achieved
water quality improvements and those that experienced the greatest
demographic pressure. Provinces such as Guizhou and Chongqing
demonstrated clear improvement in water quality, but these improvements
were often concentrated in sparsely populated areas. In contrast, south-
western karst regions where degradation persisted coincided with areas of
rapid population growth and urba nization. As a result, the bene ﬁts of
ecological restoration efforts did not fully translate into reduced exposure
risk, underscoring the importance of aligning water quality interventions
with patterns of human settlement.
Notably, improvements in water quality do not always translate into
reduced human exposure, especially when degradation persists in demo-
graphically sensitive regions. Therefore, targeted interventions are essential,
focusing not just on improving water quality but also on aligning efforts with
population risk proﬁles and urban planning. Without such alignment, even
successful water quality programs may yield limited public health gains in
the most vulnerable karst zones.
Projections of surface water quality under climate change
Building upon the previously trained RF model, we projected surface water
quality in karst regions from 2030 to 2100 under three Shared Socio-
economic Pathways (SSP119, SSP245, and SSP585). Furthermore, Monte
Carlo simulations were employed to assess model uncertainty for the years
2050 and 2100, demonstrating the robustness and reliability of the projec-
tions across all three SSPs (Supplementary Figs. S5 and S6). The simulation
under three scenarios reveals starkly divergent trajectories for surface water
quality across China, with substantial implications for ecological health and
human well-being. For scenario projections after 2020, the surface-water
extent is held constant using the 2020 baseline footprint. Accordingly,
changes in class-speciﬁc areas represent shifts in water-quality composition
within this ﬁxed surface-water mask. Because surface-water extent in karst
regions can be highly sensitive to climatic variability and human regulation,
the projected absolute class areas are interpreted conditional on the
constant-extent assumption.
Under the most optimistic scenario (SSP119), water quality improves
markedly over the century, with the proportion of high-quality water bodies
rising from 89.8% in 2023 to 95.4% by 2100 (Fig.6). This is accompanied by
a sharp decline in severely polluted waters (Grade IV–V) from 965.9 km² to
just 166.5 km² (Supplementary Fig. 7). These trends reﬂect effective pollu-
tion control measures and robust environmental governance aligned with
global sustainability targets. These improvements in water quality translate
directly into public health and environmental bene ﬁts: the population
beneﬁting from high-quality surface water increases from 4.94 million in
2050 to over 5.39 million by 2100, while the number of people exposed to
polluted waters drops sharply from 1.70 million to 579,808 (Figs. S8, S9).
The underlying drivers of this improvement are primarily pollution control

Discussion
Dominance of anthropogenic pollution in karst surface waters
This study conducted a large-scale, high-resolution assessment of surface
water quality dynamics in karst regions of China, revealing the sustained
dominance of anthropogenic polluti on. Within the factorial framework
adopted here, the relative contribution of a given driver category reﬂects the
extent to which time-varying information from that category is required to
reproduce observed and reconstructed water quality changes, rather than a
strict quantiﬁcation of physical causality. From this perspective, the high
contribution attributed to the pollutioncategory indicates that surface water
quality dynamics in karst regions are most sensitive, within the model, to
variables representing direct human pressure pathways, such as industrial
discharge, agricultural nutrient inputs, and energy-related emissions. When
these pollution-related inputs are held constant, the modeled water quality
deviates most strongly from the full-signal simulations, highlighting their
central role in explaining recent temporal variability. This dominance does
not imply that other processes are negligible, but rather that their inﬂuences
are more indirect, operate over longer timescales, or are partially expressed
through pollution-related changes.
In contrast, the contributions asso ciated with climate and land use
represent broader hydroclimatic modulation and landscape-mediated
processes that shape water quality th rough dilution, transport, and bio-
geochemical regulation. These eff ects become more evident in higher-

Existing nationwide products in China have mainly reconstructed
lake/reservoir water-quality parameters from remote sensing and/or com-
piled large spatiotemporal monitoring datasets to support broad assess-
ment, providing important baselines for large water bodies and indicator-
speciﬁc analyses20. However, these efforts have rarely targeted the hydro-
logically distinctive karst surface-water network, and karst-related research
has traditionally emphasized groundwater vulnerability and contamination
pathways rather than surface systems35,36.
Prior research often emphasized the diffuse and hidden nature of karst
hydrology, which limited large-scale surface assessments37. This research
shifts attention to surface systems and demonstrates that surface waters in
karst landscapes are equally sensitive topollution and climate, yet capable of
delivering substantial public beneﬁts. Our approach, combined with long-
term reconstruction with future scenario modeling, reveals that pollution
overwhelmingly dominates Class IV–V water degradation (up to 98%), and
that the climate inﬂuence becomes more pronounced in high-quality waters
(41.1% in Class I by 2020).
Hydroclimatic extremes and long-term climate shifts are known to
affect key water-quality constituents by altering stream ﬂow (dilution
capacity), water temperature, and in-stream transformation rates, with
particularly strong effects on di ssolved oxygen and nutrient-related
indicators38. In karst headwaters where Class I waters are more common,
conduit–ﬁssure ﬂow paths can produce rapid rainfall–runoff responses and
short lag times, enhancing the sensitivity of water chemistry to precipitation
variability39. Event-scale studies in Southwest China further show that
rainstorms can rapidly dilute karst surface water hydrochemistry and shift
concentration–discharge behavior, highlighting strong hydrologic control
under relatively low pollution pressure40. By contrast, in Class IV–V waters,
persistent and high anthropogenic inputs dominate water-quality dynamics
and can mask climate-driven variability, leading to a smaller apparent cli-
mate contribution, consistent with the stronger human imprint observed in
managed or highly disturbed watersheds41.
The contribution attributed to land-use change is comparatively small,
particularly in urbanized eastern basins. This result contrasts with previous
studies that have highlighted land-use change as a major driver of surface
water degradation42,43. The difference may be because the factorial attribu-
tion employed here, which isolates the contribution of time-varying signals
within predeﬁned driver categories, rather than the cumulative or long-term
effects of land-use conﬁguration. Consequently,within the present frame-
work, land-use effects are expressedmore gradually or indirectly, whereas
pollution-related variables capture more immediate temporal variability in
water quality. Moreover, while previous projections often treated climate
change as a growing threat, our SSP-based modeling shows that climate

Spatial heterogeneity and implications for water– population
interactions
It is worth noting that in these geologically fragile zones, human dependency
on surface water was previously underestimated. By 2020, over 4.86 million
people beneﬁted from high-quality surface water, a sharp increase from 2.51
million in 2010. Our projections furt her demonstrate that under low-
emission pathways, the bene ﬁted population cou ld exceed 5 million by
2100, reinforcing the strategic ecological and public health value of karst
surface waters.
The observed spatial patterns underscore pronounced heterogeneity in
surface water quality trajectories across karst regions in China. National
improvements demonstrate that targeted interventions can be effective at
the basin scale. However, these gains are unevenly distributed and do not
translate uniformly across regions. In particular, Yunnan exhibits a per-
sistently deteriorating trajectory that deviates from the broader national
improvement trend. In Yunnan, the convergence of lake- and basin-
dominated karst systems, constrained hydrological ﬂushing, strong
surface–groundwater coupling, and concentrated agricultural and urban
activities intensiﬁes pollutant retention and limits natural puri ﬁcation
capacity. Similar mechanisms oper ate in other karst regions, yet their
combined intensity is typically lower,allowing water quality improvements
to emerge more readily31–33.T h e s eﬁndings suggest that sustained progress
will require both basin-scale management strategies andﬁner-scale inter-
ventions tailored to the geomorphological and socio-economic character-
istics of each sub-region. The continued increase in population exposure
from 2010 to 2020 to degraded waters further emphasizes the need to align
water quality restoration with spatially differentiated development and
planning strategies.
Sources of uncertainty and methodological limitations
Several limitations should be acknowledged.
First, label and input-data uncertainty may affect model training and
spatial prediction. Water-quality grades derived from station observations
can be in ﬂuenced by sampling frequency and missing records. Another
important source of uncertainty arises from the mixed spatial resolution of
predictor datasets. Several key pollution-related variables were only avail-
able at the provincial scale and were therefore uniformly assigned to
monitoring stations within each province. This simpliﬁcation introduces
structural uncertainty by neglecting sub-provincial heterogeneity in pollu-
tion sources, management practices, and socioeconomic conditions, which
is not captured by Monte Carlo perturbations. As a result, localized pollu-
tion impacts may be attenuated, parti cularly in environmentally hetero-
geneous karst regions.
Second, the assumption that surface water extent remainsﬁxed after
2020 constitutes a strong constraint, especially in hydrologically sensitive
karst landscapes where surface water bodies can respond rapidly to climate
variability and human regulation. Potential changes in river width, seasonal
inundation, and surface –groundwater connectivity are therefore not
reﬂected in post-2020 reconstructions, which may inﬂuence both long-term
projections and estimates of population exposure.
Third, while the driving factor decomposition captures basin-level
patterns, speciﬁc pollution sources such as industrial and agricultural dis-
charge may not be fully represented. In addition, our exposure analysis uses
the population and exposed water body ratios in speciﬁc regions as alter-
native indicators of risk, without considering water usage patterns, infra-
structure access, or treatment technologies. These simpli ﬁcations may
underestimate localized exposure risks or overestimate beneﬁts in regions
with limited accessibility.
Future work could reduce these uncertainties by integrating ﬁner-
resolution pollution and socioecono mic datasets and by incorporating
additional monitoring information t o better constrain labels and sub-
provincial heterogeneity. It would also be valuable to relax the post-2020
ﬁxed-extent assumption by coupling water-quality projections with
dynamic surface-water extent and to re ﬁne attribution/exposure assess-
ments with more explicit source indicators and infrastructure- and water-
use–related information.
Conclusion
This study provides a data-driven reconstruction of surface water quality
dynamics in China’s karst regions over the past two decades. The results
show a general improvement in water quality, with increasing proportions
of high-quality water and several million people beneﬁting by 2020. How-
ever, exposure to polluted water persists in some regions. Across basins and
time, anthropogenic pollution is identiﬁed as the dominant driver of water-
quality variability, while climate andland-use effects play secondary roles.
Importantly, the observed improvements are spatially heterogeneous and
do not always align with population dist ribution, highlighting persistent
exposure risks in rapidly developing karst areas. Theseﬁndings emphasize
the need for integrated water quality management strategies that combine
pollution control with spatially targeted interventions in environmentally
and socially vulnerable regions.
Methods
Data collection and process
Surface water quality data used in this study were derived from the China
National Environmental Monitoring Centre, spanning the period from
2004 to 2023. A total of 448 monitoring stations located within China’s karst
regions were selected, covering a broad spatial range across multiple basins
(Supplementary Fig. 10). These stations concentrate on the
Yunnan–Guizhou Plateau and surrounding areas, where karst geomor-
phology is particularly prominent. Water quality at each station was clas-
siﬁed based on the national environmental standards (GB3838-2002),
which deﬁne ﬁve surface water quality grades (I–V), with Grade I repre-
senting the highest quality. To ensure data reliability and temporal repre-
sentativeness, we only included stations that had monitoring data for at least
two-thirds of the days within a given month. This ﬁltering criterion mini-
mized the inﬂuence of data gaps and seasonal anomalies, ensuring that the
observed water quality patterns were robust and comparable across time.
These observations served as the training/validation dataset for the machine
learning model described below.
To capture the dynamic changes in surface water quality across karst
landscapes, we compiled a comprehensive dataset incorporating environ-
mental, climatic, geospatial, and soilv a r i a b l e sa sp r e d i c t o r s( T a b l eS 1 ) .T h e
selection of predictive factors was grounded in the unique hydrological,
geological, and anthropogenic characteristics of karst systems, which are
highly sensitive to both surface pro cesses and subsurface connectivity.
Anthropogenic pollution-related indicators were a central component of
our model, reﬂecting the inﬂuence of anthropogenic emissions on water
degradation. Speciﬁcally, we included industrial wastewater discharge,
industrial solid waste discharge, carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions, pesticide
consumption, fertilizer consumption, plasticﬁlm consumption, wastewater
treatment plants, environmental investment, coal consumption, crude oil
consumption, and natural gas consumption. These variables capture key
dimensions of agricultural intensiﬁcation and energy consumption, both of
which have been shown to signi ﬁcantly contribute to non-point source
pollution in rural and peri-urban karst areas44–47. In addition, industrial solid
waste discharge and industrial wastewater emissions were incorporated to
account for localized industrial pollution, which poses acute risks to surface
water bodies, especially in regi ons undergoing rapid economic
development48.
Recognizing the spatial heterogeneity and ecological fragility of karst
environments, we further included geospatial variables that describe land-
scape structure and vegetative dynamics. These included elevation, slope,
land use categories, Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), and
Net Primary Productivity (NPP). Topographic and land cover features are
crucial in determining runoff generation, erosion potential, and pollutant
transport, while NDVI and NPP provide proxies for vegetation cover and

Communications Earth & Environment Article in Press
ecosystem productivity. Climate data were also integrated to account for
hydrometeorological inﬂuences on water quality. Given that karst systems
are particularly responsive to precipitation patterns, we included average
monthly rainfall, temperature, potent ial evapotranspiration, and aridity
index. These variables allowed us to capture both seasonal water availability
and longer-term climate variability, which can amplify or dampen pollutant
loading and dilution processes.
Finally, we incorporated a suite of soil-related indicators. On the one
hand, during rainfall events, soil particles and associated nutrients can be
mobilized and transported into nearby rivers and streams, directly
degrading surface water quality through sedimentation and nutrient load-
ing. On the other hand, soil propertie s also strongly affect groundwater
quality by regulating in ﬁltration, ﬁltration, and subsurface nutrient
dynamics. Given the well-documentedhydrological connectivity between
surface and subsurface waters in karstlandscapes, such as rapid conduitﬂow
and frequent exchanges between rivers and aquifers, groundwater is highly
responsive to surrounding environmental conditions49,50. As a result, the
chemical and physical characteristics ofsoils can indirectly but substantially
inﬂuence surface water quality through their impact on groundwater. To
capture these effects, we included variables such as lithology, topsoil texture,
carbonate content, pH, electrical conductivity, soil organic carbon, total
nitrogen, total phosphorus, and particle-size composition (sand, silt, and
clay). These parameters help characterize erosion potential, pollutant
mobility, and subsurface transport processes that are especially active in the
fragile and highly permeable karst environment.
Surface water quality data were compiled at a monthly resolution. For
consistency and spatial alignment, all predictor variables were resampled or
aggregated to match the spatial and temporal scales of the water quality
observations. Remotely sensed geospatial datasets, e.g., land cover, NPP,
NDVI, and topographic features, were resampled to a 100 m × 100 m spatial
resolution. This resolution strikes a balance between computational ef ﬁ-
ciency and the capacity to capture the heterogeneity of landscapes pertinent
to surface water processes within karst terrains. Several anthropogenic
variables, including pesticide and fer tilizer usage, industrial wastewater
discharge, and industrial solid waste production, were available only at the
provincial level. For these datase ts, we assigned the same value to all
monitoring stations located within agiven province during a given month.
While this approach may smooth out sub-provincial variability, it effectively
preserves the broader spatial signal of pollution intensity driven by regional
socioeconomic activities, which is often a dominant control on water quality
in China. To prepare the dataset for machine learning modeling, all pre-
dictor variables were spatially and temporally matched to the water quality
monitoring records. For each station-month observation, the correspond-
ing values of all predictor variables were extracted based on the station ’s
geographic coordinates and the month of observation. This ensured that
each data point fed into the model represented a consistent and temporally
synchronized snapshot of surface water quality and its potential environ-
mental drivers.
Machine learning model
To model and classify surface water quality levels in karst regions, we
employed four widely used machine learning algorithms: Random Forest
(RF)51, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)52, Light Gradient Boosting
Machine (LightGBM)53, and Logistic Regression 54. These models were
selected to cover a range of tree-based ensemble learners and linear classi-
ﬁers, allowing for comprehensive comparison of performance and robust-
ness under varying data distributions a nd feature interactions. An initial
predictor dataset of 34 predictors wa sa s s e m b l e d ,i n c l uding topographic,
anthropogenic, and climatic factors. Toreduce multicollinearity, six highly
correlated variables were excluded based on correlation analysis: coal con-
sumption, crude oil consumption, potential evapotranspiration, pesticide
consumption, surface soil electrical conductivity, and bulk density (Sup-
plementary Fig. 11). This reﬁnement improved model stability and inter-
pretability by eliminating redundant inputs.
For each water quality class (Classes I–V), we performed a stratiﬁed
random split in which 80% of samples were assigned to the training set and
the remaining 20% to the testing set, preserving the class proportions. This
stratiﬁed sampling strategy maintained the class-level representation and
avoided bias introduced by imbalance d class distributions during model
training and evaluation. Such an approach is particularly crucial in multi-
class classiﬁcation tasks, such as grading waterquality, where certain classes
(e.g., Class V) may occur less frequently. Without stratiﬁcation, the model
could become biased towardmajority classes, leading to poor generalization
and underestimation of rare but environmentally signiﬁcant water quality
conditions. To avoid an imbalanced partition dominated by a single year,
station, or basin, the split was imp lemented with group-coverage con-
straints, ensuring that both the training and testing sets contained samples
spanning multiple years, monitoring stations, and basins. By enforcing
broad coverage across years, stations, and basins in both subsets, the eva-
luation better re ﬂects the model ’s ability to generalize beyond narrowly
sampled spatiotemporal contexts. Model performance was evaluated using
10-fold cross-validation on the training data to minimize over ﬁtting and
assess generalizability. To optimize each model ’s hyperparameters, we
adopted a Bayesian grid search stra tegy, which iteratively updates the
parameter space based on past evaluations to effectively identify the optimal
conﬁgurations.
To systematically evaluate model performance, we adopted four widely
used classiﬁcation metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, and the confusion
matrix. These metrics were selected to assess both the overall predictive
capability and the model’s sensitivity to different water quality classes.

Surface water quality reconstruction and prediction
To reconstruct the historical distribution of surface water quality and project
its future evolution across China’s karst regions, the trained RF model was
applied in a hindcasting–projection framework by inputting the predictor
layers for target years outside the observational window, thereby generating
gridded water-quality classi ﬁcations for 2000 –2100 conditional on the
availability and consistency of predictors. For the historical reconstruction
(2000–2023), we ﬁrst obtained annual surface water distribution data from
the Global Surface Water55, which were generated based on Landsat imagery
and offer global 30-meter-resolutionmaps of surface water presence. These
data, accessed via the Google Earth Engine platform, allowed us to identify
grid cells classiﬁed as surface water within the karst region for each year from
2000 to 2020.
For each water grid cell in each year, we spatially aligned and tempo-
rally matched the full set of predictor variables, including climate, land use,
emissions, topography, vegetation, and soil attributes, as described in the
previous sections. These variables represent both direct and indirect drivers
of water quality and were preprocessed to ensure temporal consistency and
spatial coverage. The resulting dataset was input into the trained RF model,
which generated categori cal predictions (Classes I –V) representing the
expected water quality class for each grid cell and year. For the years 2021 to
2023, due to the unavailability of surface water maps, we assumed that the
surface water distribution remained constant as in 2020. While this
assumption introduces a degree of uncertainty, it allows temporal continuity
in water quality reconstruction. Theﬁnal output is a time series of surface
water quality maps for the karst region from 2000 to 2023, at a 100 m
resolution.
For future projections (2030–2100), we applied the same trained model
using scenario-based predictor inputs under the SSP pathways. To simulate
future water quality trajectories under changing environmental and socio-
economic conditions, we conducted projections under three representative
CMIP6 scenarios: SSP1-119 (low emissions), SSP245 (intermediate path-
way), and SSP585 (high emissions). These scenarios re ﬂect contrasting
f u t u r e si nt e r m so fe n e r g yu s e ,l a n d - use policies, climate mitigation, and
technological development. Meteoro logical predictors, including pre-
cipitation, temperature, and aridity index, were derived from the EC-Earth3
global climate model under each SSP s cenario, providing internally con-
sistent climate inputs. Land use variables, such as urban expansion, cropland
area, and forest cover, were sourced from the SSP-RCP dataset, which
provides spatially explicit land-use simulations consistent with CMIP6.
For pollutant-related predictors such as CO₂ emissions, chemical fer-
tilizer and plastic ﬁlm consumption, industrial wastewater, and industrial
solid waste discharge, direct high-resolution projections at the provincial or
ﬁner scale are not available. To bridge this gap, we adopted a scaling
approach to approximate future values based on the socioeconomic and
policy assumptions of each SSP. Under SSP119, which emphasizes sus-
tainability, environmental steward ship, and strong emission controls,
pollution-related variables were decreased by 3% relative to the baseline,
reﬂecting expected advancements in clean technology, stringent regulation,
and behavioral changes. In contrast, SSP245 reﬂects a“middle-of-the-road”
trajectory in which moderate progress is made in climate mitigation and
pollution control; Thus, we assumed a1% increase in pollutant emissions,

fuel-driven development with minimal environmental regulation, aggres-
sive industrial expansion, and risingconsumption levels. Accordingly, a 3%
increase in pollutant emissions was applied to represent accelerated input of
anthropogenic contaminants into the environment. These scaling ratios

and global scenario literature, and servea sp l a u s i b l ee s t i m a t e si nt h ea b s e n c e

Estimating exposed and beneﬁciary populations
In this study, Grade I and II surface waters were classiﬁed as high-quality
water (HQ), as they meet the most stringent standards for drinking water
sources and ecological integrity. Grade III waters were excluded from the
high-quality category due to their relatively lower quality and limited eco-
logical value. Grades IV and V are considered polluted and potentially
hazardous to surrounding populations and ecosystems. Grade III waters,
together with Grades IV and V, were collectively classi ﬁed as non-high-
quality water (NonHQ). On this basis, we deﬁned the beneﬁciary population
as the population potentially associated with high-quality waters, and the
exposed population as the population potentially associated with non-high-
quality waters.
In karst mountainous basins, human settlements are strongly clustered
along valleys and transportation corridors, and are therefore unevenly
distributed within administrative u nits. An area-weighted allocation of
county population to surface-water areas may over-allocate population to
sparsely inhabited high-relief zones and under-allocate population in den-
sely inhabited corridors. To better re ﬂect this spatial heterogeneity, we
reﬁned the population exposure and beneﬁt assessment using a night-time
light (NTL)59—informed dasymetric allocation approach combined with
surface-water proximity buffers. Speciﬁcally, for each county, we delineated
a surface-water proximity buffer with aﬁxed distance of 10 km. The buffer
was deﬁned as the area within 10 km of the mapped surface-water boundary
(for polygonal waterbodies such as lakes and reservoirs) or within 10 km of
the river centerline (for linear river features), and was then intersected with
county boundaries. This approach r educes bias from assuming uniform
settlement distribution within mountainous karst counties and provides a
more spatially grounded approximation of populations concentrated along
near-water corridors.

Pbenefit;c ¼ Pwater;c × PropHQ;c ð14Þ
Pexposed;c ¼ Pwater;c × PropNonHQ;c ð15Þ
Given that NTL may underrepresent low-electriﬁcation rural settle-
ments and may be biased by non-resid ential lighting, our estimates are
intended to represent proximity-based potential beneﬁciary and exposed
populations, not direct measuremen ts of actual water use or contact
behavior.
