Site-specific nutrient management based on target yield
improves productivity and economic returns of African
marigold (Tagetes erecta L.)
Chaitra Lamani1, Anand G Patil2, Spoorti Tirki3, Harshad Padasalagi4, Aishwarya S
Jeevanagi5, Rushikesh Bharsakale6
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/26174693.2026.v10.i9S.w.10091
A BSTRACT
A field experiment was conducted at the College of Horticulture, Bidar, Karnataka, during 2022 to
evaluate soil test- and target yield-based nutrient management in African marigold (Tagetes erecta L.)
cv. Tall Yellow. The experiment was laid out in a randomized complete block design with seven treat-
ments and three replications, comprising target flower yields of 15, 20, 25, 30 and 35 t ha ⁻ ¹, recom-
mended dose of fertilizers (RDF), and farmer practice. The target-yield treatments were formulated
based on soil-test values and crop nutrient requirement, enabling nutrient application according to the
desired yield level. Significant differences were observed among treatments for vegetative growth,
flowering, flower yield, quality attributes, leaf nutrient concentration and economic returns. The 35 t
ha ⁻ ¹ target treatment (T5) recorded the highest plant height (67.12 cm), stem girth (3.71 cm), plant
spread (42.98 × 40.29 cm), primary branches (21.33 plant ⁻ ¹), secondary branches (32.33 plant ⁻ ¹), leaf
area (43.53 cm²) and leaf area index (2.70). It also recorded the highest chlorophyll content at 60 days
after transplanting (3.02 mg g ⁻ ¹), dry matter production (283.33 g plant ⁻ ¹), flower number (88.14
plant ⁻ ¹), flower weight (2.28 kg plant ⁻ ¹), flower diameter (6.65 cm), individual flower weight (25.89 g)
and shelf life (4.27 days). The maximum flower yield of 35.21 t ha ⁻ ¹ was obtained under T5, closely
achieving the targeted yield with only 0.60% deviation. The 15, 20, 25 and 30 t ha ⁻ ¹ target treatments
similarly achieved 15.12, 20.27, 25.30 and 30.27 t ha ⁻ ¹, respectively, demonstrating close correspon-
dence between targeted and actual yields. T5 also recorded the highest gross return ( ₹ 7,04,200 ha ⁻ ¹) and
net return ( ₹ 4,91,542 ha ⁻ ¹). Overall, the study demonstrated that soil test and target yield-based nutrient
management can effectively synchronize nutrient application with crop requirements and desired pro-
ductivity, thereby improving growth, yield, flower quality and profitability of African marigold under
the experimental conditions. The findings support further multi-season and multi-location validation of
the target-yield approach for site-specific nutrient management in marigold. The targeted-yield concept
is consistent with earlier marigold research demonstrating the usefulness of soil-test-based fertilizer pre-
scriptions for achieving specified yields.

1. Introduction
Floriculture has emerged as an important component of diversified horticultural production, contributing to
farm income, employment generation and value addition through the production of loose flowers, cut flow-
ers, planting material and floral products. Among the commercially cultivated loose flowers, marigold
(Tagetes spp.) occupies a prominent position because of its wide adaptability, relatively short crop duration,
attractive flower colour, ease of cultivation and sustained market demand. African marigold (Tagetes erecta
L.), in particular, is extensively cultivated for loose flowers used in garlands, religious ceremonies, social
functions, decoration and other value-added applications. The crop is also recognized as an important source
of natural carotenoids, particularly xanthophylls, which have applications beyond ornamental use. Its broad
adaptation and responsiveness to crop management have made nutrient management an important determi-
nant of productivity and flower quality (Ahmad et al., 2011) [3].
Productivity and quality of marigold are strongly influenced by the availability and balance of essential plant
nutrients. Nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K) are particularly important because they regulate
vegetative development, root growth, photosynthetic activity, assimilate production, flowering and flower
development. Adequate nitrogen promotes canopy development and photosynthetic capacity, phosphorus

can substantially influence plant height, number of branches, leaf
area, chlorophyll content, number of flowers, flower diameter, flower
weight, flowering duration and total flower yield in marigold (Ahmad
et al., 2011) [3]. However, the response to fertilization is not simply a
function of increasing nutrient application; rather, it depends on the
interaction between crop nutrient demand, native soil fertility, nutrient
recovery and the prevailing environmental conditions.
Conventional fertilizer recommendations are generally developed
from multilocation experiments and are often expressed as a common
recommended dose for a crop over a relatively broad geographical re-
gion. Although such recommendations provide a practical basis for
fertilizer application, they may not adequately account for differences
in initial soil fertility, nutrient-supplying capacity, crop yield potential
and management conditions among individual fields. Consequently,
application of a uniform fertilizer dose may result in nutrient under-
application in nutrient-deficient soils or unnecessary application in
soils with substantial indigenous nutrient supply. Contemporary nutri-
ent-management research therefore increasingly emphasizes the need
to integrate soil fertility information with crop nutrient requirements
and realistic yield objectives. Recent STCR research has reiterated
that blanket recommendations may overlook spatial variability in soil
fertility and consequently reduce the efficiency and economic preci-
sion of fertilizer use (Krishna Murthy et al., 2024).
The Soil Test Crop Response (STCR) approach provides a quantita-
tive framework for addressing this limitation by establishing relation-
ships among soil-test nutrient status, crop nutrient requirement, fertil-
izer contribution and targeted crop yield. The targeted-yield concept,
originally developed through the work of Truog and subsequently for-
malized for fertilizer prescription by Ramamoorthy et al. (1967), is
based on the premise that fertilizer requirements can be calculated ac-
cording to the nutrient requirement of a crop for a specified yield tar-
get while accounting for the contribution of nutrients already avail-
able in the soil. Thus, instead of prescribing a single fertilizer dose ir-
respective of soil fertility, the approach enables fertilizer application
to be adjusted according to both the initial soil nutrient status and the
desired yield level. The basic fertilizer-prescription framework uses
parameters such as nutrient requirement per unit of produce and the
relative contributions of soil and applied fertilizer nutrients to crop
uptake.
The principal advantage of the targeted-yield approach is therefore its
ability to link soil fertility, crop nutrient demand and yield expectation
within a single fertilizer-management framework. This is particularly
relevant to intensive horticultural crops, where excessive nutrient ap-
plication may increase production costs without producing a propor-
tional increase in yield, whereas inadequate nutrient supply can re-
strict vegetative growth and reproductive development. The approach
also provides a scientific basis for estimating the amount of fertilizer
required to achieve a predetermined yield rather than simply maxi-
mizing fertilizer input. Recent long-term investigations in southern
India have demonstrated that STCR-based nutrient prescriptions, par-
ticularly when integrated with farmyard manure, can improve crop
productivity, nutrient uptake, nutrient-use efficiency and soil fertility
compared with conventional fertilizer recommendations (Krishna
Murthy et al., 2024).
The importance of this approach is especially evident in the context of
sustainable nutrient management. Excessive or imbalanced fertilizer
use can contribute to declining nutrient-use efficiency, nutrient accu-
mulation or depletion, soil degradation and increased risk of nutrient
losses to the environment. Conversely, insufficient nutrient applica-
tion can limit crop productivity and reduce the economic return from
land and other production inputs. The objective of site-specific nutri-
ent management is therefore not necessarily to reduce fertilizer use in
absolute terms, but to improve the synchronization between nutrient
supply and crop demand. Recent research on STCR-based nutrient
management has emphasized that targeted-yield prescriptions can si-
multaneously improve productivity and nutrient-use efficiency by
considering indigenous soil nutrient supply, crop nutrient removal and
the contribution of applied fertilizers and organic nutrient sources
(Krishna Murthy et al., 2024).
Evidence supporting the applicability of soil-test-based targeted nutri-
ent management is available for several field and horticultural crops.
For example, long-term STCR experiments conducted in southern
India across soybean, sunflower, dry chilli, aerobic rice, foxtail millet,
okra and kodo millet demonstrated that STCR-based NPK integrated
with farmyard manure consistently improved crop productivity rela-
tive to conventional recommendations in several cropping situations.
The authors attributed these responses to balanced nutrient applica-
tion based on soil-test values, crop nutrient removal and targeted yield
requirements (Krishna Murthy et al., 2024). Similarly, recent work on
beetroot demonstrated the development and validation of STCR-inte-
grated plant nutrition models for establishing fertilizer prescriptions
under specific soil conditions, further demonstrating the applicability
of the targeted-yield concept to horticultural crops.
Marigold provides a particularly suitable crop for evaluating such an
approach because of its substantial nutrient requirement and strong re-
sponse to nutrient availability. Earlier studies have clearly demon-
strated that balanced NPK fertilization can modify both vegetative
and reproductive performance. Ahmad et al. (2011) [3], for instance,
reported significant responses of African and French marigold to dif-
ferent NPK combinations, with improvements in plant height, branch-
ing, leaf area, chlorophyll concentration, flower number, flower diam-
eter, flower weight and overall flower production under suitable nutri-
ent combinations. Such findings establish the importance of nutrient
management but also demonstrate that the optimum nutrient combina-
tion for one parameter may not necessarily be identical to that re-
quired for another parameter.
More specifically, soil-test-based targeted-yield research has already
demonstrated the potential of this methodology in marigold. Durga et
al. (2017) [18] quantified the nutrient requirement of marigold under
Inceptisol conditions at New Delhi and estimated that approximately
0.46 kg N, 0.07 kg P and 0.49 kg K were required to produce one
quintal of flowers. The study also developed fertilizer-prescription
equations and demonstrated that the inclusion of farmyard manure
with NPK could reduce the quantity of fertilizer required for attaining
a specified yield target. For a target yield of 30 t ha ⁻ ¹, the integrated
approach saved 9.3 kg N, 10.17 kg P ₂ O ₅ and 3.09 kg K ₂ O ha ⁻ ¹ com-
pared with NPK alone at specified soil-test values, illustrating the po-
tential of targeted nutrient management to improve fertilizer-use
efficiency.
Similarly, Tripathi and Srivastava (2019) developed and evaluated
STCR-based fertilizer prescriptions for marigold grown on Mollisols
of Uttarakhand. Their study demonstrated that fertilizer recommenda-
tions based on targeted yield could outperform general recommended
doses, and fertilizer-adjustment equations were developed using soil-
test values and crop-response data. These findings are particularly im-
portant because they establish that the nutrient requirement and fertil-
izer response of marigold can vary with soil type and production envi-
ronment, thereby reinforcing the need for location-specific nutrient
prescriptions rather than indiscriminate extrapolation of fertilizer rec-
ommendations from one agro-ecological region to another.
Recent research continues to support the importance of precision nu-
trient management in African marigold, although much of the recent
literature has focused on integrated nutrient management rather than
soil-test-based targeted yield. Studies published during 2025 reported
that combinations of mineral fertilizers with organic manures and
biofertilizers improved vegetative growth, flowering and flower yield
in African marigold. Dhaker et al. (2025), for example, reported en-
hanced growth, flowering and flower yield under integrated nutrient
management involving reduced mineral fertilizer rates combined with
vermicompost and biofertilizer. Similarly, Sharma et al. (2025) ob-
served improvements in nutrient availability, flowering characteris-
tics, flower number, flower size and flower weight under integrated
nutrient management involving nitrogen and biofertilizers. These re-
cent findings reinforce the broader principle that nutrient management

Further, recent studies have expanded the sustainability perspective
by examining nutrient management together with other resource-use
strategies. For example, research conducted during 2023-2025 evalu-

Despite these advances, important gaps remain in the application of
the targeted-yield approach to African marigold under specific soil
and agro-climatic conditions. Fertilizer prescriptions developed for
Tagetes patula or for African marigold under Inceptisols or Mollisols
cannot automatically be assumed to be appropriate for T. erecta
grown under lateritic red soils. Indeed, a recent 2026 study from
Odisha further highlighted the need to develop location-specific tar-
geted-yield equations for African marigold and estimated nutrient re-
quirements of approximately 0.55 kg N, 0.09 kg P and 0.56 kg K per
quintal of flowers under its experimental conditions. The variation
among these estimates and earlier studies illustrates the influence of
soil type, cultivar, environment and experimental conditions on crop
nutrient requirements.
The present investigation was therefore undertaken to evaluate soil-
test- and target-yield-based nutrient management in African marigold
(Tagetes erecta L.) cv. Tall Yellow under the North Eastern
Transitional Zone of Karnataka. The experiment evaluated progres-
sively increasing target-yield levels of 15, 20, 25, 30 and 35 t ha ⁻ ¹ in
comparison with the recommended dose of fertilizers and farmers'
fertilizer practice. The study was conducted on lateritic red soil at
Bidar, and the farmers' practice treatment was established from a sur-
vey of 30 marigold-growing farmers in the surrounding area, thereby
providing a locally relevant benchmark for comparison. The experi-
mental framework was designed to determine whether nutrient appli-
cation calculated according to soil fertility and target yield could im-
prove vegetative growth, flowering, flower yield, quality, nutrient up-
take and economic returns relative to conventional fertilizer-manage-
ment practices.
Accordingly, the study was aimed at establishing a practical relation-
ship between soil-test status, target yield and fertilizer requirement for
African marigold under the experimental conditions. Particular em-
phasis was placed on identifying the target yield that could be realisti-
cally achieved without disproportionate fertilizer expenditure and on
determining whether soil-test-based nutrient management could pro-
vide economic advantages over conventional fertilizer recommenda-
tions and prevailing farmers' practice. The resulting information is ex-
pected to contribute to the development of more precise nutrient-man-
agement strategies for African marigold and provide a basis for im-
proving fertilizer-use efficiency, productivity and profitability in com-
mercial loose-flower production.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1 Experimental site and climatic conditions
The field experiment was conducted during 2022 at the College of
Horticulture, Floriculture and Landscape Architecture, Bidar,
Karnataka, India. The experimental site falls within the North Eastern
Transitional Zone of Karnataka (Agro-Climatic Zone I) and is situ-
ated at an altitude of approximately 615 m above mean sea level at
17.91° N latitude and 77.51° E longitude. The experimental field was
characterized by naturally occurring lateritic red soil.
Meteorological observations were recorded during the crop period
from January to July 2022. The maximum temperature ranged from
27.7 to 38.5 °C, while the minimum temperature ranged from 14.3 to
24.1 °C. Relative humidity varied from 33 to 93% during the experi-
mental period.
2.2 Experimental material and design
African marigold (Tagetes erecta L.) cv. Tall Yellow was used as the
test crop. Seeds of the cultivar were obtained from HyVeg Pvt. Ltd.
The experiment was laid out in a randomized complete block design
(RCBD) comprising seven nutrient-management treatments with
three replications. Each net plot measured 1.8 × 1.8 m, and plants
were maintained at a spacing of 40 × 40 cm.
The treatments consisted of five target-yield levels, one recommended
dose of fertilizer (RDF), and one farmer-practice treatment:

2.3 Survey of farmers and estimation of farmer-practice nu-
trient application
A preliminary survey was conducted among 30 marigold-growing
farmers in villages surrounding the experimental site. Information on
fertilizer-use practices was collected, and the average fertilizer quanti-
ties used by the surveyed farmers were used to formulate the farmer-
practice treatment (T7).
2.4 Soil sampling and characterization
Before establishment of the experiment, soil samples were collected
from the experimental plots and characterized for physical and chemi-
cal properties. The soil was classified as lateritic red soil. The physi-
cal composition comprised 36.03% sand, 39.32% silt and 30.13%
clay.
For chemical analysis, composite soil samples were air-dried, gently
ground and passed through a 2-mm sieve. A finer fraction was pre-
pared by passing soil through a 0.2-mm sieve for organic carbon de-
termination. Soil pH was measured potentiometrically, while electri-
cal conductivity (EC) was determined using a conductivity meter.
Organic carbon was estimated by the Walkley and Black method.
Available nitrogen was estimated by the alkaline permanganate
method of Subbiah and Asija (1956), available phosphorus by the col-
orimetric method of Olsen et al. (1954/1957) and available potassium
using the flame-photometric procedure described by Stanford and
English (1963). The available nutrient concentrations were expressed
in kg ha ⁻ ¹.

2.5 Calculation of nutrient requirements and fertilizer doses
Target-yield nutrient management was based on the nutrient require-
ment of marigold and the initial soil-test status. The nutrient require-
ment adopted for production of one tonne of marigold flowers was
4.60 kg N, 0.70 kg P ₂ O ₅ and 4.90 kg K ₂ O. This value was derived
from the targeted-yield work of Durga et al. (2017) [18], who quanti-
fied nutrient requirement and developed soil-test-based fertilizer pre-
scriptions for marigold.
For each target yield, the total nutrient requirement was first calcu-
lated from the target yield and the corresponding nutrient requirement
per tonne. For example, a target yield of 15 t ha ⁻ ¹ required 69.0 kg N,
10.5 kg P ₂ O ₅ and 73.5 kg K ₂ O before adjustment for soil-test status.
The soil-test categories were subsequently used to adjust the nutrient
requirement. For nutrients classified as low, 30% more than the calcu-
lated nutrient removal was applied; for nutrients classified as
medium, the calculated removal quantity was applied; and for nutri-
ents classified as high, 30% less than the calculated removal quantity
was applied. Thus, the low-N status resulted in a 30% upward adjust-
ment of N, high P ₂ O ₅ resulted in a 30% downward adjustment of
P ₂ O ₅ , and medium K ₂ O resulted in application equivalent to the calcu-
lated K ₂ O removal.
This approach follows the general principle of soil-test- and target-
yield-based nutrient management, in which fertilizer requirements are
adjusted according to crop nutrient requirement and the nutrient-sup-
plying capacity of the soil. Similar targeted-yield approaches have
been successfully evaluated in marigold under Inceptisols and
Mollisols.
Important manuscript note: because the present experiment used pre-
determined nutrient-requirement values and soil-test rating adjust-
ments, it should be described as a soil-test and target-yield based nu-
trient-management approach, rather than stating that new fertilizer
prescription equations were developed from the present experiment.
2.6 Nursery raising and transplanting
Marigold seedlings were raised in protrays filled with cocopeat, with
one seed sown per cell. The trays were initially stacked and covered
with polyethylene to facilitate germination. After six days, the trays
were transferred to a shade-net environment and irrigated daily using
a rose can. A 19:19:19 water-soluble fertilizer solution at 5 g L ⁻ ¹ was
applied as a drench at 20 days after sowing.
The experimental field was ploughed and harrowed to a depth of ap-
proximately 30 cm two to three times, followed by fine leveling.
Stones, weeds and other debris were removed, beds were prepared,
and farmyard manure was incorporated according to the respective
treatment requirements. Irrigation channels were provided around the
beds.
Healthy, approximately one-month-old seedlings were transplanted at
a spacing of 40 × 40 cm. Light irrigation was provided immediately
after transplanting.
2.7 Irrigation and crop management
Following establishment, irrigation was provided through furrows in
the morning at intervals of approximately 3-4 days, depending on soil
moisture and prevailing weather conditions.
The required quantities of N, P and K were supplied through urea,
single superphosphate (SSP) and muriate of potash (MOP), respec-
tively. Fertilizers were manually applied according to the respective
treatment. One week after transplanting, a circular fertilizer band ap-
proximately 10 cm in diameter and 3-4 cm deep was prepared around
each plant. Half of the N dose and the full doses of P and K were ap-
plied at this stage, while the remaining half of the N dose was applied
as top dressing 30 days after transplanting.
Gap filling was carried out at 7 and 10 days after transplanting to
maintain uniform plant population. Manual weeding was performed
periodically. Shoot tips of approximately 2.5 cm were removed at 30
days after transplanting to suppress apical dominance and promote
lateral branching, followed by earthing-up to maintain plant stability.
Plant protection measures were adopted whenever required. Acephate
at 1.5 mL L ⁻ ¹ was used for management of insect pests such as leaf
miner, while wettable sulphur at 1 g L ⁻ ¹ was used for powdery
mildew management.
2.8 Recording of growth, flowering and yield attributes
Five plants were randomly selected and tagged in each experimental
plot. Observations on growth, flowering, yield and quality character-
istics were recorded on these plants, and treatment means were calcu-
lated from the tagged plants.
Growth observations were recorded at 30, 60 and 90 days after trans-
planting (DAT). The parameters included plant height, stem girth,
plant spread and number of branches. Leaf area and leaf area index
were also recorded, while chlorophyll content was estimated at 30 and
60 DAT. Total dry matter was determined at harvest.
Plant height was measured from ground level to the growing tip of the
main stem. Stem girth was measured using a Vernier caliper at three
positions near the base of the stem. Plant spread was measured in the
north-south and east-west directions. Primary and secondary branches
were counted at 30, 60 and 90 DAT.

flowers from tagged plants using an electronic balance. Flower diam-
eter was measured at full bloom using a Vernier caliper. Shelf life was
assessed by maintaining freshly harvested flowers at room tempera-
ture on paper plates and recording the number of days until loss of
marketable appearance.

2.10 Plant nutrient analysis
Plant samples were collected at harvest, oven-dried at 60 °C, ground
using stainless-steel blades and stored in polythene bags until analy-
sis. Total N, P and K concentrations were determined following stan-
dard procedures.
For nitrogen estimation, 0.5 g of powdered plant material was di-
gested using concentrated H ₂ SO ₄ in the presence of
K ₂ SO ₄ :CuSO ₄ ·5H ₂ O:Se digestion mixture in a 100:20:1 ratio.
Following digestion, the released ammonia was distilled under alka-
line conditions, trapped in boric acid-containing mixed indicator solu-
tion and titrated against standard H ₂ SO ₄ .
For P and K determination, 0.5 g of powdered sample was pre-di-
gested with 5 mL concentrated HNO ₃ followed by digestion with
HNO ₃ :HClO ₄ (10:4). The digest was diluted to 100 mL with distilled
water. Phosphorus was estimated by the vanadomolybdophosphoric
yellow-colour method, while potassium was determined using a flame
photometer.
2.11 Economic analysis
Economic analysis was performed using prevailing input and labour
costs during 2022. Treatment-wise costs included the expenditure on
fertilizers, organic inputs, planting material, labour and other produc-
tion inputs. Gross returns were calculated from flower yield and the
prevailing market price of marigold flowers.

2.12 Statistical analysis
The experimental data were subjected to statistical analysis appropri-

3.1 Effect of soil-test and target-yield based nutrient manage-
ment on vegetative growth
Soil-test- and target-yield-based nutrient management significantly
influenced the vegetative growth of African marigold cv. Tall Yellow.
In general, plant growth increased progressively with increasing tar-
get yield from 15 to 35 t ha ⁻ ¹, with the 35 t ha ⁻ ¹ treatment (T5) record-
ing the highest values for most growth attributes. The farmer-practice
treatment (T7) consistently recorded the lowest growth, while the rec-
ommended fertilizer dose (T6) generally performed below the target-
yield treatments.
3.1.1 Plant height and stem girth
Plant height increased significantly with increasing target yield at 30,

under the 35 t ha ⁻ ¹ target-yield treatment (T5). At 60 DAT, T5 recorded
the maximum plant height (62.25 cm), followed by T4 (60.42 cm),
whereas T7 recorded the minimum (53.14 cm). At 90 DAT, plants un-

was 0.17 cm, confirming significant differences among treatments.
At 90 DAT, the 35 t ha ⁻ ¹ target-yield treatment therefore increased
plant height by approximately 17.3% and stem girth by 50.2% rela-
tive to farmer's practice. Compared with RDF, the corresponding in-

Discussion
The present study demonstrated that soil-test- and target-yield-based
nutrient management substantially modified the growth, flowering,
yield, flower quality, nutrient concentration and economic perfor-
mance of African marigold (Tagetes erecta L.) cv. Tall Yellow. Across
most measured parameters, the response increased with increasing
yield target from 15 to 35 t ha ⁻ ¹, with the 35 t ha ⁻ ¹ treatment (T5) con-
sistently recording the highest values. This response indicates that nu-
trient supply adjusted according to the soil fertility status and desired
yield level was more closely aligned with crop demand than the RDF
and farmer-practice treatments. The principle is consistent with the
STCR concept, in which fertilizer requirements are derived from soil
nutrient-supplying capacity, crop nutrient requirement and the desired
yield target rather than from a uniform recommendation. Recent long-
term work in southern India has similarly demonstrated that STCR-
based nutrient management can improve crop productivity, nutrient
uptake and soil quality relative to generalized fertilizer
recommendations.
The improvement in vegetative growth under increasing target yields
was particularly evident in plant height, stem girth, plant spread and
branching. At 90 DAT, T5 produced the maximum plant height
(67.12 cm), stem girth (3.71 cm), east-west spread (42.98 cm), north–
south spread (40.29 cm), primary branches (21.33 plant ⁻ ¹) and sec-
ondary branches (32.33 plant ⁻ ¹), whereas farmer's practice consis-
tently produced the lowest values. This response is physiologically
plausible because adequate and balanced availability of N, P and K
supports cell division and expansion, photosynthetic development and
formation of vegetative sinks, ultimately increasing plant architecture
and assimilatory surface. The greater leaf area (43.53 cm²) and LAI
(2.70) under T5 compared with 37.20 cm² and 2.32 under farmer's
practice further indicate improved canopy development under tar-
geted nutrient management. Similar positive effects of mineral nutri-
tion on growth and yield attributes of marigold have been reported
previously, including improvements in plant growth, flowering and
yield following NPK fertilization.
The progressive improvement in vegetative growth was accompanied
by greater chlorophyll concentration and dry matter accumulation.
Treatment T5 recorded 3.42 and 3.02 mg g ⁻ ¹ chlorophyll at 30 and 60
DAT, respectively, compared with only 2.27 and 1.57 mg g ⁻ ¹ under
farmer's practice. Dry matter production was also highest under T5
(283.33 g plant ⁻ ¹), compared with 120.00 g plant ⁻ ¹ under T7. The
greater chlorophyll concentration suggests improved nutritional status

greater dry matter indicates that the additional assimilates were trans-
lated into overall biomass production. Importantly, the response was
not simply a consequence of greater vegetative growth; it was ulti-
mately reflected in reproductive productivity.
Flowering behaviour showed a somewhat different response from
vegetative growth. The earliest bud initiation occurred under the 15 t
ha ⁻ ¹ target (41.25 days), while the earliest first flower opening was
observed under the 20 t ha ⁻ ¹ target (43 days). In contrast, T5 reached
50% flowering earliest (49 days) and maintained the longest flower-
ing duration (50 days), whereas farmer's practice required 59 days to
reach 50% flowering and had only 39 days of flowering duration.
These findings indicate that higher nutrient availability did not neces-
sarily accelerate every stage of flowering, but it substantially im-
proved the duration and intensity of reproductive development. Thus,
the effect of nutrient management should be interpreted in terms of
the entire flowering period rather than only the date of first flowering.
The strongest response was observed in flower production and yield.
Number of flowers increased from 39.36 plant ⁻ ¹ under farmer's prac-
tice to 88.14 plant ⁻ ¹ under T5, while flower yield increased from 0.76
to 2.28 kg plant ⁻ ¹. At the hectare scale, yield increased from 11.82 t
ha ⁻ ¹ under farmer's practice to 35.21 t ha ⁻ ¹ under T5. This approxi-
mately three-fold difference in yield is consistent with the improve-
ment in branching, leaf area, chlorophyll concentration and dry matter
production observed under T5. Greater branching provided more po-
tential sites for flower formation, while the larger assimilatory canopy
likely supported greater assimilate production and partitioning to-
wards reproductive sinks.
A particularly important finding was the close agreement between the
specified yield targets and the yields actually obtained. The 15, 20,
25, 30 and 35 t ha ⁻ ¹ treatments produced 15.12, 20.27, 25.30, 30.27
and 35.21 t ha ⁻ ¹, respectively. Thus, all five target treatments achieved
yields very close to their respective targets. This finding provides di-
rect experimental support for the applicability of the targeted-yield
approach under the soil and environmental conditions of the study.
Earlier STCR research in marigold has also demonstrated the utility
of soil-test-based fertilizer prescription. Durga et al. (2017) [18],
working with marigold in an Inceptisol of New Delhi, developed fer-
tilizer prescriptions based on nutrient requirement and soil contribu-
tion and reported that targeted-yield recommendations could reduce
fertilizer inputs under integrated NPK + FYM management.
Similarly, Tripathi and Srivastava (2019) reported that targeted-yield-
based fertilizer prescriptions for marigold grown on Mollisols were
superior to general recommended fertilization in verification trials.
The present study therefore extends this evidence to T. erecta cv. Tall
Yellow under the agroecological and soil conditions of Karnataka.
The improvement in yield was accompanied by better flower quality.
T5 produced the largest flowers (6.65 cm diameter), greatest individ-
ual flower weight (25.89 g) and longest shelf life (4.27 days), whereas
farmer's practice produced 5.72 cm flowers weighing 19.40 g with a
shelf life of 2.97 days. Adequate nutrient availability during flower
development may have supported greater assimilate accumulation and
flower expansion, resulting in larger and heavier capitula. The con-
current increase in flower number and individual flower weight is
particularly important because it indicates that the higher yield under
T5 was not achieved at the expense of flower size.
The higher target-yield treatments also resulted in greater leaf N, P
and K concentrations. T5 recorded 3.98% N, 0.24% P and 1.11% K,
compared with 3.50, 0.13 and 0.92%, respectively, under farmer's
practice. These results suggest that targeted nutrient application im-
proved plant nutritional status and helped maintain adequate nutrient
availability during active vegetative and reproductive growth.
However, these data represent nutrient concentrations rather than nu-
trient uptake; therefore, the manuscript should avoid interpreting them
as total nutrient uptake unless biomass-based uptake calculations are
available. Modern STCR studies similarly emphasize the importance
of balancing fertilizer application with crop nutrient demand and soil
nutrient-supplying capacity.
The economic results further strengthened the agronomic response.
Although T5 incurred the highest cultivation cost ( ₹ 2,12,658 ha ⁻ ¹), its
substantially greater yield resulted in the highest gross return
( ₹ 7,04,200 ha ⁻ ¹) and net return ( ₹ 4,91,542 ha ⁻ ¹), with a reported B:C
ratio of 2.3. Farmer's practice generated only ₹ 29,101 ha ⁻ ¹ net return
and a reported B:C ratio of 0.1. Similar economic advantages from
soil-test-based targeted nutrient management have been documented
in other crops, where balanced nutrient application improved produc-
tivity and returns compared with generalized fertilizer
recommendations.
Overall, the results indicate that the 35 t ha ⁻ ¹ target-yield treatment
provided the highest agronomic and economic performance under the
conditions of the experiment, while the lower target treatments pro-
duced progressively lower yields corresponding closely to their speci-
fied targets. The findings support the use of soil-test-based targeted
nutrient management as a more site-specific approach for marigold
than generalized fertilization. Nevertheless, the present conclusion is
specific to the cultivar, soil, location and experimental season; valida-
tion across additional seasons, soil fertility classes and production en-
vironments would be necessary before recommending the 35 t ha ⁻ ¹
prescription broadly. This is particularly important because STCR
prescriptions are inherently site-specific and their performance de-
pends on soil nutrient status and the nutrient contribution of soil, fer-
tilizer and organic inputs. Recent validation studies in horticultural
crops likewise demonstrate that targeted-yield models can be highly
effective but require crop- and soil-specific calibration.
Conclusion
The present study demonstrated that soil test- and target yield-based
nutrient management was effective in improving the growth, flower-
ing, yield and quality of African marigold (Tagetes erecta L.) cv. Tall
Yellow under the red lateritic soils of Bidar, Karnataka. Among the
nutrient management treatments, the 35 t ha ⁻ ¹ target-yield treatment
(T5) consistently produced superior vegetative growth, greater leaf
area and chlorophyll content, prolonged flowering duration, higher
flower number and flower yield, and improved flower quality com-
pared with RDF and farmer practice. Most importantly, the targeted-
yield approach achieved the prescribed yield levels with close agree-
ment between target and actual yields, with deviations of only 0.6–
1.35% for the 15–35 t ha ⁻ ¹ targets. The highest actual flower yield of
35.21 t ha ⁻ ¹ was obtained under T5, while farmer practice produced
only 11.82 t ha ⁻ ¹. The improvement in yield was accompanied by
higher leaf N, P and K concentrations and greater economic returns.
These findings support the use of soil test-based target yield nutrient
management as a site-specific approach for optimizing nutrient appli-
cation and improving productivity of African marigold. However, the
results represent a single-season, location-specific evaluation and
should be further validated across seasons, soil types and production
environments before wider recommendation. The targeted-yield con-
cept is consistent with earlier marigold STCR research demonstrating
the potential of soil-test-based fertilizer prescriptions to achieve spec-
ified yields while improving nutrient-use efficiency.
