Abstract
Preserving water quality has become a critical priority due to the increasing pressure 
on water resources from population growth, production activities and climate 
change. This article reviews recent studies on water quality monitoring in lakes, 
rivers and reservoirs, with a focus on lessons relevant to local decision-making. 
Using a structured literature review approach, we synthesised and interpreted 
research on water quality monitoring systems used in aquatic ecosystems affected 
by eutrophication and turbidity. Particular attention was given to turbidity sensors 
and autonomous monitoring approaches. The findings show that traditional manual 
quality measurements continue to coexist with autonomous monitoring systems, 
each contributing different strengths to water quality assessment. However, studies 
on low-cost sensors and permanent autonomous measurement systems remain 
limited, likely because of other research priorities. The limited number of published 
studies suggests either that these systems remain uncommon in practice or that 
their use is insufficiently documented. Current monitoring efforts largely rely on field 
manual sampling carried out by specialised technical personnel employed in state 
services or research centres. Looking ahead, it appears promising to complement 
current methods by increasingly integrating low-cost autonomous sensor networks 
and expanding opportunities for citizen participation. Such developments 
could support the creation of real-time information systems that facilitate local 
decision-making.

1 Introduction
1.1 Water quality: a global challenge
Ensuring water quality is one of the major environmental and societal challenges of the 
twenty-first century. Water quality plays a crucial role in public health and ecosystem 
conservation [ 28]. It is also central to sustainable development, as access to safe and 
reliable water supports economic growth and social well-being [ 10]. However, progress 
towards achieving the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal on clean water

and sanitation remain insufficient, and improvements in water supply quality continue 
to fall short of global targets [ 99]. This challenge is particularly evident in Latin America 
and the Caribbean, where water quality continues to deteriorate due to combined effects 
of climate change and socioeconomic pressures [ 98]. The risk of water shortages due to 
pollution is a growing concern [ 104], exacerbated by extreme hydroclimatic events that 
increase the likelihood of droughts and degrade water quality [29]. Human activities also 
contribute to pollution along entire river systems, from upstream to downstream [ 44]. 
The two main threats identified for freshwater bodies are nutrient pollution, which leads 
from eutrophic to hypertrophic conditions and toxic stress from chemical pollution [97]. 
The World Bank has warned of the urgent need to address water quality issues before 
they escalate [ 23]. Over the past decade, water-related conflicts have increased world -
wide, [45], and social movements advocating for the protection of water resources have 
also emerged [100].
1.2 Monitoring water quality to reduce exposure to pollution and its effects
Limited water quality information in low-income countries remains a major challenge 
that must be addressed to enable informed decision-making [ 99]. Beyond technological 
innovation, the evolution of water-quality monitoring systems reflects a broader tran -
sition in environmental science towards data-intensive observation frameworks that 
support anticipatory and evidence-based water governance. Improved knowledge of 
water quality enhances the capacity to anticipate health risks. Poor-quality or contami -
nated water affects mental and psychological well-being as much as physical health [ 28], 
increasing the incidence of infectious diseases, which are often linked to microbiologi -
cal contamination. Contaminated water can also cause organ damage and cancer due to 
exposure to inorganic and organic contaminants [19].
Effective water purification strategies are essential for maintaining public health 
while supporting agriculture and conserving biodiversity. Yet, as water challenges vary

1.3 Emerging WQMS: examples from Latin America
With abundant surface and groundwater resources, Latin America has the highest per 
capita water availability in the world , [ 16]. However, land use strongly influences water 
quality, particularly through increasing urbanisation [ 91]. Numerous cases of pollu -
tion have been documented across the region, linked to agricultural activities [ 16, 35], 
industrial discharges [ 33] and domestic wastewater [ 16, 76]. Pollution remains difficult 
to control due to high financial costs, inadequate wastewater-treatment systems [44] and 
insufficient water quality monitoring programmes , [16].
Many Latin American countries operate permanent public WQMS which focus on 
major surface waters or key watersheds. Nevertheless, these data often consist of irregu -
lar point-in-time measurements rather than real-time observations. Government agen -
cies often manage online platforms that provide public access to this data, such as the 
Water Quality Information System (SINA) in Mexico [ 22] and the National Informa -
tion System for Integrated Water Resource Management (SINIGIRH) in Costa Rica [ 57] 
in Central America. Other examples include the online monitoring system of Brazil’s 
National Water and Basic Sanitation Agency [3], the Environmental Information Centre 
(CIAM) in Argentina [ 83] and the information systems of the General Directorate of 
Water in Chile [26].
However, within that world region, these measures remain insufficient to address 
persistent water quality problems. The uneven distribution of the thousand monitor -
ing stations limits the availability of comprehensive data for real-time decision-making 
[66]. The implementation of on-site sensor networks and the establishment of addi -
tional monitoring stations will be essential to achieving adequate territorial coverage. 
Another important consideration is that citizen monitoring remains underrepresented 
and undervalued in water quality monitoring systems (WQMS).These initiatives aim 
to track changes in drinking water quality and safeguard the surrounding aquatic eco -
systems. However, its range of analysis methods can be adapted to local environmental 
and socio-economic conditions, complementing official hydrological data monitoring by 
facilitating the detection of pollution and environmental changes in areas lacking for -
mal monitoring [ 13]. Similar to the challenges observed in Latin America, a research 
gap remains regarding non-interconnected lakes in emerging economies [ 55]. Where 
autonomous monitoring is still limited, the combination of multivariate analysis with 
GIS mapping is essential to identify critical pollution areas and guide local management 
strategies.

1.4 Turbidity and eutrophication as main factors of disturbance
Turbidity is a key water quality parameter and is widely used as an indicator of trophic 
status and hydrosystem productivity. It may reflect the presence of suspended particles 
in the water column, arising from both organic matters [ 12] and inorganic suspended 
solids [36]. Elevated turbidity reduces light penetration through increased light scatter -
ing, thereby affecting aquatic ecosystem functioning [82, 85].
Turbidity exhibits strong seasonal and interannual variability [ 1, 43, 79, 96], driven by 
meteorological conditions that affect biological, and sediment transport processes [ 36]. 
Depending on local climatic conditions, turbidity peaks may occur during summer or 
wetter autumn/winter periods [ 1, 39, 50, 51, 102]. Extreme events, including storms, 
strong winds, typhoons and monsoons, can substantially increase sediment resuspen -
sion and run-off, often contributing disproportionately to annual sediment loads [ 1, 
42, 109]. In addition, the relative contribution of inorganic particles and phytoplankton 
varies among systems, with algal blooms commonly intensifying turbidity during warm 
periods [53]. In shallow lentic systems, turbidity is closely associated with nutrient con -
centrations particularly total phosphate and nitrogen, highlighting the critical role of 
nutrient loading in determining water quality [55].
Both natural and anthropogenic factors influence turbidity and nutrient dynamics. 
Catchment characteristics, including topography, soil properties and land-use practices, 
regulate erosion and sediment delivery [ 38, 54], while hydrodynamic conditions, water 
depth and sediment resuspension control turbidity within lakes and rivers [ 38, 54]. At 
the lake or river scale, turbidity is affected by water column depth [ 43, 68], hydrody -
namic conditions [ 43, 82], bed and bank erosion, sediment deposition and resuspen -
sion. Within lakes, turbidity differs between the fluvial zone [ 78] and deeper or bottom 
regions. Human activities such as agriculture, urbanisation, industry and aquaculture 
increase sediment and nutrient inputs, particularly nitrogen and phosphorus, through 
runoff and tributary inflows [60, 70, 74, 92, 103, 108, 112]. Nitrogen pollution is strongly 
associated with agricultural activities in catchment areas, primarily as a result of exces -
sive fertiliser application [ 1, 56, 60, 108]. Tributaries redistribute pollutants from urban 
and agricultural areas, resulting in cumulative downstream impacts [ 43, 52, 108, 115]. 
These inputs stimulate algal growth, increase chlorophyll-a concentrations and can 
reduce dissolved oxygen levels, ultimately degrading water quality and ecosystem health 
[48, 84].
The combined effects of climate change and nutrient enrichment contribute to eutro -
phication, one of the main causes of degradation in aquatic ecosystems in lakes and 
wetlands [18, 25, 88] [39, 48, 50, 51, 60, 102, 108]. Rising temperatures accelerate algal 
productivity and may concentrate dissolved constituents through increased evaporation, 
exacerbating water quality degradation. [ 48, 55, 60, 78, 103]. As a result, eutrophication 
threatens key ecosystem services such as recreation, irrigation and drinking water provi-
sion [89]. These interactions underline the need for monitoring systems capable of cap -
turing the temporal and spatial variability of turbidity and nutrient dynamics to support 
effective water resource management.
1.5 Objective, hypothesis and article outline
As environmental monitoring rapidly transitions toward continuous, data-inten -
sive observation and advanced analytical approaches that support decision-making,

a synthesis is needed to understand how these advances are reshaping both scientific 
understanding and governance. This article aims to synthesise the literature on the role 
of autonomous systems within water quality monitoring systems used to address envi -
ronmental risks in rivers, lakes and reservoirs. Our research focuses on surface waters 
affected by runoff, erosion, and acute eutrophication, and as such does not include 
groundwater. Indeed, the cost-effective, autonomous optical turbidity sensors and telem-
etry equipment presented in this article are designed for surface deployment to capture 
high-frequency spatial and temporal dynamics induced by weather events and land-use 
practices. While groundwater quality is a critical component of water resource manage -
ment, its monitoring presents entirely different hydrogeological dynamics, access limita-
tions, and sensor deployment challenges, which fall outside the technological framework 
of the affordable surface water telemetry examined here.
We prioritised turbidity as a proxy variable and required these keywords to ensure 
comparability across systems. Our review was guided by two hypotheses. First, pre -
ferred methods for monitoring the quality of continental would rely primarily on time-
consuming, labour-intensive field sampling campaigns or, alternatively, satellite imagery. 
And second, the adoption of low-cost, autonomous and easy-to-use local technologies 
would remain relatively uncommon, creating gaps in real-time data availability for local 
decision-makers and concerned communities. In this context, local monitoring systems 
could serve as powerful tools for community-level decision-making and may be read -
ily adopted by local populations. We paid attention to the most used tools for assessing 
water quality, seeking to address the challenges associated with developing and imple -
menting low-cost monitoring systems.
1.6 The novelty of the review
This paper highlights the emerging use of low-cost, autonomous sensor networks as a 
rapidly evolving approach to water quality monitoring in high-resource countries. In 
contrast to traditional systems that rely on manual, point-in-time sampling, these tech -
nologies generate continuous, real-time data, addressing longstanding limitations asso -
ciated with irregular or delayed information. This enables early detection of quick or 
short-lived disturbances such as turbidity and eutrophication, which are particularly 
relevant in the region. Moreover, the reviewed studies point to the potential for stron -
ger community participation in water monitoring, helping close persistent gaps in timely 
and actionable information for decision-making, in lower income countries, particularly 
in Latin America. Beyond their technical advantages, these systems represent a shift 
toward high-frequency environmental observation capable of capturing transient pro -
cesses that traditional sampling often overlooks. This transition has important implica -
tions for hydrological modelling, early-warning capacity and adaptive water governance, 
particularly in regions where delayed information constrains timely decision-making, 
reinforcing the transition from reactive to anticipatory water management.

2 Material and methods
In order to review and synthesise the literature on the subject, we followed the PRISMA 
reporting guidelines, applying its procedural steps, flowchart and checklist [ 58, 59].1 The 
article selection process is summarised in Table SI-1 and Fig. 1.
2.1 Literature search
In October 2025, scientific articles were retrieved from the Web of Science (WoS) data -
base, selected for its extensive peer-reviewed literature, multidisciplinary coverage and 
advanced filtering tools. We used keyword-based search equations:

Each search query was applied to all fields (topic, title, publication titles, abstract and 
keywords) and a filter was applied to include only articles. While no timespan filter was 
applied, the initial search returned 6,710 articles.
2.2 Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Due to the large number of results, inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to 
obtain a more focused dataset.
The inclusion criteria were:
i) For Eq. 1: articles had to contain the keywords ‘lake’ , ‘quality’ and ‘turbidity’ (criterion 
1).
ii) For Eq.  2: articles had to contain the keywords ‘monitoring’ , ‘sensors’ and ‘quality’ 
(criterion 2).
The exclusion criteria were:
iii) Articles not written in English or Spanish (criterion 3). The language criterion applied 
to screened journal articles; background references in other languages were retained 
where relevant.
iv) Duplicate articles were removed from the list (criterion 4).
These inclusion and exclusion criteria were subsequently automated using a custom R 
script. The script was designed to cross-reference target keywords within article titles 
and abstracts, which had been retrieved from the Web of Science (WoS) database using 
its built-in export functionality. The dataset was exported in CSV format for subsequent 
analysis. Application of these criteria reduced the dataset to 236 articles.
2.3 Screening procedure
The abstracts were screened independently by two of the listed authors (one from engi -
neering, one from social sciences, both specialised in water studies), who coded each 
abstract. Articles were classified as:
i) Clearly related to the topic
ii) Potentially related; to be discussed
iii) Unrelated to the topic
Articles coded ‘i’ by both reviewers were retained, while those coded ‘iii’ by both were 
discarded. For articles coded “ii” , inclusion or exclusion was determined through collab-
orative discussion between the reviewers. Reasons for exclusion included: overly general 
approaches, lack of remote-sensing or machine-learning components or WQ monitor -
ing based solely on algae or fish sampling. A total of 172 articles were discarded, leaving 
64 for analysis (see Fig. 1).

3 Results. Water quality monitoring systems: from manual sampling to 
autonomous techniques. Benefits and prospects
In many countries, water authorities are responsible for ensuring the safety of water 
use and consumption, including maintaining standard biological and chemical levels in 
aquatic ecosystems [ 1]. To assess the condition of surface waters, monitoring is funda -
mental for identifying cycles and changes in water quality. As various studies suggest, 
these cycles—observed in rivers, estuaries, lakes, reservoirs and lagoons—are influenced 
by both natural factors and anthropogenic pressures [1].
Over time, water quality monitoring has become increasingly technical and diversi -
fied. Methods range from manual sampling [ 41] to automated approaches, temporary 
campaigns [ 49] to continuous monitoring [ 62], and small-case analyses [ 49] to exten -
sive basin-level assessments [ 87]. Given this diversity and the growing need for reliable 
data, analysing existing water quality measurement methods is particularly important 
for resource-constrained regions.

3.2 Traditional tools used for monitoring vital signs of surface waters
3.2.1 Traditional sampling to identify key correlations between the main water quality 
indicators for calibration purposes
In the reviewed case studies, water quality monitoring systems based on traditional sam-
pling methods confirm their usefulness and effectiveness in many respects. Water qual -
ity parameters are recognised as indicators of aquatic ecosystems’s ecological health [ 9, 
14, 41, 62, 72, 77, 113]. Measurement campaigns carried out on a wide range of variables 
at the start of the study make it possible to determine the key water quality indicators 
specific to each waterbody that locally influence eutrophication and turbidity processes. 
Cross-analysis of the results of these measurements also helps to identify correlations, 
which provide an understanding of the complexity of interactions between physical–
chemical and/or biological variables in the degradation of water quality. Jia et al. [43] and 
Lin and Li [ 52] reported that turbidity and eutrophication of water bodies were corre -
lated, with a system’s sensitivity to nutrient depending on internal factors (resuspension 
of sediments) and external factors (upstream water discharges). Similarly, Abalasei et al. 
[1] demonstrated a relationship between turbidity and organic matter in their analysis 
of Chirita Lake’s water quality data, which could contribute to the sustainable manage -
ment of drinking water in Romania. For their part, Mequanent et al. [ 56] showed a posi-
tive correlation between turbidity and: 1) low vegetation cover in the buffer zone, 2) % 
of human disturbance assessment and 3) total nitrogen. Identifying these associations 
is important to long-term monitoring, making it possible to better calibrate predictive 
models.
Dissolved oxygen emerges as a key variable for assessing rivers, lakes or reservoirs 
integrity, as its near-bottom depletion is closely linked to eutrophication processes, ther-
mal stratification and the accumulation of organic matter [ 2]. The spatial and temporal 
dynamics of dissolved oxygen, particularly in deeper or poorly mixed zones, can exhibit 
pronounced variability that is not captured by low-frequency or point-based measure -
ments, underscoring the value of continuous and sensor-based monitoring approaches 
[2]. Therefore, long-term monitoring of each body of water is essential to understand 
the interactions that influence water quality throughout the year. [ 49, 87]. At the same 
time, the precise spatial resolution of this type of measurement is necessary to calibrate 
remote sensors and in-situ loggers to determine the strategic locations for their installa -
tion in the basin or water body.

3.2.2 The benefits and complementarity of in-situ and ex-situ sensors
Satellite-based sensors are widely used in several research areas. Yet, as Lepistö et al. 
[49] highlight, satellite-based remote sensing offers high spatial and temporal resolution, 
but it needs to be calibrated using field campaign measurements. Caroni et al. [ 17] used 
global satellite observations to study 307 shallow lakes (< 3m depth) worldwide over the 
last 20 years, assessing trends of water quality as chlorophyll-a and turbidity – related to 
phytoplankton biomass and suspended sediments, respectively. Their study revealed the 
benefits of low-cost, large spatial coverage and high spatial resolution for water monitor-
ing. The performance of satellite-based techniques varies. Tavares et al. [ 94] used satel-
lite imagery (Sentinel-2 MSI Imagery) for monitoring superficial events in small lakes 
(<10 km2) located in the Adour Garonne river basin, calibrated with more than 600 in-
situ data from 108 lakes. Laneve et al. [ 47] compared different methods using Sentinel-2 
and Landsat 5.7-8, implementing atmospheric corrections in 4 lakes in Italy, obtaining 
high performances (RMSE close to 3 mg/m3) in lakes with low turbidity. Kiwanuka et al. 
[46] developed predictive models to improve the health of lakes, based on the experience 
using Landsat 8 and 9 imagery and in-situ samples. In contrast, Mequanent et al [ 56] 
used remote sensing, GIS, human disturbance assessment and manual sampling in four 
sites with varying degrees of alteration in the Lake Tana buffer zone in Ethiopia. Their 
study showed the value of cross-referencing the accuracy of data measured using a mul -
tiparameter probe and a Secchi disk –following Tantu et al. ’s [93] approach in lake Abaya 
in Ethiopia– with data from ex situ sensors to better understand interactions between 
land use and water quality.
In addition, several studies have developed different on-site monitoring systems 
that yield valuable insights in aquatic ecosystems. García-Avila et al. [ 31], for example, 
assessed eutrophication levels in a high Andean lake in Ecuador using multiple trophic 
indices (OECD, Carlson, and others). Similarly, Prasetyo et al. [70] evaluated water qual-
ity in Lake Batur, Indonesia, through a Water Quality Index based on seven parameters, 
identifying the suitability of water for different uses. More recently, machine-learning 
approaches have proven effective for calibrating water-quality models, suggesting a 
strong capacity to identify relationships among in-situ parameters [37, 54] and [75]).
Finally, manual data help to programme hydrological models, which are used to pre -
dict water quality. In Canada, Zolfaghari et al. [ 113] employed a combination of mod -
elling, optical sensors and laboratory analyses to study variables such as chlorophyll-a, 
turbidity and dissolved organic carbon, illustrating the ecological and socio-economic 
risks associated with high turbidity. Using a quantitative approach, Jamal et al. [ 41] 
examined how land use affects the water quality of Wular Lake in the Kashmir Valley, 
India, based on manual sampling of 22 physicochemical parameters. The analyses led to 
the design of strategies aiming to protect both the wetland ecosystem and the health of 
the water body.
Field campaigns remain a standard approach, often requiring extensive logistics, per -
sonnel and equipment. Although per -sample costs can be moderate in well-resourced 
programmes, laboratory analyses are often prohibitively expensive for citizen initiatives. 
In contrast, the deployment of in-situ sensors enables continuous monitoring of water 
quality parameters, offering temporal precision that traditional field campaigns often 
lack.

3.3 International experiences in the use of autonomous and continuous sensors, with 
emphasis on turbidity sensors
In-situ turbidity monitoring relies on the temporary or continuous deployment of 
instruments in surface waters. This literature review reports on the use of turbidimeters, 
nephelometers, multiparameter and multimeter probes for manual sampling, and mul -
tiparameter probes and turbidity sensors for automated sampling (Fig.  3). Some equip -
ment is used for all types of monitoring.
Among the studies we reviewed, Skarbøvik et al. [ 87] provided the most comprehen -
sive comparison between in-situ turbidity sensors and existing suspended sediments 
concentration northwestern European streams. The relevance of this study is under -
scored by the Water Framework Directive, introduced by the European Commission in 
2000, which aims to achieve good water quality status across member states. As noted 
by the authors, optical turbidity sensors offered a promising solution for monitoring sus-
pended sediments concentrations. However, their study also highlighted two limitations

3.4 Towards more effective hydrological models using data from affordable, remote and 
autonomous sensors for measuring water turbidity
Excess sediment and nutrient loads can pose serious challenges for aquaculture, tour -
ism and public health [ 4, 21]. Furthermore, factors such as land cover, topography and 
catchment size, along with meteorological data, can be integrated into semi-distributed 
hydrologic models [ 65, 95]. Extending this line of inquiry, several studies have demon -
strated that hydrological modelling, when combined with variables like sediment and 
nutrient concentrations in the water column, is a widely used and effective approach [34, 
63, 71, 110]. However, high-frequency sampling –ranging from minutes to daily inter -
vals– over extended periods (e.g., 2 to 7 years) is essential for reliable model calibration 
and validation. Therefore, acquiring such data in the most cost-effective manner is cru -
cial [6, 63].
In parallel, advanced data fusion techniques, including machine-learning algorithms, 
can be employed to integrate diverse datasets, allowing for a more nuanced understand -
ing of the relationships between variables and improving the accuracy of model predic -
tions. According to Arnold et al. [ 7], the widely used Soil and Water Assessment Tool 
(SWAT) is a simple yet reliable model for simulating hydrological fluxes alongside water 
quality variables. Similar findings were reported by Yeo et al. [ 110] and Arabi et al. [ 5], 
who used SWAT to simulate water flow, soil erosion, SS concentration and nutrient 
transport at the catchment scale, as well as to assess the impacts of land-use change, best 
management practices and climate change.
In contrast, Lepistö et al. [ 49] used a multi-technical approach to monitor water qual -
ity in Pyhajarvi Lake, southwest Finland, using remote sensing data from the MEdium 
Resolution Imaging Spectrometer (MERIS) and transect measurements collected from a 
moving boat. Their methodology included manual water sampling and laboratory analy -
sis to compare the results with remote sensing data. Although their findings suggest that 
transect measurements provide accurate estimates of water quality parameters, the costs 
of measurements remain high, and remote sensing techniques require improvement for 
certain estimations.
3.5 AI-assisted sensor fusion and cloud-based environmental monitoring as tools that 
enhance data reliability and accessibility
Beyond data acquisition, several recent studies focused on how large monitoring datas -
ets can be analysed and translated into management decisions. The latter has introduced 
new methodologies such as hybrid machine-learning models. Gandhimathi et al. [ 30] 
integrated regression and boosting techniques to improve river water quality classifi -
cation, demonstrating the downstream analytical potential of monitoring data. Other 
Page 14 of 25
Goff Le et al. Discover Water (2026) 6:110 
studies have emphasised the use of monitoring data as direct inputs for decision-support 
and monitoring network design. Batina and Šiljeg [ 11], for example, combined in-situ 
water quality measurements with a GIS-based multi-criteria decision analysis frame -
work to optimise the spatial configuration of monitoring stations in a coastal shallow 
lake. By integrating physicochemical parameters, anthropogenic pressures and meteo -
rological drivers, their approach illustrated that water quality monitoring systems can 
indeed support early-warning capabilities and management decisions under practical 
resource constraints.
Based on experiences in various countries, the reviewed literature supports the use of 
water quality monitoring systems for local-level decision-making. Moreover, the instal -
lation of networked sensors offers clear economic advantages. Postolache et al. [ 69] 
introduced a low-cost wireless system based on ZigBee technology to monitor surface 
water quality, highlighting its potential for scalable deployment. In this regard, the co-
design of low-cost underwater wireless sensor networks has proved to be a viable alter -
native for enhancing continuous monitoring systems without incurring the prohibitive 
costs associated with conventional commercial stations [ 105]. Another example is the 
use of high-frequency monitoring buoys, as implemented by Stevens et al. [ 90] in Lake 
Erie, North America, which provide a real-time application network for environmental 
parameters (e.g., temperature, dissolved oxygen, chlorophyll-a, phycocyanin and turbid -
ity). In aquaculture systems, the use of sensors also represents a cost-effective solution 
for supplying water quality monitoring systems. Petrea et al. [67] applied machine-learn-
ing techniques to develop algorithms embedded in soft sensors (models complement -
ing physical sensors), allowing the estimation of parameters such as pH, temperature, 
ammonia, enabling the prediction of nitrate events in aquaponics systems.
Machine-learning techniques can partially compensate for the lack of continuous in-
situ turbidity monitoring by reconstructing long-term satellite-derived products. Zou 
et al. [ 114] reconstructed a continuous turbidity time series (2004–2021) for Tonle Sap 
Lake by combining MODIS imagery with Random Forest and XGBoost models to fill 
multi-year data gaps in Copernicus products. Their results revealed strong seasonal 
and spatial patterns in turbidity, along with an overall increasing trend, indicating that 
anthropogenic factors exerted a stronger influence on turbidity dynamics than natural 
drivers or dam construction. Although this approach does not provide real-time infor -
mation, it underscored the potential of data-driven methods to recover long-term tur -
bidity dynamics in systems where permanent monitoring infrastructure is limited and 
can serve to support autonomous in-situ monitoring strategies.
3.6 Recommendations for implementing autonomous continuous sensors as an early 
warning system for real-time decision-making
In recent years, the availability of low-cost sensors and microprocessors at moderate 
prices has revolutionised field science and environmental observations, democratis -
ing access to data collection and analysis [ 32, 40, 105]. It has facilitated the modelling 
and integration of various variables that were previously costly and challenging to mea -
sure [ 73]. As noted by Borah et al. [ 15] and Rollo and Robin [ 81], model calibration 
and validation performance are evaluated through statistical metrics. Once validated, 
such models can be used for scenario analysis, forecasting the impacts of different 
Page 15 of 25
Goff Le et al. Discover Water (2026) 6:110 
environmental changes on turbidity levels, with acknowledging the limitations of low-
cost optical telemetry.
Three factors are widely recognised as having the potential to improve future water 
quality measurement systems: turbidity as a key parameter, the use of optical sensors 
and the temporal continuity of measurements. First, according to Robertson et al. [ 80], 
turbidity is the most cost-effective variable to measure. Integrating hydrological models 
with turbidity data can help predict when and under what conditions turbidity events 
may occur. As demonstrated by Rele et al. [ 77], combining low-cost sensor data with 
public databases can further enhance the prediction of turbidity in riverine systems.
Second, while Sirabahenda et al. [ 86] argue that optical sensors should be used to 
monitor changes in turbidity to ensure accurate water quality assessment, Lepistö et al. 
[49] emphasise that the resolution offered by these sensors is superior to the imperfect 
results obtained from interpreting satellite imagery, even when remote sensing is com -
bined with manual field measurements using optical and electrochemical sensors.
Third, as suggested by Zeng et al. [ 111], high-frequency and continuous time series of 
measurements seem essential to overcome the biases associated with seasonal variability 
of water quality parameters. To ensure the reliability of model predictions, long-term 
time series (exceeding three years) are required, as Robertson et al. [ 80] demonstrated 
in their load estimation using two regression models developed by combining the sur -
rogate model (continuous flow, seasonality and surrogates) and the UV-flow model (con-
tinuous Unit-Value flow and a seasonality factor). This study offers an interesting option 
for providing baseline information on water quality and loads, as well as allowing the 
calculation of historical and real-time daily loading. Similar findings were reported by 
de Vicente et al. [24], who showed that multi-temporal variability (inter-annual, seasonal 
and daily) in episodes of water quality deterioration, together with the unpredictability 
of their drivers –particularly in shallow lakes– highlights the need to increase the fre -
quency of data collection to obtain a more comprehensive long-term dataset. This sug -
gests that a sensor capable of capturing the entire water column would be very useful. 
However, given the challenges of maintenance and reliance on costly fixed infrastruc -
ture, it would be better to develop monitoring based on wireless sensor systems.
4 Discussion
4.1 Clean water is of vital importance
Monitoring the quality of hydrosystems is essential. Favouring the concept of ‘clean 
water scarcity’ (using both quantitative and qualitative criteria) over that of ‘classic water 
scarcity’ (using quantitative criteria alone) is projected to triple the number of river 
basins at risk worldwide between 2010 and 2050 [ 104]. About 40% of South America’s 
population could be living in a ‘clean water scarcity’ hotspot by 2050 (under the SSP5—
RCP8p5 scenario) [104]. Synthetic fertilisers and domestic waste are the main contribu -
tors to river pollution, due to the lack of wastewater collection networks and treatment 
infrastructures. Furthermore, from an economic perspective, Li et al. [ 51] have dem -
onstrated that water pollution will affect the economic resilience of foreign trade, as is 
currently happening in China’s industrialised provinces where productive activities are 
altering water quality, sometimes irreversibly. Indeed, by 2017, estimated economic 
losses due to the risk of water shortages had already doubled when water pollution was 
Page 16 of 25
Goff Le et al. Discover Water (2026) 6:110 
included in the evaluation criteria, with economic activities located downstream of 
catchment areas being the most affected.
4.2 The development of sensors and monitoring networks in Latin America
There is an urgent need for a robust assessment of pressures on aquatic ecosystems 
and for tools that democratise water quality information for bodies of water – this gap 
directly impacts societies. Given the limited understanding of the complex interactions 
between biological, physical, chemical, climatic and hydrological processes across time 
and space [1], a reliable assessment of water status is therefore of great importance. Iden-
tifying patterns of water quality variability can provide surface waters managers, local 
governments and scientists with valuable insights into the impacts of various human-
driven stressors [ 89]. The degradation of lakes, rivers and reservoirs worldwide threat -
ens the maintenance of essential ecosystem services. In that sense, Latin America is a 
key stakeholder with strong potential and growing interest in the development of public 
monitoring of its waterbodies.
The review revealed that acoustic and optical sensors are important for measuring 
water quality. On one hand, acoustic sensors often work in environments with high par -
ticle density and low visibility, where light-based sensors might falter. The use of sound 
waves allows penetration through turbid water, enabling measurements even in chal -
lenging conditions. Their robustness makes them suitable for deployment in harsh envi -
ronments where durability is essential. On the other hand, optical sensors, such as those 
based on Nephelometric Turbidity Units (NTU) and Formazin Attenuation Units (FAU), 
leverage light to measure turbidity [ 40]. Optical sensors are highly sensitive to small 
particles, providing accurate data that are crucial for detailed water quality assessment. 
One significant benefit of optical sensors over acoustic ones under specific environmen -
tal conditions is their cost-effectiveness, making them accessible for widespread use. 
Additionally, their precision and sensitivity make them particularly suitable for qual -
ity monitoring, where detailed and accurate data is paramount. Both sensor types have 
their place in water quality measurement. Optical sensors tend to be favoured for their 
high-resolution capabilities and affordability. In Latin America, where there is generally 
a lack of water quality information, combining both sensor types can offer a compre -
hensive approach to water quality monitoring, supporting the adoption of low-cost yet 
reliable equipment. Hydrological modeling serves as another critical tool for effective 
water quality management, as proposed by Arnold et al. [ 7], by simulating water quality 
fluctuations driven by hydrological processes at the catchment scale. These models can 
simulate sediment transport and localised pollution by incorporating diverse point and 
non-point sources into the equation. Furthermore, the integration of machine-learning 
with continuous monitoring—using in-situ sensors or satellite remote sensing data—has 
enhanced measurement precision.
The reviewed literature suggests that there is a need to increase the capacity to moni -
tor hydrosystems in different countries, taking advantage of more affordable equipment 
available on the market. Skarbøvik et al. [ 87] indicate that more harmonised methods 
are needed in monitoring programmes in northern Europe, including the development 
of coordinated measurement plans for various aquatic ecosystems and the integration 
of results into data networks that allow spatial comparisons of water quality variability. 
In contrast, the few cases presented in this review that concern Latin America tend to 
Page 17 of 25
Goff Le et al. Discover Water (2026) 6:110 
relate to isolated measurements that are not yet systematically even linked to ongoing 
monitoring programmes.
Political measures, such as the Water Framework Directive (WFD; 2000/60/EC) 
applied by the European Commission, promote the monitoring of watercourses, increas-
ing the amount of information available. National or regional monitoring programmes 
and policies are tools for decision-making and the mitigation and restoration of water 
quality in lakes and rivers. In Latin America, as shown in this review, multiple bodies of 
water show signs of deterioration. These cases indicate the need for measures to improve 
water quality in order to help to prevent contamination, turbidity and eutrophication.
4.3 Connecting citizen science and easy-to-use autonomous sensor networks for 
widespread water quality monitoring in Latin America
Several factors have influenced the growing number of citizen science initiatives focused 
on water quality monitoring since 2014 [ 61]. On one hand, institutional vulnerabili -
ties, high financial constraints and the rigidness of regional discharge legislations may 
explain the need to produce water quality data from local communities themselves using 
low-cost devices. On the other hand, this phenomenon could be linked to the rise of 
low-cost emerging technologies and the growing interest in this field [ 61]. The authors 
highlighted the challenge of implementing costly public water monitoring in low-income 
countries, for which citizen monitoring represents an interesting alternative. How -
ever, citizen science in hydrology appears more frequently documented in the Global 
North (North America and Europe) than in the Global South [ 27, 61]. This geographical 
imbalance represents a fundamental diagnostic finding of our review: the under-repre -
sentation of Latin American literature in major indexing databases does not reflect an 
absence of water quality crises, but rather a structural asymmetry in research funding 
and technological adoption. Unlike in the Global North, where data collected through 
participatory science is sometimes integrated by the authorities, citizen water monitor -
ing in the Global South is frequently carried out more to defend territories exposed to 
threats and to challenge public authorities, which, however, do not universally recognise 
these measurements [ 27]. In Latin America, this asymmetric relationship is aggravated 
by obsolete regulatory frameworks. For instance, the systematic delay in updating key 
environmental standards—such as Decree D.S. N°90/2000 in Chile—creates an institu -
tional vacuum where modern parameters of ecological integrity are ignored in official 
enforcement, forcing local populations to operate under high financial constraints to 
generate their own defensive environmental data.
Extractive industrial activities, especially mining, are the main concerns behind citizen 
water initiatives in Latin America [ 27]. The presence of cyanobacteria associated with 
nutrient enrichment and increased water turbidity in lakes is another source of inter -
est [29]. In contrast, despite expanding rural change processes, concerns over pollution 
risks to drinking water and recreational surface waters remain limited, particularly from 
direct wastewater discharge or septic tanks [8].
These projects primarily focus on measuring surface water and collecting chemical, 
physical and biological data [27]. The main parameters measured are clarity, nitrate, pH, 
dissolved oxygen, phosphate, temperature, macroinvertebrates, electrical conductiv -
ity and faecal coliform bacteria ( E. coli) [13]. Strip-type colorimetric tests are the most 
widely used despite their lack of accuracy, followed by the Secchi disk for turbidity and 
Page 18 of 25
Goff Le et al. Discover Water (2026) 6:110 
a series of test kits approved by accredited organisations [ 13]. These initiatives rely on 
manual sampling campaigns, which are often sporadic and constrained by the high cost 
of laboratory analyses. Consequently, these localized networks currently function as 
reactive, point-in-time responses to environmental contamination. The integration of 
low-cost continuous telemetry into these existing Latin American networks holds the 
potential to structurally transform these sporadic efforts. By overcoming the economic 
barrier of laboratory analysis and traditional station maintenance, continuous telemetry 
can elevate community-led monitoring into automated, high-frequency early-warning 
systems capable of capturing sudden eutrophication and turbidity events that currently 
escape both official state surveillance and intermittent citizen campaigns. However, [ 61] 
qualify that training and certification programmes make it possible to produce relatively 
reliable databases. The development of low-cost methods based on camera or smart -
phone-based systems and wireless sensor networks, such as Zigbee, seems promising for 
promoting continuous water citizen science in territories facing water quality challenges 
[61]. Therefore, citizen science networks are increasingly complementing government 
monitoring programs by addressing data deficiencies, and the integration of low-cost 
continuous telemetry could elevate these sporadic observations into systematic and reli -
able early-warning systems within the limits of low-cost optical telemetry.
4.4 Common way forward
Across the reviewed studies, and although some articles may have been inadvertently 
omitted, it became clear that maintaining water quality requires a multifaceted approach 
combining advanced monitoring technologies, hydrological modelling and proactive 
management strategies. Although challenging, a widely supported path forward involves 
integrating low‐cost, autonomous sensor networks with traditional monitoring methods 
and remote sensing techniques. This integrated approach presents a range of advantages 
and limitations that merit careful consideration.
However, moving beyond a descriptive synthesis requires a critical evaluation of the 
socio-technical friction points inherent to these hybrid frameworks. The transition 
toward low-cost environmental observation is not merely a matter of technological 
substitution, but a structural shift in how environmental uncertainty is managed and 
validated.
4.4.1 Advantages
 • Availability of high-frequency data: While manual sampling may be limited by 
seasonal weather constraints [56], autonomous sensors provide high-frequency data, 
enabling early detection of water quality changes year-round and the development 
of timely alert systems [ 49, 113]. This high temporal resolution effectively shifts the 
paradigm from reactive monitoring to anticipatory limnological management
 • Cost-effectiveness: Low-cost sensors reduce reliance on expensive and labour-
intensive field campaigns, making extensive monitoring—especially in remote or 
under-resourced regions—more feasible [69].
 • Enhanced decision-making: Real-time data integrated into hydrological models 
supports informed management and regulatory decisions. It also opens opportunities 
for citizen science initiatives that can empower local communities.
Page 19 of 25
Goff Le et al. Discover Water (2026) 6:110 
 • Scalability and flexibility: Combining sensor data with remote sensing and modelling 
enables assessments over wide spatial and temporal scales, essential for developing 
sustainable public policies and legislation [ 1] to address both local and global water 
quality challenges.
4.4.2 Limitations:
 • Calibration and data quality issues: Despite their advantages, low-cost sensors require 
regular calibration and validation to ensure data accuracy, given seasonal variations 
and spatial heterogeneity [ 49, 87], before they can be used for robust hydrological 
models, for example. Their wider adoption also depends on demonstrating 
performance and quantifying measurement uncertainty, as they are often perceived 
as less reliable than conventional monitoring methods. Standardized protocols, 
such as EN 17075+A1:2023 and ISO 15839:2005, provide frameworks for evaluating 
sensor performance under real-world conditions. From a critical standpoint, the 
trade-off of spatial flexibility in low-cost arrays is a continuous vulnerability to 
operational drift. Biological fouling—such as biofilm accretion on optical windows—
and lens sedimentation cause progressive signal attenuation that requires systematic, 
often resource-intensive field maintenance, partially offsetting the initial economic 
advantage of these devices.
 • Technical and logistical challenges: Deploying and maintaining autonomous 
sensor networks can be challenging, particularly in harsh or remote environments. 
Challenges include ensuring continuous power supply, data transmission integrity 
and sensor longevity. In addition, integrating data from various sources (manual 
sampling, satellite imagery, in-situ sensors) into unified, robust hydrological models 
is complex and demands significant technical expertise, which is frequently lacking. 
Moreover, prolonged use of the sensors leads to biological fouling, which causes 
progressive data drift and signal attenuation. Therefore, to ensure the reliability 
and continuity of autonomous systems, they should be equipped with antifouling 
mechanisms, such as wipers or compressed air purges.
 • Policy and coordination needs: While the successful implementation of these 
technologies requires updated technical guidelines and the coordination of multiple 
stakeholders, including water utilities, local governments and research institutions, 
systemic institutional barriers continue to limit the integration of community-
validated citizen science data into official regulatory frameworks.
A review of this literature provides a deeper understanding that the most promising 
strategy is to use the complementary strengths of traditional and emerging water quality 
monitoring systems, for example, training local communities to employ low-cost sen -
sors. Nevertheless, several challenges need to be addressed, such as sensor calibration, 
ensuring robust data integration and nurturing collaborative governance frameworks. 
To enhance the long-term reliability and interoperability of low-cost sensor networks, 
future implementations should incorporate regular validation procedures, rigorous 
site-specific calibration protocols and maintenance strategies to mitigate the effects of 
biofouling, lens sedimentation and seasonal temperature variability. In addition, the 
development of harmonised data-processing frameworks is recommended to improve 
data comparability and facilitate the integration of citizen-generated observations into 
Page 20 of 25
Goff Le et al. Discover Water (2026) 6:110 
regional and public monitoring networks. By engaging diverse stakeholders, it becomes 
possible to develop effective early warning systems and sustainable water management 
practices. This integrated approach not only enhances scientific understanding of water 
quality dynamics in monitored ecosystems but also equip local decision-makers with 
actionable insights to safeguard critical hydrosystems amid increasing environmental 
pressures.
5 Conclusion
Water quality is increasingly threatened by population growth, urbanisation, land-use 
change and climate-driven pressures. This review has synthesised the core literature on 
the evolution of water quality monitoring systems applied to aquatic ecosystems affected 
by turbidity and eutrophication, drawing primarily on peer-reviewed studies indexed in 
Web of Science. The analysed studies consistently highlight growing environmental and 
public-health risks associated with declining water quality.
In line with the hypotheses guiding this review, published monitoring practice in 
lakes and rivers remains dominated by grab sampling campaigns and satellite-based 
approaches, while continuous autonomous in-situ monitoring is comparatively less 
represented, except in rivers. At the same time, the literature reveals a persistent gap in 
accessible real-time information, particularly in regions with limited resources, where 
monitoring often depends on specialised technical capacity and uneven station cover -
age. Despite the increasing availability of affordable sensors capable of generating high-
frequency datasets, their systematic integration into coordinated monitoring networks 
remains limited. These insights suggest that the principal barrier to improving monitor -
ing is no longer technological availability but the institutional and operational integra -
tion of autonomous systems into structured observation frameworks. From a research 
perspective, the evidence supports deploying sensors both at basin scale and at strate -
gically selected locations within water bodies to capture spatial variability and identify 
pollution sources. Such configurations can strengthen model calibration, improve early-
warning capacity and enhance long-term environmental assessment.
To ensure future water security worldwide, two key public policy measures appear 
promising. On the one hand, the deployment of sensor networks in surface waters is 
crucial for preserving ecological processes while supporting socio-economic, produc -
tive, recreational and educational activities that depend on reliable water resources. On 
the other hand, the development of autonomous and participatory monitoring systems 
involving local communities could significantly improve real-time decision-making and 
promote more adaptive water governance.
In looking ahead, the decisive question will be whether systems can transform increas-
ingly available high-resolution data into effective environmental action alongside cur -
rent monitoring frameworks. Global challenges of water quality will thus require not 
only an effective data-to-decision chain, but also a broader transition from fragmented 
monitoring architectures towards more coordinated and responsive governance.
