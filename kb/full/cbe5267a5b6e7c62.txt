Modeling and optimization of adsorption parameters 
for hexavalent chromium removal from wastewater 
using Box-Behnken design and response surface 
methodology
Tarique Ahmad  · Ubhat Ali · Mohammad Ehtisham Khan
Received: 7 May 2026 / Accepted: 4 September 2026
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract
Chromium discharged from industries has the potential to severely damage the environment. This study focuses 
on the optimization of adsorption parameters for successful elimination of hexavalent chromium, i.e., Cr(VI) 
from wastewater by utilizing activated carbon. The study used an economical Box-Behnken Design (BBD) and 
response surface methodology (RSM), as a novel approach to design the experiments for pH varied from 2 
to 6, adsorbent dosage and contact time ranging from 0.8 to 1.6 g/L and 30–150 min respectively. Thereafter, 
using BBD-RSM, the adsorption parameters were modelled, and optimized to get maximum Cr(VI) removal. A 
second-order regression model was developed, which predicted a removal of 98.6% with a desirability function 
of 0.989 at the optimum pH of 2, contact time of 150 min, and the adsorbent dose of 1.2 g/L. Kinetic studies 
showed that a pseudo-second-order model, with R² of 0.999 fitted well, indicating chemisorption. Additionally, 
equilibrium studies conformed to a Langmuir isotherm with an R² value greater than 0.98, indicating mono -
layer adsorption onto the surface of activated carbon. Hence, these models, combined with the optimization of 
adsorption parameters, enabled high removal efficiencies of Cr(VI) from wastewater and a reliable prediction 
of adsorption behavior.

Introduction
Chromium is considered as toxic heavy metal, although widely utilized in industries, such as, leather, tanning, 
dyeing, electroplating, pigment production, metallurgy etc. Kerur 1,. As a result, large volumes of chromium-
laden wastewater are discharged into the environment, posing serious threats to ecological and human health 1,2. 
Chromium has multiple oxidation states; however, the utmost common and stable states include bivalent (Cr(II), 
trivalent (Cr(III), and hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI). Cr (II) is found in various compounds, Cr (III) is an essential 
trace element for glucose and lipid metabolism while Cr(VI) is highly toxic, carcinogenic, and mobile in aquatic

systems, making it a critical pollutant of concern 2–5. Chromium enters inside a human body via respiratory and 
digestive system, as well as through sensory organs. The toxicity of Cr(VI) may cause bleeding from nose, respi-
ratory tract infections, skin rashes and irritation, and lung carcinoma 4,6,7. According to the International Agency 
for Research on Cancer (IARC), Cr(VI) is identified as the most potent carcinogen. World Health Organization 
(WHO) has set the limit for Cr(VI) as 0.05 mg/L, whereas Cr(III) should be below 2 mg/L4.
For the sequestration of heavy metals, several methods are employed, including chemical precipitation, coagu-
lation, adsorption, membrane filtration, microfiltration, ultrafiltration, nanofiltration, ion exchange, reverse osmo-
sis, and advanced electrochemical processes, such as electrodialysis and electrocoagulation 1. The elimination 
of chrome is also achieved through adsorption, chemical precipitation, membrane separations, ion exchanges, 
electrocoagulation and electrodialysis1,8. However, most of these methods are expensive, energy intensive, and 
produce toxic byproducts1,9. As a result, adsorption as a wastewater treatment method has become more popular 
in recent years, particularly activated carbon adsorption due to its lower energy and maintenance costs, simplicity, 
and reliability10,11.
Although numerous studies have reported Cr(VI) removal using activated carbon, limited work has system -
atically optimized the process using a Box-Behnken Design (BBD) coupled with Response Surface Methodol -
ogy (RSM) for activated carbon. The present study addresses this gap by evaluating the combined effect of key 
operational parameters such as pH, adsorbent dose and contact time on removal efficiency, and applying BBD-
RSM to statistically optimize these parameters. BBD efficiently evaluates interactions among process variables 
with fewer experimental runs by avoiding experimental combinations at extreme factor levels, thereby reduc -
ing experimental cost and improving statistical reliability 12; Nair et al., 2015). Thus, a quadratic model was 
developed and validated to predict and maximize the Cr(VI) removal. Furthermore, the adsorption behavior was 
evaluated through kinetic and isotherm analyses. The key objectives of this study are therefore to determine the 
optimum operating conditions for Cr(VI) removal from wastewater using commercial-grade activated carbon, 
and to establish a reliable, statistically robust model that can be extended to real industrial wastewater treatment.
Methodology
Chemicals and equipment used
Activated carbon of a commercial grade was used as an absorbent. Cr(VI) stock solution of 1000 mg/L was pre-
pared by dissolving 2.83 g of analytical-grade potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇, Merck) in 1 L of distilled water. 
pH measurement was done by using pH meter (Hanna, USA). Hydrochloric acid (HCl) and sodium hydroxide 
(NaOH) were used for maintaining the pH. Working solutions having initial concentration of 10 mg/L were pre-
pared by diluting the stock solution with double-distilled water. After adsorption, the residual chromium concen-
tration was measured using UV-VIS (Ultraviolet-Visible) spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, USA) using 
colorimetric method as described by Sanchez-Hachair and Hofmann13.
Design of experiments (DoE) for chromium removal
Firstly, preliminary studies were conducted to decide the range of independent variables i.e. pH of the solution, 
adsorbent dosage and contact time. Initial Cr(VI) concentration was fixed to 10 mg/L throughout the experimen-
tation. During the preliminary studies, pH of the solution was varied from 2 to 7, whereas contact time varied 
over the period of 30 to 180 min and absorbent dosing ranged from 0.2 to 1.4 g/L. Results of preliminary studies 
are shown in Figs. 1 and 2. Based on the inference drawn from the preliminary studies, optimization study was 
planned and carried out. BBD was used for the design of experiments for optimization study. BBD was chosen 
for its efficiency, rotatability and lesser experimental runs as compared to that of CCD (central composite design) 
or full factorial design. Hence, it will reduce experimental cost and improve the statistical reliability. The range

Results and discussion
Effect of pH, adsorbent dose and contact time
Cr(VI) adsorption onto activated carbon is strongly influenced by the pH of medium, dosage of the adsorbent and 
its contact time with adsorbate. Preliminary experiments were done using a one-factor-at-a-time approach. Ini -
tially, experiments were carried out to get the idea of equilibrium contact time. It was observed that equilibrium 
reached after the contact time of about 180 min. Therefore, by varying the pH and adsorbent dosage and provid-
ing a contact time of 180 min, Cr(VI) removal was assessed. Figure 1 shows the role of pH and adsorbent dosage 
on the removal of Cr(VI) from aqueous solution. It was observed that pH of the solution has a substantial influ -
ence on the removal of Cr(VI) from the solution (Acut et al., 2024; 17; Bansal et al., 2009; Juturu et al., 2024 18;. 
Maximum removal efficiencies (above 90%) were found under strong acidic condition (pH 2), and removal 
efficiencies fell below 20% at pH levels above 6. Many studies supported the sharp decline in adsorption as the 
pH is increased19,20. This decline in the Cr(VI) removal upon an upsurge in the pH values can be attributed to the 
fact that the activated carbon surface is protonated and positively charged at low pH, thus promoting strong elec-
trostatic attraction with chromate/dichromate anions (e.g. HCrO₄⁻, Cr₂O₇²⁻) 20,21. However, at higher pH levels, 
the hydroxide ions compete for adsorption-active sites, making carbon surface negatively charged and causing 
Cr(VI) ions repulsion, thus decreasing adsorption20,22. Furthermore, Cr(VI) may undergo hydrolysis or reduction 
to Cr(III) under higher pH conditions, which further affects the adsorption behaviour23.

Figure 1 also elucidates that increasing the adsorbent dosage increases the removal efficiencies at the same pH. 
Further, Fig. 1 clearly illustrates that as the dosage increases from 0.2 to 1.2 g/L, removal rate increases over 61% 
to 98% at the optimum pH value of 2. At a lower adsorbent dose, each added gram of activated carbon provides 
new active sites, and therefore largely improves the Cr(VI) uptake 24. However, beyond an optimum dose, fur -
ther increases in adsorbent dosage result in a minimal increase in Cr(VI) adsorption, likely due to site diffusion 
and particle accumulation reducing the effective surface area (Kano et al., 2022). Adsorption increased notably 
while raising the dose at pH 2–5, but plateaued at higher doses, which is more clearly visible from Fig. 2. Similar 
behaviour was also observed by Alouiz et al.25,, where removal rises as dose increases until reaching a near maxi-
mum value, after which additional dose gives little benefit. Overdosing may even reduce unit adsorption capacity 
(mg/g) due to site redundancies and diffusion limitations. Kumar et al., (2024) has explained the same that if the 
mass of the adsorbent increases relative to a fixed concentration of adsorbate, the ratio of adsorbate molecules 
to available surface sites decreases. Consequently, many active binding sites remain vacant and unsaturated at 
equilibrium, thus reducing the removal efficiency.
Preliminary experiments and literature both indicate that Cr(VI) removal is highly effective when pH of the 
solution is 210,26. Therefore, in the next level of preliminary experiments, pH was fixed (pH = 2) and removal of 
Cr(VI) was assessed over the varying adsorbent dosage and contact time, as shown in Fig. 2. Cr(VI) adsorption 
onto activated carbon is spontaneous in nature and maximum removal occurs in the first 30 min due to abundant 
vacant sites. As time proceeds, the rate slows as fewer active sites remain, and intraparticle diffusion becomes 
dominant until equilibrium is achieved. In many earlier studies, the equilibrium has been reached between 120 
and 240 min, depending on the adsorbent and conditions 27. In one H₃PO₄-treated palm-frond activated carbon 
study, removal improved over 180 min, reaching ~ 99%23. The slowdown in the later phase is attributed to mass 
transfer resistance, and once all reachable active sites are occupied, no further net adsorption occurs. Further -
more, Fig. 2 clearly shows that at a particular pH, the removal efficiencies are significantly impacted by adsorbent 
dose as well as contact time. Increasing the adsorbent dosage reduces the time required for equilibrium attain -
ment, and beyond a certain threshold limit, increasing the adsorbent dose yields the same removal results, as the 
Cr(VI) may be insufficient to utilize all available sites. Both the factors, adsorbent dosage and contact time, found 
critical for optimizing Cr(VI) removal; therefore, a careful balance is required for achieving maximum removal 
efficiency (Kano et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2024).

Optimization and model validation
Optimization of the adsorption process parameters was done with the help of Design Expert software, employ -
ing RSM integrated with a desirability function approach. The desirability function quantitatively converts each 
response variable into dimensionless scale ranging from 0 (undesirable response) to 1 (fully desirable response), 
allowing for multi-response optimization under defined experimental constraints 31. In order to set the desired 
goal for each variable and response, Design Expert software provided five options, including within range, equal 
to, target, minimize, and maximize. Thus, for best possible response, the optimization was done by assigning 
the variable criteria as ‘in range’ for pH and contact time, ‘target = 1.2’ for adsorbent dose, and ‘maximize’ for 
Cr(VI) removal efficiency and by giving same importance and weightage to all the variables. The optimal operat-
ing parameters can be derived from the second-order model. An optimal condition which can be easily verified

For equilibrium isotherms, the experimentally obtained adsorption results have been analyzed fitted over Lang-
muir and the Freundlich isotherm models at pH = 2. The Langmuir isotherm considers the monolayer adsorption 
of Cr (VI) metal ions over the surface of activated carbon, whereas Freundlich isotherm assumes multilayer 
adsorption of ions on heterogeneous surfaces. A better fitting isotherm model has been selected based on its coef-
ficient of determination, R². A linearized Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm can be seen in Fig. 7. The Langmuir 
and Freundlich isotherm parameters are shown in Table 6. Both the adsorption isotherms fitted well and can 
describe the adsorption equilibrium; however, the R 2of Langmuir model is greater than 0.98, thus fitting better 
than the Freundlich model. Hammad 23, also reported that the activated carbon produced from palm fronds fit -
ted well to PSO kinetic and Langmuir isotherm models, confirming monolayer adsorption and the presence of 
reasonably homogeneous surfaces and well-defined adsorption sites. In some studies, the Freundlich model also 
reported to fit well, when the adsorbent has more heterogeneous surface properties or when there is multilayer 
adsorption but tends to show lower R² values compared to Langmuir. Freundlich fits are also allowable but less 
descriptive of monolayer saturation behavior40.

Conclusions
Chromium pollution is causing environmental damage. The study has demonstrated that activated carbon is 
effective in removing Cr(VI) from wastewater/aqueous solutions. Preliminary experimental results clearly indi -
cated that pH, contact time and adsorbent dose have significantly influenced the adsorption process. In general, 
Cr(VI) removal is higher when pH is lowered however, adsorbent dosage and its contact time also influence the 
Cr(VI) removal, but up to a certain extent. The ANOV A results of the developed quadratic model for predict -
ing the Cr(VI) removal specified that the model is significant and can be used for the optimization of adsorp -
tion conditions. At an optimum pH of 2, contact time ~ 150 min, and an adsorbent dose ~ 1.2 g/L, the developed 
model predicted Cr(VI) removal of 98.6%, whereas the experimentally observed result was also close to pre -
dicted value. Kinetic studies have shown that the PSO model has described the adsorption process in a better 
way, with R² ~ 0.999, indicating chemisorption as the dominant mechanism. The equilibrium study data better 
fitted to Langmuir isotherm, with R² greater than 0.98, suggesting monolayer adsorption. Hence, these models, 
combined with parameter optimization (pH, dose, contact time), enable high removal efficiencies and reliable 
prediction of adsorption behavior. Although, the study has promising Cr(VI) removal performance, but limited 
to synthetic wastewater under controlled laboratory conditions, which may not fully capture the influence of 
competing ions and the complexity of actual industrial wastewater containing competing ions and other contami-
nates. Adsorption–desorption and long-term regeneration behavior should be evaluated further to establish the 
operational stability and economic feasibility of repeated use. Therefore, future research should evaluate the pro-
posed approaches using real industrial wastewater, investigate adsorbent regeneration and long-term reusability, 
and focus on safe management of Cr-containing spent adsorbent and regeneration solutions to establish practical 
feasibility and sustainability.
