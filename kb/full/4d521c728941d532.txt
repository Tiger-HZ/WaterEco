Call for the mainstreaming of eutrophication
in global water assessments and policies
Olli Varis & Dandan Zhao
 Check for updates
This comment prompts scholars to include
eutrophication more often to water quality
assessments as it is globally one of the major water-
related environmental problems but remains
underrepresented in contemporary assessments,
water policies and international targets such as the
United Nations Sustainable Development Goals
(SDGs). What we scholars can do is to mainstream
eutrophication in our global water assessments,
and that way ram it to a higher position in political
agendas. The recent advances in global nutrient
models and datasets, as well as Earth observation
approaches, allow novel horizons in this regard.
Now is the time to scrutinize these novel
opportunities. Our take to this quest here is to
develop a model protocol that produces gridded
eutrophication maps from total nitrogen and total
phosphorus data. We also developed analogous
methods for biochemical oxygen demand and total
dissolved solids.
Elevated attention to eutrophication is needed in research
and policy
Eutrophication is one of the major water quality problems of surface waters
globally1– 3, causing massive deterioration of aquatic ecosystems, habitats,
and biodiversity as well as remarkable environmental nuisance to human
water uses. Eutrophication stands for enrichment of plant nutrients, most
importantly nitrogen and phosphorus, resulting in increased biomass
production4,5. Eutrophication is a natural phenomenon, but human activ-
ities, such as discharge of waste and wastewater, and land use change result
in increased erosion and leaching of nutrients. This greatly accelerates the
input of nutrients to waters across the world, causing nuisance in terms of
excessive biomass of algae and vegetation6. This, in turn, is prone to lead to
an enhanced degradation of biomass, often leading to hypoxia. Warming of
climate enhances the severity of these problems.
Whereas eutrophication has been identiﬁed and recognized as a major
problem for over a century 7,8, and being recently highlighted within the
Earth system boundaries context 9, it keeps worsening 9.T h i sd e s p i t eo f

Appreciating the progress of the SDG 6.2 on wastewater treatment
and the SDG 6.3 on halving the proportion of untreated wastewater 13,
Jones et al.14 call for more ambitious development goals than those we see
at present to curtail the further deterioration of water quality. To bear out
this concern, eutrophication of freshwaters is gaining only occasional
mention in UN Agenda 2030 reports, and is absent from the SDGs 13.
Alike, eutrophication has been virtually missing from global freshwater
models and related assessments, although analyses and scenarios are now
emerging15– 17, with initiatives to incorporate eutrophication into global
data and model portals such as the ISIMIP 18. At the same time, we see a
progress in estimating the level of eutrophication from Earth observation
data19– 21. Hopefully, these pioneering works will attract others within the
modelling community to pay elevated attention to eutrophication since
rigorous approaches are needed.
In general, the key factors that contribute to freshwater eutrophication
are total nitrogen (TN) and total phosphorus (TP), being the most
important drivers of the abundance of phytoplankton and the eutrophic
c o n d i t i o no faw a t e rb o d y22. With growing number of gridded datasets and
models on concentrations of TN and TP in surface inland waters that have
recently become available6,18,23– 25, making it possible to understand and
quantify the global patterns and dynamics, their temporal evolution and
driving factors of the fate of these key nutrients in aquatic systems on higher
spatial resolution and scales. Thus, i nspired by increasing water-related
nutrient studies and datasets, this s tudy makes this contribution by pro-
posing a eutrophication model protocol that is implementable to gridded
global and regional models employing TN and TP data. Such datasets allow
a building ground for relatively straightforward ways to assess
eutrophication.
We propose one based on the N and P co-limitation concept, and not
solely on P limitation as has been common thus far17. It produces gridded
eutrophication estimates using any spatial TN and TP datasets. We used the
data of van Vliet et al.25 on a 0.5° × 0.5° resolution for the 2000– 2010 period
to investigate and illustrate how to create water quality classiﬁcation-based
eutrophication maps globally.
We employ the water quality classiﬁcation scheme, an approach that
has mainstreamed policy documents in eutrophication over decades26– 28.I t
is based on the Trophic State Index (TSI) concept29, in which the waters are
classiﬁed according to their primary production level. We derive this index
from TN and TP concentrations as has been done in the above-mentioned
documents, but today it can also be derived from Chlorophyll-a con-
centration observable visually, e.g., from remote sensing data21. TSI instead
of concentrations makes sense because ecosystem response to increased P
and N concentrations (as well as many other pollutants), and consequently

The three-step protocol for eutrophication modelling
From TN and TP concentrations to eutrophication classiﬁcation. First,
the TN and TP concentrations are transferred into eutrophication classes
(Fig. 1) using TSI29. Typically, the classes used are ultra-oligotrophic (very
low), oligotrophic (low), mesotrophic (intermediate), eutrophic (high),
and hypertrophic (very high), implying an increasing level of primary
production and thus eutrophication. The eutrophication literature of the
past several decades abounds with various water quality
classiﬁcations26,27,30. They vary across various agencies and geographic
locations, and speci ﬁc classi ﬁcations exist for numerous fractions of
nutrients and different types of water bodies, such as rivers, streams, deep
lakes, shallow water bodies and more31.
Nevertheless, many water quality guidelines for policy and manage-
ment purposes are reasonably consistent with one another. Based on the
most important of them26– 28,30, we produced the values presented in Table1
for the purposes of our study.

Examples. As an example of the use of the protocol, we estimated the
global exposure of humans to eutrophication by continent38, by country38,
and by river basin 39 (Fig. 3). This yields an estimate of 73% of humanity
being exposed to eutrophication. Asia, as the most populated continent,
dominates with 60% of the world’s total exposure. In turn, Europe has the
world’s highest population share of those exposed (85%), Africa having
only 51%. China and India are by margin the countries with biggest
exposure. 72% of the world’s population live in TP-limited areas, whereas
28% live in TN-limited ones. We forward other eutrophication exposure
analyses to further studies. They could includeﬁsh abundance and species,
threatened species, biodiversity hotspots, and others.

Discussion
Within the water quality management and policy domain, the use of water
quality classiﬁcations that estimate the harm caused by deteriorated water
quality to humans and human uses of water has been the standard approach.
For eutrophication, such an approach is the TSI 26,27,29– 31. We developed a
TSI-based method for producing water quality classi ﬁcation values for
eutrophication and present also analogous schemes for BOD and TDS as
Supplementary Information. Our water quality index datasets can readily be
used to estimate the nuisance, exposure and risk of humans and human uses
to those water quality problems. Such a straightforward methodology has
not been available before. By employing TSI, the methodology is compatible
with standardized policy approaches, and, besides, with the evolving Earth
observation-based approaches, typically relying also on TSI19– 21.
The co-limitation concept allows consideration of other fractions of N
and P besides TN and TP. For instance, the orthophosphate would be a
remarkably better estimation for b iologically available P than TP 3,22,b u t
gridded datasets of it are not available on a global scale. Similar limitations
occur to other fractions of N and P, such as the often-used lumped indicators
DIN and DIP and their ratio36. The inclusion of other limiting factors, such
as Silica, can be included with the same logic as what we have shown here,
although we would rather pay attention to looking closer at N-ﬁxing Cya-
nobacteria; their co-limitation scheme would differ notably from that of
other phytoplankton42. For the inclusion of factors such as water tempera-
ture and irradiance, we would recommend employing approaches such as
the classical multiplicative use of the Michaelis-Menten kinetics, frequently
used in water quality models42. A discussion of the reliability of our protocol
is presented in the Supplementary Information. Thus far, it is precondi-
tioned by the reliability of the TN and TP data, but we recommend devel-
oping hybrid approaches linking nutrient concentration datasets with Earth
observations to allow more rigorous uncertainty analyses.
Compared with traditional approaches that rely on in situ sampling of
water quality parameters to calculate the TSI, the widespread availability of
Earth observation data such as remote sensing19– 21, together with the rapid
growth of publicly accessible water quality databases6,23– 25, effectively over-
comes many limitations of manual monitoring, including sparse sampling
sites, high costs, and the difﬁculty of obtaining long-term continuous time
series. This allows now to quantitatively assess the degree of eutrophication
across different spatial and tempora l scales. In this context, given that
eutrophication is one of the major water-related risk factors1– 3,41, affecting
large populations and posing substantial ecological and public health risks,
incorporating eutrophication indicators into global water quality assess-
ment frameworks, including SDG 6, is of great importance for improving
water quality monitoring capacity, strengthening water risk identiﬁcation
systems, and enhancing access to clean water.
