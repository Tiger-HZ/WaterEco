Role of nano Zn and Mn on growth, productivity, quality,
soil and leaf nutrient status of turmeric (Curcuma longa
L.)
Anasuya Sil1, Jitesh Kumar Hore2
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/26174693.2026.v10.i9Sx.10141
A BSTRACT
A field experiment was conducted at horticultural research station, Mondouri, Bidhan Chandra Krishi
Viswavidyalaya, India, to assess the effect of nano and non nano micronutrients on turmeric. The whole
experiment was laid out in randomized block design with twelve treatments and three replications con-
taining three different levels (0.3, 0.4 and 0.5%) of nano and non nano zinc and manganese along with
full recommended dose of fertilizers (N: P2O5: K2O @ 150:60:150 kg ha−1) at 45 and 90 days after
planting. Foliar application of 0.5% nano-Zn and 0.4% nano-Mn (T11) recorded maximum plant height
(112.68 cm), leaf length (56.53 cm) and breadth (14.35 cm) at 120 DAP, projected yield (30.84 t ha −1),
dry recovery (23.28%) and total curcumin yield (433.27 kg ha−1). In contrast T2 (RDF + 0.4% ZnSO4
and MnSO4) recorded minimum projected yield (25.54 t ha−1), dry recovery (20.91%) and total cur-
cumin content (267.36 kg ha−1). Plants grown under nano micronutrients recorded better leaf NPK and
available soil nutrient status also. The study demonstrated that nano-micronutrient application effec-
tively improves growth, productivity, quality, and soil and leaf nutrient status of turmeric.
Keywords: Dry recovery, Nano zinc, Nano manganese, Total curcumin, Turmeric, Yield
Introduction
Turmeric (Curcuma longa L.), a perennial rhizomatous herb belongs to the family Zingiberaceae, originated
from Southeast Asia. It is widely recognized as Herbal Gold owing to significant medicinal, nutritional and
economic value. The crop is cultivated primarily for its underground modified stem (rhizome), which con-
tain several bioactive constituents, including curcumin, oleoresin, essential oils and phenolic compounds.
Among these, curcumin imparts the characteristic yellow colour and possesses diverse medicinal properties
such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, anticancer, hepatoprotective and immunomodulatory
activities (Nguyen et al., 2024) [1]. Turmeric is extensively utilized in traditional medicine, pharmaceutical
formulations, nutraceutical products, food preservation, cosmetics and as a natural colorant. India is the lead-
ing producer, consumer and exporter of turmeric (Sil et al., 2026) [2]. Despite its commercial importance,
sustaining high productivity and superior quality remains challenging due to inefficient nutrient management
practices.
Being a long duration crop, turmeric requires heavy and balanced nutrition for optimal growth and produc-
tivity. While considerable emphasis has been placed on primary nutrients, the importance of micronutrients
has often been overlooked. Among various micronutrients zinc and manganese regulate key enzymatic, pho-
tosynthetic and metabolic processes. The efficiency of conventional micronutrient is frequently constrained
by fixation, leaching, precipitation, and other soil related losses, limiting nutrient availability to plants.
Nanotechnology offers a promising alternative through the development of nano-fertilizers possessing high
surface area, enhanced reactivity, controlled nutrient release, and improved nutrient use efficiency while re-
ducing environmental losses. However, information regarding the combined effects of nano zinc and nano
manganese on turmeric is limited. Therefore, the present study was undertaken to evaluate the influence of
nano Zn and Mn on growth, productivity, quality, soil and leaf nutrient status in turmeric.
Materials and Methods
The experiment was carried out during 2022-23 and 2023-24 at the Horticultural Research Station,
Mondouri, under the Faculty of Horticulture, Bidhan Chandra Krishi Viswavidyalaya, West Bengal. The site
is situated at 23°05′ N latitude and 89°00′ E longitude, with an altitude of 9.75 m above MSL. The experi-
mental soil belongs to the Gangetic New Alluvial Plains and possesses a sandy clay loam texture with pH of
6.62 (Jackson, 1967) [3], organic carbon 0.67% (Walkey and Black, 1934) [4], containing 229.71 kg ha−1
available N (by modified Kjeldahl’s method), 18.53 kg ha−1 available P (Olsen et al., 1954) [5] and
210.65 kg ha−1 available K (by flame photometer). The experiment was laid out in randomized block design

with 12 treatments and 3 replications (Table 1) containing 3 different
levels (0.3, 0.4 and 0.5%) of non-nano and nano zinc and manganese
at 45 and 90 DAP. Seed rhizomes (25-30 g) of variety Suguna were
planted in a raised bed of 15 cm with a diameter of 3.0 m × 1.0 m at a
spacing of 30 cm × 25 cm which accommodate 40 plants per bed alto-
gether 1440 plants in total 36 plots. In both years, planting was done
in the middle of June, while harvesting was completed by the end of
January. Before planting seed rhizomes were treated with blitox @ 4
g L−1 for 30 minutes followed by shade drying to avoid any fungal in-
fection. Full recommended fertilizer dose of NPK @ 150:60:150 kg
ha−1 (Medda et al., 2003) [6] was supplied through urea, single super
phosphate and muriate of potash to all the treatments. 40% nitrogen
along with the full dose of phosphorus were applied as a basal along
with FYM @ 25 t ha−1, while the remaining nitrogen and potassium
were applied in two split doses at 45 and 90 DAP. The irrigation was
done immediately after planting and rest of other irrigations were
given based on soil moisture availability. Mulching was done with
paddy straw @ 10 t ha−1 just after planting and additional mulching
was provided at 45 and 90 DAP using paddy straw at 5 t ha−1 and
earthing up was carried out prior to the second and third mulching
operations.
Foliar spray of nano and non-nano Zn and Mn (Table 2) were pre-
pared freshly by dissolving the required quantity of nano and non
nano micro nutrients in distilled water according to the treatments.
During this time 2-3 drops of sticker (balanstick) were added in the
solution to enhance leaf retention. Spraying was uniformly done dur-
ing morning hours until near runoff to ensure efficient nutrient uptake
while minimizing drift and environmental losses.
Observations
Growth attributes such as plant height (cm), number of tillers and
leaves per plant, leaves length (cm) and breadth (cm) were recorded
at 120 DAP from 5 randomly selected plants of each plot. Projected
yield was estimated from the total harvested plot yield, considering
75% effective cropped area and 25% area occupied by pathways and
irrigation channels, using the formula: Projected yield (t ha−1) = plot
yield (kg 3 m−2) × 2500 (Anonynous, 1995) [7].
Dry recovery was determined by drying 100 g of freshly chopped rhi-
zomes in a mechanical dryer at 55 °C until constant weight and ex-
pressed as a percentage. Total curcumin yield was computed by fol-
lowing the formula:
Total curcumin (kg ha−1) = Dry recovery × Projected yield × curcumin percentage
Available nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium were estimated using
the alkaline permanganate, Olsen’s extraction and flame photometric
methods respectively and expressed as kg ha−1. For leaf nutrient
analysis, the third fully expanded leaf was sampled at 120 DAP,
washed in distilled water, oven-dried at 55-65 °C and ground.
Nitrogen content was determined by the Kjeldahl method, while
phosphorus and potassium were analysed by using colorimeter and
flame photometer respectively and was expressed in percentage
(Jackson, 1967) [3].
Data analysis
Recorded data was analysed by ANOVA using Microsoft Excel 2016
and OPSTAT online as outlined by Gomez et al. (1984) [8]. The treat-
ment significance and critical differences assessed at 5% probability
following the procedure described in the statistical tables of Fisher et
al. (1953) [9] for comparison of treatment means.
Results and Discussions
The data presented in Table 3 and 4 revealed a significant variations
among the treatments due to application of nano micronutrients.
Growth parameters
The foliar application of nano-Zn @ 0.5% and Mn @ 0.4% (T11)
recorded maximum plant height (112.68 cm), which is statistically at
par with T8 (111.91 cm). The plants grown under treatment combina-
tions of RDF + 0.4% ZnSO4 and MnSO4 (T2) showed the shortest
(93.09 cm) plant height. Tiller numbers increased markedly across all
treatments at 120 days after planting, with the values ranging from
1.08 to 1.92. Treatment receiving 0.5% nano-Zn and Mn at 45 and 90
DAP (T12) produced maximum number of tillers (1.92) followed by
T10 (1.83) and T9 (1.83), whereas T2 recorded minimum number of
tillers per plant (1.08). The data presented in Table 3 reported that
maximum leaf length (56.53 cm) and breadth (14.35 cm) were
recorded under T11 which is statistically at par with T9 and T8.
Treatment combinations of 0.4% ZnSO4 and MnSO4 (T2) recorded
the shortest leaf length (50.04 cm) whereas minimum breadth
(11.92 cm) was recorded under foliar application of 0.3% ZnSO4 and
MnSO4 (T1). It is clearly observed from Table 4 that at 120 DAP the
maximum number of leaves (12.07) was recorded under T12 which is
statistically at par with T11 (12.05) and T9 (11.85) in contrast to T1
that recorded minimum leaf numbers (8.57).
Projected yield
The projected yield per hectare followed the similar trend, with values
ranging from 25.54 t ha−1 to 30.84 t ha−1 presented in Table 4. The
maximum projected yield (30.84 t ha−1) was recorded in T11 which is
statistically at par with T12 (30.72 t ha−1) and T10 (30.08 t ha−1). The
lowest projected yield (25.54 t ha−1) was observed in T2 (RDF +
0.4% ZnSO4 and MnSO4 at 45 and 90 DAP).
Quality attributes
The data presented in Table 4 revealed that maximum dry recovery
(23.28%) and total curcumin content (433.27 kg ha−1) were recorded
in the plants treated with 0.5% nano Zn and 0.4% nano Mn (T11) as
compare to T2 (0.4% ZnSO4 and MnSO4) which recorded minimum
dry recovery (20.91%) and total curcumin content (267.36 kg ha −1).
The treatments combined nano Zn and Mn application at 45 and 90
DAP (T11) showed better results for most of the vegetative parame-
ters, projected yield and quality attributes suggests that split applica-
tion of nano micronutrients may ensures better nutrient supply during
critical vegetative growth phases rather than conventional. Similar
findings have been reported in turmeric by Khattab et al., 2023 [10]
where application of nano-Zn @ 10 mg L−1 significantly improved
plant height, leaf numbers and yield as compare to control. In another
study Alkhlefawi, (2022) [11] observed application of nano-Mn @ 2 g
L−1 significantly enhanced plant height, number of leaves per plant
and leaf area in fenugreek compared to conventional fertilizers. Nano-
Zn and nano-Mn exhibit high surface to volume ratios, increased re-
activity and greater solubility compared to bulk fertilizers. Their
nanoscale size facilitates enhanced penetration through leaf cuticles
and stomatal openings in foliar application, rapid mobility within
apoplastic and symplastic pathways, controlled nutrient release syn-
chronized with crop demand (Semenova et al., 2024) [12]. In a study
Sharma et al. (2013) [13] reported that Zn enhances yield through acti-
vation of carbonic anhydrase, improving CO2 assimilation efficiency,
stimulation of tryptophan synthesis, a precursor of IAA, promoting
cell division and elongation, improved synthesis of nucleic acids and
proteins, supporting vigorous vegetative growth where nano-Mn im-
proves yield by strengthening photolysis of water and electron trans-
port activity, increasing chlorophyll stability and light-harvesting effi-
ciency, enhancing photosynthetic carbon fixation and assimilate accu-
mulation (Pradhan et al., 2013). The findings of present experiment
are closely related to Poornima et al. (2019) [14] who reported that fo-
liar application of nano-ZnO at 500 ppm significantly influenced plant
height, leaf area, leaf area index and total dry matter compared with
1000 ppm bulk ZnSO ₄ .

Soil nutrient status
Data presented in table 5 revealed that the highest available soil nitro-
gen (238.41 kg ha−1) was recorded under T12 (0.5% nano-Zn and
Mn), followed by T10 (237.68 kg ha−1) as compared to T1 which
recorded minimum available nitrogen content (223.09 kg ha−1) after
harvesting. In case of available phosphorus maximum value (20.57 kg
ha−1) was recorded in T4 which is statistically at par with T9
(20.21 kg ha−1). The minimum phosphorus content (17.48 kg ha−1)
was observed in T10. The soil available potassium content ranged
from 223.25 kg ha−1 to 232.85 kg ha−1. T11 showed the highest value
followed by T8 (230.74 kg ha−1) while T1 recorded the lowest avail-
able potassium (223.25 kg ha−1). The results demonstrate that nano-
formulated micronutrients integrated with commercial fertilizers sig-
nificantly improved soil nitrogen and phosphorus availability, while
potassium content remained largely unaffected. Treatments T12, T11,
T10 and T9 performed better in maintaining higher soil nutrient status,
suggesting that nano micronutrient based nutrient management can
enhance soil fertility and sustainability in turmeric cultivation.
Leaf nutrient status
Leaf NPK content is a key indicator of plant nutritional status and
photosynthetic capacity. Maximum leaf nitrogen was recorded under
T8 (1.58%), leaf phosphorus was recorded under T12 (0.43%) where
T6 recorded highest potassium content (3.13%) at 120 DAP. In con-
trast the lowest nitrogen was recorded under T4, while T2 showed
lowest leaf phosphorus (0.33%) and potassium (2.34%). The im-
proved leaf nutrient status under nano-fertilizer treatments may be
due to enhance metabolic activity due to application of nano micronu-
trients which ultimately contributed to improved growth, yield, and
quality attributes observed in the study.
Conclusion
The findings of the present investigation established that considering
the projected yield, total curcumin content and growth attributes, T11
(0.5% nano-Zn and 0.4% nano-Mn) emerged as the most effective
treatment, followed by T12 (0.5% nano-Zn and Mn). Overall, the
study demonstrates that foliar application of nano-formulated mi-
cronutrients in combination with commercial fertilizers represents a
promising strategy for optimizing micronutrient use efficiency and
maximizing turmeric productivity and quality under the Gangetic
New Alluvial Plains of West Bengal.
