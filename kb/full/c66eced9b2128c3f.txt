Abstract The northern North Pacific forms a het-
erogeneous deep-sea environment with dynamic cur -
rents, distinct water masses, and complex bathymetry, 
including the Kuril–Kamchatka (KKT) and Aleutian 
Trenches (AT). As a climate-sensitive region, warm-
ing and sea-ice loss alter surface productivity and 
organic matter flux, potentially affecting deep-sea 
benthic communities. We quantified metazoan mei-
ofaunal densities across abyssal and hadal zones of 
the KKT and AT, integrating samples from the 2022 
AleutBio (SO293) expedition with legacy datasets. 
Gradient-Forest modeling captured broad productiv -
ity patterns across higher taxonomic levels, while 
generalized linear mixed effects models (GLMMs) 
quantified taxon-specific responses to environmen-
tal factors. Meiofaunal densities varied substantially, 
peaking on the abyssal KKT plain and reaching min-
ima in the hadal AT. Gradient -Forest analysis identi-
fied surface chlorophyll a, bottom-water temperature, 
and monthly primary production as primary drivers 
of regional density patterns. However, taxon-specific 
GLMMs revealed that once spatial effects and over -
dispersion were controlled, bottom-water chemistry 
and hydrography exerted the most consistent con-
trol on local abundances. These results demonstrate 
a  two-tier ecological control: surface-derived food 
availability governs meiofaunal densities at regional

Introduction
The deep sea, here defined as areas beyond the con-
tinental shelf break at > 200  m depth (Tyler et  al., 
2016), encompasses diverse environments charac-
terized by unique topographies, seabed properties, 
water-mass characteristics, and energy availability. 
These conditions influence the distribution and com-
position of life across various geomorphic features 
including vast abyssal plains, steep continental slopes 
and ridges, as well as incisive canyons and hadal 
trenches that extend to the ocean’s greatest depth 
(Ramirez-Llodra et  al., 2010). In these settings, the 
resident biota depends heavily on organic matter pro-
duced in the euphotic zone and subsequently trans-
ported to the deep sea as the primary food resource 
(i.e., allochthonous inputs from external sources 
rather than autochthonous, locally produced mate-
rial). However, only a fraction of the organic material 
produced in surface waters reaches the seafloor (Lutz 
et al., 2002), resulting in decreasing faunal densities 
and biomass with increasing depth (Rex et al., 2006; 
Wei et al., 2010). Yet, the strength of this decline is 
not uniform across different size classes. Specifically, 
meiofaunal communities experience a less severe 
decrease in densities compared to larger (macro- and 
megafaunal) organisms, indicating shifting ecological 
significance toward smaller biota with lower energy 
demands at greater depths (Thiel, 1975, 1979; Rex 
et al., 2006; Wei et al., 2010; Zeppili et al., 2018).
Meiofauna, i.e., minute benthic eukaryotes 
including metazoans, protists and fungi that pass 
through a 1  mm sieve  but are retained by a lower 
mesh size of 32 µm (Mare, 1942; Higgins & Thiel, 
1988), play an integral role in deep-sea ecosystems 
(Zeppilli et al., 2018; Schmidt-Rhaesa, 2020; Mar -
tínez et  al., 2025). Their ecological relevance is 
highlighted by their contributions to ecosystem pro-
cesses through their wide distribution, high abun-
dance, diversity, and rapid generation times (Wood-
ward, 2010; Schratzberger & Ingels, 2018). As 
essential components of the food web, meiofauna 
enhances nutrient cycling and sediment bioturba-
tion, improving nutrient exchange within the sedi-
ment (Green & Chandler, 1994; Meadows & Mead-
ows, 1994; Bonaglia & Nascimento, 2023). They 
also support sediment remineralization by fostering 
microbial activity and facilitating the breakdown 
and assimilation of organic matter (Findlay & Ten-
ore, 1982; Moens et al., 2007; Ingels et al., 2023). 
Moreover, meiofauna serve as indicators for assess-
ing the impacts of human activities on marine envi-
ronments, including climate change effects (Coull 
& Chandler, 1992; Pusceddu et  al., 2014; Zeppilli 
et al., 2015; Moens et al., 2022; Schratzberger et al., 
2023). Yet, despite their significant ecological role, 
there exists a notable scarcity of comprehensive 
data on meiofaunal communities in certain regions, 
such as the deep North Pacific (Rosli et  al., 2018) 
limiting our capacity to identify the environmental 
drivers shaping these communities and to predict 
their responses to environmental change.
The northern North Pacific is characterized by 
distinct water masses and strong current systems 
coupled with varied bathymetric features, including 
trenches, ridges, and seamounts (Warren & Owens, 
1988; Harris et al., 2014). Prominent among these are 
the southwest-to-northeast directed Kuril Kamchatka 
Trench (KKT) and the east-to-west directed Aleutian 
Trench (AT) along the Aleutian Ridge. The Bering 
Strait, a shallow marine passage, constrains faunal 
migration from and into the Arctic (Lee et al., 2020; 
Lemenkova, 2021). This area is sensitive to climate 
perturbations, particularly in the south-eastern Ber -
ing Sea, which has undergone demonstrated ocean 
warming and changes in sea-ice coverage with subse-
quent effects on surface productivity and marine food 
webs (Coyle et  al., 2007; Brown et  al., 2011; Hunt 
et  al., 2022; Overland et  al., 2024). Such changes 
in surface-water conditions and the frequency and 
magnitude of organic-matter export to the deep sea 
can also profoundly affect deep-sea floor organisms 
(Smith et al., 2013). These environmental transitions 
make the North Pacific particularly suitable for inves-
tigating meiofaunal responses across multiple gra-
dients of food availability, sediment characteristics,

benchmarks for detecting and forecasting ecological 
changes in response to climate variability.
Incorporating new samples from the eastern 
AT (Brandt, 2022; Bonk et  al., 2025), this study 
extends upon existing meiofaunal data from prior 
investigations in the KKT (Schmidt & Martínez 
Arbizu, 2015; Schmidt et  al., 2019) to identify the 
environmental drivers affecting densities of metazoan 
meiofauna at higher taxonomic levels across these 
abyssal and hadal areas. Earlier studies identified 
seafloor particulate organic carbon (POC) flux 
and sediment characteristics, among others, as key 
determinants of meiofaunal densities (Pfannkuche, 
1985; Pfannkuche & Thiel, 1987; Vanreusel et  al., 
1995; Hoste et  al., 2007; Ostmann & Martínez 
Arbizu, 2018; Ingels et  al., 2023). Building on 
this foundation, we aimed to (1) identify key 
environmental predictors of meiofaunal densities 
across bathymetric and geographic gradients; (2) 
quantify the relative importance of these predictors 
and their taxon-specific effects using Gradient Forest 
and generalized linear mixed effects models (Poisson-
GLMM); and (3) assess community thresholds along 
environmental gradients to elucidate the key drivers 
of deep-sea biodiversity patterns. This approach 
provides a baseline for predicting how ongoing 
changes in surface productivity and deep-water 
conditions may affect benthic communities in the 
northern North Pacific.
Material and methods
Study area
The study area covers two trench systems in the 
northern Pacific: the KKT adjacent to Japan, the Kuril 
Islands, and Kamchatka Peninsula, and the AT, which 
spans over 3,000 km from Siberia to the Alaska Pen-
insula (Fig. 1). The KKT is formed by the subduction 
of the Pacific Plate beneath the Okhotsk Plate toward

its northern edge marked by the strong westward-

northward transport of the Lower Circumpolar Deep 
Water (LCDW) (Kawabe & Fujio, 2010). Some of 
this LCDW propagates into the AT via deep western-

Sampling and taxonomic procedures
During the boreal summers of 2012, 2016, and 2022, 
samples were collected from the north-western and 
north-eastern Pacific Ocean in the course of three 
expeditions onboard RV Sonne: KuramBio (SO223), 
KuramBio II (SO250), and AleutBio (SO293) 
(Fig.  1, Online Resource 4). A multicorer (MUC) 
was used, employing cores with an inner diameter 
of 94  mm. Each core thereby provided a sampling 
surface area of 69.4 cm2. MUC sampling was 
carried out across 33 distinct sampling areas, with 
multiple deployments per area, resulting in a total 
of 66 stations. Previously published data from the 
KuramBio and KuramBio II expeditions can be found 
in Schmidt & Martínez Arbizu (2015) and Schmidt 
et  al. (2019), respectively. Due to unit discrepancies 
between these earlier datasets and the most recent 
samples from SO293, a re-assessment of the raw data 
from Schmidt & Martínez Arbizu (2015) and Schmidt 
et al. (2019) was necessary. Specifically, meiofaunal 
densities were calculated per core and harmonized 
to a standardized unit (number of individuals per 
69.4 cm2). Furthermore, samples from the Bering 
Sea were excluded from the downstream analysis 
due to low sample coverage comprising eight cores, 
of which only two covered raster cells. This study 
includes data from a total of 66 individual stations 
comprising a total of 190 cores, with sampling depths 
ranging from 4,304 to 9,541  m (Bonk et  al., 2026; 
Online Resource 4).
The sample protocol and processing of the MUC 
samples collected during SO293 are based on the 
methods described by Bonk et  al. (2025). Briefly, 
three cores were analyzed at each station to evalu-
ate the composition of meiofaunal assemblages. On 
board, the top 5 cm of each sediment core was pre-
served in buffered formalin (4%). The samples were 
then centrifuged and categorized into supra-specific 
taxa at the German Center for Marine Biodiversity 
Research (DZMB) in Wilhelmshaven, Germany.
Taxonomic resolution focused on supra-specific 
meiofaunal groups routinely distinguished in deep-
sea meiofaunal studies. Nauplii, although represent-
ing larval stages of Copepoda rather than a separate 
taxon, were retained as an independent analytical 
category because they form a substantial and mor -
phologically distinguishable component of benthic 
meiofaunal assemblages and may exhibit ecological 
responses distinct from copepodid and adult stages 
(e.g., Guidi-Guilvard et  al., 2009; Guidi-Guilvard & 
Dallot, 2014; Ayón Dejo et  al., 2023). This practice 
is commonly applied in meiofaunal ecology, particu-
larly where nauplii contribute substantially to total 
abundance and can respond differently to environ-
mental gradients. We therefore treat nauplii here as an 
operational ecological category rather than a formal 
taxonomic entity. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that 
this approach may underestimate total copepod repre-
sentation when Copepoda and nauplii are considered 
separately. Conversely, typical macrofaunal taxa (e.g., 
Amphipoda, Isopoda, Polychaeta) were excluded, 
consistent with previous meiofaunal studies (e.g., 
Rowe et al., 1991; Berkenbusch et al., 2011).

A set of environmental predictors was assembled to 
model meiofaunal distributions using GLMMs and 
Gradient Forests (Table 1). Benthic and water-column

Poisson-GLMM
We fitted generalized linear mixed–effects models 
(GLMMs) with a Poisson error distribution to assess 
how each environmental parameter influences mei-
ofauna densities, while accounting for variation in sam-
ple area and spatial clustering. The predictors (depth 
and the focal parameter) were centered to zero mean 
and scaled to unit variance to facilitate model conver-
gence and interpretation. For each focal taxon–param-
eter combination, we built a model with taxon counts 
as the response and the focal predictor and sampling 
depth as fixed effects and log(sampled sediment area) 
as an offset. This approach allowed us to estimate direct 
effects on densities (individuals/m 2), while preserv -
ing the count data structure. We did not build a full 
model containing all predictors as the number of sam-
ples with unique values for the environmental predic-
tors was restricted to the number of stations. For the 
random effect structure, we nested sampling stations 
(n = 66) within sampling areas (n = 33). We then added 
an observation‐level random effect (OLRE) by creating 
a unique factor for each sample (n = 190) to control for 
overdispersion. Before modeling, records with missing 
values were omitted.

package (Bates et al., 2015), using a Poisson distri-

ble convergence. We compared the two models via 
Akaike information criterion (AIC) and formally 
assessed overdispersion using the R performance 
package (Lüdecke et al., 2021) and a custom Pear -

earity, random-effects structure, residual distribu-
tion) were further evaluated using the respective 
plots and functions.
To quantify the proportion of variance explained 
by each fixed effect, we computed semi–partial R2 
statistics with the partR2 package (Stoffel et  al., 
2021), performing 99 parametric bootstraps for con-
fidence intervals. We extracted overall marginal R2, 
the inclusive R2 attributable to each predictor (IR2), 
and the associated structure-component (SC) and 
beta‐weight (BW) metrics. We used IR2 and BW to 
interpret the importance of each parameter, and the 
respective strength and direction of each effect.

where SCj is the structure coefficient, i.e., the Pearson 
correlation between the centered-and-scaled predictor 
xj and the model’s fitted values. R2total is the marginal 
 R2 of the full model, i.e., the variance explained by all 
fixed effects.

direction of individual environmental effects, 
we extracted the beta-weight (BW) for every 
centered-and-scaled predictor from the final 
Poisson-GLMMs. A BW is simply the model’s

have been standardized to zero mean and unit vari-
ance; it therefore represents the expected change in 
the density and hence the multiplicative change in 
the expected organism abundance associated with 
a one-standard-deviation increase in that predictor.

Compositional data analysis
Grain-size composition was quantified as the per -
centage of sand, mud (combined silt and clay frac-
tion), and gravel (Schmidt & Martínez Arbizu, 2015; 
Schmidt et  al., 2019; Sindlev et  al., 2025). Because 
these components are constrained to sum to 100%, 
they constitute compositional data and cannot be 
analyzed as independent variables, as an increase in 
one component necessarily results in a decrease in at 
least one of the others. Therefore, we applied compo-
sitional data analysis (Aitchison, 1986) to transform 
grain-size measurements into orthogonal, i.e., uncor -
related and isometric log-ratio (ILR) components 
(Egozcue et  al., 2003). These components describe 
the relative balance between percentages of sand 
and mud vs. gravel (ILR1) and sand vs. mud (ILR2). 
Components were created using the package compo-
sitions (van den Boogart et  al., 2025). The effect of 
sediment grain size on meiofaunal densities was then 
assessed using the same Poisson GLMM with off-
set described above, with ILR1 and ILR2 included 
as fixed effects. Owing to incomplete sediment data, 
this analysis was based on a reduced dataset. In total, 
52 samples (27.4%) from 17 stations (25.8%) were 
excluded because grain-size measurements were 
unavailable or were erroneous (Online Resource 7). 
Acari were not analyzed due to insufficient data.
Results
Meiofaunal abundance and taxonomic composition
The dataset comprised a total of 647,202 meiofaunal 
individuals sampled at 66 stations in the KKT and 
AT. Benthic densities varied substantially across the 
investigated regions (Fig.  2, Online Resources 1&2). 
The highest densities were recorded on the abyssal 
plain of the KKT, ranging from 37 to 1,589 individu-
als per 10 cm2 (492.9 ± 379.0, mean ± SD). In con-
trast, the lowest densities occurred in the hadal zone 
of the AT, with values between 45 and 315 individu-
als per 10 cm2 (178.5 ± 108.8). Intermediate densities 
were observed in the hadal KKT (82–1,115 ind./10 
 cm2; 399.6 ± 281.3) and the abyssal AT (107–478 
ind./10 cm2; 249.4 ± 203.9).
The meiofaunal communities were dominated by 
Nematoda (92.2%), followed by Copepoda (4.1%) and 
nauplii (3.2%). These taxa were present in all sam-
ples and reached their highest densities on the abys-
sal KKT plain. Other taxa displayed more localized 
peaks: Kinorhyncha were most abundant in the hadal 
KKT, while Loricifera reached their maximum den-
sities on the abyssal AT. Less abundant groups such 
as Kinorhyncha, Loricifera, Ostracoda, Tantulocarida, 
and Gastrotricha were detected at 8 (Tantulocarida) to 
39 (Ostracoda) stations and consistently occurred at 
densities below 5 individuals per 10 cm2. Neverthe-
less, these taxa were retained for further analysis due 
to their consistent presence across multiple stations. 
In contrast, Acari were encountered only sporadically 
and in extremely low numbers, and were therefore 
excluded from subsequent analyses.
Gradient Forest
The weighted-R2 ranking (Fig. 3) shows that only few 
predictors account for most of the variance in meio-
faunal composition. Mean surface chlorophyll-a (Chl) 
tops the list (wR2 ≈ 0.05), followed closely by mean 
potential temperature at the seafloor (Temp), mean 
monthly surface primary production (VGPM_mean), 
the seasonal variability of that production (VGPM_
svi), and mean salinity (SO). Together, these five 
variables explain roughly one-third of the cumulative 
importance, indicating that (i) pelagic productivity 
proxies, i.e., chlorophyll and VGPM metrics, form a 
strong trophic gradient, and (ii) hydrographic proper -
ties, bottom-water temperature and salinity, form an 
independent physical gradient that further structures 
the assemblage.
The next set of predictors comprises dissolved 
nutrients (phosphate, nitrate, and silicate) and dis-
solved oxygen as well as global sediment thickness, 
all with intermediate wR2 values (0.02–0.03). Their 
coherent positioning suggests a second environmen-
tal axis linked to nutrient availability, but their lower

Finally, gravity roughness, SCOC and Lutz-POC

model parameters played a comparatively minor role 
in shaping the broad-scale distribution of taxa in our

Because wR2 represents the average variance in 
species density that each predictor explains across all 
taxa, the steep drop-off after the top five reinforces 
the conclusion that meiofaunal communities in the 
study area are primarily associated with two orthogo-
nal drivers: a productivity gradient (food supply) and 
a hydrographic gradient (temperature–salinity water 
mass), while nutrient concentrations contribute sec-
ondarily and sediment characteristics only marginally.

The compositional data analyses revealed no sig-
nificant effect of the relative proportion of sand 
and mud combined versus gravel (ILR1) on the 
abundance of any taxon; consequently, ILR1 was 
not considered further. In contrast, the balance 
between sand and mud (ILR2) significantly affected 
meiofaunal abundance (Table  2). Negative coef-

sediments, and all estimated effects were consist-

after Benjamini–Hochberg correction were detected 
for the overall meiofaunal assemblage as well as 
for Gastrotricha, Tantulocarida, Nematoda, and

nominally significant. The consistent direction of 
the effects suggests a broadly shared preference for 
fine-grained sediments within the meiofaunal com-
munity, although not all taxon-specific responses 
reached statistical significance.

Discussion
This study characterizes the environmental determi-
nants of metazoan meiofaunal densities at abyssal 
and hadal depths of the KKT and AT, applying dif-
ferent statistical modeling techniques to identify the 
main predictors and quantify taxon-specific effects. 
Given the paucity of baseline data for the region, the 
findings serve as a key reference for predicting ben-
thic community responses to projected climate-driven 
changes (e.g., shifts in temperature, dissolved oxygen, 
and primary productivity; Sweetman et al., 2017).
Several environmental predictors of meiofaunal 
densities were identified (Figs.  3, 4, Table  2). The 
GLMMs refine, and in part rearrange, the picture 
that emerged from the Gradient-Forest analysis. The 
Gradient-Forest analysis ranked surface productiv -
ity proxies (i.e., mean chlorophyll-a and VGPM) 
alongside bottom-water temperature and salinity 
as the most important predictors when their vari-
ance was summed across all taxa. In contrast, taxon-
specific  GLMMs, when  accounting for random 
effects,  identified  dissolved nutrients (nitrate, phos-
phate, silicate) and oxygen as  consistently signifi-
cant drivers, whereas the productivity metrics remain 
significant only for a few taxa such as Kinorhyncha 
and, to a lesser extent, Copepoda. In addition, grain 
size seemed to be important, with a higher proportion 
of mud associated with elevated meiofaunal densi-
ties. The importance of hydrographic conditions is 
supported by both approaches: Bottom-water tem-
perature and salinity rank among the most influential 
predictors in the Gradient-Forest analysis and emerge 
with clear, opposing slopes in the GLMMs. Together, 
these patterns indicate a differentiation of the deep-
sea assemblage along a hydrographic gradient, with 
taxa associated with cooler, more oxygenated condi-
tions contrasting with those occurring preferentially 
under warmer, less oxygenated bottom waters.
These divergences highlight the complementary 
nature of the two methods. Gradient Forest is a mul-
tivariate machine-learning approach that integrates 
responses across multiple taxa to identify environ-
mental gradients associated with the strongest com-
munity turnover and compositional change, while 
accounting for complex, non-linear relationships 
(Ellis et al., 2012). By optimizing split points to max-
imize overall variance explained, it emphasizes gradi-
ents that structure variation across the entire assem-
blage, meaning that productivity-related variables 
such as chlorophyll-a and VGPM can appear highly 
important when they consistently influence many 
taxa simultaneously, even if their effects are relatively 
weak or inconsistent at the level of individual groups. 
In contrast, the GLMMs evaluate each taxon sepa-
rately and estimate the direction and strength of its 
response, while accounting for variation among sta-
tions and samples (Bolker et al., 2009), thereby focus-
ing on relationships that are robust within taxa once 
other sources of variability are taken into account. 
By providing taxon-specific regression coefficients 
and confidence intervals, GLMMs reveal, which of 
the shared gradients actually alter local density, once 
other factors are held constant. As a result, predictors

oxygen and dissolved nutrients, emerge more clearly 
as significant in the taxon-specific models, whereas

inconsistent effects on individual taxon abundances. 
Thus, Gradient Forest captures the broad productiv -
ity gradient structuring community-wide composi-
tional turnover, whereas GLMMs identify the nutri-
ent–redox axis as exerting the strongest and most 
consistent control on taxon-specific abundances.
Ecologically, this implies a two-tier control: sur -
face-derived food availability (chlorophyll-a, VGPM, 
phosphate) establishes the regional capacity to sup-
port meiofaunal communities and influences broad-
scale distributional patterns and community com-
position (see also Rex et al., 2006; Wei et al., 2010; 
Schmidt & Martínez Arbizu, 2015; Ostmann & Mar -
tínez Arbizu, 2018), while bottom-water chemistry 
(oxygen, dissolved nutrients), hydrography and grain 
size exert stronger, more consistent controls on local 
population densities and determine which taxa can 
successfully exploit available resources at a given 
location (see also Ostmann & Martínez Arbizu, 2018; 
Wang et  al., 2019; Kamenev et  al., 2022). Interest-
ingly, salinity and temperature also showed substan-
tial influence, despite their relatively narrow spatial 
gradients across the KKT and AT (Online Resource 
3), suggesting that these variables primarily reflect 
water-mass characteristics and broader hydrographic 
regimes rather than acting as direct drivers of mei-
ofaunal densities.
In our study, meiofaunal densities differed mark -
edly between the two trench systems, with the KKT 
generally supporting higher densities than the AT, 
although notable variability was observed within each 
trench (Fig. 2, Online Resource 1). Elevated densities 
at the trench axes were evident in the KKT, but not in 
the AT, compared to surrounding abyssal sites. Com-
parisons across trenches and depth zones (abyssal 
vs. hadal) indicate that trench axes serve as localized 
hotspots of meiofaunal abundance. Similar patterns 
have been reported in the Atacama, Japan, and Tonga 
trenches, where trench-axis sediments consistently 
exhibit higher densities relative to adjacent abys-
sal plains (Danovaro et  al., 2002; Itoh et  al., 2011; 
Ichino et  al., 2015; Schmidt & Martínez Arbizu, 
2015; Schmidt et  al., 2019; Shimabukuro et  al., 
2022). These elevated densities are likely driven by 
downslope focusing of organic material along steep 
trench topography (Itoh et  al., 2011; Ichino et  al., 
2015; Schmidt & Martínez Arbizu, 2015; Schmidt 
et al., 2019; Shimabukuro et al., 2022).
The lack of a clear trench-axis pattern in the AT 
may be due to a mass-wasting event, with hadal sedi-
ments overlain by a fluid mud layer (Sindlev et  al., 
2026), resulting in locally depressed abundances 
(see Preikschardt et al., 2025 for macrofauna). More 
broadly, trenches are geologically unstable environ-
ments subject to frequent seismic activity and subma-
rine turbidites. Such disturbances—likewise inferred 
for parts of the KKT—act as broad, non-selective 
suppressors of meiofaunal abundance and likely 
contribute to the high spatial variability observed in 
both trench systems (Beliaev & Brueggeman, 1989; 
Bigham et al., 2021; Kamenev et al., 2022).
Shimabukuro et  al. (2022) further demonstrated 
that, across three hadal trench systems underlying 
regions of highly variable surface productivity, meio-
faunal densities were more closely linked to microbial 
benthic activity, such as oxygen consumption rates, 
than to traditional proxies for food availability (e.g., 
total organic carbon or phytopigments). Similarly, in 
the KKT this microbial pathway is likely important: 
the widespread occurrence of hadal cold-seep com-
munities hosting endosymbiotic chemosynthetic bac-
teria on trench slopes and the trench floor (Fukumori 
et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2025) indicates that chemos-
ynthetically produced organic carbon can supplement 
surface-derived material. These findings suggest that 
microbial biomass represents a critical food source 
for hadal meiofauna and that variations in food deliv -
ery and microbial activity largely drive the spatial 
heterogeneity of meiofaunal abundance both within 
and across trench systems.
Several studies have shown that sediment grain 
size is an important factor in determining the abun-
dance and community structure of meiofauna (Gray, 
1981; Carman et al., 1987; Wang et al., 2019; Zhao 
et  al., 2020). Across the assemblage, abundances 
were higher in mud-rich sediments, with signifi-
cant responses detected for the total meiofauna as 
well as several individual taxa, including Nema-
toda, Gastrotricha, Tantulocarida, and Ostracoda. 
This pattern is ecologically plausible, as fine-grained 
sediments, associated with hydrodynamically quiet 
environments, typically accumulate larger quanti-
ties of organic matter and microbial biomass than

coarser sediments, thereby providing enhanced food 
resources for deposit-feeding meiofauna and their 
associated microbial food webs (e.g., Diesing et  al., 
2021). The observed preference for mud-rich habitats 
therefore likely reflects more indirect effects of food 
availability and sediment biogeochemistry rather than 
a direct response to sediment texture itself (but see 
Tong et al., 2021).
Our analysis revealed distinct responses among 
meiofaunal taxa to environmental predictors. Spe-
cifically, changes in Nematoda and Tardigrada 
abundances were more strongly linked to mean 
chlorophyll-a concentrations, whereas Ostracoda den-
sities showed a stronger association with VGPM_svi. 
Copepoda exhibited significant correlations with 
salinity (mean sea-surface salinity) and phosphate 
concentrations. Nauplii partly differed from cope-
podid and adult copepods in their responses to envi-
ronmental predictors, particularly with respect to 
VGPM_svi and grain size (Fig.  5, Table  2). Notably 
the threshold for split changes in meiofaunal densi-
ties in response to chlorophyll-a concentrations was 
consistent with that reported by Ostmann & Martínez 
Arbizu (2018), specifically within the 0.6–0.8 µg L⁻ 1 
range.
The separation of nauplii from adult and cope-
podid Copepoda remains a subject of methodologi-
cal debate, as nauplii do not constitute an independ-
ent taxon. Nevertheless, their retention as a separate 
ecological category in this study is predicated on the 
assumption that they possess a distinct morphology, 
developmental stage, and potentially diverging envi-
ronmental requirements. The observed differences 
in responses between nauplii and copepodids/adult 
copepods in our study further support this view. Simi-
lar stage-specific responses to environmental vari-
ability have previously been documented in deep-sea 
meiofaunal studies (e.g., Guidi-Guilvard et al., 2009; 
Guidi-Guilvard & Dallot, 2014), suggesting that nau-
plii can provide an ecologically informative opera-
tional category in meiofaunal assessments.
As elsewhere, Nematoda dominated the meiofau-
nal communities in our study (Rosli et  al., 2018; 
Zeppilli et al., 2018). However, some taxa exhibited 
markedly low densities, which posed challenges for 
statistical analyses and interpretation. Add to this, 
the observed high variability in meiofaunal densi-
ties at small spatial scales, even within single MUC 
deployments poses significant challenges in modeling 
meiofaunal abundances (see also Uhlenkott et  al., 
2020). This variability reflects both the natural patch-
iness of meiofaunal distributions—driven by aggre-
gated reproductive strategies, limited dispersal, and 
biotic interactions (Findlay, 1981)—and local envi-
ronmental heterogeneity, including factors such as 
food availability, disturbance history, sediment grain 
size, porosity, and organic matter quality (Rosli et al., 
2018; Shimabukuro et al., 2022; Kamenev et al., 2022 
and citations therein). Small-scale habitat complex-
ity, including microtopography and biogeochemi-
cal microniches, can further amplify this patchi-
ness, with meiofaunal densities varying by orders of 
magnitude over centimeter to meter scales (Gallucci 
et al., 2009; Leduc et al., 2012). Such fine-scale vari-
ability generates overdispersion in predictive models, 
where observed variance exceeds expectations based 
on standard probability distributions (Guisan & Thu-
iller, 2005; Ver Hoef & Boveng, 2007), and can mask 
broader environmental signals if sampling intensity is 
insufficient to capture the underlying spatial structure 
(Warwick et al., 1990).
To mitigate these effects, we applied two different 
approaches, i.e., Gradient Forests and GLMM with 
one fixed and a focal parameter. Nevertheless, many 
taxa had too few observations or very low individual 
counts (e.g., Acari), which limited model quality and 
statistical power. The GLMM analyses were based on 
single-predictor models to avoid overparameteriza-
tion given the available sample size. Consequently, 
potential interactions among environmental variables 
could not be assessed. In addition, environmental 
predictors were derived from Bio-ORACLE, which 
provides broad-scale environmental conditions at a 
coarser spatial resolution than the biologic sampling. 
As a result, fine-scale environmental heterogeneity 
may not be fully captured, and some temporal mis-
match between environmental and biologic data can-
not be excluded.
Future studies based on larger datasets, finer-res-
olution environmental information, and increased 
replication across stations will help refine these rela-
tionships and improve model robustness. A hierarchi-
cal sampling design that explicitly targets the main 
environmental gradients identified here—productiv -
ity, bottom-water temperature, salinity, and oxygen—
while ensuring sufficient spatial spacing to capture 
regional-scale variation (e.g., > 10  km; Levin et  al., 
2001) would be especially valuable. At the same time,

Vol.: (0123456789)
substantially increasing within-station replication is 
essential; specifically, increasing the number of cores 
per station from three to at least five, and ideally ten 
or more, would allow robust estimation of local vari-
ance and better separate true environmental effects 
from stochastic patchiness (Rose et  al., 2005; Gal-
lucci et  al., 2009; Uhlenkott et  al., 2020). Addition-
ally, modeling taxon presence–absence using bino-
mial GLMMs would complement abundance models 
and provide insights into environmental controls on 
occurrence probabilities, which are often more tracta-
ble and ecologically interpretable when densities are 
low or highly variable (Warton et al., 2015; Hui et al., 
2013).
Conclusion
By combining Gradient  Forest and GLMM, we dis-
entangled broad-scale vs. taxon-specific drivers of 
metazoan meiofauna density across the abyssal and 
hadal zones of the KKT and AT. Both approaches 
revealed consistent hydrographic influences but dif-
fered in the relative weight assigned to surface pro-
ductivity versus bottom-water chemistry, highlighting 
the value of complementary statistical frameworks. 
Overall, the results suggest that surface-derived food 
availability establishes the regional backdrop for 
meiofaunal density, while local factors, such as oxy -
gen levels, hydrographic conditions, and disturbance 
events, modulate community composition at finer 
scales. In the KKT, chemosynthetically-derived car -
bon associated with cold-seep communities may sup-
plement surface-derived organic matter, suggesting 
that hadal food webs are supported by multiple car -
bon sources and underscoring the complex interplay 
between regional productivity gradients and local 
environmental conditions.
Importantly, this study provides critical baseline 
data for these understudied regions, serving as a refer-
ence for assessing benthic responses to projected cli-
mate-driven changes, including shifts in temperature, 
oxygen, and primary productivity (Sweetman et  al., 
2017). At the same time, the observed high variabil-
ity in meiofaunal densities at fine spatial scales, even 
within single MUC deployments, emphasizes the 
need for comprehensive sampling strategies that cap-
ture natural patchiness and environmental heterogene-
ity. Such variability presents challenges for predictive 
modeling by inflating prediction uncertainty and 
masking broader environmental signals in statistical 
models, thus highlighting the importance of refining 
methodologies and expanding spatial coverage. Fur -
ther, methodological constraints should be acknowl-
edged: GLMMs were restricted to single-predictor 
structures due to sample size limitations, preventing 
the evaluation of interactions among environmental 
drivers, and environmental data from Bio-ORACLE 
may not fully capture fine-scale variability at the scale 
of biological sampling. Future studies incorporating 
larger datasets, higher-resolution environmental data, 
and improved replication will be essential to resolve 
these processes and strengthen predictive frameworks 
for deep-sea ecosystems.
Given their sensitivity to environmental change, 
meiofauna are valuable indicators of ecological 
responses to climate-driven and other anthropogenic 
impacts in deep-sea environments, and integrat-
ing such baseline data into predictive frameworks is 
essential for anticipating cascading effects on biodi-
versity and ecosystem function (Zeppilli et al., 2015).
