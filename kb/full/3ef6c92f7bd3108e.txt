Abstract 33 
Microcystin-LR (MC-LR) is a hepatotoxic metabolite that naturally occurs during some cyanobacterial 34 
blooms in eutrophic waterbodies, and irrigation of edible plants with MC -LR-contaminated water 35 
causes bioaccumulation of the toxin. However, sufficient information about accumulation and 36 
depuration mechanics in hydroculture -grown herb plants is still lacking. This w ork aimed at 1) 37 
investigating bioaccumulation and depuration of MC -LR in basil, 2) verifying the possible MC -LR 38 
detoxification mechanisms in the plant, and 3) detecting the natural occurrence of MC-LR in basil (n = 39 
50) collected from the Belgian market. Basil plants grown in a hydroculture were exposed to MC -LR 40 
(5, 20, and 50 µg L−1) spiked in a Hoagland solution for seven days. MC-LR depuration was also studied 41 
by transferring the plants to a non -contaminated Hoagland solution after exposure to MC -LR for 42 
another seven days . MC -LR concentrations in Hoagland solution, basil leaves, and roots were 43 
quantified using a validated UHPLC –MS/MS method. In addition, ELISA and LC –HRMS (only basil 44 
leaves) were used for confirmation. The results showed an increase in the accumulated levels of MC-45 
LR at higher exposure dose s, with higher MC-LR levels in roots than in leaves for all the treatment 46 
conditions. For MC-LR depuration, significant reductions were observed in all the treatment conditions 47 
for roots only. No MC-LR conjugates, potentially related to metabolism, were detected by LC –HRMS. 48 
Finally, MC-LR was detected in one store -bought basil sample, representing the first occurrence of 49 
cyanotoxins in an edible crop from Belgium. 50

1. Introduction 54 
Cyanobacterial blooms are common in eutrophic waterbodies worldwide (Svirčev et al., 2019) , 55 
promoted by, but not limited to, increasing temperatures and other cl imate change phenomena 56 
(O’Neil et al., 2012). During the formation of these blooms, the production and release of a wide array 57 
of toxic metabolites into the water can happen (Abdallah et al., 2021; Ibelings et al., 2014) . Irrigation 58 
of food crops with cyanotoxin-contaminated water from the environment (e.g., basins, lakes, ponds, 59 
or canals) may result in bioaccumulation and translocation of the cyanotoxins to edible parts (Xiang 60 
et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2021). Besides their possible negative impacts on the plant by lowering yield 61 
and quality (Peuthert et al., 2007), cyanotoxins pose a human health risk (Codd et al., 1999; 62 
Tsoumalakou et al., 2021). The most common cyanotoxin is microcystin-LR (MC-LR), a hepatotoxic and 63 
possibly hepatocarcinogenic agent (group 2B) in humans (IARC, 2010; World Health Organization, 64 
2020). Accumulation of MC -LR was primarily shown in soil -based systems for different edible crops 65 
(Bittencourt-Oliveira et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2012; Codd et al., 1999; Corbel et al., 2016; Machado et 66 
al., 2017). Moreover, MC accumulation has already been studied in some crop plants. Lettuce is the 67 
prime example, as MC accumulation (originating from the environment or fortified irrigation water ) 68 
was observed in roots and leaves (Bittencourt-Oliveira et al., 2016; Codd et al., 1999; Cordeiro-Araújo 69 
et al., 2016; Crush et al., 2008; Hereman and Bittencourt -Oliveira, 2012; Levizou et al., 2017; 70 
Mohamed and Al Shehri, 2009) . Accumulation of cylindrospermopsin, another hepatotoxic 71 
cyanobacteria metabolite, was also observed in lettuce (Cordeiro-Araújo et al., 2017; Llana -Ruiz-72 
Cabello et al., 2019) . Exposure-dependent accumulation of MCs was also established in rice (Oryza 73 
sativa) and root vegetables such as carrots, radishes , and rape (Chen et al., 2012, 2004; Crush et al., 74 
2008; Levizou et al., 2020; Machado et al., 2017; Wijewickrama and Manage, 2019). Other laboratory-75 
based studies showed the accumulation of MCs in fruit -bearing plants, including chili, tomato , and 76 
strawberries (Corbel et al., 2016; Redouane et al., 2023; Romero -Oliva et al., 2014) . Yet, primary 77 
accumulation in these products was observed in the roots, stems , and leaves. Most of the observed 78 
accumulation of MCs in crops originated from soil-grown crops (Bittencourt-Oliveira et al., 2016; Chen 79 
et al., 2004; Codd et al., 1999; Crush et al., 2008; Hereman and Bittencourt -Oliveira, 2012; Levizou et 80 
al., 2017; Mohamed and Al Shehri, 2009) . However, plants grown in hydroculture systems may be 81 
especially vulnerable to MC -LR accumulation due to the direct contact between the contaminated 82 
water and plant roots. Accumulation of MCs in hydroculture systems has already been documented 83 
for several rice varieties (Wijewickrama and Manage, 2019) and leafy vegetables such as lettuce and 84 
spinach (Llana-Ruiz-Cabello et al., 2019; Wijewickrama and Manage, 2019). 85

In Belgium, MC-LR contamination has been reported in freshwater reservoirs (Van Hassel et al., 2022a; 86 
Willame et al., 2005) . Recently, cyanobacterial blooms from multiple lakes, ponds, and canals were 87 
sampled, and eight microcystins (MCs) were quantified in 86% ( n = 79) of the samples, with 88 
concentrations ranging from 0.1 μg L -1 to 2800 μg L -1 (Van Hassel et al., 2022a) . These water sources 89 
can potentially b e used for irrigation of crops. Therefore, potential MC -LR accumulation in Belgian 90 
crops, including basil (Ocimum basilicum L.), is a valid concern that might contribute to a public health 91 
risk. Basil is an important crop that is usually grown in hydroculture systems in several countries (Saha 92 
et al., 2016). The plant is generally used as a flavoring agent and a main ingredient in Pesto Genovese, 93 
a sauce used in many European dishes, especially in Italian cuisines. In addition to foods, basil is used 94 
in the cosmetic and pharmaceutical industries since it is a good source of essential oils, valuable 95 
antioxidants, and other bioactive compounds (Trajkovska-Broach et al., 2023). In Europe, basil makes 96 
up 60–75% of the total consumed herbs, and Belgium is among the top four countries (after Germany, 97 
the Netherla nds, and France) that import fresh herbs (Centre for the Promotion of Imports from 98 
developing countries, 2020) . Also, national producers contribute to the total marketed basil in 99 
Belgium. 100 
The objectives of this work were to investigate the accumulation a nd depuration of MC -LR in basil 101 
plants grown in a hydroculture, verify the possible mechanisms involved in the depuration, and survey 102 
the natural occurrence of MC-LR in basil samples collected from the Belgian market using an in-house 103 
validated ultra -high performance liquid chromatography -tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC–104 
MS/MS) method for the quantification of nine cyanotoxins. The toxins include eight MCs (MC-LR, MC-105 
RR, MC -LA, MC -LF, MC -LY, MC -LW, MC -YR, and MC-WR) and a structurally related cyanotoxin, 106 
nodularin-R (NOD-R). 107 
2. Materials and Methods 108 
2.1. Experimental setup 109 
Certified basil seeds (Italian large green variety; La Belle Portagere brand, Sebio, Belgium) and potting 110 
soil for herbs and aromatic plants (Campo brand, Brico, Belgium) were commercially obtained. After 111 
sowing, seeds germinated and grew inside a growth chamber (Mammoth Lite+ 45 type, 450 x 450 × 112 
1200 mm, Mammoth net, Netherlands ) where the light (around 200 μmol m -2 s-1 for 16 hours/day), 113 
temperature (24.6 ± 1.6 °C), and relative humidity (57.8 ± 12.0) were monitored and kept constant. 114 
The plants were watered every other day and after 60 days, the plants were collected and transferred 115 
to a Hoagland solution (Hoagland and Arnon, 1950) . Each plant was placed in a 100 mL amber glass 116 
bottle containing Hoagland solution fortified with MC -LR and sealed with parafilm. Each treatment 117

group (n = 6) was exposed to 5, 20, or 50 µg L−1 of MC-LR (Enzo Life Sciences, Belgium) for seven days, 118 
in addition to an untreated control group. Fortified Hoagland was prepared before the incubation of 119 
the plants. A stock solution of 10 µg mL −1 of MC-LR in ethanol was used to make a 1:200 dilution in 120 
Hoagland solution to obtain the 50 ng mL −1 concentration. After that, serial dilutions were used to 121 
make 1 L of Hoagla nd solution at 50, 20, and 5 µg L −1 MC-LR. To study the depuration of MC -LR, the 122 
same experimental setup was performed, parallel to the accumulation experiment, but the plants 123 
were transferred into clean (i.e., non -spiked, MC-LR-free) Hoagland solution for another seven days 124 
after exposure to MC-LR as described above. As a preliminary step to investigate any effect of MC -LR 125 
on basil, the weight and length of each plant were measured before and after exposure to the toxin. 126 
Also, the plant leaves and roots were visually inspected during and af ter the experiment . To assess 127 
the bioaccumulation and depuration of MC-LR, the toxin was quantified in basil leaves, roots, and the 128 
Hoagland solution. The basil roots, obtained after the accumulation testing period, were dipped for 129 
two seconds into distilled water and dried to ensure that no MC -LR was taken up from the Hoagland 130 
solution itself before processing, extraction, and UHPLC –MS/MS analysis. The experiment was 131 
repeated twice. Additionally, the accumulation part of the experimental setup was tested b efore the 132 
start of the experiment using an extract of Microcystis sp. ULC642 culture obtained from the 133 
BCCM/ULC culture collection (Liège, Belgium). The cultures were grown in BG11 at 22 °C and 700 Lux 134 
warm-led light intensity on a 10:14 day:night cycle. This extract contained a mixture of MCs, mainly 135 
MC-LR and MC-YR (Table S1), and was used to fortify the Hoagland solution to contaminate six basil 136 
plants at 50, 20, and 5 µg L-1 total MCs. 137 
2.2. Quantification of eight MCs congeners and NOD-R quantification in basil 138 
Samples from Hoagland solution and basil (separated leaves and roots) were collected and kept at −20 139 
°C until analysis. The extraction and UHPLC -MS/MS analysis methods for MC -LR from the different 140 
matrices were previously published (Van Hassel et al., 2022b, 2022c). These methods are suitable for 141 
analyzing eight MC congeners (MC -LR, MC-RR, MC-LA, MC-LF, MC-LY, MC-LW, MC-YR, MC-WR) and 142 
NOD-R in fruit and vegetable matrices and drinking water. The validity of the methods for fruits and 143 
vegetables was confirmed for the basil matrix, reassessing repeatability, reproducibility, recovery, 144 
specificity, linearity, limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ), measurement 145 
uncertainty (MU), and matrix effect. In brief, this method consisted of ex tracting the targeted toxins 146 
from 0.5 g of homogenized basil material (leaves or roots) using a combination of liquid extraction 147 
(methanol-water 80:20), sonication (BRANSON 2510, Analis, Belgium), overhead mixing (Heidolph 148 
Reax 2 Mixer, Analis, Belgium), a nd centrifugation (Sorvall Legend XT, Thermo Scientific, Belgium) at 149 
15303 g for 15 min, after which the extracts were partially evaporated and further purified using 150

Agilent C18 cartridges (6 mL, 500 mg) as described by Van Hassel et al., 2022 (Van Hassel et al., 2022b, 151 
2022c). Methanol was UPLC/MS grade (Biosolve B.V., Valkenswaard, The Netherlands). Milli -Q water 152 
was produced in-house (conductivity ≥ 18.2 MΩ and TOC ≤ 4 ppb). After this purification step, the MCs 153 
and NOD-R were separated and quantified via UHPLC –MS/MS (Llana-Ruiz-Cabello et al., 2019; Van 154 
Hassel et al., 2022c). Except for MC-RR, matrix-matched calibration curves in blank basil matrices were 155 
prepared from 0.25 ng L −1 to 50 ng L −1. The latter was from 0.75 ng L −1 to 50 ng L −1 due to matrix 156 
interference (Table S2). The blank refers to a sample of the same food matrix free of MCs. The quality 157 
control (QC) sample was a blank matrix spiked with 25 µL of toxin standard solution (100 ng mL −1) at 158 
5 ng g−1, giving a known toxin concentration. Both served to check the q uality of the analysis. Diluted 159 
toxin standards were prepared in MeOH and Milli -Q water (50:50) with 1% acetic acid. Blank and QC 160 
samples were included in the batch to check the sensitivity of the instrument during the analysis. 161 
Validation of the method fo r the basil matrix follow ed the same methodology described in earlier 162 
work for the fruit and vegetables (Van Hassel et al., 2022c) . An MC and NOD mixture was spiked in 163 
homogenized basil leave matrix at 1, 5, and 25 ng g −1 before extraction. However, the limit of 164 
quantification (LOQ) was only 1 ng g −1 for MC-LR and NOD -R. Therefore, additional concentration 165 
levels were spiked at 5 ng g−1 for MC-RR and 2.5 ng g−1 for MC-YR, MC-LA, MC-LF, MC-WR, MC-LW, and 166 
MC-LY. The LOQ was defined as the concentration for which the quantification of the compound 167 
adhered to all validation parameters. 168 
2.3. ELISA analysis 169 
Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to confirm MC -LR accumulation and 170 
depuration in basil leaf samples (Samdal et al., 2014) . Minor adjustments to concentrations of ELISA 171 
assay reagents, such as 0.5 µg mL −1 of the plate-coating antigen, 1:4000 of antiserum 80289 -5b, and 172 
1:5500 of the donkey–antisheep IgG (H + L)−horseradish peroxidase conjugate (antisheep−HRP from 173 
Agrisera antibodies (Vännäs, Swed en)) were made to optimize the ELISA. The MC -LR standard (NRC 174 
CRM-MC-LR (Lot#20070131)) in 50% MeOH (500 ng mL −1) was diluted in phosphate -buffered saline 175 
with Tween to give a MeOH concentration of 10% and then in a threefold dilution series in sample 176 
buffer, resulting in a standard series of 50, 16.7, 5.56, 0.62, 0.20, 0.069, 0.023, 0.0076, and 0.0025 ng 177 
mL−1. Standards and samples were analyzed with serial dilutions and in duplicate on the plate. All 178 
incubations were performed at 20 °C. Absorbances were measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax 179 
i3x plate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Assay standard curves were fitted using a 4-180 
parameter logistic treatment of the data using SoftMax Pro version 6.5.1. (Molecular Devices, 181 
Sunnyvale, CA, USA). The assay working range was defined as the linear region at 20–80% of maximum 182 
absorbance (Amax). 183

2.4. LC–HRMS analysis 184 
LC-HRMS analysis was performed with a Q Exactive HF Orbitrap mass spectrometer equipped with a 185 
heated electrospray ionization interface (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using an Agilent 186 
1200 G1312B binary pump, G1367C autosampler and G1316B column oven (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, 187 
USA) connected to a Symmetry C18 column (3.5 µm, 150 × 2.1 mm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) held 188 
at 40 °C. Analyses were performed with mobile phases A (H2O) and B (CH3CN), each of which contained 189 
formic acid (0.1% v/v). Gradient elution (0.3 mL/min) was from 15 –100% B over 21 min, then held at 190 
100% B (6 min), then returned to 15% B over 0.1 min with a hold at 15% B (2.9 min) to equilibrate the 191 
column (total run time 30 min), and the injection volume was 2 µL. Full -scan MS data were acquired 192 
at 2–27 min, using alternating positive (m/z 500–1400) and negative (m/z 750–1400) ion scan modes, 193 
with spray voltages of ±3.7 kV, a capillary temperature of 350 °C, sheath and auxiliary gas flow rates 194 
of 25 and 8 units, respectively, a resolution setting of 60,000, an AGC target of 1 ×106, and a max IT of 195 
100 ms. Extracted-ion chromatograms were obtained using exact m/z values with the mass tolerance 196 
set to ±5 ppm. 197 
2.5. Basil sample survey 198 
Commercial samples (n = 50) were collected from Belgian food stores between February and October 199 
2022 to screen for the natural occurrence of MCs in basil. The samples were collected from 12 200 
supermarket chains covering 13 brands. More information on sample number, packaging and sample 201 
type, sampling date, and origin are available in Table S3. 202 
2.6. Statistical analysis 203 
Data processing and statistical analysis were performed with GraphPad Prism version 10. 2.0 204 
(GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA). A Two-way ANOVA test was used to investigate the 205 
effect of two independent variables (1- different treatments and 2- exposure and depuration) on the 206 
detected MC-LR concentrations. Multiple comparisons were performed for all the tested groups and 207 
Tukey’s test was selected as a recommended post-hoc analysis to identify differences between mean 208 
values. Normal (Gaussian) distribution was verified using the Shapiro –Wilk normality test. The 209 
threshold for P-value comparison was set at 0.05 for the significant difference. To investigate the 210 
effect of different treatments on the length and weight of basil plant, a two -way repeated measures 211 
ANOVA test was applied after checking the sphericity and normality . The standard deviation (SD) of 212 
the measured values is presented after each value of the calc ulated mean for these measurements. 213 
The SD represents the spread of biological repeats and thus does not constitute a measure of 214

3. Results 217 
3.1. Effect of MC-LR on basil growth 218 
As a preliminary step to investigate whether there is an effect of MC -LR on basil growth, the weight 219 
and length of each individual plant were measured before and after the MC-LR exposure (seven days 220 
post-spiking). Also, the plant leaves and roots were visually inspected before, during and after the 221 
experiment. There were no significant differences in the average basil weight and length between the 222 
control and the treatment groups ( Figure 1 ). Furthermore, there no lesions or discoloration were 223 
observed on the basil plants. This indicate that the exposure to MC -LR at 5, 20, or 50 µg L −1 for one 224 
week did not have any noticeable inhibitory effects. 225

3.3. Accumulation and depuration of MC-LR in basil 265 
Exposure of basil plant to higher doses of MC-LR resulted in more accumulation in basil roots and 266 
leaves ( i.e., higher accumulation occurred with a higher concentration of MC -LR in the Hoagland 267 
solution). Moreover, MC-LR was detected in higher concentrations in roots than in leaves for all three 268 
treatment groups (Figure 2). The mean values of the MC-LR concentration in basil leaves were 1.6 (SD 269 
= 0.8), 5.7 (SD = 2.0), and 24 (SD = 15) ng g-1 fresh weight (fw) for the 5, 20, and 50 µg L −1 treatment 270 
groups, respectively. In roots, the MC -LR concentrations were 8.5 (SD = 3.1), 17.3 (SD = 3.1), and 49 271 
(SD=17) ng g−1 fw for the 5 µg L−1, 20 µg L−1, and 50 µg L−1 treatment groups, respectively. During the 272 
preliminary experiment with the culture extract, both MC-LR and MC-YR accumulated in the roots and 273 
leaves, similar to their ratio in the original extract ( Table S1). This preliminary test shows that MCs 274 
other than MC-LR could also accumulate in plants, which is essential because multiple MCs are often 275 
present in natural cyanobacterial blooms. 276

4. Discussion 340 
4.1. Effect of MC-LR on basil growth and its accumulation and depuration potentials 341 
Several plant species readily take up MCs, accumulating them in leaves, stems, and roots (Abdallah et 342 
al., 2021; Bittencourt-Oliveira et al., 2016; Peuthert et al., 2007; Xiang et al., 2019). This accumulation 343 
depends on various factors, including the concentration of the toxin in water or soil, the plant species, 344 
and the stage of plant growth (Xiang et al., 2019). Plants are generally more susceptible to MC uptake 345 
during their vegetative growth stage when they actively take up water and nutrients from the soil. The 346 
results obtained from the preliminary assessment of the plant length and weight before and after MC-347 
Journal Pre-proof
15 
 
 
LR treatment showed that MC-LR had no detectable inhibitory effects on basil growth. Therefore, no 348 
further work or assessment was done to investigate the impact of the toxin on the plant. However, 349 
the primary basil roots did decay after the transfer to the Hoagland solution, while smaller secondary 350 
roots developed. This change in root structure probably resulted from the manipulation, as it was also 351 
observed in the untreated control group. Previous work reported different potential negative or 352 
positive effects of MCs (including MC -LR on plant root growth depending on the toxin concentration 353 
and the plant species (Chen et al., 2012; Freitas et al., 2015; McElhiney et al., 2001; Pflugmacher et 354 
al., 2001). The effects of MC exposure on plant growth have been observed in tomatoes (Corbel et al., 355 
2015), potato shoots and mustard seedlings (McElhiney et al., 2001), wheat, carrots, cucumber, beans, 356 
and spinach (Bittencourt-Oliveira et al., 2016; Llana -Ruiz-Cabello et al., 2019; Machado et al., 2017; 357 
Mohamed et al., 2022; Peuthert et al., 2007). 358 
The results obtained during this study clearly show that basil can accumulate MC-LR when the toxin is 359 
available at relevant concentrations during naturally occurring cyanobacterial blooms. Accumulation 360 
of MCs, including MC-LR, through water irrigation was reported in a range of edible crops (Abdallah 361 
et al., 2021; Hereman and Bittencourt-Oliveira, 2012; Machado et al., 2017). In general, this matches 362 
the results obtained in the current work. Peuthert et al. investiga ted MC accumulation both in the 363 
shoots and roots of several agricultural plants (e.g., soybean, lentil, alfalfa, pea, and wheat) using ELISA 364 
(Peuthert et al., 2007) . The roots of these plants accumulated MC -LR at higher concentrations than 365 
leaves. The MCs concentration in the roots and leaves, detected with ELISA, ranged from 13 to 127 µg 366 
kg−1 fw and 3.1 to 31.1 µg kg −1 fw, respectively. Moreover, foliar bioaccumulation of MCs in lettuce 367 
(8.3 to 178 µg kg −1 fw) was shown to be linearly proportional to t he treatment concentration (0.6 to 368 
12.5 µg L−1) (Hereman and Bittencourt-Oliveira, 2012), which is inconsistent with our findings in basil 369 
roots and leaves. 370 
Accumulation of MC -LR in crops could also depend on the irrigation method used (Machado et al., 371 
2017). Earlier ELISA research established that spray irrigation with water contaminated with MC -LR 372 
results in accumulation in leafy crops (Bittencourt-Oliveira et al., 2016; Codd et al., 1999; Crush et al., 373 
2008). Our study showed that MC -LR can also be taken up through the roots and transported to the 374 
leaves. In previous research, lettuce and spinach were exposed to a mixture of MCs and 375 
cylindrospermopsin, each at 5 and 25 µg L−1, using hydroculture, resulting in 0.2±0.1 to 1.3±0.1 µg kg−1 376 
fw of MCs in roots and no detected MCs (LOD = 0.06 µg kg −1 fw) in leaves while using UHPLC-MS/MS 377 
(Llana-Ruiz-Cabello et al., 2019). Similarly, MC-LR was quantified with UHPLC-photodiode-array (PDA) 378 
in edible parts of rice and water spinach ( Ipomoea aquatica), 429.8±4.4 to 567.5±4.9 µg kg −1 fw and 379 
350.8±2.9 µg kg −1 fw respectively, after exposure to cyanobacteria -contaminated water through 380 
Journal Pre-proof
16 
 
 
hydroculture (Wijewickrama and Manage, 2019) . Since plants are constantly in contact with the 381 
enriched solution, the bioavailabil ity of MCs in hydroculture cultures might be higher than in soil -382 
based systems because, in soil, the toxins can be adsorbed to clay minerals and organic matter, flow 383 
to deeper layers, or be degraded by microorganisms (Zhang et al., 2021). 384 
The depuration of natural toxins by plants is a well -known phenomenon. A few studies have 385 
documented this for MCs, including MC -LR in edible crops. One study showed that 25% of the 386 
accumulated MC-LR in lettuce leaves could be depurated after seven days of exposure (10 µg L−1 MC-387 
LR), followed by seven days of irrigation with clean water, while full detoxification of the toxin was 388 
estimated to occur after 37 days (Cordeiro-Araújo et al., 2016) . The study also reported that the 389 
depuration was less efficient at higher levels or concentrations of exposure. In a second study, MC-LR 390 
depuration rates of 9.5 and 8.1 μg kg −1 dw day−1 were reported over 12 days for lettuce and spinach 391 
leaves, respectively (Cao et al., 2019) . Those depuration rates were obtained after a 12 -day 392 
bioaccumulation period of irrigation with water containing MC -LR at 10 µg L −1 preceded the 393 
depuration period of equal length (Cao et al., 2019) . These two studies are consistent with our 394 
findings, with the observed trends in the accumulation and the depurat ion of MC -LR in basil plants 395 
depending on the MC concentration in the Hoagland solution. However, further investigation of the 396 
MC-LR depuration in edible crops, including basil, should be considered under other lab and field 397 
conditions. 398 
Different metabolic pathways may be relevant for the derivatization of MCs in plants (Cao et al., 2019; 399 
Chen et al., 2012) . However, in our study, no known conjugates of MC -LR were detected during LC –400 
HRMS screening of the basil leaf samples. An explanati on could be that conjugates might be present 401 
at ultra-low concentrations in the basil samples and, therefore, not possible to detect with our current 402 
methods. Metabolic pathways should be examined in the future with other approaches. For instance, 403 
radio-labeled MCs could be used to explore the mechanics and kinetics of accumulation and 404 
depuration, as was previously applied to study MC-LR accumulation in tomatoes (Corbel et al., 2016). 405 
Crop type should also be taken into account when studying metabolic mec hanisms. Finally, 406 
understanding the depuration dynamics for different crops might allow the development of 407 
approaches to salvage crop harvests exposed to irrigation water contaminated with MCs. 408 
4.2. Natural occurrence of MC-LR in basil samples collected from the Belgian market 409 
The detection of MC -LR in basil leaves during this (limited) survey further showed that human 410 
exposure to MC-LR through food crops is a growing concern, even with the presence of MC-LR in only 411 
one basil sample. The concentration of MC-LR in the sample can be estimated between 0.6 (LOD) and 412 
Journal Pre-proof
17 
 
 
1 ng g−1 (LOQ), which would probably not constitute a health risk. However, it does confirm that water 413 
containing naturally occurring toxic cyanobacterial blooms is being used to irrigate crops in Belgium . 414 
Until now, two studies found higher MC accumulation in multiple field samples in Asia irrigated with 415 
contaminated water (Wijewickrama and Manage, 2019; Xiang et al., 2019) . These field samples 416 
consisted of various crops (e.g., rice, beans, fruits, leafy and root vegetables). Rice and water spinach 417 
contained 21 and 133 µg kg −1 fw MC-LR, respectively, as quantified with UHPLC - PDA (Wijewickrama 418 
and Manage, 2019). Leafy vegetables, including lettuce, celery, cabbage, and spinach, contained the 419 
highest concentration of MCs (mean values from 9.2 to 118 µg kg−1 fw) compared to fruit (mean values 420 
from 1.4 to 47 µg kg −1 fw) and root vegetables (mean values from 4.9 to 17 µg kg −1 fw) (Xiang et al., 421 
2019). For some samples, the content of MCs exceeded the tole rable daily intake (i.e., maximum 422 
allowed daily exposure to a compound before it becomes harmful) (World Health Organization (WHO), 423 
2020; Xiang et al., 2019). The current work shows that MC -LR accumulation in basil is concentration -424 
dependent. Similar res ults were observed as vegetables cultivated around two Chinese lakes with 425 
higher concentrations of MCs (mean values from 66 to 276 µg L −1 fw) accumulated more MCs 426 
compared to vegetables associated with a third lake with lower a lower concentration of MCs (mean 427 
values from mean 7.7 to 53 µg L−1) (Xiang et al., 2019). Consequently, higher concentrations of MC-LR 428 
in the irrigation water will probably lead to a significantly higher level of bioaccumulated MC-LR in the 429 
edible parts of the plant. This scenario is also likely in Belgium due to prevalent cyanobacterial blooms 430 
throughout Belgium and the dwindling of available water sources during climate -change-driven hot 431 
summers. Ideally, the occurrence of cyanotoxins in crops on the Belgian market should be regul arly 432 
monitored in the future. Additionally, processed foods based on basil leaves, where their water 433 
content is reduced (e.g., pesto sauce and herbs), could lead to a concentration of the toxin, resulting 434 
in potential health risks. The effect of storage, c ooling, and heating on MC -LR stability during the 435 
preparation of basil-based foods and herbs should be considered for future studies. 436 
5. Conclusions 437 
Accumulation of MCs in food crops is becoming more relevant since it poses an environmental and 438 
public health risk. The current study showed that basil can bioaccumulate MC -LR in roots and leaves 439 
when grown in a hydroculture system with MC-LR-contaminated water. More MC-LR concentrations 440 
were detected in basil roots and leaves at higher MC-LR exposure doses. The bioaccumulations were 441 
significantly higher in roots than in leaves for all the treatment conditions. Partial depuration was 442 
achieved when basil plants were placed in a clean Hoagland solution. The detected MC-LR levels after 443 
the depuration were significantly lower than the accumulated MC-LR levels in roots but not in leaves. 444 
Both accumulation and depuration of MC -LR were analyzed using UHPLC –MS/MS, ELISA, and LC –445 
Journal Pre-proof
18 
 
 
HRMS. Basil plants did not show any obvious signs of phytotoxicity since no significant differen ces in 446 
the weight and length were found between the different basil groups before and after the treatment. 447 
As the LC-HRMS analysis did not show any MC -LR conjugates formed by the plant, more research is 448 
required to unravel the depuration mechanism of MC -LR in basil. Although the collection of basil 449 
samples from the market showed a contamination of only one sample with MC-LR, this confirms that 450 
contamination via cyanotoxin-contaminated irrigation water is a fact in Belgium. Given that the effects 451 
of climate change are getting more serious, the occurrence of cyanotoxins in crops might increase. 452 
This study describes the first detection of MC -LR in basil and food crops from the Belgian market. 453 
Therefore, more research on MC accumulation in basil is needed, and y early monitoring of human 454 
food crops for MCs is recommended. 455

Experimental accumulation and depuration kinetics and natural occurrence of 
microcystin-LR in basil 
Highlights: 
 
• Microcystin-LR accumulates dose-dependently in basil grown on a hydroculture medium. 
• Basil plants significantly depurated microcystin-LR after one week of no toxin exposure. 
• The possible detoxification mechanisms in the plant are yet to be verified. 
• The occurrence of microcystin-LR was first-time detected in basil intended for human consumption.
