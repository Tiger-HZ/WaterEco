ABSTRACT 26 
Natural regeneration has been proposed as a cost -effective forest restoration 27 
approach for both small and large -scale initiatives. However, a ttributes for assessing 28 
the success of forest restoration th rough natural regeneration may var y among them 29 
in spatial patterns depending on the scale of analysis and on environmental gradients. 30 
Here w e analy sed the spatial patterns of recovery completeness (i.e. how similar 31 
attributes in restored forests are to the same attributes in reference forests) in 32 
 
2 
response to environmental factors in a Mediterranean forest landscape of Central 33 
Chile. We evaluated (1) forest recovery completeness using basal area (BA), quadratic 34 
mean diameter (QMD), adult species de nsity (ASD), adult species richness (ASR), and 35 
seedling species richness (SSR); (2) the spatial congruence of recovery completeness 36 
estimated by each of these indicators; and (3) the environmental factors potentially 37 
shaping these spatial patterns. We used field measurements and geospatial 38 
information sources to quantify and predict indicator responses by fitting boosted 39 
regression tree models. To assess the spatial congruence of predictions we overlaid 40 
high-level recovery completeness values for all indica tors. Overall recovery 41 
completeness in the study area was 72.7%, suggesting positive prospect s for attaining 42 
fully restored forests. Recovery completeness had a resulted higher for diversity 43 
(92.3% - 99.6%) than structural forest attributes ( 33.5% - 76.9%); h owever, spatial 44 
congruence among recovery indicators was low due to the uneven spatial responses of 45 
each indicator. The maximum potential spatial congruence was <10%, and was 46 
predicted only by two environmental variables (soil bulk density and slope). Our results 47 
suggest that low spatial congruence among forest recovery indicators may hinder the 48 
monitoring of restoration at large scales. The implications of such divergence in 49 
defining restoration success can be enormous given the current global challenge of 50 
forest restoration. Although our research was tested in a threatened region of global 51 
importance, our results may have wider significance for restoration planning providing 52 
cautionary notes and recommendations for the appropriate use of forest recovery 53 
indicators when monitoring large-scale restoration projects. 54

1. Introduction 59 
Forest l oss continues to be a major global environmental problem, even in high -60 
conservation-value areas (Allan et al., 2017; Potapov et al., 2017). Even minimal forest 61 
loss may have severe consequences for biodiversity (Betts et al., 2017) , and 62 
compromise carbon s torage and other ecosystem services (Millennium Ecosystem 63 
Assessment, 2005; Rappaport et al., 2018). Forest loss due to advance s of the 64 
 
3 
agricultural frontier and fires greatly increase s the human footprint in areas not 65 
subject to outright deforestation (Venter et al., 2016), and this can be exacerbated by 66 
climate change (e.g. by more frequent droughts and longer dry seasons; Seidl et al., 67 
2017). Ecological restoration has emerged to counteract these transform ative 68 
processes by promoting a progressive increa se in the extent and functionality of 69 
imperilled ecosystems (McDonald et al., 2016). Some global and regional commitments 70 
have been made to deal with these issues; most notably, the Bonn Challenge (IUCN, 71 
2011) and the New York Declaration on Forests (Summi t, 2014) aim to restore 150 72 
million hectares of forest worldwide by 2020 and 350 million hectares by 2030 73 
respectively. 74 
Achieving these ambitious goals requires a combination of different approaches 75 
that need to be implemented on a large scale and provide cost-efficient solutions. 76 
Natural regeneration has been proposed as a cost -effective approach to forest 77 
restoration, as it is a nature -based tool that enhances resilience, supports local 78 
biodiversity, and supplies multiple ecosystem goods and servi ces (Chazdon, 2017). 79 
Natural regeneration can play a beneficial economic and ecological role in large -scale 80 
forest and landscape restoration (Chazdon and Guariguata, 2016). However, it may be 81 
challenging in some forest types , such as the Mediterranean fore sts in southeast 82 
Australia, California, southwest South Africa, the Mediterranean Basin, and Central 83 
Chile (Acácio et al., 2007; Rey Benayas et al., 2007; Mucina et al., 2017). 84 
Restoring Mediterranean forests through natural regeneration may be a hard 85 
task (Vallejo et al., 2012), but it can offer an alternative or complementary approach to 86 
restoration under certain circumstances (Mucina et al., 2017). For instance, 87 
spontaneous increase of forest cover in degraded areas in Central Chile has mostly 88 
been restricted to the proximity of remnant forests and the presence of nurse plants 89 
(Schulz et al., 2010, 2017; Fuentes -Castillo et al., 2012). These regenerating forests 90 
occur in areas with high levels of environmental heterogeneity and inter -annual 91 
climatic variation (Doblas-Miranda et al., 2015; Venegas -González et al. 2018). Current 92 
predictions of land cover changes, increasing temperature and precipitation reduction 93 
(Sillmann et al., 2013) , which may lead to desertification (Kefi et al., 2007), are not 94 
favourable for the natural regeneration of Mediterranean forests (Garreaud et al., 95 
2017). Restoring this forest type is crucial, considering that it is present only in regions 96 
 
4 
with a Mediterranean climate, and even covering less than 5% of the Earth’s surface it 97 
contains a relatively high proportion of human population . This ecosystem type is one 98 
of the least protected in the world (Millennium Ecosystem Assessment, 2005), and is 99 
highly sensitive to global change (Sala et al., 2000). Moreover, ecosystems in these 100 
regions have usually suffer ed long-standing anthropogenic pressure ; they are among 101 
the most seriously threatened in the world due to s ignificant current human activity 102 
(Gauquelin et al., 2018). 103 
Under restoration scenarios, including either natural regener ation or tree-104 
planting, the attributes used to monitor forest recovery are important not only for 105 
understanding regeneration success, but also for defining realistic goals (McDonald et 106 
al., 2016). Several attributes have been proposed for monitoring and quantifying the 107 
recovery of forest structure and functions. Criteria such as easy and cost -effective 108 
measurement and easy interpretation have most often been preferred; for instance, 109 
species diversity (e.g. tree richness and diversity), community structure (e.g. tree 110 
diameter and height), abiotic conditions (both microclimatic an d edaphic), and some 111 
ecological processes (e.g. litterfall production, nutrient cycling, pollination, erosion 112 
control) are widely used to assess outcomes of forest restoration (Ruiz -Jaén and Aide, 113 
2005; Wortley et al., 2013; Gatica -Saavedra et al., 2017). In general, studies consider a 114 
forest as successfully restored when the values of the attributes measured are 115 
indistinguishable from, or at least similar to , the values in reference forest (McDonald 116 
et al., 2016; Suganuma et al., 2018). In this context, the attributes of recovery 117 
completeness (i.e. how similar attributes in restored forests are to the same attributes 118 
in reference forests) may be strongly spatially correlated (Kacholi et al., 2015), but may 119 
also show large -scale variation and a low degree of spatial congruence depending on 120 
environmental gradients. Therefore evaluating the spatial patterns of forest attributes 121 
is an important task for quantifying forest recovery and moni toring forest restoration 122 
at large scales. 123 
The main question of this study was what degree of spatial congruence exists 124 
among the typical attributes used to assess the success of forest restoration? To 125 
address this question we quantitatively evaluated spatial patterns of recovery 126 
completeness and their responses to environmental factors in a Mediterranean forest 127 
landscape in Central Chile using five common indicators of forest restoration success. 128 
 
5 
We evaluate : (1) forest recovery completeness of basal area, quadratic mean 129 
diameter, adult species density and richness, and seedling species richness; (2) the 130 
spatial congruence of these five indicators; and (3) the importance of climatic, 131 
topographical and edaphic environmental factors in predicting the spatial distribution 132 
(and thus the spatial congruence) of the responses of these indicators. We discuss how 133 
the insights from this research may be used to inform future actions and to facilitate 134 
the implementation of more cost -effective forest restoration program mes in this 135 
global biodiversity hotspot (Myers et al., 2000), and also in large -scale restoration 136 
initiatives. We expect that our results will help others to define realistic restoration 137 
goals and actions in a context of global change. 138

2. Methods 140 
2.1. Study site 141 
Our study was conducted in the Mediterranean region of Chile (Fig. 1). It includes the 142 
Valparaíso and Metropolitan administrative regions located between 33° and 34° 143 
South and covers 31,640 km 2, with elevations ranging between the sea level and 1,835 144 
m.a.s.l. Mean annual precipitation is 500 mm, and is concentrated in winter (June – 145 
August). Mean annual temperature is 14.5°C (Leubert and Pliscoff, 2006), ranging from 146 
21°C in summer (January) to 7°C in w inter (July). Summer temperatures often reach 147 
above 30°C. Local microclimatic conditions can differ widely between coastal and 148 
inland areas; the Coastal range has milder conditions in terms of temperature and 149 
drought stress and is therefore more favourable for seedling establishment (Holmgren 150 
et al., 2000). Plant species composition differs according to these microclimatic 151 
variations but also to topographical and latitudinal differences (Villagrán, 1995; 152 
Luebert and Pliscoff, 2006). Sclerophyllous Mediterranean forest is the domina nt 153 
ecosystem, composed mainly of woody species such as Cryptocarya alba and Peumus 154 
boldus in the Coastal range, and Lithraea caustica and Quillaja saponaria inland and in 155 
the pre -Andean range (Leubert and P liscoff, 2006). The region has historically been 156 
subjected to landscape fragmentation and degradation, with profound transformation 157 
of the original vegetation due to intense urbanization and agricultural activities (Schulz 158 
et al., 2010; Vergara et al., 201 3). It presents the highest historical relative habitat loss 159 
 
6 
in South America, with just 20% remaining of the original vegetation cover (Salazar et 160 
al., 2015). 161 
 162 
2.2. Quantification of recovery completeness 163 
We used panchromatic aerial photographs dating fr om the 1970s and 80s, acquired 164 
from the Aerophotogrammetric Service ( www.saf.cl) and recent Google Earth satellite 165 
imagery from 2013. By visual analysis we identified all patches where forest cover was 166 
absent in the aerial photographs (1970-80s) but fully recovered in the Google Earth 167 
images (2013). This implies that we identified fo rest patches under regeneration 168 
ranging from 25 to 35 years old, which we will call "regeneration sites". We identified 169 
200 regeneration sites, but due to accessibility constraints we visited only 85 (47%) of 170 
them between December 2014 and October 2015. Fo r each regeneration site visited, 171 
we selected a "reference site" which was: (i) the nearest remnant forest existing from 172 
the 1970s according to the aerial images; and (ii) located with the same topographical 173 
aspect as the regeneration site. Reference sites were chosen to enable us to compare 174 
their forest attributes with those in the regeneration sites. 175 
In each regeneration and reference site we established a plot of 20x20 m (400 176 
m²) in which we estimated five forest attributes (Appendix 1) : (1) basal area (BA); (2) 177 
quadratic mean diameter (QMD); (3) adult species density (ASD); (4) adult species 178 
richness (ASR); and (5) seedling species richness (SSR). We used these attributes 179 
because they are those most frequently used to assess restoration success ( Wortley, 180 
2013). For the first four indicators we only considered tree and shrub species with 181 
DBH >5 cm. For seedling species richness, we counted all seedlings with DBH <5cm and 182 
height <2 m along two cross-sectional transects up to 1 m wide on the main axes of the 183 
plot oriented North -South and East-West. We only considered seedlings developed 184 
from seeds because they represent new individuals. 185 
For each indicator and plot we estimated recovery completeness as RC = ln[(Xreg 186 
+0.01)/(Xref+0.01)], where X reg represents the attribute value in the regeneration plot 187 
while Xref represents the value of the same attribute in the reference plot. This formula 188 
is an adapted version of the response ratio proposed by Hedges et al. (1999) to 189 
compare relative changes among indicato rs using different unit measure ments. The 190 
formula linearizes the metric, such that deviations in the numerator are treated in the 191 
 
7 
same way as deviations in the denominator, and the variable typically shows a normal 192 
distribution. The formula includes a corr ection (+0.01) in both the numerator and 193 
denominator to account for zero-percentages in both regeneration and reference sites 194 
(De Frenne et al., 2011). RC tends toward zero as the Xreg value approaches the Xref 195 
value. To facilitate interpretation , RC values were back -transformed and converted to 196 
percentages. Thus RC may vary between 0 % (no recovery) and 100% (full recovery). 197 
We established a minimum value of 70% for RC to be considered as a ‘high level’. 198

2.3. Spatial congruence of recovery completeness 200 
We evaluated the spatial distribution of only high -level RC for all indicators on the 201 
landscape scale, and finally examined the ir spatial congruence by overla ying the 202 
individual maps. RC p redictions were calculated for the landscape dominated by 203 
natural forest and shrubland land cover types (1,291,656 ha or 41% of the whole study 204 
area). We considered shrublands for these estimations because this land cover type 205 
represents the successional stage previous to forests. 206 
 207 
2.4. Modelling recovery completeness using environmental predictors 208 
We constructed a set of environmental variable maps from spatially explicit data on 209 
climate, topography, and soil characteristics (Appendix 1). We used the following 210 
climatic variables: maximum temperature of warmest month (Tma x), mean 211 
temperature of warmest quarter (Tmea), mean temperature of coldest quarter (Tmin), 212 
precipitation seasonality (Psea), precipitation of driest quarter (Pmin), heat load 213 
(Hload), and aridity index (Aind). For topography, we used elevation (Elev), slo pe 214 
(Slop), and distance to coast (Dcoas). Finally, for soil variables, we used bulk density 215 
(Bden), cation exchange capacity (Cec), soil pH measured in H 2O (SpH), and soil organic 216 
carbon (SoC) (Appendix 1 and 2). We examined the correlation matrix of all t hese 217 
explanatory variables and excluded those that were highly correlated (|r| > 0.6) to 218 
avoid multicollinearity for model-building purposes. 219 
For statistical modelling, we used boosted regression trees (BRT). Decision trees 220 
are generated linking the expla natory variables to the dependent variables. This 221 
technique generates many regression trees that are combined into one ultimate 222 
regression tree model, boosting the ultimate model’s accuracy and predictive 223 
 
8 
performance drastically (Elith et al. , 2008). We generated regression trees using the 224 
gbm package in R (Ridgeway, 2007). This procedure uses thr ee variables: learning rate 225 
(lr), bag fraction ( bf), and tree complexity ( tc). To obtain the optimal combination of 226 
these variables, a series of models was generated using different values of lr and tc 227 
based on previous studies (Elith et al., 2008). Then w e calculated measure ments of 228 
model performance for each fitted model (i.e. percentage explained deviance; D²) 229 
(Littke et al., 2014) , the relative root mean square error (RMSE) (Aertsen et al., 2010) , 230 
and the correlation between observed and predicted values. For each model, we chose 231 
those p redictor variables which presented a strong relationship with the response 232 
variable (i.e. >10% of influence in BTR models). The model estimates the relative 233 
influence of predictor variables (influence). The influence is based on the number of 234 
times a variable is selected for splitting, weighted by the squared improvement to the 235 
model as a result of each split, and averaged over all trees ( Elith et al., 2008 ). The 236 
relative influence of each variable was scaled so that the sum totalled 100, with higher 237 
numbers indicating stronger influence. 238

3. Results 240 
3.1. Recovery completeness by indicators 241 
Reference plots mostly showed higher values than regeneration plots for all recovery 242 
indicators except seedling species richness (Appendix 2). The highest values were 243 
found for maximum basal area and quadratic mean diameter in reference plots, which 244 
were 42.4 m² ha-1 and 36.3 cm respectively. 245 
 Overall RC was 72.7% and ranged between 99.6% and 33.5% (CV = 36.3%), 246 
showing a high variation among indicators (Fig. 2). Species richness, both for seedlings 247 
(99.6%) and adults (92.3%) showed almost full RC and intermediate spatial variation 248 
(coefficient of variation 54% and 42% respectively). Structural attributes showed 249 
intermediate to low RC values: 76.9% (CV = 48%) for adult s pecies density, 61.4% (CV = 250 
34%) for quadratic mean diameter, and only 33.5%, with the highest spatial variation 251 
(CV = 67%), for basal area. 252 
 253 
3.2. Spatial patterns and congruence of recovery completeness 254 
 
9 
Landscape predictions of recovery completeness sho wed marked ly different spatial 255 
patterns (Fig. 3a -e). Basal area presented predominantly low levels of RC; the highest 256 
values corresponded to small patches mainly restricted to the pre -Andean range (Fig. 257 
3a). Recovery completeness of quadratic mean diameter showed a heterogeneous 258 
distribution with the highest values concentrated in the centr e of the Coastal range 259 
(Fig. 3b). Higher recovery completeness for adult species density was mainly confined 260 
to patches located in the Coastal and pre -Andean range s in the study area (Fig 3c). 261 
Adult species richness showed a clear pattern of low levels of recovery completeness 262 
distributed throughout the whole study area (Fig. 3d), while seedlings species richness 263 
mostly exhibited intermediate levels of recovery completenes s, with the few high 264 
levels concentrated in the southern and northern parts of the study area (Fig. 3e). 265 
 The area of high -level RC varied among indicators, being higher for adult 266 
species density and quadratic mean diameter and lower for adult species rich ness and 267 
basal area (Table 1). Predictions at landscape scale estimated a potential area of 268 
maximum spatial congruence for high -level RC (i.e. ≥70%) at 94,576 ha (7% of the 269 
landscape area) (Fig. 3f). However, when the minimum of two indicators was used , 270 
spatial congruence of high -level RC represented 424,697 ha (33% of the landscape 271 
area). 272 
 273 
3.3. Environmental predictors of recovery completeness 274 
All models showed good performance in terms of explained deviance (D 2) and errors 275 
(rRMSE <20%), and all relied on few explanatory variables, reducing the likelihood of 276 
statistical artefact (Table 2). BA, QMD and ASR models provided the best goodness of 277 
fit (i.e. highest explained deviance and highest correlation between the observed and 278 
fitted values). Two e nvironmental variables predicting RC showed the highest spatial 279 
congruence (i.e. in more than two models): bulk density (for models BA, QMD, ASR and 280 
SSR), and slope (for models QMD, ASD, and ASR). The most important predictor 281 
variables were bulk density, mean temperature of warmest quarter and distance to 282 
coast, which had ≥40% influence on RC indicator responses (Fig. 4). 283

4. Discussion 285 
 
10 
This study provide s an example of monitoring natural forest recovery in a globally 286 
important biodiversity region (Mittermeier et al., 2004). We visually selected forest 287 
patches under regeneration ranging from 25 to 35 years old. Recovery completeness 288 
was high for almost all the five indicators used; the spatial patterns of their responses 289 
were uneven and spati al congruence among indicators was low. These results have 290 
implications for the efficient provision of guidance for large-scale restoration efforts, 291 
as we discuss below. 292 
 293 
4.1 Recovery completeness levels and spatial patterns 294 
RC gave a higher result for spe cies diversity indicators – namely species richness of 295 
seedlings and of adult individuals, which attained nearly full recovery – than for the 296 
vegetation structure indicator. Diversity indicators tend to rise faster in the first years 297 
of plant colonization and later may stabilize or even decrease (Rosenzweig, 1995) ; 298 
however, long life-span pioneer species can persist until later successional stages in 299 
some forests (Gutiérrez et al., 2008) , maintaining species diversity. Moreover, high 300 
seedling diversity can be associated with pioneer species after disturbance (e.g. forest 301 
fire, harvesting) (Castoldi et al., 2015; Duah -Gyamfi et al., 2015). Diversity indicators , 302 
in particular species richness, are common ly used to assess natural forest recovery 303 
(e.g. Orsi et al., 2011; Karsten et al., 2014; Gatica -Saavedra et al., 2017), but they 304 
should be used with caution as high levels of diversity outcomes do not necessarily 305 
imply successful forest recovery (Meli et al., 2017). Restoring plant species diversity of 306 
a target community might be unrealistic , given that recovery could take several 307 
decades or even more. In any case, species diversity cannot be taken as the sole 308 
criterion for evaluating restoration success (Engest et al., 2016) ; therefore other 309 
metrics must be considered, such as species composition or functionality diversity that 310 
may potentially show slower recovery (Petchey and Gaston, 2006; Meli et al., 2017). 311 
The highest and lowest spatial variations of RC were found for basal area and 312 
quadratic mean diameter respectively. Quadratic mean diameter would present low 313 
variability since it represents the average diameter of all the trees of each site, 314 
whereas basal area is rather used as an indicator of forest successional stage (e.g. 315 
canopy closure) (Gutiérrez and Huth, 2012) . Additionally, basal area is strongly 316 
influenced by the time elapsed since regeneration started , land use history and site 317 
 
11 
quality (i.e. climate, soil), and is therefore a good indicator of forest recovery (Toledo 318 
et al., 2011). 319 
 320 
4.2. Spatial congruence of recovery completeness 321 
The low spatial congruence in the RC of the individual indicators used in this work 322 
highlights the importance of indicator selection for monitoring restoration (Gatica-323 
Saavedra et al., 2017) . Given the high variation of RC and their spatial distribution 324 
throughout the study area, our results suggest that targeting appropriate indicators is 325 
critical for assessing the success of natural forest recovery. We observed low spatial 326 
congruence of high-level values of all indicators, and s patial congruence levels 327 
increased as fewer indicators were used. Furthermore, in the early stages of the 328 
successional process ‒ when the tree canopy still appear s highly dynamic – forest 329 
recovery is strongly influenced by environmental gr adients, landscape structure and 330 
land use history, restoration effort, and elapsed time since the project took place 331 
(Crouzeilles et al., 2016; Meli et al., 2017). 332 
Our most remarkable finding is probably the low spatial congruence of RC 333 
indicators. The ma ximum spatial congruence of all recovery indicators is lower than 334 
10%. Congruence increases as our restriction s are softened, for instance when using 335 
only two indicators ; however, this higher congruence was found in basal area and 336 
quadratic mean diameter, which are high ly correlated. Low spatial congruence of 337 
indicators was also consistent with the congruence of environmental predictors. In 338 
fact, only two environmental variables (bulk density and slope) predicting responses of 339 
RC indicators presented higher spatial congruence. This implies that if we use these 340 
indicators separately to plan large -scale forest restoration , the resulting location area 341 
would differ considerably. Then, one of the main advantages of natural regeneration 342 
(i.e. cost-efficiency; Birch et al., 2010) as a strategy for large-scale restoration planning 343 
may be compromised if we fail to choose suitable indicators. As a result of this 344 
mismatch, the potential of natural regeneration to restore the multiple social and 345 
ecological benefits of landscapes could be reduced (Chadzon and Uriarte, 2016). 346 
 347 
4.3. Implications for planning and monitoring large-scale restoration 348 
 
12 
The high variation among the selected RC indicators stimulates reflection about 349 
indicators selection. The goals of a restoration project are decisive for how the 350 
project’s recovery success is measured, and also for defining restoration priorities (Orsi 351 
and Geneletti, 2010). Holl and Aide (2011) highlighted the importance of clearly 352 
identifying goals at the onset of a restoration project, ideally in consultation with all 353 
stakeholders, and using cost -effective methods (Almeida et al. 2019) . For example, if 354 
the goal of a restoration project is to maximize carbon sequestration t hrough natural 355 
forest recovery , then an indicator of crown cover may be advisable (e.g. basal area, 356 
tree density), whereas if the goal is to increase biodiversity, a measure of plant 357 
diversity could be a suitable indicator (e.g. species diversity, number o f target species) 358 
(e.g. Feest , 2013 ). In summary, we must ask: what attributes of forests should 359 
characterize the success of ecosystem service s supply? The answer will guide the 360 
development and assessment of the restoration project , but in turn we must consider 361 
that on a large-scale these questions are influenced by environmental gradients. 362 
When focus ed at large-scale, the restoration agenda should be embedded in 363 
the methodological approach of ‘Forest Landscape Restoration’, the aim of which is 364 
the recovery of multifunctional landscapes beyond the restoration of natural 365 
ecosystems (Schulz and Schröder, 2017). This approach broadens the restoration 366 
targets toward the recognition that ecosystems and landscapes need to be recovered 367 
to provide goods and services to human society (Stanturf et al., 2014; Wang et al., 368 
2014). Therefore, restoration projects addressing multifunctional landscapes must 369 
explicitly consider different indicators of RC linked to ecosystem functions. Some 370 
studies have assessed resto ration success by applying a functional perspective (e.g. 371 
Engs et al., 2016; Hedberg et al., 2014; Laughlin, 2014), sometimes directly measuring 372 
function indicators such as nutrient and water supply (Little et al., 2009; Van Bodegom 373 
et al., 2006). However, recovery of ecosystem functions can be slower than other 374 
attributes and therefore its application in early stages of restoration projects may be 375 
limited (Meli et al., 2017; Moreno -Mateos et al. 2018). Likewise, the outcomes of 376 
ecosystem function s are observed on other spatial and temporal scales (Greene and 377 
Millward, 2017). 378 
A salient characteristic of natural regeneration as an approach for forest 379 
restoration is that it is highly cost-effective (Birch et al., 2010), since it does not involve 380 
 
13 
direct financial costs, including seedlings, planting, fences , and maintenance 381 
(Schiappacasse et al., 2012). Therefore, it is highly desirable to decide on suitable RC 382 
indicators, which will help to ensure that the indicators that we use to monit or our 383 
restoration goals are consistent with those goals . Regardless of the restoration 384 
strategy used, the global challenge of forest restoration implies rethink ing the stages 385 
of defining the large-scale, long-term aims of projects. As part of this challen ge, large-386 
scale, long-term forest restoration projects must include the ecological, economic, and 387 
social dimension in order to regain ecological integrity and enhance human well -being 388 
in deforested or degraded forest landscapes (Chazdon and Uriarte, 2016). 389 
Given the current global challenge of ecological restoration , the implications of 390 
low spatial congruence of RC for the definition of restoration success can be 391 
enormous. The CBD and several countries have established goals on this issue. Target 392 
15 of the Aichi Targets for 2020 sets a numerical goal of restoration of 15% of 393 
degraded ecosystems, achieving which will require billions of d ollars per year (CBD, 394 
2010). Chile is no exception , and the Government is com mitted to achieving the same 395 
goal by 2020 (Smith -Ramírez et al., 2015). However, forest restoration needs to 396 
become successful at landscape scale in order to achieve its goals (Ba nnister et al., 397 
2018). Thus, how the restoration success of these degraded ecosystems is measured is 398 
an important task anywhere. 399 
Our results have wider significance for restoration planning around the world, 400 
providing cautionary notes and recommendations for appropriate use of forest 401 
recovery indicators to monitor large-scale restoration projects. 402

Appendix 1. Description of forest attributes and environmental variables from 705

Forest attributes: 707 
Basal area (BA): the cross-sectional area of all trees in the sampling plot. To estimate 708 
BA the tree diameter is measured at breast height (1.3m ) above the ground. BA is 709 
expressed in m2 ha-1. 710 
Quadratic mean diameter (QMD): diameter of a tree possessing the average basal area 711 
per tree in the sampling plot. QMD is expressed in cm. 712 
Adult species density (ASD): total number of adult individuals of different species. 713 
Adult species richness (ASR): total number of different adult species. 714 
Seedling species richness (SSR): total number of different seedling species. 715 
 716 
Climatic variables: we initially obtained 19 climatic variables from the WorldClim 717 
database ( www.worldclim.org). WorldClim is a set of global climate layers (climate 718 
grids) with a spatial resolution of 1 x 1 km (Hijmans et al., 2005). This set includes 719 
temperature, rainfall and bioclima tic variables. We carefully examined the correlation 720 
matrix to determine the degree of collinearity and redundancy between these climatic 721 
variables (and the other explanatory variables), as well as their correlations with 722 
indicators of forest recovery. As a result we selected the following climatic variables 723 
for our analyses: Maximum temperature of warmest month (Tmax), Mean 724 
temperature of warmest quarter (Tmea), Mean temperature of coldest quarter (Tmin), 725 
Precipitation seasonality (Psea), Precipitation of driest quarter (Pmin). We also 726 
estimated the heat load (Hload) and the aridity index (Aind). To estimate heat load we 727 
used aspect, which was transformed into a relative heat -load measurement (McCune 728 
and Keon, 2002). The a ridity index was extracted from the ‘Global Aridity and PET 729 
Database’ developed by Zomer (2007) and Zomer et al. (2008). The complete dataset is 730 
available at http://www.cgiar-csi.org. The aridity dataset was modelled using the 731 
WorldClim database with a spatial resolution of 1 km. 732
