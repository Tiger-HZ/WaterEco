Atmospheric water harvesting (AWH) is limited by the high energy demand for
sorbent regeneration, while batterythermal management dissipates sub-
stantial low-grade heat unused. Here, we propose a thermally symbiotic
architecture integrating these systems. Battery discharge waste heat regen-
erates a MOF-303 sorbent layer via desorption, while the sorption process
passively stabilizes battery temperature. The sorbent’s regeneration enthalpy
aligns with the battery’s thermal output, triggering desorption under−12 to
−20 A discharge. Within 46 min, the MOF exceeds 50 °C, releasing vapor for
condensation in a sealed setup. Concurrently, sorption extracts heat from the
battery, reducing peak temperatures by ~10 °C. Notably, this integrated system
achieves a water production rate of 1.39 g g−1 day−1, a 117% increase over solar-
driven counterparts. Finite-element simulations validate the heat transfer
dynamics and identify key thermal efﬁciency parameters. This work estab-
lishes a closed-loop thermal cycle that repurposes energy-storage waste heat
to power AWH, enabling sorbent regeneration without solar heating. The
strategy offers a pathway to enhance thermal efﬁciency across various energy
and environmental systems through internal heat recovery.

environment supported by the host 1,2. A similarly interdependent

In engineered thermal systems, symbiotic coupling of compo-
nents with opposing thermal requirements offers an opportunity to
enable efﬁcient energy utilization. Lithium-ion batteries—compared to
traditional battery chemistries such as nickel–cadmium, nickel–metal
hydride, and lead –acid—provide a superior combination of high
energy density, long cycle life, minimal self-discharge, high voltage,
low maintenance, and rapid charging 7,8. However, lithium batteries
generate signiﬁcant heat during discharge due to internal resistance
and electrochemical reactions, which can lead to thermal runaway
once it rises to overtemperature 9–11. In this hazardous condition, the
battery’s temperature increases uncontrollably, potentially causing
ﬁres or explosions12,13. To mitigate this risk, battery thermal manage-
ment (BTM) is widely employed to enhance the battery ’s heat dis-
sipation capabilities, which is crucial for maintaining optimal operating
temperatures below 55 °C and ensuring safety, performance, and
longevity13,14. Based on the cooling medium, BTM can be classiﬁed into
air cooling, liquid cooling, and composite phase change material
(CPCM) cooling BTMs15,16. While effective in limiting thermal buildup,
these cooling strategies are inherently dissipative, discarding the bat-
tery’s thermal energy into the environment without functional recov-
ery, thus representing a major limitation in thermal resource
utilization.
In contrast to BTM systems that require heat extraction, certain
emerging environmental technologies depend on continuous heat
input to function. One such application is sorption-based AWH, which
has gained attention for its ability to extract water vapor from ambient
air and convert it into liquid water 17–19. This technology captures
atmospheric moisture even at low relative humidity (RH) and converts
it into potable water, typically using solar irradiance for desorption20,21.
A key advantage of sorption-based AWH is its geographical adapt-
ability and scalability. The performance of these systems hinges on the
properties of the sorbent materials, primarily MOFs, covalent organic
frameworks (COFs) 22, zeolites 23,s i l i c ag e l24, hygroscopic salts 25,
hydrogels26,27,a n dt h e i rh y b r i d s28–31. Among these, MOFs such as MOF-
30332,M O F - 8 0 133, Al-Fum31,M I L - 1 6 034,a n dU i O - 6 635 stand out due to
their high surface area, tunability, low regeneration temperature, and
exceptional selectivity, enabling adsorption at sub-30% RH
conditions36,37. Recent advances in continuous-operation AWH devices
have enabled multiple adsorption–desorption cycles per day, drama-
tically improving water output. 33,38,39 However, in regions lacking
consistent sunlight or where solar irradiance is insufﬁcient, such sys-
tems struggle to maintain continuous regeneration, prompting alter-
native strategies that require externally paid thermal input 40,41.T h i s
approach may not be viable in resource-limited arid environments,
where both water scarcity and energy access are major constraints37.
Interestingly, the thermal demands of BTM and AWH systems are
inherently complementary: BTM produces low-grade heat that is
typically dissipated, while AWH systems require precisely such low-
grade thermal energy for desorption. The operating temperature
range of lithium batteries during high-rate discharge42 coincides well
with the initial regeneration temperatures of water-saturated MOFs
used in AWH43, creating a thermodynamic basis for coupling the two.
In a conceptual BTM–AWH hybrid system, thermal energy generated
during battery discharge can be redirected to regenerate the sorbent
through passive heat transfer. In an integrated BTM –AWH system,
thermal energy released during battery discharge is harnessed to drive
desorption in the sorbent layer, while the sorbent simultaneously acts
as a thermal sink, which absorbs latent and sensible heat during
moisture uptake, reducing peak b attery temperature and enabling
passive regeneration. This bidirectional exchange, in which the ther-
mal output of one subsystem drives the operation of the other while
simultaneously receiving stabilizing feedback, establishes a closed-
loop, self-regulating partnership. This integrated approach is termed
“Thermal symbiosis ”. It extends beyond traditional unidirectional
waste-heat recovery by forming a mutually reinforcing system that
enhances thermal management and functional output without a
dedicated external heat source for sorbent regeneration.
Despite this conceptual promise, there have been no prior
demonstrations of such a thermally symbiotic strategy, nor estab-
lished frameworks for integrating AWH and BTM subsystems through
direct thermal coupling. To address this gap, we developed a proto-
type system that couples a lithium battery pack with a passive
sorption-based atmospheric water generator (AWG) containing a
MOF-303 layer. This architecture enables bidirectional thermal inter-
action: waste heat from battery discharge is transferred through a
thermally conductive copper-based interface to drive MOF regenera-
tion, while the sorbent layer simultaneously buffers temperature
spikes during cycling. Experimental results show that at a discharge
current of −16 A, the MOF layer passively reaches temperatures above
50 °C within 46 min, initiating effective desorption and moisture
release. Concurrently, the sorption module extracts both latent and
sensible heat, reducing the battery ’s peak temperature by approxi-
mately 10 °C and allowing safe operation under elevated current loads.
To overcome the mismatch in temporal operating pro ﬁles between
battery discharge and sorbent regeneration, we implement an asyn-
chronous strategy in which three battery modules alternately supply
heat to a common AWG unit, enabling up to 15 regeneration cycles
per day without relying on solar heating. Notably, the 24 h continuous
operation enabled a water production rate of 1.39 g(water) g(MOF)
−1 day−1,
a 117% increase over the solar-driven system. This enhancement,
stemming from the doubled operational time and a steady, weather-
independent heat source, establishes the method as a more ef ﬁcient
and reliable strategy, particularly for sun-limited regions.
To elucidate the internal therma l behavior governing this ther-
mally symbiotic system, we performedﬁnite-element thermal simula-
tions that trace spatiotemporal heat-transfer pathways within the
coupled modules. The simulations reveal critical design parameters—
including interface thermal conductivity and battery discharge proﬁles
—that in ﬂuence thermal transport ef ﬁciency and desorption perfor-
mance. Together, our experimental and computational analyses
demonstrate a self-regulating, closed-loop thermal integration strat-
egy that refunctionalizes waste heat from energy storage systems to
drive decentralized water harvesting. This is a report to realize AWH
and BTM as a single thermally symbiotic design. By harmonizing dis-
parate thermal requirements through shared heat infrastructure, this
framework offers a versatile blueprint for integrating heat-intensive
and heat-generating subsystems. The broader principle of “thermal
symbiosis” introduced here can be extended to a wide range of energy
and environmental technologies seeking improved thermal efﬁciency
through intra-system heat circulation.

A thermally symbiotic BTM–AWH architecture
As illustrated in Fig. 1a, AWH with MOFs typically involves a two-step
cycle: adsorption and desorption. Initially, at lower ambient tempera-
tures, the MOF adsorbs water vapour, reaching a hydrated state.
Subsequently, during the regeneration phase, the temperature is ele-
vated—typically above 40 °C—inducing desorption and returning the
MOF to a dehydrated state 39,44. This desorption step is energy-inten-
sive, often requiring an external heat source. Interestingly, this heat
demand aligns with the thermal management characteristics of
lithium-ion batteries. During operation, batteries generate signiﬁcant

waste heat, which must be dissipated to maintain temperatures below
a safe operating threshold of 55 °C. This creates a complementary
supply-demand relationship: the AWH system requires heat for deso-
rption, while the battery produces excess heat that must be removed.
We captured infrared images of battery packs with and without an
integrated MOF-based AWH system. Under identical discharge condi-
tions, the battery pack with the B TM-AWH system exhibited a lower
temperature. This temperature reduction indicates that a portion of
the battery’s waste heat was transferred to drive the AWH process,
thereby supporting the concept of thermal symbiosis.
The system comprised two main components, as depicted in
Fig. 1b. The ﬁrst component, referred to as the sorption unit, consisted
of a 2.5 × 6.5 × 7.5 cm copper container housing a 5 g MOF-303 layer for
water absorption and desorption processes (Suppl. Fig. 1). MOF-
30332,43, which has superior adsorption kinetics compared to other
benchmark MOFs or salts under arid conditions (Suppl. Table 5), was
characterized in terms of its crystal structure, microstructure, and
pore size distribution (Suppl. Figs. 2–4 and Suppl. Table 1). The MOF
layer reaches adsorption equilibrium within 30–40 min across 20–60%
RH (Suppl. Fig. 40a). Fitted with a pseudo‑ﬁrst‑order kinetic model, the
adsorption rate constant (k1, Suppl. Equation 6) at 30% RH and 25 °C
was determined to be 0.14 min−1 (Suppl. Fig. 40b, Suppl. Table 4). This
copper container with high thermal conductivity facilitated heat
transfer between the two components, reducing interfacial thermal
resistance. The second component was a 14 Ah battery pack, assem-
bled with four 3.5 Ah lithium cells in parallel using nickel sheets, which
was further encased in plastic support for shape stabilization and
partial heat insulation (Suppl. Fig. 5). To ensure optimal contact within
the system, silicone thermal grease with a high thermal conductivity of
approximately 5 W m−1 K−1 was utilized to ﬁll the air gaps between the
sorption unit and battery pack (Suppl. Figs. 6 and 7). The silicone layer
is relatively thick and non‑uniform, as illustrated in Suppl. Fig. 43. This
promoted efﬁcient heat transfer by conduction through the silicone
thermal grease, which replaces the air gaps.
In operation, the sorption unit ﬁrst adsorbs atmospheric water
before being coupled with the discharging battery pack to achieve
thermal symbiosis, involving the sorption unit recycling heat from the
battery pack for water desorption, while the battery pack simulta-
neously dissipates heat to the sorption unit. This reciprocal heat
exchange helped maintain the battery pack within a safer temperature
range while concurrently facilitating water production by the
MOF layer.

Battery waste heat drives sorbent regeneration
The sorption behavior of the sorption unit was examined as follows.
MOF-303 exhibits relatively high desorption energy due to its hydro-
philic functional groups, which strongly interact with water
molecules32,43. In Fig. 3a, MOF-303’s desorption activation energy was
ﬁtted and calculated based on its desorption performance under var-
ious heating rates (Suppl. Fig. 9), using the Kissinger equation 47,48
(Suppl. Equation 1), amounting to approximately 40 kJ mol-1. This value
aligned with the reported literature, indicating its high capacity to
absorb heat generated by the battery pack.
In Fig. 3b, we compared the desorption process to traditional
solar-driven desorption, with MOF layers pre-adsorbing water at 30%
RH and 25 °C for 30 min (Suppl. Fig. 10). Under a 1.0 kW m −2 light
irradiance, the MOF layer was heated to 39 °C in roughly 20 min, due to
limited light absorbance (Suppl. Fig. 11). The solar-driven MOF layer’s
temperature exceeded the battery pack-driven counterpart around
20 min. However, after 20 min, the battery pack-driven MOF layer
displayed a higher temperature, which may lead to a superior
desorption rate.

Cling ﬁlm was employed to seal the sorption element to simulate
an actual AWG, creating a simple closed system as depicted in Fig.3c.
Before desorption (discharge), there was no water vapor condensation
on the inner cling ﬁlm (Suppl. Fig. 12). During discharge, heat was
transferred from the battery pack to the MOFs for desorption, leading
to a closed cavity with high water vapor partial pressure due to the
abundance of desorbed water. Concurrently, the copper container ’s
temperature increased due to its high thermal conductivity. This
temperature rise increased the air temperature within the cavity due to
heat transfer from the heated copper container, accompanied by
convective air mixing. The temperature gradient led to cooling of the
inner cling ﬁlm surface, facilitating condensation of water vapor, as
illustrated in Fig.3d. This lab-scale AWG experiment demonstrated the
potential for water production facilitated by the battery pack.
Battery pack-driven water production can be achieved without
sunlight, broadening the application of sorbents in regions with lim-
ited sunlight and extending their daily production time to increase
water yield. In this study, we initially employed an isochronous and
one-to-one mapping approach, pairing one sorption unit with one
battery pack during the thermal symbiosis process. To preserve bat-
tery life, the charging current was limited to 0.5 C (7 A), resulting in a
charging time of approximately 133 min (Suppl. Fig. 13). As illustrated
in Fig. 3e (the clearer schematic diagram is shown in Suppl. Fig. 39),
one cycle consisted of a 62 min desorption and water generation
period (during this period, the battery pack completed the discharge
process), followed by a 30 min adsorption period at 30% RH and 25 °C
(Supplementary Fig. 10), allowing the sorption to equalize. Con-
currently, the battery pack began recharging for 133 min. However, the
battery pack’s charging time exceeded the sorption unit’s adsorption
time, creating a timing mismatch in which the battery pack required
more time before initiating the next sorption cycle. Consequently, one
cycling time of the sorption unit was extended to approximately
195 min, including a 62 min water generation and a 133 min sorption
unit’s charge time, which reduced the daily (24 h) theoretical cycle
number to only 7 times.
In contrast to the isochronous battery-to-sorbent pairing, we
adopted an asynchronous con ﬁguration that eliminates downtime
associated with the battery ’s prolonged charging period. By coordi-
nating three battery packs (Packs 1, 2, and 3) with a single sorption unit,
the system enables continuous regeneration cycles —eliminating the
need to wait for the same pack to recharge. As shown in Fig. 3e, the
sorption unit can complete one cycle in 92 min using a single battery
pack. The three battery packs contributed to water generation during
the 0 –92, 92 –184, and 184 –276 min intervals, respectively. After
completing its ﬁrst discharge, Pack 1 had enough time to charge,
preparing for its second water generation cycle at 276 min. This
asynchronous design reduced the sorption unit’s cycle time to 92 min,
increasing its theoretical daily cycle count to 15, representing a 114%
improvement over the isochronous con ﬁguration. The increased
production frequency of the BTM-AWH system resulted in a higher
daily water yield than that achievable under standard 1.0 kW m−2 solar
irradiance, the typical condition for AWH, as conclusively demon-
strated in Fig. 5.
MOF-303 is well recognized for its exceptional stability. To eval-
uate its cycling stability, we tested the adsorption behavior of MOF-
303 after 50 sorption cycles; the detailed procedure is provided in the
Supporting Information (Supplem entary Fig. 14). Notably, the
adsorption kinetics of MOF ‑303 showed minimal change over 50
cycles, as supported by nearly identical pore ‑size distributions, pre-
served crystalline frameworks, and stable granular morphology
(Suppl. Figs. 2–4). This structural and performance consistency high-
lights the material’s robustness and strongly indicates the long ‑term
operational stability potential of the BTM‑AWH system. Moreover, the
introduction of the AWH module enhances the long‑term performance
of the battery pack, manifesting as superior electrochemical char-
acteristics (Suppl. Fig. 45).

Thermo-symbiotic heat sharing pathways
During the 46 min heating period, both the MOF and battery pack
reached peak temperatures (Fig. 2c, d). To analyze the thermo-
symbiotic mechanism of this system, a thermal simulation was con-
ducted, observing the temperature tendencies and peak temperatures
of each component. The model mesh, calculation equations, and
assumed conditions are detailed in Supplementary Note 16 (Suppl.
Figs. 16–25). Firstly, a pure battery pack model without the sorption
unit was established, termed Model 1 (Suppl. Fig. 18). In this model, the
experimental and simulated battery pack temperatures demonstrated
a strong correlation, indicated by a low deviation value ( Dv, Suppl.
Equation 4) ranging from −0.57 to 1.00 K and a low deviation rate (Dr,
Supplementary Equation 5) ranging from−0.17% to 0.33%. This result
conﬁrms the model’s excellent reliability (Suppl. Figs. 19–21).
On this basis, we analyzed the temperature pro ﬁle of the pure
battery pack cross-section (Suppl. Fig. 26) that passes through the

Closed-chamber water generation
To evaluate the actual water production of the BTM-AWH system, we
constructed a closed-chamber water generator (Fig. 5a, b). The main
body consisted of a transparent acrylic plate (Suppl. Fig. 34), inside
which the BTM-AWH unit was housed. A semiconductor cooler was

installed on the top side of the plate to facilitate water condensation.
The bottom of the chamber was insulated to minimize heat loss,
maintain internal temperature, and promote a thermal gradient with
the external environment. Within this system, 5.0 g of MOF was used
for water harvesting. The adsorption phase was conducted for 30 min
at 25 °C and 30% RH (Supplementary Fig. 10), followed by a 60 min
desorption phase, during which the MOF temperature reached
approximately 50 °C (Suppl. Fig. 35). Notably, as shown in Fig. 5c, the
peak temperature of the battery pack was reduced to 54.5 °C despite
the acrylic enclosure hindering natural convection. This temperature is
lower than that observed in the open system (Fig. 2c), indicating that
the active cooling from the semiconductor cooler effectively offset the
heat buildup. This conclusion is supported by a control experiment in
which the cooler was removed (Suppl. Fig. 36), resulting in a peak
battery temperature of 56.5 °C, which exceeds the safe operating
threshold. The operational process of the BTM-AWH system is shown
in Fig. 5d, e. Following the simultaneous activation of the battery pack
and semiconductor cooler, the cooler was temporarily displaced,
revealing a distinct cooling region on the generator ceiling. Within this
region, a hazy mist initially formed and subsequently coalesced into
visible water droplets as the temperature gradient increased (Suppl.
Fig. 37), with prominent condensation evident after 60 min (Fig. 5f).

Under dynamic conditions (e.g., vibration or tilting), liquid con-
densate may leak or accumulate near electrical contacts, thereby
posing a risk of short circuits or insulation failure. To mitigate this risk
in future practical designs, the following safety measures are pro-
posed: First, electrical contacts should be insulated and physically
separated from the condensate pathway to reduce the risk of moisture
exposure. Second, structural optimization of the prototype is sug-
gested: as illustrated in Suppl. Fig. 44, securing the BTM-AWH assem-
bly with a ﬁxed cover can help mitigate vibration and tilting while also
shielding the assembly from dripping water. Furthermore, elevating
the assembly on a platform would allow any condensate toﬂow safely
beneath it, ensuring no direct contact between water and electrical
components.
Discussion
This study introduces a thermo-symbiotic system in which waste heat
generated during battery discharge drives MOF-based atmospheric
water harvesting, while the sorption unit concurrently moderates the
battery temperature. The coupled system effectively utilizes battery
waste heat to drive MOF desorption, maintaining safe operating tem-
peratures (below 55 °C) under high discharge currents while simulta-
neously achieving a substantial enhancement in water yield compared
to solar-driven analogues. The implementation of an asynchronous
operational mode further enables continuous operation without solar
heating for sorbent regeneration. These results collectively demon-
strate a scalable strategy for synergistic energy and water manage-
ment. Beyond the speci ﬁcc o n ﬁguration presented, the “thermo-
symbiosis” framework is generalizable. Its core principle—repurposing
low-grade waste heat for a useful thermal demand—c a nb ee x t e n d e dt o
larger battery systems (e.g., in electric vehicles or stationary storage)
and is compatible with other sorbents featuring low regeneration
temperatures. This design offers a practical solution for off-grid or
resource-constrained environments, such as telecommunication sta-
tions or remote facilities, where concurrent thermal management and
water supply are critical. Looking forward, this work opens promising
avenues for material innovation (e.g., composites with enhanced
thermal properties) and system-level integration with renewable
energy sources, paving the way toward fully autonomous, sustainable
water-energy systems. Thus, the present study not only establishes a
functional prototype but also provides a versatile blueprint for
synergistic resource recovery in a wide range of applications.

Preparation of MOF-303
Dissolve 1.88 g Al(NO) 3·9H2O in 10 mL deionized water, denoted as
Solution-1. Dissolve 0.78 g C5H4N2O4·H2Oa n d0 . 6 gN a O Hi n t o1 5 m L

Solution-2 drop-by-drop into Solution-1. Stir the mixture and place it at
100 °C for 24 h, and clean the residue with deionized water twice. Dry
the residue in a 120 °C vacuum oven for 24 h.
Preparation of the CPCM
A PCM solution was prepared by dissolving 7.2 g Na 2S2O3·5H2O, 2.8 g
CH3COONa·3H2O, and 0.2 g SrCl 2·6H2O in a sealed glass bottle. The
mixture was stirred at 70 °C for 3 h. Subsequently, 8.5 g of the resulting
liquid PCM was added dropwise to 1.5 g of EG under physical stirring to
form the CPCM.
Program setting of the battery pack
The battery pack discharges at constant currents of −12, −1 4 ,- 1 6 ,- 1 8 ,
and -20 A until the voltage decreases to 2.7 V. The battery pack is then
charged at a constant current of 7 A until the voltage reaches 4.25 V,
followed by constant-voltage charging at 4.25 V until the current
decreases to 0.7 A.
