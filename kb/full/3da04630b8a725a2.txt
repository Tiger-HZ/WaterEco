Nutrient management in little millet (Panicum 
sumatrense L.) for different row spacing 
 
Sukanya R Khadaskar, Seema M Nemade, NB Mohod, VV Goud, RV 
Mahajan, Vaibhav G Badkhal, Tushar S Burale and Harsh M Dixit 
 
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/26174693.2026.v10.i9Su.10072 
 
Abstract 
A field experiment was conducted during kharif 2025 at the All India Coordinated Research Project on 
Weed Management , Dr. Panjabrao Deshmukh Krishi Vidyapeeth, Akola, Maharashtra, to study the 
effect of row spacing and nutrient management on growth and yield of little millet ( Panicum 
sumatrense L.). The experiment was laid out in Factorial Randomized Block Design with t hree 
replications and twelve treatment combinations. Factor A comprised three row spacing viz., S 1: paired 
row, S 2: 45 cm and S 3: 30 cm, while Factor B consisted of four nutrient levels viz., N 0: control, N 1: 
100% GRDF (40:20:20 NPK kg ha⁻¹), N 2: 125% GRDF (50:25:25 NPK kg ha⁻¹) and N 3: 150% GRDF 
(60:30:30 NPK kg ha⁻¹). At harvest, 30 cm spacing recorded significantly higher plant height (116.52 
cm), number of tillers (169.67 m⁻²) and leaf area index (3.37), whereas 45 cm spacing recorded 
significantly higher leaf area (526.76 cm² plant⁻¹), leaf dry matter (9.01 g plant⁻¹), stem dry matter (9.11 
g plant⁻¹), root dry matter (3.73 g plant⁻¹) and total dry matter (21.71 g plant⁻¹). Among nutrient levels, 
150% GRDF recorded the highest values of major growth par ameters and remained statistically at par 
with 125% GRDF for most characters. The 45 cm spacing recorded higher grain weight plant⁻¹ (4.15 g) 
and number of panicles plant⁻¹ (3.87), while 30 cm spacing recorded significantly higher grain yield 
(1430 kg ha⁻¹ ) and straw yield (4387 kg ha⁻¹). Application of 150% GRDF recorded the highest grain 
yield (1493 kg ha⁻¹) and straw yield (4300 kg ha⁻¹), which were statistically at par with 125% GRDF. 
The interaction of 30 cm spacing with 150% GRDF recorded the highest grain yield (1682 kg ha⁻¹), 
which was at par with 30 cm spacing with 125% GRDF (1602 kg ha⁻¹). Similarly, the highest straw 
yield was recorded with 30 cm spacing combined with 150% GRDF (4882 kg ha⁻¹), which was at par 
with 30 cm spacing combined with 125% GRDF (4867 kg ha⁻¹). 
 
Keywords: Little millet, row spacing, nutrient management, plant population, growth, grain yield, 
straw yield 
 
Introduction 
Little millet ( Panicum sumatrense L.) is an important small millet crop cultivated 
predominantly under rainfed conditions. The crop possesses considerable adaptability to 
moisture stress and relatively poor soil conditions and therefore has an important role in 
sustainable dryland agricul ture. In recent years, millets have received increased attention 
because of their nutritional value, climate resilience and potential contribution towards 
diversification of cereal -based production systems. Productivity of little millet is influenced 
by se veral agronomic factors, among which crop geometry and nutrient management are 
particularly important. Row spacing regulates plant population, canopy development, light 
interception and the availability of soil moisture and nutrients to individual plants. An 
excessively wide spacing may result in underutilization of available resources, whereas 
closer spacing increases plant population per unit area and may enhance yield per hectare 
despite reduced productivity on an individual plant basis. 
Nutrient management is another important determinant of crop growth and yield. Little millet 
is commonly cultivated in low -input conditions, and inadequate nutrient supply may restrict 
tillering, leaf development, dry matter accumulation and grain formation. Adequate nitr ogen, 
phosphorus and potassium supply can promote vegetative growth, root development, tillering 
and assimilate production, thereby improving yield formation. 
Despite the inherent advantages of little millet, it faces several agronomic, environmental and 
practical challenges.

Variability in the soil properties, rainfall pattern and 
temperature affect plant responses and make it difficult to 
establish universal applicable recommendations. Therefore, 
multi-location and multi-season research including diverse 
soil types, climatic conditions, and integrated nutrient 
management approaches are required for developing more 
comprehensive and widely applicable recommendations for 
sustainable little millet production. 
 
Materials and Methods 
The field experiment was conducted during the kharif 
season of 2025 at the All India Coordinated Research 
Project on Weed Management, Dr. Panjabrao Deshmukh 
Krishi Vidyapeeth, Akola, Maharashtra to study the effect of 
row spacing and nutrient management on growth and yield 
of little millet (Panicum sumatrense L.). 
The experiment was laid out in Factorial Randomized Block 
Design with two factors, three replications and twelve 
treatment combinations. The crop variety used was PDKV -
Tejashree. The gross plot size was 4.00 m × 3.60 m and the 
net plot size was 3.40 m × 2.70 m. The experimental site 
was located in the Central Vidarbha agro -climatic zone at 
20°42′ N latitude, 77°02′ E longitude and an altitude of 
307.42 m above mean sea level. The soil of the experimental 
site was medium black in texture. The initial soil analysis 
indicated low available nitrogen (160.80 kg ha⁻¹), medium 
available phosphorus (20.00 kg ha⁻¹) and high available 
potassium (306.93 kg ha⁻¹). The soil had pH 7.81, electrical 
conductivity 0.21 dS m⁻¹ and organic carbon 0.430%. The 
available nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium were, and 
respectively. During the crop season, a total rainfall of 615.9 
mm was received from the 27th to 40th meteorological 
week, against a normal rainf all of 532 mm. The crop 
experienced 33 rainy days during the growing period.

Results and Discussion 
Effect of spacing and nutrient levels on growth 
parameters 
Plant height 
Row spacing significantly affected plant height at harvest. 
The 30 cm spacing recorded the highest plant height of 
116.52 cm, foll owed by paired row spacing (114.92 cm), 
while the lowest plant height was recorded with 45 cm 
spacing (113.39 cm). The greater plant height under closer 
spacing may be associated with increased competition for 
incident radiation, resulting in greater verti cal growth of 
plants. Similar responses to closer plant geometry have been 
reported in little millet by of Asewar et al. (2023) [1]. 
Nutrient management significantly influenced plant height. 
Application of 150% GRDF recorded the highest plant 
height (122.86 cm), followed by 125% GRDF (122.20 cm). 
These two treatments were statistically at par. The control 
recorded the lowest plant height (96.83 cm). Improved plant 
height under higher nutrient levels may be attributed to 
better availability of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, 
which supported cell division, cell elongation and vigorous 
vegetative growth. The result is in agreement with Niharika 
et al. (2021) [9] and Janani et al. (2022) [5]. 
 
Number of tillers 
The number of tillers m⁻² was significantly influenced by 
row spacing. At harvest, 30 cm spacing produced the 
highest number of tillers (169.67 m⁻²), followed by 45 cm 
spacing (128.80 m⁻²), while paired row spacing recorded the 
lowest value (120.97 m⁻²). The higher tiller population per 
unit area under 30 cm spacing was primarily due to the 
greater plant population accommodated within a given land 
area. The findings are in confo rmity with Syed et al. (2022) 
[15] in little millet 
Application of 150% GRDF produced 151.72 tillers m⁻², 
which was statistically at par with 125% GRDF (148.57 
m⁻²). The control recorded only 124.03 tillers m⁻². The 
increase in tiller production with higher nutrient supply may 
be attributed particularly to greater nitrogen availability 
during the tillering phase. Similar observations were 
reported by Raundal et al . (2017) [11] and Niharika et al . 
(2021) [9]. 
 
Leaf area and leaf area index 
Among spacing, 45 cm spacing recorded significantly 
higher leaf area of 526.76 cm² plant⁻¹ compared with paired 
row spacing (516.39 cm² plant⁻¹) and 30 cm spacing (505.72 
cm² plant⁻¹). Greater leaf area under wider spacing can be 
attributed to greater availabili ty of physical space, light, 
moisture and nutrients to individual plants, resulting in 
enhanced leaf expansion. . These results collaborated with 
the findings of Syed et al . (2022) [15] and Hiwale et al . 
(2025) [4] in little millet. 
In contrast, leaf area index was significantly higher under 30 
cm spacing (3.37), followed by 45 cm spacing (2.34) and 
paired row spacing (2.29). The higher LAI under closer 
spacing was associated with the greater number of plants per 
unit area, which compensated for the smaller individual leaf 
area. Similar observations have been reported by Hebbal et 
al. (2018) [3] and Janani et al. (2022) [5]. 
Among nutrient levels, 150% GRDF recorded the highest 
leaf area (552.41 cm² plant⁻¹) and LAI (2.86), which were 
statistically at par with 125% GRDF recording 548.97 cm² 
plant⁻¹ and 2.84, respectively. The control recorded the 
lowest values. Improved nutrie nt availability favoured leaf 
initiation and expansion and consequently increased canopy 
development. These findings corroborate the report of 
Charate et al. (2018). 
 
Dry matter accumulation 
The 45 cm spacing recorded significantly higher total dry 
matter (21.71 g plant⁻¹) at harvest. Wider spacing allowed 
individual plants greater access to light, moisture, nutrients 
and growing space and consequently promoted greater 
biomass accumulation on an individual plant basis. Similar 
results were reported by Praka sha et al . (2018) in finger 
millet. 
Among nutrient levels, 150% GRDF produced the 
significantly higher total dry matter (22.48 g plant⁻¹), which 
remained statistically at par with 125% GRDF, which 
recorded 21.72 g plant⁻¹. The greater dry matter 
accumulation under higher nutrient levels may be attributed

and enhanced photosynthetic activity. Similar responses 
have been reported by Melavanki et al . (2024) [8] and

Effect of spacing and nutrient levels on yield attributes 
Grain weight and number of panicles 
Row spacing significantly affected grain weight plant⁻¹ and 
number of panicles plant⁻¹. The 45 cm spacing recorded the 
significantly higher grain weight (4.15 g plant⁻¹) and 
number of panicles (3.87 plant⁻¹), followed by paired row 
spacing with 3.92 g plant⁻¹ and 3.77 panicles plant⁻¹. The 
lowest values were observed under 30 cm spacing, with 3.42 
g grain weight plant⁻¹ and 3.61 panicles plant⁻¹. 
The superior performance of individual plants under wider 
spacing may be associated with greater availability of light, 
moisture, nutrients and growing space, thereby facilitating 
greater reproductive growth. In contrast, closer sp acing 
increased competition among plants and restricted 
reproductive development on an individual plant basis. 
These results were supported by Sarala et al. (2020) [12] in 
little millet. 
Nutrient management significantly increased grain weight 
and number of panicles plant⁻¹. Application of 150% GRDF 
recorded the highest grain weight (4.30 g plant⁻¹) and 
number of panicles (4.23 plant⁻¹), which were statistically at 
par with 125% GRDF recor ding 4.27 g plant⁻¹ and 4.17 
panicles plant⁻¹, respectively. The improvement in yield 
attributes under higher nutrient levels may be attributed to 
improved tillering and greater assimilate availability for 
reproductive structures. These results are in clos e agreement 
with those of Padmaja et al. (2024) [10] in finger millet. 
 
Test weight 
Test weight was not significantly influenced by either row 
spacing or nutrient management. Numerically, 45 cm 
spacing recorded the highest test weight (1.61 g), while 30 
cm spacing recorded 1.57 g. Among nutrient treatments, 
150% GRDF recorded 1.62 g compared with 1.58 g under 
125% GRDF. The non -significant response indicates that 
test weight was comparatively stable under the treatments 
imposed and may have been governed la rgely by the 
inherent characteristics of the variety.

Effect of spacing and nutrient levels on grain yield, straw 
yield and harvest index 
Grain yield 
Row spacing significantly influenced grain yield. The 30 cm 
spacing recorded significantly higher grain yield of 1430 kg 
ha⁻¹, followed by paired row spacing (1245 kg ha⁻¹), 
whereas the lowest grain yield was recorded under 45 cm 
spacing (1216 kg ha⁻¹). 
The higher grain yield under 30 cm spacing, despite lower 
grain weight and number of panicles on an individual plant 
basis, may be attributed to the substantially greater plant 
population per unit area. Thus, the higher number of plants

compensated for lower individual plant productivity and 
resulted in greater grain production per hectare. Similar 
observations have been reported by Bitew and Asargew 
(2014) [2] in finger millet and Jyothi et al . (2021) [7] in 
foxtail millet. 
Nutrient management significantly influenced grain yield. 
Application of 150% GRDF recorded the highest grain yield 
(1493 kg ha⁻¹), which was statistically at par with 125% 
GRDF (1440 kg ha⁻¹). The control recorded the lowest grain 
yield (955 kg ha⁻¹). Increased grain yield under higher 
nutrient supply may be attributed to enhanced plant growth, 
tiller production, leaf area development, dry matter 
accumulation and yield attributes. The significant response 
to higher fertility levels agrees with Asewar et al. (2023) [1] 
and Sujathamma and Na ik (2022) [14], who reported 
improved growth and yield of little millet with increasing 
fertilizer levels. 
 
Straw yield 
The 30 cm spacing recorded significantly higher straw yield 
(4387 kg ha⁻¹), followed by paired row spacing (3647 kg 
ha⁻¹), while the lowest straw yield was recorded under 45 
cm spacing (3507 kg ha⁻¹). The higher straw production 
under 30 cm spacing may be attributed to the greater plant 
population, plant height and LAI, resulting in greater 
cumulative vegetative biomass per unit area. These findings 
are in close conformity with those reported by Nigus and 
Melese (2018) in finger millet and Jhansi et al. (2025) [6] in 
foxtail millet. 
Among nutrient levels, 150% GRDF recorded the highest 
straw yield (4300 kg ha⁻¹), which was statistically at par 
with 125% GRDF (4242 kg ha⁻ ¹). Higher nutrient 
availability enhanced vegetative growth and dry matter 
production, thereby increasing straw yield. Similar results 
were reported by Janani et al. (2022) [5] in barnyard millet. 
 
Harvest index 
Harvest index was not significantly influenced by row 
spacing or nutrient management. The highest numerical 
harvest index under row spacing was recorded with 45 cm 
spacing (25.60%), followed by paired row (25.33%) and 30 
cm spacing (24.31%). Among nutrient treatments, 150% 
GRDF recorded the highest nu merical harvest index 
(25.53%), followed by 125% GRDF (25.41%), whereas 
control recorded 24.10%.

Interaction effect 
The interaction between row spacing and nutrient 
management was significant for grain and straw yield. The 
combination of 30 cm spacing with 150% GRDF recorded 
the highest grain yield of 1682 kg ha⁻¹, which was 
statistically at par with 30 cm spacing combined with 125% 
GRDF (1602 kg ha⁻¹). The lowest grain yield reported was 
899 kg ha⁻¹ under 45 cm spacing combined with the control 
treatment. The significant interaction indicates that the 
response of grain yi eld to nutrient supply varied with row 
spacing. The higher plant population established under 30 
cm spacing apparently benefited from increased nutrient 
availability, allowing the crop to sustain greater growth and 
reproductive production per unit area. 
A similar response was recorded for straw yield. The 
combination of 30 cm spacing with 150% GRDF produced 
the highest straw yield of 4882 kg ha⁻¹, which was 
statistically at par with 30 cm spacing combined with 125% 
GRDF (4867 kg ha⁻¹). The lowest straw yiel d was recorded 
under 45 cm spacing with control nutrient management 
(2657 kg ha⁻¹).

Conclusion 
The present investigation demonstrated that Sowing of Little 
millet at 30 cm spacing recorded significantly higher values 
of growth parameters. Whereas, 45 cm spacing produced 
higher leaf area plant-1, dry matter accumulation plant-1 and 
yield attributing characters. However both grain and straw 
yield were significantly higher in spacing 30cm. The highest 
values of growth contributing characters, yield attributing 
characters and actual yield was highest in treatment with 
150% GRDF (60:30:30) which were at p ar with 125% 
GRDF (50:25:25). Among spacing treatments, 30 cm 
spacing exhibited superior nutrient use efficiency indices. 
However, 125% GRDF (50:25:25) recorded the highest 
Agronomic Efficiency and Recovery Efficiency of N, P and 
K. Whereas, highest values of Partial Factor Productivity 
and Physiological efficiency were recorded in 100% GRDF 
(40:20:20). Spacing 30 cm recorded the highest values of 
gross monetary returns, net monetary returns and benefit -
cost ratio. Among nutrient management practices, 150% 
GRDF (60:30:30) recorded highest values of gross monetary 
return, net monetary return and B:C ratio, which were 
statistically at par to those obtained under 125% GRDF 
(50:25:25). Considering the higher nutrient use efficiency 
and only marginal economic adv antage of 150% GRDF 
(60:30:30), application of 125% GRDF (50:25:25) can be 
considered a more balanced and economically sustainable 
nutrient management practice for little millet at 30 cm 
spacing.
