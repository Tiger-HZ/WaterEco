This article examines this phenomenon through the global transition from iron–
folic acid supplementation to antenatal multiple micronutrient supplements 
(MMS), using Indonesia as a critical contemporary case. It introduces the 
concept of a normative -material corridor: a governance structure in which 
authoritative scientific standards, evidence hierarchies, financing mechanisms, 
product availability, procurement support, technical assistance, and monitoring 
processes collectively narrow the range of practica lly available national policy 
choices. The corridor does not eliminate state sovereignty or legally mandate 
adoption. Instead, it makes one policy option progressively more legitimate, 
feasible, and administratively actionable than its alternatives. Using doctrinal 
analysis and socio-legal document analysis, this article traces the development 
of the MMS standard, the translation of World Health Organization authority 
into policy influence, the expanding role of philanthropic and catalytic capital, 
and the domestic implementation of these forces through Indonesian health, 
administrative, budgetary, and procurement frameworks. The analysis 
demonstrates that legal responsibility remains concentrated within the state, 
even when agenda setting, evidence generati on, and material capacity are 
distributed among transnational actors. Indonesia therefore appears neither as a 
passive policy recipient nor as an entirely unconstrained sovereign actor, but 
rather as an agent exercising structured agency within a policy fi eld whose 
prevailing conditions have already been shaped. To address the resulting 
accountability gap, this article proposes six safeguards: domestic legal 
translation, public reason-giving, financial and relational transparency, adaptive 
review mechanisms , a systems obligation against the reduction of stunting 
management to tablet -based interventions, and a distributed accountability 
matrix. MMS consequently reveals a broader challenge for medical law: how 
to govern forms of power that are non-coercive in appearance but regulatory in 
effect. 
 
INTRODUCTION 
Maternal nutrition is often framed as a clinical problem: identify deficiency, provide 
supplementation, and measure biological responses. Although this approach is necessary, it 
remains incomplete. Micronutrient deficiencies are embedded within broader sys tems 
involving food availability, poverty, gendered resource allocation, access to antenatal care, 
infectious diseases, pharmaceutical supply chains, and public administration (Stevens et al., 
2022; Passarelli et al., 2024). Their consequences extend across maternal morbidity, fetal 
growth, gestational duration, birth weight, and neonatal survival. A legal analysis must 
therefore examine not only whether an intervention is effective, but also who defines the 
intervention, how it becomes a standard, which institutions make it feasible, and where 
responsibility lies when a transnational policy is implemented through domestic systems 
(Sekalala & Masud, 2021; Ralston et al., 2024). These questions frame the central inquiry of 
 
59 
this article: how transnational governance shapes national maternal nutrition policy without 
relying on binding international law (Biswas, 2021). 
The intergenerational implications make this inquiry particularly important. Conditions 
during gestation can influence childhood growth, long -term health, educational attainment, 
and economic participation. Maternal nutrition is therefore not merely an element of prenatal 
care; it is a domain in which present distributive decisions influence future capabilities. 
Although the language of human capital often captures the magnitude of these effects, it may 
also obscure women as rights -bearing individuals by framing their bodies primarily as 
instruments for producing healthier children (Paiero & de Moraes, 2027). 
Anaemia illustrates the limitations of a single -variable approach. It remains prevalent 
among women, yet its occurrence reflects multiple factors, including iron deficiency, other 
micronutrient deficiencies, infection, inflammation, haemoglobin disorders, and social 
conditions (GBD 2021 Anaemia Collaborators, 2023). A policy that equates maternal 
nutrition solely with iron supplementation may be administratively convenient while 
remaining biologically and socially incomplete (Benson et al., 2021; Turawa et al., 2021). The 
same caution applies to stunting. Comparative evidence identifies multiple prenatal and 
postnatal determinants, including maternal characteristics, fetal growth restriction, infection, 
dietary inadequacy, water and sanitation conditions , and socioeconomic factors (Beal et al., 
2018; Vaivada et al., 2020). Height -for-age is consequently a population -level indicator of 
cumulative deprivation rather than a diagnosis that identifies a single causal pathway or a 
single corrective intervention (Vaivada et al., 2020). 
Against this background, multiple micronutrient supplements (MMS) have moved from 
being an intervention under investigation to an increasingly prominent component of global 
maternal nutrition policy. This transition raises a legal puzzle. The World Health Organization 
(WHO) does not generally compel states to procure particular antenatal supplements through 
binding legal mechanisms. Philanthropic foundations, United Nations agencies, and technical 
consortia do not possess general legislative authority over national health systems. 
Nevertheless, their standards, financing mechanisms, product specifications, market -shaping 
activities, and implementation support can substantially influence the range of rea listic 
domestic policy choices (Mazzucato et al., 2020; Mazzucato & Kattel, 2026; Mazzucato & 
Li, 2021; Mazzucato & Penna, 2016; Wagner et al., 2026). 
This article argues that the binary distinction between binding and non -binding law is 
insufficient to explain this form of influence. It introduces the concept of the normative -
material corridor: a governance structure in which scientific authority and ma terial 
enablement operate together. Normative authority identifies a preferred intervention, 
establishes the evidentiary framework through which it is justified, and provides a vocabulary 
of international legitimacy. Material enablement makes that interven tion available through 
financing, product supply, technical assistance, procurement templates, data systems, and 
implementation partnerships. Neither element alone determines national policy. Together, 
however, they can make one course of action easier to justify, finance, procure, and scale than 
its alternatives. The concept therefore provides an analytical framework for understanding 
how non-coercive forms of power can generate regulatory effects. 
Indonesia provides a critical case for examining this dynamic. It is a large middle -
income democracy with a decentralized health system, substantial domestic administrative 
 
60 
and industrial capacity, persistent challenges related to maternal anaemia and child 
undernutrition, and an established constitutional and statutory framework for health. It is 
neither a weak state passively receiving externally designed programs nor a sta te operating 
independently of transnational governance. Indonesia’s adoption and scaling of MMS 
demonstrate a form of structured agency: the state selects, co-finances, and adapts policy, but 
does so within an environment in which evidentiary standards, product configurations, and 
mechanisms for policy acceleration have already been shaped by global actors. Indonesia 
therefore provides an instructive case for examining how domestic law mediates transnational 
governance. 
This article makes four contributions. First, it distinguishes soft governance from soft 
law and identifies the interaction between normative and material forms of power. Second, it 
explains why formal state consent alone does not fully resolve legitimacy concerns when the 
practical conditions of policy choice are externally structured. Third, it provides a 
contemporary legal analysis of Indonesia’s MMS scale-up. Fourth, it develops safeguards that 
preserve the speed and potential health benefits of transna tional cooperation while 
strengthening public reasoning, transparency, reversibility, and rights protection. Sections II 
to V develop the conceptual and institutional argument; Sections VI to VIII apply it to the 
governance architecture and Indonesian impl ementation; Section IX develops legal and 
ethical safeguards; and Section X concludes. By integrating these contributions, this article 
aims to advance theoretical understanding of global health governance and contribute to the 
practical governance of maternal nutrition policy in Indonesia. 
 
METHOD 
The article combined doctrinal analysis with socio -legal document analysis. The 
doctrinal component examined the legal status and accountability implications of WHO 
recommendations, international health and human rights instruments, and Indonesian 
constitutional, health, administrative, transparency, public service, financial, and procurement 
laws. The socio -legal component examined how evidence, standards, funding, and 
implementation arrangements interacted across institutional levels. The analysis was based on 
publicly available materials available up to 16 July 2026. It did not seek to measure program 
impact, infer the contents of unpublished donor agreements, or substitute documentary 
analysis for empirical research involving pregnant women, health worke rs, procurement 
officials, or local manufacturers. 
Document analysis was appropriate for addressing the legal question because relevant 
forms of power were often embedded in institutional instruments and governance 
relationships rather than expressed through direct commands. However, documents also 
represented curated expressions of institutional objectives. Guidelines emphasized evidentiary 
approaches, press releases highlighted institutional commitments, and legislation established 
formal authority. Therefore, this article treated official descriptions as evidence of declared 
governance structures rather than proof of implementation outcomes. It also distinguished 
three propositions that are often conflated: that MMS may generate beneficial health 
outcomes; that national adoption may be a reasonable policy choice; and that the governance 
process through which adoption occurs must remain legally accountable. Acceptance of the 
first two propositions did not determine the third.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
From Ifa To Mms: The Making Of A Global Standard 
1. The Administrative Appeal of a Single-Nutrient Default 
Iron-folic acid supplementation (IFA) became a durable feature of antenatal policy 
because it combined a clear clinical rationale with an administratively legible product. Iron 
addressed a major cause of anaemia, folic acid reduced the risk of neural tube defects, and a 
standard tablet could be incorporated into routine antenatal contacts. WHO's 2016 antenatal 
care guideline located nutrition within a broader positive -pregnancy-experience framework, 
but IFA remained an established default in many national systems. Defaults matter because 
health administrations do not choose among interventions on efficacy alone. They inherit 
formularies, training materials, supply contracts, reporting indicators, public expectations and 
professional routines. 
The emergence of the United Nations International Multiple Micronutrient Antenatal 
Preparation (UNIMMAP) altered the object of governance. Rather than addressing a single 
deficiency, the formulation assembled 15 vitamins and minerals into a standard produc t for 
use in programme and research settings. Standardisation enabled trials to become comparable, 
manufacturers to work from a common specification, donors to finance a recognisable 
commodity and ministries to contemplate procurement at scale. A tablet th us became both a 
biomedical intervention and a regulatory artefact: its composition stabilised expectations 
across research, markets and public programmes. 
2. Evidence, Heterogeneity and the Meaning of Benefit 
Systematic review evidence generally indicates that antenatal MMS can reduce low 
birth weight and small -for-gestational-age births relative to IFA, while effects on other 
outcomes are smaller, uncertain or context dependent. Individual-participant-data analysis has 
further suggested that benefits may vary according to maternal anaemia, nutritional status, 
timing and other characteristics. Such heterogeneity is not a reason to dismiss the intervention. 
It is a reason to resist translating a population-average estimate into an unconditional legal or 
administrative mandate. 
Earlier syntheses already showed that the evidentiary question was not whether every 
outcome improved, but whether a bundle of modest benefits justified a change in standard 
practice. Meta-analysis of birth size and gestational outcomes supported the possi bility of 
population-level gains while also exposing differences among trial settings, formulations and 
baseline risks. The trial literature itself spans distinct health systems. A community trial in 
rural Nepal, for example, tested alternative supplement combinations under conditions that 
cannot be assumed to match routine service delivery elsewhere. 
The JiVitA-3 trial in rural Bangladesh added large-scale evidence on birth outcomes and 
infant mortality, but it also demonstrated the distance between a carefully managed trial and 
a national programme that must maintain supply, counselling, adherence and referral across 
diverse facilities. Cost-effectiveness modelling has strengthened the policy case by estimating 
that small per -pregnancy cost differences can be outweighed by reductions in adverse birth 
outcomes. Yet modelling necessarily embeds assumptions about prices, coverage, adherence, 
effect size, service delivery and valuation. A cost -effective intervention in a model becomes 
a lawful and legitimate public programme only after those assumptions are examined within 
domestic institutions.

3. The WHO Recommendation as a Policy Signal 
WHO's 2020 update did not present MMS as a simple universal command. It 
recommended antenatal MMS containing iron and folic acid in the context of rigorous 
research, thereby combining endorsement with a continuing demand for learning. That 
formulation is legally non-binding and epistemically cautious, but it is also a powerful policy 
signal. Ministries, donors and advocates can cite the recommendation as evidence that MMS 
lies within the range of internationally legitimate action. The research condition may then be 
operationalised broadly through implementation research, monitor ing or phased scale -up 
rather than through conventional randomised trials. 
Inclusion of an antenatal multiple micronutrient supplement in the WHO Model List of 
Essential Medicines further changes the institutional meaning of the product. Essential -
medicine status does not compel national listing or procurement. It does, however, strengthen 
claims of medical necessity, inform model formularies, reduce the reputational risk of 
adoption and support market forecasting. The movement from formulation, to trial evidence, 
to guideline, to essential -medicine recognition illustrates how a s cientific proposition 
accumulates normative weight across institutional sites. 
Soft Law, Soft Governance And The Normative-Material Corridor 
1. Why the Hard-Law/Soft-Law Binary Is Insufficient 
International legal scholarship has long described rules as harder or softer according to 
the degree of obligation, precision and delegation. That framework clarifies why a WHO 
recommendation differs from a treaty obligation, but it does not fully explain how a non -
binding guideline becomes the operational default of a national programme. Soft-law analysis 
correctly observes that non-binding instruments can influence expectation, interpretation and 
later lawmaking. It also shows that treaties and soft instruments frequently interact rather than 
occupy separate worlds. 
The deeper issue is that legal softness can coexist with practical firmness. An 
intervention may be optional in formal doctrine yet difficult to reject once it is endorsed by an 
authoritative organisation, attached to finance, embedded in procurement speci fications, 
supported by implementation partners and measured through programme indicators. Soft and 
hard law may function as alternatives, complements or antagonists, but the MMS case adds a 
material dimension: the cost of choosing outside the endorsed pathway can rise even without 
a sanction. 
Contemporary international rulemaking increasingly occurs through dynamic 
institutional processes rather than a single act of legislation. Global administrative law 
responded to this reality by focusing on transparency, participation, reason-giving, review and 
accountability in transnational administration. Yet health programmes also depend on 
commodities, logistics and finance. A framework centred only on decision procedure misses 
the way material capacity selects which norms can travel. 
2. Defining Soft Governance 
Soft governance, as used here, is the coordinated production of policy effects through 
non-binding standards, expertise, financing, market organisation, technical assistance, 
indicators and institutional relationships. It is broader than soft law because n ot all of its 
instruments claim normative status. A grant agreement, a product donation, a pooled 
 
63 
procurement arrangement or a digital reporting platform may be legally ordinary, but its 
placement within a policy architecture can determine which intervention is feasible. 
The normative -material corridor has five defining features. First, it is directional: it 
privileges a pathway without legally eliminating alternatives. Secondly, it is cumulative: the 
force of the corridor arises from the interaction of multiple instrument s. Thirdly, it is 
distributed: no single actor necessarily controls the whole process. Fourthly, it is reversible in 
theory but potentially costly to exit in practice. Fifthly, it transfers implementation and legal 
responsibility to domestic institutions even when important conditions have been shaped 
elsewhere. 
The concept has boundaries. Not every policy diffusion process is a corridor, and not 
every grant attached to a guideline amounts to problematic influence. A corridor exists only 
where normative endorsement and material support are sufficiently aligned to alter the 
comparative feasibility of domestic options. Its presence can be disproved where governments 
have robustly assessed competing interventions, possess independent financing and supply 
capacity, retain low-cost exit, and provide meaningful avenues o f contestation. The concept 
is therefore not a rhetorical label for global influence. It is an analytic claim about the 
architecture and intensity of choice-shaping. 
The corridor should not be equated with coercion or conspiracy. It may enable a 
government to implement an intervention it independently values but cannot yet finance or 
organise. It may reduce transaction costs, improve quality and accelerate access. Its legal 
significance lies precisely in that ambivalence. Power can be productive and beneficial while 
still requiring justification and control. 
3. Expertise, Standards and Polycentric Authority 
Epistemic communities help explain how shared causal beliefs and professional 
standards coordinate policy across borders. International organisations add bureaucratic 
authority: they classify problems, define appropriate knowledge and produce categories th at 
states use to govern. In maternal nutrition, the definition of the relevant deficiency, the choice 
of outcomes, the formulation of the product and the threshold for recommendation all shape 
the eventual policy. 
Standards are especially influential because they coordinate without appearing to 
command. They reduce uncertainty for laboratories, manufacturers, procurers, clinicians and 
evaluators. Private and transnational standard setters can consequently exercise r egulatory 
authority even when national governments retain formal lawmaking power. This authority is 
polycentric: WHO, UNICEF, expert groups, foundations, researchers, manufacturers, 
ministries, regulators and service providers each control different parts of the pathway. 
Polycentric governance creates a legitimacy problem because influence and 
responsibility do not align automatically. An actor may shape a standard without funding it, 
fund a product without implementing it, procure it without counselling users, or implement it 
without controlling the supply chain. Legitimacy must therefore be constructed and contested 
across the regime rather than attributed to one sovereign centre. Experimentalist governance 
offers one response by treating standards as revisable, requiring reporting and allowing local 
learning to reshape common goals. The challenge is to ensure that experimentation is genuine 
rather than a label applied to a predetermined scale-up

Who Normative Authority: When Evidence Becomes Regulatory Power 
1. Constitutional Authority without Direct Command 
The WHO Constitution authorises the organisation to act as a directing and coordinating 
authority on international health and permits the World Health Assembly to make 
recommendations to members. Recommendations under this framework are not treaties and 
generally do not create directly enforceable domestic duties. Their influence instead depends 
on institutional reputation, expert procedure, repeated use, alignment with other international 
actors and domestic uptake. 
Guideline production converts a heterogeneous evidence base into a decision -oriented 
statement. WHO's own handbook structures this process through question formulation, 
evidence review, assessment of benefits and harms, values, resource use, equity, acceptability 
and feasibility. These are not purely scientific questions. They are mixed judgments about 
evidence and public values. Their disciplined consideration strengthens legitimacy, but it also 
shows why a guideline cannot be understood as science speaking without mediation. 
2. Evidence Hierarchies and the Politics of Recommendation 
The GRADE approach separates confidence in evidence from the strength of a 
recommendation and thereby makes explicit that policy may depend on more than effect 
estimates. This is a major advance over informal expert opinion. Still, the conversion from 
evidence to recommendation is vulnerable to overstatement, especially when strong 
recommendations are issued despite low confidence in estimated effects. In MMS governance, 
the wording of the recommendation, the interpretation of the research condition and the 
treatment of contextual uncertainty become legally relevant because they influence national 
reason-giving. 
Global health priorities are also shaped through knowledge claims and moral narratives. 
Problems framed as urgent, measurable and solvable through a scalable intervention are more 
likely to attract political and financial support. Maternal anaemia and adverse birth outcomes 
fit this pattern, while slower structural reforms in food security, wages, sanitation or gender 
equality are harder to package as discrete, countable interventions. The resulting asymmetry 
does not make MMS misguided. It means the visibility of a solution is partly produced by the 
governance system that can implement it. 
3. Independence, Participation and Non-State Engagement 
WHO must engage with non-state actors because expertise, finance, manufacturing and 
implementation capacity are widely distributed. Its Framework of Engagement with Non -
State Actors seeks to manage conflicts of interest, reputational risks and undue influence while 
preserving beneficial cooperation. For MMS, legitimacy depends on more than disclosure of 
individual conflicts. It also requires attention to structural influence: which questions are 
funded, which products are standardised, which outcomes are pr ioritised and which national 
actors can participate in interpreting the evidence. 
A conditional WHO recommendation can therefore perform three regulatory functions. 
It certifies that adoption is internationally defensible; it provides a common language for 
donors and ministries; and it relocates disagreement from whether MMS is legitimate to how 
quickly and under what monitoring conditions it should be implemented. These effects remain 
compatible with state sovereignty, but they reduce the explanatory value of formal consent 
alone. 
 
65 
Domestic authorities may properly defer to WHO on matters requiring specialised 
expertise, especially where duplicating a global evidence review would waste scarce capacity. 
Deference, however, should be reasoned rather than automatic. The adopting institution must 
still identify whether the global question matches the domestic question, whether baseline 
nutritional conditions and service capacity are comparable, whether resource assumptions 
hold, and whether implementation can satisfy constitutional and statutory duties. The legal 
error is not reliance on WHO; it is treating reliance as a substitute for domestic judgment. 
Epistemic authority can justify a rebuttable presumption, but not an irrebuttable policy 
conclusion. 
Philanthropic And Catalytic Capital As Policy Accelerators 
1. From Funding Research to Organising a Field 
Large philanthropic foundations have become significant actors in global health through 
grants to research institutions, international organisations, civil society and implementation 
partnerships. Their influence is not limited to the amount of money suppl ied. Funding can 
connect research agendas, advocacy, product development, market forecasting and country 
programmes. It can also provide continuity across electoral cycles and move faster than public 
budgeting. 
Historical comparison of the Rockefeller and Gates foundations shows how private 
wealth can shape global health priorities while operating through different institutional styles. 
The Gates -oriented model has often emphasised measurable interventions, techn ological 
solutions and partnerships capable of scaling them. Studies of GAVI demonstrate both the 
capacity of such arrangements to mobilise resources and the risk that health -system 
strengthening becomes defined around the requirements of a particular intervention. 
Public-private partnerships can combine complementary capacities, but their 
performance and legitimacy vary with agenda control, transparency, conflicts of interest and 
accountability. The critical legal question is not whether private funding should be excluded. 
It is how public authorities should govern dependence on resources that come with product 
definitions, timelines, indicators or co-financing expectations. 
2. Material Enablement and Democratic Asymmetry 
Philanthropic power presents a democratic asymmetry. Donors can select problems and 
strategies without receiving an electoral mandate from the populations affected, while 
governments remain answerable for results. At the same time, refusing external suppor t may 
impose immediate opportunity costs on women and children. A legally serious account must 
avoid two simplifications: treating philanthropy as neutral generosity and treating every 
donor-supported intervention as illegitimate domination. 
The contemporary MMS field illustrates ecosystem building. The Healthy Mothers 
Healthy Babies Consortium hosts technical advisory work, advocacy resources and a broad 
membership while acknowledging support from foundations and humanitarian organisations. 
Such networks can improve coordination and disseminate knowledge. They can also align 
expert discourse, advocacy messages and funding around a standardised product. Material 
enablement thus reinforces normative authority: the recommended intervention is not only 
endorsed but surrounded by institutions capable of making it happen.

3. Matching Funds, Market Making and Exit 
Matching finance is especially significant. It frames external support as a catalyst for 
domestic commitment rather than a substitute for it. This can strengthen sustainability by 
requiring government investment. It can also create a ratchet: once coverage expands, political 
and ethical costs make retrenchment difficult even if external finance ends. The legal 
adequacy of a programme therefore depends on an exit architecture from the beginning, 
including budget projections, procurement transition, local production st rategy, quality 
assurance and continuity obligations. 
Market making is equally consequential. Product specifications, demand forecasts and 
pooled purchasing can lower prices and stabilise supply. But they may also narrow the field 
of eligible products, shape domestic industrial policy and create dependence on a small 
supplier base. Public law must treat these effects as part of health governance, not as technical 
matters outside legal analysis. 
Assessment of donor influence also requires a counterfactual. The relevant comparison 
is not between a donor-supported programme and a perfectly autonomous public system. It is 
often between external support and delayed, fragmented or absent implementation . The 
baseline may already reflect fiscal limits, inherited procurement patterns and unequal research 
capacity. A government can rationally accept support while still negotiating conditions and 
protecting future autonomy. The legal objective is therefore not purity from influence, which 
is impossible, but governance of influence: visibility, justification, proportionality and the 
capacity to revise the relationship. 
The Architecture Of Mms Governance 
1. Six Stages of the Corridor 
The governance architecture can be described in six stages. First, evidence production 
defines outcomes and estimates effects. Secondly, normative consolidation converts evidence 
into a formulation, guideline and essential -medicine status. Thirdly, materia l enablement 
supplies grants, matching finance, product availability, technical assistance and market 
coordination. Fourthly, domestic translation places the intervention within budgets, 
regulations, formularies, procurement documents and service standards . Fifthly, street -level 
execution turns the policy into counselling, dispensing, adherence support and data. Sixthly, 
feedback and exit determine whether evidence from implementation changes the standard and 
whether domestic institutions can sustain or revise the programme. 
The stages are analytically distinct but institutionally interdependent. A scientifically 
defensible standard may remain inert without products and money. Funding may fail without 
lawful procurement and trained workers. A national regulation may be symboli cally strong 
but operationally weak if supply is interrupted. Monitoring may generate large datasets while 
missing women who discontinue supplementation because of side effects, mistrust or 
inconsistent counselling. 
2. Power without Command, Responsibility without Exclusive Control 
The corridor distributes control while concentrating legal responsibility. Global actors 
can define the preferred standard and enable scale, yet the state authorises public expenditure, 
regulates product quality, sets service obligations and interacts coer cively or authoritatively 
with residents. The state therefore cannot answer a rights claim by pointing to WHO or a 
 
67 
donor. Conversely, accountability will remain incomplete if only the state is examined while 
the transnational conditions that shaped its decision are treated as background. 
This produces a dual legal demand. Domestic institutions must comply with 
constitutional and administrative duties when adopting and implementing MMS. 
Transnational institutions must satisfy legitimacy standards appropriate to the influence they 
exercise, including transparency, reason -giving, conflict management, participation and 
responsiveness. The relevant question is not whether every influential actor becomes a state-
like duty bearer. It is whether accountability follows function closely enough to prevent zones 
of power without answerability. 
3. A Test for Corridor Intensity 
The intensity of a normative -material corridor can be assessed through five questions. 
How specific and authoritative is the global standard? How dependent is implementation on 
external finance, commodities or technical capacity? How completely has the sta ndard been 
translated into domestic law and routine administration? How costly would it be to pause, 
revise or exit? And how accessible are mechanisms through which affected persons, 
professionals and local institutions can contest the programme? A corridor is strongest where 
normative specificity and material dependence are high, domestic alternatives are weak, exit 
is costly and contestability is low. 
This intensity assessment has consequences for legal form. A low-intensity corridor may 
be adequately governed through ordinary clinical guidance and routine procurement. A high-
intensity corridor calls for a more visible domestic decision, stronger disclosure, independent 
evaluation and a defined transition plan. The degree of proceduralisation should therefore 
track the practical regulatory effect rather than the formal label placed on the international 
instrument. A recommendation joined to nationwide ma tching finance and a standardised 
supply chain deserves more public -law scrutiny than an advisory document that leaves 
domestic resources and alternatives unchanged. 
Indonesia: Structured Agency In A Transnational Policy Field 
1. Constitutional and Statutory Location 
Indonesia's Constitution recognises a right to obtain health services and assigns the state 
responsibility for adequate health-service facilities. Those provisions do not prescribe MMS. 
They establish the public -law setting in which a supplementation progr amme must be 
justified: maternal nutrition policy is an exercise of state responsibility, not a charitable 
concession. 
Law No 17 of 2023 on Health provides an integrated framework covering rights and 
duties, central and regional governmental responsibility, health efforts, facilities, health 
resources, pharmaceutical resilience, health technology, information systems, fina ncing, 
coordination, public participation and oversight. This breadth matters because MMS touches 
each of those domains. It is simultaneously a preventive health intervention, a pharmaceutical 
or health-supply product, a procurement object, a data point, a budget commitment and a 
service delivered through decentralised institutions. 
Government Regulation No 28 of 2024 operationalises the Health Law and supplies a 
further regulatory basis for maternal, child and nutritional health measures. Presidential 
Regulation No 72 of 2021 frames stunting reduction as a holistic, integrated and qu ality-
oriented national strategy involving adolescents, prospective brides and grooms, pregnant and 
 
68 
breastfeeding women, and children under five, with coordination, monitoring, evaluation, 
reporting and funding. This framework is legally important because it resists reducing stunting 
to one commodity. MMS must fit within, rather than displace, the multi-sectoral strategy. 
The maternal-service pathway is further structured by Minister of Health Regulation No 
21 of 2021, which addresses care before pregnancy, during pregnancy, childbirth and the 
postpartum period, together with contraception and sexual health services. Adoption of MMS 
therefore occurs inside an existing continuum of care. Its legal quality depends on whether it 
strengthens that continuum or becomes a stand -alone distribution target detached from 
examination, counselling, referral and informed choice. 
2. The 2026 National Programme 
On 5 March 2026, the Ministry of Health and UNICEF announced a five -year national 
programme under the Child Nutrition Fund. The programme is intended to operate across all 
38 provinces, includes MMS within an expanded maternal nutrition package, aims to 
strengthen supply chains and digital nutrition information systems, supports local production, 
aligns with the 2025-29 national development plan, and anticipates that more than 4.3 million 
pregnant women will benefit by 2029. The Fund is to match resources a llocated by the 
Government of Indonesia. These features make the programme an unusually clear example of 
the normative-material corridor. 
The programme should not be characterised as external imposition. Indonesia has 
articulated its own nutrition priorities, contributes public funds, convenes multiple ministries 
and agencies, and seeks local production. Those acts constitute meaningful agen cy. Yet the 
product standard, international evidence narrative, catalytic finance and implementation 
ecosystem are not domestically generated in isolation. Agency is exercised within a structured 
field. 
Decentralisation complicates that agency. A national decision must travel through 
provincial and district planning, health offices, primary -care facilities, logistics systems and 
local political priorities. Central authorities may define the standard and f inance part of the 
programme, while local governments bear operational burdens and encounter heterogeneous 
needs. The corridor is therefore vertical as well as transnational: global authority shapes 
national policy, and national policy structures local dis cretion. Legitimacy requires upward 
and downward accountability. Local institutions should report implementation constraints to 
the centre, but they should also receive reasons, resources and genuine capacity to adapt rather 
than merely instructions and targets. 
3. Administrative Legality and Public Reason 
Indonesian administrative law requires governmental action to rest on lawful authority, 
proper procedure and general principles of good governance. For MMS, legality should be 
understood substantively. The adopting authority should identify the evidence re lied on, 
explain the treatment of WHO's research condition, assess alternatives, disclose material 
implementation assumptions and specify review arrangements. A bare reference to 
international best practice is not enough because it substitutes external authority for domestic 
reason. 
Public information law supports disclosure of policy rationales, agreements, budgets, 
procurement information and monitoring results, subject to lawful exceptions. Transparency 
is particularly important where donor support, technical advice and public proc urement 
 
69 
intersect. Disclosure should extend beyond the headline value of a grant to the architecture of 
influence: product specifications, co -financing conditions, indicators, intellectual 
contributions, data access, branding, exit terms and the role of implementing partners. 
Public service law adds a user -facing dimension. Women are not merely beneficiaries 
counted against a coverage target; they are service users entitled to standards, information, 
complaint channels and accountable delivery. The relevant quality indicators therefore include 
consistent availability, understandable counselling, respectful treatment, management of side 
effects, ability to ask questions, and continuity when a woman moves between facilities or 
regions. 
4. Budget, Procurement and Industrial Policy 
State-finance law requires public expenditure to be authorised, accountable and directed 
to public purposes. Matching funds do not relax those requirements. They make multi -year 
fiscal analysis more urgent. Government must know the full recurrent cost afte r external 
support, including procurement, warehousing, distribution, training, information systems, 
supervision, pharmacovigilance or product-quality monitoring, and communication. 
Government procurement rules provide the legal channel through which a global 
product standard becomes a domestic commodity. Specifications should protect quality 
without unjustifiably foreclosing competition or domestic capability. Support for local 
production may improve resilience and bargaining power, but it must be accompanied by 
transparent qualification, regulatory oversight and realistic demand forecasting. Procurement 
decisions also need contingency plans for shortages, supplier failure and changes in the 
recommended formulation. 
Product governance continues after contract award. Regulatory approval, batch quality, 
labelling, storage conditions, distribution integrity, complaint handling and recall capacity are 
part of the legal architecture of implementation. A standardised global formulation does not 
guarantee that every locally supplied batch, package or instruction will be suitable. Nor does 
classification as an essential medicine displace national regulatory judgment. Quality failures 
would be especially damaging because they c ould erode trust in antenatal services beyond 
MMS itself. The programme should therefore integrate regulatory data and field complaints 
into the same adaptive review that evaluates coverage and outcomes. 
5. MMS within Indonesia's Structural Nutrition Problem 
Indonesia-specific research reinforces the need for a systems approach. Evidence has 
linked child stunting to water, sanitation and hygiene conditions alongside maternal and 
household factors. Broader review identifies a dense set of determinants, includin g maternal 
nutrition, birth outcomes, infant feeding, infection, food insecurity, care practices, 
socioeconomic position and service access. The legal risk is not that MMS lacks value, but 
that a countable tablet becomes the visible substitute for harder obligations across sectors. 
This is the problem of tabletisation: translating a structural condition into a commodity-
centred programme whose success is measured mainly by distribution. Tabletisation can occur 
even when policymakers acknowledge multiple causes. Budgets, dashboards an d 
accountability meetings may still privilege what can be purchased and counted. Presidential 
Regulation No 72 of 2021 provides a legal counterweight because its holistic design can be 
used to insist that supplementation remains one component of a broader duty.

Street-Level Governance: Where The Standard Meets The Woman 
1. Discretion in Routine Maternal Care 
National adoption does not determine how MMS is experienced. Frontline workers 
interpret eligibility, explain benefits, respond to nausea or other complaints, reconcile new 
and old stock, record adherence and decide when to refer. Street -level bureaucracy theory 
shows that public workers inevitably exercise discretion under conditions of limited time, 
resources and organisational pressure. A formally uniform programme will therefore generate 
locally variable practices. 
Public accountability at street level cannot be secured by central rules alone because 
workers answer simultaneously to professional norms, supervisors, indicators and service 
users. If coverage targets dominate, counselling may be compressed into tablet h andover. If 
stock is unstable, workers may ration or switch products without clear communication. If 
reporting systems penalise discontinuation, side effects may be under-recorded. 
Research on nurses as street-level bureaucrats demonstrates how frontline personnel can 
remain committed to public goals while experiencing frustration, constraint and moral burden. 
MMS implementation should therefore avoid treating non -compliance as a fai lure of 
motivation by either the woman or the worker. Administrative design must provide time, 
training, stock reliability, escalation channels and discretion bounded by rights. 
2. Organisational Power and the Production of Compliance 
Implementation organisations are sites of power, not neutral delivery pipes. Rules, 
performance systems, hierarchy and professional status affect whose knowledge counts and 
which problems become visible. A pregnant woman who reports discomfort or distrust may 
encounter a system organised to achieve coverage rather than to negotiate informed 
adherence. Local managers may also lack authority to alter supply or reporting rules even 
when they recognise implementation problems. 
Experiences with iron supplementation show that women's understandings of anaemia, 
perceived benefits, side effects, family influence, access and quality of counselling shape use. 
MMS adds nutrients but does not remove these behavioural and relational cond itions. 
Communication must avoid implying that a woman is individually responsible for fetal growth 
despite poverty, food prices, work demands or inadequate services. 
3. The Implementation Gap and Adaptive Learning 
Classic implementation analysis shows how policy objectives are transformed by long 
chains of decisions and dependencies. Indonesia's geography, decentralisation and service 
diversity lengthen that chain. A credible research -context implementation should t herefore 
publish not only aggregate coverage and outcome trends but also supply interruptions, 
discontinuation, complaints, regional inequities, counselling quality and reasons for non-use. 
Digital nutrition information systems can support learning, but data architecture should 
not be confused with knowledge. Indicators select what is seen. Data quality may vary with 
workload and incentives. Privacy, access control and secondary use require governance. Most 
importantly, feedback must have institutional consequences: the ability to revise 
communication, procurement, dosage instructions, target populations or programme pace. 
Equity analysis should move below the national average. Island geography, remoteness, 
disability, language, migration status, adolescent pregnancy, poverty and uneven facility 
capacity can affect both access and the quality of counselling. A programme can report high 
 
71 
aggregate coverage while reproducing sharp differences in continuity and informed use. 
Distribution data should therefore be disaggregated where lawful and methodologically 
sound, and qualitative complaints should be treated as evidence rather than anecdot e. Equity 
is not an additional outcome after scale; it is a condition of lawful scale. 
Legal And Ethical Implications: Accountability Without Command 
1. The State Remains the Primary Duty Bearer 
The right to health in the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural 
Rights requires states to take steps towards the prevention and treatment of disease and the 
creation of conditions assuring medical service. External support can help discharge that duty 
but cannot transfer it. The state remains responsible for deciding whether MMS is appropriate, 
ensuring quality and equitable access, and maintaining the rest of the maternal health system. 
The availability, accessibility, acceptability and quality framework provides a practical 
rights test. Availability concerns reliable stocks and trained personnel. Accessibility includes 
geographic, economic, informational and non -discriminatory access. Ac ceptability requires 
culturally and ethically respectful counselling and attention to autonomy. Quality includes 
product standards, storage, prescribing or dispensing practice, adverse -event response and 
accurate information. 
Women's equality rights reinforce the need to resist maternal instrumentalisation. 
CEDAW requires appropriate services in connection with pregnancy and adequate nutrition 
during pregnancy and lactation. The Convention on the Rights of the Child likewise 
recognises maternal and child health, nutrition, clean drinking water and preventive care 
within a connected set of obligations. A rights -based programme must therefore serve the 
woman and the child without making either a justification for neglecting the other. 
2. Law as a Determinant of Health and a Constraint on Governance 
Law can improve health by creating entitlements, financing institutions, regulating 
products and structuring accountability; it can also generate exclusion, fragmentation or 
harmful incentives. In the MMS corridor, domestic law should perform two functions . It 
should enable timely implementation where evidence and need justify action. It should also 
slow the process at specific points where justification, participation, procurement integrity or 
rights protection require scrutiny. 
Proportionality offers a useful discipline. Adoption should pursue a legitimate health 
aim, use means reasonably connected to that aim, avoid unnecessary burdens on autonomy or 
competing services, and maintain a defensible balance between expected benefit, uncertainty, 
cost and opportunity cost. Precaution should not be invoked mechanically either for or against 
MMS. Where potential benefit is meaningful and serious harm is unlikely, uncertainty can 
justify monitored action. Where uncertainty concerns syste m displacement, dependence or 
inequitable implementation, the appropriate response is not paralysis but safeguards, staged 
commitment and reversible design. 
Global governance institutions derive legitimacy not simply from beneficial outcomes 
but from acceptable procedures, epistemic quality and capacity for correction. Accountability 
likewise requires standards against which conduct is judged, information abou t performance 
and consequences or responses when standards are not met. These conditions apply differently 
to WHO, donors, ministries, regulators, suppliers and health facilities, but no influential node 
should be wholly outside them. 
 
72 
3. Six Safeguards for the Normative-Material Corridor 
First, domestic legal translation. International guidance should be converted into a 
publicly identifiable domestic instrument or decision that states the legal authority, target 
population, product standard, place within antenatal care, responsible institutions and review 
date. Translation prevents the guideline from operating as an uncodified external command. 
Secondly, public reason -giving. The government should publish a proportionate 
evidence-to-decision account addressing benefits, uncertainty, local anaemia and dietary 
conditions, feasibility, equity, costs, alternatives and the interpretation of the resear ch 
condition. The purpose is not to reproduce a global evidence review, but to show why the 
intervention is justified in Indonesia and how contrary considerations were handled. 
Thirdly, financial and relational transparency. Donor agreements, matching formulas, 
product or supplier relationships, technical -assistance roles, data arrangements and exit 
assumptions should be disclosed at a level that permits public understanding and audit. 
Transparency must illuminate influence, not merely publish a total funding figure. 
Fourthly, adaptive authorisation. Scale -up should be linked to pre -specified review 
points and decision rules. Data on coverage, birth outcomes, anaemia, side effects, adherence, 
equity, supply reliability, counselling and opportunity costs should be capable of changing the 
programme. Shared responsibility for structural injustice requires institutions to revise the 
conditions they help produce rather than assigning blame only to individuals at the endpoint. 
Fifthly, a systems obligation. Public authorities should demonstrate that MMS 
expenditure and performance targets do not displace necessary measures addressing food 
security, primary care, infection, water and sanitation, social protection and respectful 
maternity services. Public -health justice is concerned with the distribution of multiple 
dimensions of well-being, not only a single biomedical outcome. 
Sixthly, a distributed accountability matrix. Each actor should be linked to its function, 
standard, information duty, review mechanism and remedy. WHO should explain and update 
the evidentiary basis of its recommendation. Donors should disclose conditions and plan 
sustainable exit. National authorities should justify, regulate and finance. Procurement bodies 
should protect integrity and resilience. Regulators should assure quality. Facilities should 
deliver respectful care and complaints mechanisms. Trust is strengthened not by demanding 
confidence in institutions, but by making institutions intelligently answerable. 
4. Experimentation, Review and Remedies 
A research -context recommendation is compatible with phased and adaptive 
governance only if learning can alter the common framework. Experimentalist models 
emphasise provisional goals, local discretion, reporting, peer comparison and periodic 
revision. App lied to MMS, this means local implementation should generate reasons for 
national and global adjustment, not merely evidence of compliance with a fixed model. 
Participation should also be designed around the decisions participants can actually 
influence. Consultation after the formulation, financing model and procurement specification 
have been settled is informational rather than deliberative. Pregnant women, p rofessional 
associations, local governments, disability and women's rights organisations, nutrition 
experts, pharmacists and domestic producers will not hold identical interests. A legitimate 
process does not require consensus. It requires timely access to information, an intelligible 
 
73 
account of trade-offs, reasoned responses to material objections and representation of groups 
that bear implementation burdens without controlling the agenda. 
Institutional design should specify who can demand an explanation, who can investigate 
failure and what happens next. Remedies need not always be judicial. They may include 
administrative complaint, procurement review, disclosure orders, audit findings, co rrective 
action, product recall, protocol revision, budget reallocation or compensation where legal 
standards are breached. Judicial review remains an important backstop where governmental 
authority, procedure or rights are violated. 
5. Maternal Autonomy and Structural Non-Blame 
Public health ethics requires attention to benefits, burdens, liberty, justice, transparency 
and the moral quality of implementation. A supplementation programme should offer clear 
information about purpose, expected benefit, uncertainty, administration an d side effects. 
Routine recommendation may be appropriate, but it should not collapse into pressure or 
stigma. 
Structural violence describes how political and economic arrangements become 
embodied as unequal risk. Maternal undernutrition is shaped by such arrangements. Public 
communication should therefore avoid representing MMS as a test of whether a woman is 
responsible, compliant or caring. The state cannot distribute a tablet and then attribute an 
adverse outcome to individual behaviour while leaving structural conditions unaddressed. 
Fair health policy must consider both health outcomes and the fair distribution of the 
social conditions that produce them. MMS may be part of that policy because a feasible 
intervention should not be withheld while structural reform proceeds. The legal re quirement 
is complementarity: rapid supplementation and slower structural measures must be governed 
as connected obligations rather than competing narratives. 
 
CONCLUSION 
The global expansion of MMS demonstrates how contemporary health governance 
operates beyond the conventional boundaries of law. WHO recommendations, essential 
medicine recognition, expert networks, catalytic finance, product standards, procurement 
support, and monitoring mechanisms do not constitute a single binding command. Instead, 
they form a normative -material corridor that guides policy by making one option more 
legitimate and feasible than others. 
Indonesia’s 2026 program demonstrates both the potential value and constitutional 
complexity of this arrangement. External cooperation can accelerate access, strengthen supply 
systems, and support domestic production. Indonesia also retains meaningful agency through 
political commitment, co -financing, regulation, and implementation. However, the state 
exercises this agency within a policy environment in which evidence, terminology, product 
configurations, and enabling resources are organized through transnational processes. Formal 
consent is therefore necessary but insufficient as a complete account of legitimacy. 
Medical law should respond by tracing power across institutional boundaries while 
maintaining a distinction between influence and legal responsibility. The state remains the 
primary duty bearer toward women and children. WHO, donors, consortia, and supplie rs 
should be accountable for the specific functions through which they influence policy 
formation and implementation. Domestic legal translation, reason -giving, transparency, 
 
74 
adaptive review, systems obligations, and distributed accountability can strengthen the 
legitimacy of the corridor without undermining its capacity to deliver beneficial interventions. 
This analysis is necessarily limited by its documentary methodology. Future research 
should examine the legal structure of program agreements, the development of Indonesian 
procurement specifications, interactions between central and local budgets, the experiences of 
frontline workers and pregnant women, the role of domestic manufacturers, and whether 
implementation evidence influences national or WHO policy. Comparative research across 
countries could further assess whether the corridor operates differentl y in contexts with 
stronger independent evidence assessment, procurement capacity, and domes tic 
manufacturing capabilities. 
The central lesson extends beyond maternal nutrition. Global health increasingly 
operates through standards connected to resources. When such combinations influence what 
states can realistically choose, public law must examine not only whether a rule is fo rmally 
binding, but also how a policy pathway becomes difficult to reject, who benefits, who bears 
the risks, and whether those shaping the pathway remain open to contestation, correction, and 
remedy.
