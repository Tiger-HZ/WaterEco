 A B S T R A C T 
This study examined the post -treatment of pre-treated paper industry wastewater 
obtained via electrocoagulation (EC) using Fe –Fe electrodes. The pre-treated wastewater 
was further treated with peroxymonosulfate (PMS) activation using platinium 
(Pt)/titanium dioxide (TiO2) anode and graphite cathod e. Process optimization was 
conducted using a response surface methodology (RSM) based on a Box –Behnken design 
(BBD) to systematically evaluate the effects of applied current, initial pH, PMS dosage, and 
reaction time on COD removal efficiency and energy c onsumption. The ANOVA results 
confirmed the statistical significance and strong predictive ability of the developed model. 
The model exhibited high R² values (R² = 0.9615 and Adj R² = 0.9230), an adequate signal-
to-noise ratio, and a non -significant lack o f fit. The optimization results of the electro -
persulfate (EP) process show that the main parameters influencing COD removal and 
specific energy consumption (SEC) are the applied current and time. These results indicate 
that the model reliably optimizes CO D removal. Optimal conditions for maximizing COD 
removal were predicted to be a current of 4.32 A, a pH of 8, a PMS dose of 16.3 mM, and a 
reaction time of 80 min. These conditions were expected to achieve 99.9% COD removal. 
However, the experimental resul ts showed 92.3% ± 2.3% COD removal, 86.3% ± 2.8% 
tannin/lignin removal, and 57.2% ± 6.03% additional color removal. These results 
underscore the EP process's effectiveness and flexibility for industrial wastewater post -
treatment, allowing for adjustments in treatment performance and energy requirements.

INTRODUCTION 
 
The paper industry produces cardboard, paper, and other 
cellulose-based products from lignocellulosic materials, such 
as wood and recycled paper. Paper mills are a significant and 
important global industry with high production and demand 
potential. Therefore, it is a crucial sector to consider in terms 
of environmental impact, particularly with regard to the 
consumption of natural resources such as wood and water, 
energy usage, and wastewater production. A significant 
amount of wastewater is produced in this industry during the 
production process, typically ranging from 75 to 275 m³ per 
ton of product. Depending on the raw material, product type, 
and applied process, this volume includes 20-25 m³ from the 
pulping process and 80 -100 m³ from the bleaching proc ess. 
While these values are subject to variation, it is important to 
note that they are well -established and widely accepted 
within the industry [1, 2]. 
Wastewater generated during post-production contains high 
levels of organic matter, suspended solids, and color.

Wastewater leaving the production line contains highly toxic 
and recalcitrant compounds, such as phenols, chlorinated 
lignosulfonic acids, tannins, lignins, fatty acids, resin acids, 
absorbable organic halides, xenobiotics, hydrocarbons, 
dibenzodioxins, and dibenzofurans. According to several 
sources, polymerized tannins, lignins, and their derivatives 
are not biodegradable and are the main cause of color in 
wastewater [3-6]. Additionally, tannins have been found to 
absorb hea t and light, which can lead to a decrease in 
dissolved oxygen (DO) and have a negative impact on the 
flora and fauna in the aquatic environment [7]. Furthermore, 
it has been observed that some substances, such as resin 
acids, long chain fatty acids and organochlorides, can be 
harmful to aquatic organisms and accumulate in the aquatic 
food chain [7]. Hence, it is imperative to process wastewater 
to eliminate these per sistent pollutants and avert their 
detrimental impact on the environment prior to releasing 
them into the receiving media. Proper treatment of these 
pollutants is necessary before discharge to prevent their

negative impact on the biodegradability of wastew ater and 
the aquatic environment [8, 9]. 
Conventional methods used for the treatment of i ndustrial 
wastewater from paper mills, such as primary treatment, 
leaching and aerobic biological treatment [10], are not 
sufficient to remove recalcitrant substances and impurities 
related to decomposition -resistant lignin [11]. Common 
physical treatment methods (eg, adsorption, filtration, 
leaching, sedimentation) remove only a small portion of the 
organic material and do not completely degrade it [12]. 
Conventional treatment approaches are therefore supported 
by advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) to meet emission 
standards and improve water quality and biodegradabi lity. 
Electrochemical methods are preferable for removing 
suspended solids and colloidal substances, as classical 
methods are inadequate. This is a crucial step towards a more 
sustainable and environmentally friendly paper mill industry. 
Electrochemical tr eatment technologies have been 
extensively researched as a viable alternative and have been 
proven to be highly effective in treating wastewaters 
containing recalcitrant and resistant organic pollutants [13-
15]. In recent years, iron (Fe) has emerged as one of the most 
suitable substances for application in wastewater treatment 
due to its abundance and versatile role as both a catalyst and 
coagulant in ele ctrochemical processes. Electrochemical 
processes based on Fe are classified as electrocoagulation 
(EC), electro-Fenton (EF) or a combination of both [16, 17]. 
Electro-oxidation (EO) is an advanced electrochemical 
treatment process in which organic pollutants degrade 
through direct electron transfer at the anode surface or 
indirectly via in situ generated oxidizing species [18]. This 
process primarily relies on the formation of hydroxyl radicals 
(•OH) on the surface of insoluble anodes with a high oxygen 
overpotential. These highly reactive radicals can promote the 
partial degradation or complete mineralization of organic 
contaminants in wastewater [19]. In recent years, sulfate 
radical-based electro -oxidation, generally called electro -
persulfate process, has gained attentio n as a method of 
driving pollutant degradation. In this process, sulfate radicals 
(SO₄•⁻) are generated, either independently or in 
combination with hydroxyl radicals [20]. 
Electro-persulfate processes involve the electrochemical 
activation of persulfate species, such as peroxymonosulfate 
(PMS, HSO₅⁻, E° = 1.82 V) and per oxydisulfate (PDS, S₂O₈²⁻, 
E° = 2.01 V), to produce highly reactive sulfate radicals 
(SO₄•⁻) [21]. Both PMS and PDS contain an O –O peroxide 
bond that can be cleaved through electron transfer, resulting 
in the formation of sulfate radicals (Eqs. (1) and (2)) [22, 23]. 
Persulfate (PS) can be added to the system externally or 
generated in situ via elect rochemical reactions. Whether 
produced electrochemically or added externally, PS generates 
sulfate radicals (SO₄•⁻) upon activation. These radicals are 
highly oxidizing and possess a higher redox potential than 
hydroxyl radicals. This enables the non-selective and efficient 
degradation of various organic compounds. The longer half -
life of SO₄•⁻ compared to •OH improves mass transfer and 
contact with pollutants. Secondary radicals formed from 
SO₄•⁻ reactions with water, such as •OH, further enhance the 
overall oxidation efficiency [21, 22] . In this process, a 
persulfate can be activated using a metal electrode, such as 
iron, silver, zinc, copper, or manganese, to form a sulfate 
radical. However, iron is the most preferred anode material 
due to its lower cost and relative nontoxicity. When sacrificial 
iron anodes are used, iron (II) ions (Fe²⁺) are generated via 
anodic dissolution, as described by Eqs. (3 -5) [20]. Due to 
their high oxidation potential and chemical stability, platinum 
(Pt) anodes have been used in electro-persulfate processes to 
treat various industrial wastewater , washing machine 
effluent dyeing [24], military ammunition plants, and 
washing machines [25], as well as other polluted waters [26].

Despite extensive studies on electrochemical and electro -
oxidation processes for industrial wastewater treatment, 
limited research has focused on the integration of iron-based 
electrocoagulation as a pretreatment step followed by 
electro-persulfate activation using TiO₂/Pt anodes for paper 
mill wastewater. To the best of our knowledge, no previous 
study has systematically investigated the integration of Fe–Fe 
electrocoagulation pretreatment with PMS-activated electro-
oxidation using TiO₂/Pt anodes for the t reatment of paper 
industry wastewater. Therefore, the main objective of this 
study is to investigate the efficiency of an integrated 
electrochemical treatment process for the removal of 
recalcitrant organic pollutants from paper industry 
wastewater. The pr oposed treatment configuration aims to 
enhance COD removal efficiency, offering a technically 
effective and scalable alternative for the treatment of 
recalcitrant paper industry effluents.

paper board manufacturing plant in Istanbul. The raw 
effluent was initially treated by EC process (achieving 35.7%

applied current, pH 6, 70 min, 0.12 M NaCl) and as it did not 
meet the discharge criteria to the receiving environment and 
sewerage, a post-treatment study was carried out using PMS

Experimental Set-up 
 
The pre -treated wastewater was further treated by EP 
process using a TiO 2/ Pt anode and a graphite cathode. 
Graphite was chosen as the cathode material because of its 
high electrical conductivity, chemical stability, and inert 
behavior under electr ochemical conditions. Unlike metal 
cathodes, graphite does not dissolve or become passivated, 
which ensures stable electrochemical performance and lower 
energy consumption [28]. Graphite cathodes are widely used 
in electroche mical oxidation and electro -persulfate systems 
because they minimize parasitic reactions and electrode 
fouling [29]. Batch experiments were carried out in a 
Plexiglas reactor (75 × 75 × 35 mm) with a working volume 
of 100 mL. Continuous mixing was maintained using a 
magnetic stirrer. A digital DC power supply (ADC-3306D) was 
used to apply the current and the electrodes were placed 2 cm 
apart inside the reactor. The setup of the EP reactor is shown 
in Fig ure 1. During the experiments, samples were taken 
periodically from the reactor to evaluate the COD removal 
performance. The study investigated the influence of several 
operating parameters, including applied current (1 -5 A), 
initial pH (2 -10), PMS dosage (5 -25 mM) and reaction time 
(10-90 min). The pH range of 2–6–10 was chosen to evaluate 
the effects of acidic, neutral, and alkaline conditions on 
persulfate activation a nd oxidation performance. Acidic 
conditions (pH ≈ 2) favor the stability and reactivity of sulfate 
radicals (SO₄•⁻). Near -neutral conditions (pH ≈ 6), which 
represent the characteristics of typical paper mill wastewater 
and practical operating conditions, were also included. 
Alkaline conditions (pH ≈ 10) were included to evaluate the 
shift from sulfate radical -dominated oxidation to hydroxyl 
radical-dominated pathways resulting from radical 
conversion reactions. Thus, this pH range allows for a 
comprehensive evaluation of oxidation kinetics, dominant 
radical species, and process efficiency under different 
operational environments.

Experimental design and statistical analysis

predictive models, design experiments, evaluate the influence 
of key factors and identify optimal operating conditions. In 
this study, RSM was used to optimise the EP process after EC 
pretreatment. A four-factor, three-level experimental design 
was used to investigate the influence of key operating 
parameters, including applied current (A), initial pH (B), PMS 
concentration (C) and reaction time (D). In addition, the 
response variables considered in the study were COD 
removal efficiency (Y 1) and specific energy consumption 
(SEC) (Y2). Table 2 presents both the actual and coded values 
of the operating parameters used in the experimental design.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
 
Experimental Design and Model Development of COD 
Removal 
 
The BBD results showed a maximum COD removal efficiency 
of 98.9% for EC pre -treated paper mill effluent. Notably, 
moderate values of SEC (481.5 kWh/m 3) was also observed 
at the experimental condition corresponding to run 11, where 
this highest removal efficiency was achieved. On the other 
hand, the l owest COD removal efficiency (23.1%) was 
obtained in run 26 with the lowest SEC (13 kWh/m 3) (Table 
3). 
Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the 
relationships between the independent variables and the 
response outcomes (Table 4). The coef ficient of 
determination (R²) was used to assess the fit of the 
polynomial model to the data, and the Fisher F-test was used 
to determine the statistical significance of the model. As seen 
in Table 4, the R 2 and adjusted R 2 (Adj-R2) values for COD 
removal efficiency were 0.9615 and 0.9230, respectively. The 
“Predicted R2” value of 0.7956 is in good agreement with the 
“Adj R2” value of 0.9230, which indicates that the model has a 
strong predictive capability. With an F -value of 24.98, the 
model produces a st atistically significant result, suggesting 
that it is highly reliable. There is only a 0.01% chance that 
such a high F -value would occur by chance or due to 
experimental noise. Model terms with "Prob > F" values less 
than 0.0500 are considered statistically significant. The "Lack 
of Fit F -value" of 3.33 indicates that the lack of fit is not 
statistically significant when compared to the pure error. This 
means there is a 12.90% probability that such a value could 
arise due to random variation (noise). A non -significant lack 
of fit is desirable, as it suggests that the model adequately fits 
the experimental data well. The significance of a model can be 
evaluated using the adequate precision and coefficient of 
variation (CV%) values. The “Adequate Precision” va lue, 
which reflects the signal -to-noise ratio, is 19.363. As a ratio 
above 4 is considered acceptable, this high value confirms 
that the model has sufficient signal strength and can be 
reliably used to explore and optimise the design space. While 
acceptable thresholds for C.V.% may vary depending on the 
field of study, in general, values below 10% indicate excellent 
model reliability. Values between 20% and 30% are 
considered acceptable, while values exceeding 30% suggest 
poor model precision and are typically deemed unacceptable 
[30, 31]. In this study, the C.V.% for COD removal efficiency 
was calculated as 9.55, indicating a high level of model 
reliability.

The contour plots and response surface graphs for COD 
removals are presented in Fig ure 3. As can be seen from the 
contour plots (Fig ures 3a, 3c and 3e), the COD removal 
efficiency increased as the applied current rose from 1 A to 3 
A. However, increasing the current beyond 3 A did not 
significantly improve COD removal efficiency. Conversely, no 
improvement in COD removal efficiency was observed as the 
initial pH increased, suggesting that the init ial pH did not 
affect COD removal in the EP process (Fig ure 3a and b). 
Similarly, increasing the PMS dosage and initial pH did not 
improve COD removal efficiency (Fig ure 3c and d). It can be 
said that the midpoint PMS dosage (15 mM) slightly improved 
the C OD removal efficiency. As previously indicated, the 
process time was also found to be a highly significant 
parameter according to the quadratic model, with an increase 
in COD removal efficiency observed as the time increased 
from 10 to 90 min (Figures 3e and f). 
In EO processes, especially those coupled with PMS 
activation, the efficiency of COD removal is heavily dependent 
on operational parameters. The applied current is critical 
because it governs the generation rate of reactive oxidants, 
such as •OH and SO₄•⁻ radicals, at the electrode surface. While 
increasing the current density generally promotes higher 
COD removal by enhancing the formation of these species, 
excessively high current levels may trigger competing side 
reactions, such as oxygen evolutio n. This lowers the overall 
current efficiency [32]. The pH of the solution strongly 
influences the speciation and stability of the generated 
oxidants. Under acidic conditions, sulfate radicals (SO₄•⁻) are 
preferentially formed, whereas hydroxyl radicals (•OH) 
dominate in neutral to alkaline media. Therefore, the most 
effective COD removal is generally achieved within slightly 
acidic to neutral pH ranges depending on the predominant 
oxidation pathway [20, 33, 34]. Prolonging the reaction time 
usually improves COD removal because it allows more time 
for the complete oxidation of organic contaminants. However, 
beyond a certain point, improvement tends to plateau, due to 
factors such as depleted oxidants or mass transfer constraints 
[35]. During electrochemical activation, PMS acts as a 
precursor to generate sulfate radicals. Increasing the PMS 
dosage increases radical availability, promoting more 
effective COD degradation. However, excessive dosing can 
lead to radical quenching and increased che mical 
consumption, which diminishes the process's overall 
efficiency.

Determination of the Optimized Conditions and 
Verification Experiments 
 
The optimization results of the EP process demonstrate how 
process parameters influence COD removal efficiency and 
SEC when treating paper industry wastewater. Setting the 
goal to maximize COD removal y ielded optimal conditions of 
a current of 4.32 A, a pH of 8, a PMS dose of 16.3 mM and a 
reaction time of 80 minutes, with a predicted COD removal of 
99.9% and a desirability value of 1.000 (Table 6). In practice, 
a COD removal efficiency of 92.3% ± 2.3 wa s achieved, 
alongside 86.3% ± 2.8 tannin/lignin removal and 57.2% ± 
6.03 additional colour removal in the post -treatment. While 
the experimental COD removal efficiency was slightly lower 
than the model prediction, these results confirm the 
effectiveness of the optimised conditions, particularly with 
regard to degrading organic compounds. The overall COD 
removal efficiency was found to be 95.05% using hybrite EC 
and EO with PMS. On the other hand, the SEC values at pre - 
and post-treatment stages were calculated at 57.75 kWh/m³ 
and 790 kWh/m³, respectively. The higher SEC observed 
during the post -treatment stage can be attributed to 
persulfate activation and the use of Pt -based anodes, which 
are known to promote strong oxidative conditions. However, 
the substa ntial COD reduction (>95%) indicates that the 
increased energy input in the post-treatment stage effectively 
enhances the degradation of recalcitrant organic compounds, 
justifying the energy expenditure.

CONCLUSION 
 
The findings of this study show that the EP process, when 
optimized through response surface methodology, is a very 
effective post -treatment strategy for pre -treated paper 
industry wastewater. The process achieved substantial 
removal of COD, tannin/lignin, and color. This was done 
under optimised conditions. This confirms its potential for 
the degradation of complex and recalcitrant organic 
pollutants. While a trade -off between treatment efficiency 
and energy consumption was observed, the optimization 
results suggest that the EP process can be adjusted according 
to treatment priorities, whether the goal is to maximize 
pollutant removal or minimize energy requirements. In 
addition, the statistical validation of the developed model 
shows that it is reliable fo r predicting and improving 
processes. These results show that EP post -treatment can be 
added to industrial wastewater treatment plans. This can 
help protect the environment more and meet the standards 
for discharging wastewater. Future studies could focus on 
how to use this technology on a larger scale and the costs and 
benefits of doing so to help companies use it.
