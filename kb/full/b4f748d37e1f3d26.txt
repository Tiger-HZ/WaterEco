Abstract
Purpose To evaluate the outcome of an adductor muscle tenotomy in patients with chronic postoperative inguinal pain 
(CPIP) with a therapy-resistant adductor tendinopathy.
Methods In this retrospective cohort study, CPIP patients who underwent an adductor tenotomy between January 2019 and 
December 2024 at MMC were identified using a CTcue search and an operative code. They were categorised according to 
the extent of surgical procedure (isolated group: adductor tenotomy only; combined group: mesh removal and/or tailored 
neurectomy combined with adductor tenotomy). Outcome was successful (excellent or good) or not (moderate or poor).
Results A total of 32 tenotomies were identified in 29 CPIP patients (25 males, mean age 59 years). All had previously 
undergone conservative therapies without achieving significant pain relief. The success rate in the isolated group was 57.1% 
(8 of 14 procedures). Success in the combined group was attained by 61.1% (11 of 18 procedures). No major complications 
were reported.
Conclusions Adductor tendinopathy can contribute to symptoms in some patients with CPIP. An adductor muscle tenotomy 
may be effective in approximately 60% of CPIP patients with a therapy-resistant adductor tendinopathy, either as an isolated 
procedure or as an adjunct during mesh removal and/or tailored neurectomy. These preliminary findings call for the need of 
further studies with standardised diagnostic criteria and validated outcome measures.

pain (CPIP), defined as groin pain persisting > 3 months fol-
lowing surgery. This pain impairs daily activities or work 
capacity in up to 6% of patients undergoing an inguinal her-
nia operation [2, 3].
The aetiology of CPIP is multifactorial and incompletely 
elucidated [4]. Contributing factors are chronic inflamma -
tory reactions of structures in proximity to the mesh, nerve 
entrapment or iatrogenic tissue injury [ 5]. When conserva-
tive treatments including pharmacological treatment and 
physical therapy fail to alleviate symptoms, surgical inter -
ventions such as tailored or triple neurectomy, mesh removal 
or a combined procedure are considered [ 2, 6–10]. While 
approximately two-thirds of these patients experience pain 
relief following these procedures, a subset of approximately 
30% of patients continues to suffer from persisting CPIP, 
potentially due to alternative sources of pain [2, 11]. In these 
patients with (refractory) CPIP, adductor muscle pathology 
may emerge as a significant contributor [12].
Introduction
Inguinal hernia repair is one of the most frequently per -
formed surgical procedures worldwide. Despite a variety of 
available operative techniques, the global gold standard is 
mesh-based repair, due to its lower recurrence rates com -
pared to non-mesh alternatives [1]. However, approximately 
10–12% of patients develop chronic postoperative inguinal

Clinical signs associated with adductor tendinopathy are 
local pain following provocative adduction of the upper leg 
or pain upon compression of the adductor tendon [13–16]. It 
has been hypothesised that mesh-induced inflammation may 
extend to the origin of the adductor tendon, leading to this 
chronic tendinopathy [ 17]. While conservative treatments 
remain the treatment of choice, adductor tenotomy may be 
considered as a last-resort therapeutic option. Precedent for this 
procedure exist in the athletic population for chronic adductor-
related groin pain [18–22], although direct comparability to 
the CPIP population is limited by differences in aetiology and 
patient characteristics. This study is the first to determine the 
efficacy of this procedure, either as an isolated option or as an 
adjunct to mesh removal and/or tailored neurectomy in CPIP 
patients with a therapy-resistant adductor tendinopathy.
Methods
Study characteristics
This retrospective cohort study was conducted in the Depart-
ment of General Surgery, Máxima Medical Centre (MMC), 
the Netherlands. MMC is a tertiary referral centre spe -
cialising in complex abdominal wall and groin pathology. 
SolviMáx, an integrated institution within MMC, focuses 
on the assessment and treatment of these patients, primarily 
referred from other hospitals, with an annual referral vol -
ume between 1000 and 1200 cases.
This study evaluates two patient groups with CPIP follow-
ing inguinal hernia repair who received an adductor tenotomy, 
either as an isolated option or as an adjunct to mesh removal 
and/or tailored neurectomy between January 2019 and Decem-
ber 2024. Patients were identified through two complemen-
tary methods. An electronic health record search (CTcue) with 
predefined terms in combination with SolviMáx entry was 
queried. Moreover, each adductor tenotomy procedure is tabu-
lated using a dedicated surgical code. Data of possible eligible 
patients were entered in a separate database for further analysis.
The study was conducted according to the Declaration of 
Helsinki. The Zuyd-Máx medical ethics committee judged 
that evaluation of the study protocol was not required since 
this effort was considered an evaluation of patient outcome 
and auditing of own results (METC number: N25.092). In 
addition, each patient who is evaluated in our SolviMáx 
department standardly provides written consent for the 
future use of data for research purposes.
Eligibility criteria participants
Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, a history of open or 
laparoscopic mesh-based inguinal hernia repair, diagnosis 
of CPIP (defined as persisting pain > 3 months following 
repair)[2], clinical signs consistent with adductor tendi -
nopathy (i.e. at least one positive finding including pain on 
resisted adduction of the upper leg, pain at the origin of 
the adductor magnus at the pubic bone, or pain upon com -
pression of the adductor magnus muscle)[ 13–16], adductor 
tenotomy, and availability of an operative report. Prior to 
tenotomy, patients had undergone a combination of conser-
vative treatment options, including several analgesic medi -
cations, repeated injections with local anaesthetic agents, 
core stability training (CSO), or pulsed radiofrequency 
(PRF). Patients were excluded if groin pain was attribut -
able to causes other than CPIP (e.g., recurrent hernia, hip 
joint pathology, meshoma, genitourinary pathology or gyn-
aecological pathology) as determined by clinical assessment 
and imaging when applicable. Neuropathic causes of CPIP 
were deemed less likely in the absence of positive or nega -
tive sensory symptoms [ 23]. No standardised algorithm or 
formal threshold was utilised. Selection for tenotomy was 
individualised and based on the treating surgeon’s expertise.
Data accrual
Electronic health records were used for extracting demo -
graphics, age, gender, height, weight, body mass index (BMI) 
and surgical history. In addition, operative details, type of 
anaesthesia, intraoperative complications, postoperative com-
plications, follow-up time and patient satisfaction were col-
lected. Follow-up was performed 6–8 weeks postoperatively 
by the surgeon who performed the operation. The follow-up 
was extended until a satisfactory result was attained, or when 
additional surgical consultation was deemed non-meaningful.
Preoperative imaging
Preoperative imaging, such as MRI or ultrasound, was not 
standardised in the diagnostic work-up for CPIP-related 
adductor tendinopathy during the study period. The diagno-
sis was based upon patient history and physical examination, 
using criteria validated in the athletic population [13, 14, 16]. 
However, in some cases, imaging had nonetheless been per-
formed. These findings were extracted from the electronic 
patient records. If the indication explicitly addressed adduc-
tor or pubic pathology and was performed in a reasonable 
time to the procedure it was considered part of the diagnostic 
stratagem. Imaging performed for unrelated indications or 
a > 2 year interval from surgery were not included.
Specifics of adductor tenotomy
Prior to the surgical procedure, patients were counselled 
on the pros and cons and potential risks associated with the

surgical procedure. Potential complications that were dis -
cussed were wound infection, haematomas, and the like -
lihood of therapeutic failure. All patients provided verbal 
consent to the specifics of the operation. 
The procedure was performed in day care under general 
anaesthesia, or occasionally spinal anaesthesia. Patients 
were positioned supine with the affected limb in abduction 
(Fig. 1). A 2–3 cm transverse incision was made over the 
adductor tendon 1–2 cm proximal to the level of the inguinal 
fold. The tendon was freed from its surroundings and ligated 
distally, followed by transection using cautery, some 1–2 cm 
from its origin at the pubic bone (Fig. 2 and 3). A complete 
adductor tenotomy was performed, as opposed to a selective 
partial release where only the pathological part of the tendon 
is released [ 24]. Tendon release was confirmed by passive 
alternating hip adduction and abduction performed by the 
surgical team (Fig. 4). The wound was closed in two lay -
ers and infiltrated with 10 cc of bupivacaine. Patients were 
advised to avoid large strides for four weeks.
The decision to concurrently perform a mesh removal 
and/or a tailored or triple neurectomy depended on patient 
history, physical examination, and previous response to 
a range of earlier treatments, including analgesic injec -
tions and PRF. Mesh removal procedures were performed 
in the case of mesh-related pathology that was not previ -
ously addressed, according to a previously published pro -
tocol [9]. In cases of pre-operative sensory disturbances or 
intraoperative identification of an entrapped inguinal nerve 
(iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal, or genitofemoral), the nerve 
was excised via a separate excision following a tailored 
approach.
Outcome
Outcome was patient satisfaction at end of follow-up, with-
out additional therapies. A four-point verbal rating scale was 
used, based on the electronic patient record (EPR) surgical

Data analysis
Data were analysed using IBM SPSS version 29 software 
(SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, US) and were expressed as means 
(± standard deviation), medians (range) or percentages 
(n) as appropriate. The outcome measure and complica -
tion rates were calculated using descriptive statistics. Each 
operation was analysed independently to allow determina -
tion of the effect of adductor tenotomy. In the case of bilat -
eral adductor tenotomy, each side was evaluated separately. 
Given the small sample size and exploratory nature of this 
study, inferential statistics usage was limited. Fisher’s exact 
test was performed to compare the proportion of favourable 
outcomes between the isolated and combined groups, and 
between shorter (< 6 months, n = 22) and longer (≥ 6 months, 
n = 10) follow-up periods. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was 
considered statistically significant.
Results
Demographics and operative specifics
A patient flowchart is depicted in Fig. 5. A total of 209 
patients with adductor tendinopathy were identified, 178 
(85%) had not undergone tenotomy and were treated con -
servatively, 32 adductor tenotomies in 29 patients fulfilled 
study criteria. Of these, 14 were isolated tenotomies, whereas 
18 were combined with mesh removal (n = 3), tailored neu-
rectomy (n = 8), or both (n = 7). In the isolated group, 78.6% 
and 21.4% of patients had previously undergone neurec -
tomy and mesh removal at the affected side, respectively, 
compared with 21.4% and 11.1% in the combined group.
Patients’ characteristics are depicted in Table 1. Opera-
tion time of the isolated tenotomy was 15 min (median) and 
64 min in the combined group.
Preoperative imaging
Preoperative imaging was conducted in 12 of 29 patients 
(41.4%), primarily for reasons unrelated to adductor tendi -
nopathy, such as recurrent hernia and mesh complications. 
Notably, in 3 patients (10.3%), MRI and/or ultrasound were 
performed to investigate potential abnormalities related to 
adductor tendinopathy. However, no abnormalities were 
found on imaging, despite a clinical diagnosis of adductor 
tendinopathy.
Outcome
Outcome is depicted in Fig. 6. At the 4.8 months follow-
up [median, 2–19], outcome after an isolated tenotomy was 
excellent in 5 procedures (35.7%) and good in 3 procedures 
(21.4%), leading to success in 8 procedures (57.1%). As the 
outcome was moderate in 5 procedures (35.7%) and poor in 
one (7.1%), the outcome was unsuccessful in 6 procedures 
(42.9%).
In the combined tenotomy group, outcome after 
4.2 months follow-up [median, 1–60] was excellent in 
5 procedures (27.8%) and good in 6 procedures (33.3%), 
resulting in success in 11 procedures (61.1%). As the out -
come was moderate after 4 procedures (22.2%) and poor in 
3 procedures (16.7%), the outcome was unsuccessful in 7 
procedures (38.9%). No statistically significant difference 
in favourable outcomes was observed between the isolated 
(57.1%) and combined group (61.1%), Fisher’s exact test 
(p = 1.000). Regarding follow-up, one redo tenotomy for 
recurrent adductor tendinopathy was performed in the iso -
lated group after 8 months, and one in the combined group 
after 60 months.
No statistically significant difference in favourable out -
come was observed between a shorter (< 6 months, n = 22) 
and longer (≥ 6 months, n = 10) follow-up period (Fisher’s 
exact test, p = 0.467).
Complications
No major complications were observed. Minor surgical 
complications were haematoma (n = 2, 1 in each group) that 
was managed conservatively.
Discussion
Chronic postoperative inguinal pain (CPIP) is a multifac -
torial and incompletely elucidated complication affecting 
10–12% of patients undergoing inguinal hernia repair [ 2, 
3]. A previous study showed that musculoskeletal patholo -
gies should be recognised in CPIP patients [ 12], and it has 
been hypothesised that mesh-induced inflammation may 
affect surrounding musculoskeletal structures, including 
the origin of the adductor tendon, potentially leading to 
chronic adductor tendinopathy [17]. However, this hypoth-
esis remains speculative due to lack of substantial evidence 
linking mesh-induced inflammation directly to the develop-
ment of adductor tendinopathy. Further research is needed

to elucidate this proposed mechanism and establish a clearer 
relationship between a mesh implant and subsequent mus -

Multiple studies on algorithms for intractable CPIP have 
mainly focused on neurectomy and/or mesh removal but fail 
to mention the role of adductor tendinopathy [ 2, 6–10, 26]. 
A multifactorial surgical approach, including neurectomy, 
partial tenotomy of the rectus and adductor longus muscle, 
and posterior hernia mesh repair, has been described for 
pubic inguinal pain syndrome (PIPS) [ 27]. To date, such a 
multifactorial approach, including tenotomy, has not been 
described in CPIP patients. Aim of the present study was 
to determine the efficacy of a tenotomy, either as an iso -
lated procedure or as an adjunct to mesh removal and/or tai-
lored neurectomy, reflecting the clinical reality that CPIP is 
often multifactorial. The results suggest that CPIP in some 
patients may in part be related to symptomatic adductor 
tendinopathy. Conservative treatment should remain the 
first-line approach for CPIP, however, in this exploratory 
study, adductor tenotomy was effective in approximately 
60% of CPIP patients with therapy-resistant adductor ten -
dinopathy, supporting its consideration as an additional 
therapy option.
Detailed history taking is crucial in CPIP. The syndrome 
should be considered as a spectrum entailing neuropathic, 
nociceptive, nociplastic or combined forms [ 4, 6]. Neuro-
pathic CPIP pain is often described as a sharp, burning or 
shooting painful sensation (‘pins and needles’) that is pro -
gressive after stimulation. In contrast, nociceptive CPIP pain 
is mostly reported as throbbing or nagging [4, 13]. Based on 
clinical history from the EPR in the present cohort, patients 
predominantly displayed a nociceptive pain pattern. In our 
clinical experience, pain originating from adductor tendi -
nopathy is characteristically triggered by lifting the affected 
leg when climbing stairs, crossing the legs while sitting, or 
turning in bed, with some patients reporting waking up due 
to their familiar pain. Getting in and out of a car is often 
painful. These specific symptoms can help to differentiate 
between the different CPIP entities and may guide patient 
selection for a tailored surgical approach.
Physical examination is also essential in diagnosing an 
adductor tendinopathy and identifying the various CPIP 
entities. Tests such as the adductor squeeze test, pain on 
resisted leg adduction, and pubic bone palpation are widely 
used in the athletic population to evaluate whether an adduc-
tor tendinopathy is present [ 13–16]. Clinical signs consis -
tent with adductor tendinopathy were required for inclusion, 
therefore, all patients in our non-athletic cohort showed at 
least one positive test. Although validated in sports medi -
cine, their data in our non-athletic CPIP cohort was clini -
cally informative, underlining the importance of physical 
examination in the diagnostic evaluation of CPIP patients.
Imaging techniques like MRI and ultrasound show 
limited diagnostic value for adductor tendinopathy. MRI 
and musculoskeletal ultrasound can assess tendon struc -
ture, tears, and differentiate adductor tendinopathy from 
other pathologies. Imaging may demonstrate features like 
tendon thickening, increased tendon signal intensity and 
tendon bulging [ 28], but there is often a poor correlation 
between imaging findings and symptoms, as asymptom -
atic individuals may also demonstrate these tendinopathic 
changes. This has been demonstrated in our own cohort, in 
3 patients (10.3%), imaging was performed but all results 
were unmarkable despite clinical diagnoses. This supports 
the notion that imaging for adductor tendinopathy often 
lacks clinical relevance. Most studies are performed in 
athletic populations [ 13, 29, 30], and the absence of stan -
dardised protocols in our cohort further restricts conclu -
sions. Therefore, patient history and physical examination

A complete adductor tenotomy was performed in this 
study, which has traditionally shown good functional results 
in athletes with chronic groin pain, despite some reduction in 
adductor strength [31]. Selective partial adductor release has 
been proposed as an alternative to prevent loss of strength in 
athletes [24]. However, this cohort was older, non-athletic 
and treated for pain relief. Therefore complete tenotomy 
was considered an appropriate treatment approach. Adduc -
tor strength and function were not systematically assessed, 
future studies should include such outcome measures. 
Recurrence after complete tenotomy has been described due 
to the body’s natural healing process, where the ligated ten-
don scars back to the pubic bone [32] . This provides a pos-
sible explanation for the two redos in our cohort.
The present study has several limitations. All patients had 
undergone conservative treatments, and most had undergone 
prior surgeries before the tenotomy, including tailored neu-
rectomy and mesh removal, which may confound outcomes. 
Patients may also not always be able to distinguish tendi -
nous from inguinal canal pain. Patient selection for tenot -
omy and subsequently, indication for isolated or combined 
(neurectomy and/or mesh removal) procedure was based on 
the surgeon’s expert judgement rather than a standardised 
algorithm. The retrospective design and the reliance on a 
single data analyst introduce the risk of selection and infor-
mation bias, follow-up assessments were not blinded, and 
systematic physical examination was absent. Given the 
small sample size and exploratory nature of this study, infer-
ential statistics were performed selectively. Fisher’s exact 
test showed no significant difference in favourable out -
comes between the isolated and combined groups (57.1% vs 
61.1%, p = 1.000) or between shorter (< 6 months) and lon-
ger (≥ 6 months) follow-up (p = 0.467) periods. Both results 
should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited statisti -
cal power. The absence of a control group and natural his -
tory comparison limits the interpretation of the results, and 
the sample size was too small to identify predictive factors 
for success. Outcome was based on a four-point satisfac -
tion scale, quantitative pain scale, symptom duration, and 
objective assessment of adductor strength and function was 
not systematically recorded. Imaging was not part of the 
standardised diagnostic work-up, in case it was available, it 
did not follow a standardised protocol. Despite these limi -
tations, the present study generates a hypothesis regarding 
the role of adductor tenotomy in CPIP patients with clinical 
adductor tendinopathy, calling for future studies.
In conclusion, this is the first study to report that adduc -
tor tenotomy may be considered in a specific group of CPIP 
patients with adductor tendinopathy failing conservative 
treatment, either as an isolated procedure or as an adjunct 
to mesh removal and/or tailored neurectomy. Future stud -
ies with standardised diagnostic and selection criteria, vali -
dated outcome measures, including objective strength and 
functional adductor assessment, standardised imaging, and 
adequate follow-up are needed to confirm these preliminary 
findings and define the role of adductor tenotomy in CPIP 
treatment.
