This study investigates O3 production and chemistry within Indochina biomass burning plumes, using
ozonesondes, satellite, and reanalysis data (2000 –2024). Elevated terrain over the burning region
produces a “terrain chimney” effect, lifting plumes into the free troposphere and con ﬁning downwind
transport largely above the boundary layer. On average, Indochina biomass burning contributes ~30
ppbv of O 3 enhancement in the lower free troposphere (LFT, 2 –5 km) over Hanoi and Hong Kong —
nearly twice the increase reported for North American wild ﬁres— indicating more ef ﬁcient O3
production. Satellite/reanalysis data reveal a NO ₓ-limited O3 formation regime over the Indochina
Peninsula and along smoke transport pathways. On peak-ﬁre days, NO2 surges while CH2O increases
modestly, distinct from North America. Despite the NO ₓ surge, the O3 formation regime remains NOx-
limited across most transport pathways, favoring sustained O 3 production via NO ₓ → O3. Combined
with strong solar radiation and high VOC background, this yields an average O3 production rate of 17.6
ppbv h⁻¹ over northern Laos, and a dilution-corrected excess LFT O 3 concentration of up to 150 ppbv
over the Greater Bay Area on smoke-laden days. Our ﬁndings underscore the urgency of biomass
burning prevention in Indochina.
Tropospheric ozone (O3) is a key atmospheric component inﬂuencing cli-
mate, air quality, and atmospheric chemistry 1. It is primarily formed
through sunlight-driven photochemical reactions between volatile organic
compounds (VOCs) and nitrogen oxides (NOx). These O3 precursors ori-
ginate from both anthropogenic emissions, such as fossil fuel combustion,
and natural sources, including biomas s burning, lightning and biogenic
processes. Open biomass burning—comprising prescribedﬁres, agricultural
burning, and wildﬁres—is estimated to contribute approximately 170 Tg of
O3 annually on a global scale, accounti ng for about 3.5% of total tropo-
spheric O3 production2. In the remote troposphere, however, O3 enhance-
ments from biomass burning emissionsc a nb ee q u a lo re v e ns u r p a s st h o s e
associated with urban emissions3.
Typical biomass burning emissions can enhance tropospheric O3 levels
not only within the burning areas but also in downwind regions, through
long-range transport and chemical reactions occurring along the transport
pathway4–9. The in-situ measurements from Wildﬁre Experiment for Cloud
chemistry, Aerosol absorption, and Nitrogen (WE-CAN) and Fire Inﬂuence
on Regional to Global Environments and Air Quality experiment (FIREX-
AQ) campaigns identiﬁed several critical factors governing O3 production
from wildﬁres in the western United States, and highlighted rapid shifts in
chemical regimes that profoundly affect O 3 formation chemistry during
smoke transport10–12. Full-coverage satellite observations of O3 precursors
from California wildﬁres indicated that nitrogen dioxide (NO2) enhance-
ments within ﬁre plumes are largely conﬁned to the immediate vicinity of
the burning areas, whereas increases in formaldehyde (CH2O, a proxy for
VOCs) extend over much greater distances, reaching from the source
regions to downwind urban locations 13. As a result, NO x-limited source
regions experience a marked rise in NO x, while downwind VOC-limited
urban areas witness a pronounced increase in CH2O, both of which enhance
O3 production efﬁciency. The 2019 Williams Flats wild ﬁre event in the
northwestern United States also exhibited a comparable chemical
mechanism that promotes O3 formation14. Nevertheless, given worldwide
differences in vegetation types, topographic features, and chemical envir-
onments, whether other ﬁre-prone regions share similar O 3 precursor
chemistry remains uncertain and warrant in-depth investigation.

Laos, Cambodia, and Vietnam, is one of the world ’s most active biomass

transport pathway of Indochina biomass burning plumes.
This study aims to enhance our understanding of O 3 production in
springtime biomass burning plumes originating from the Indochina
Peninsula. By integrating multi-site ozonesonde observations (Hanoi, Hong
Kong, and Taipei, including Pengchia yu), multi-satellite remote sensing
data, and meteorological and chemical reanalysis products over the period
2000–2024, we systematically quantify tropospheric O 3 enhancements
attributable to Indochina biomass burning, evaluate the sensitivity of O 3
production to precursor species, a nd identify the dominant chemical
pathways driving O3 build-up within these plumes along a gradient from
near-source to downwind regions. In addition, we compare our ﬁndings
with previous results from extra-tropical North America to highlight the
principal similarities and differences between the two regions.

burning plumes

precursor varies depending on various factors, particularly the relative
availability of NOx versus VOCs. Previous studies indicated that wildﬁre-

Satellite-derived CH2O/NO2 ratios (FNR) can provide regional pho-
tochemical information with good temporal and spatial continuity. Figure4
shows the spatial distributions of sa tellite-based FNR, the corresponding

the area, while other regions show no signiﬁcant changes. On rare-ﬁre days,
the OFS regime across most of the Indochina Peninsula is NO ₓ-limited,
indicating that O3 production is primarily controlled by NOₓ availability.
Thus, large amounts of NOₓ from biomass burning on peak-ﬁre days can
efﬁciently promote O3 formation via the NOₓ → O3 pathway. In northern
Laos—a key biomass burning hotspot—nearly 20% of the region shifts to
transitional OFS conditions, additionally activating the VOC→ O3 path-
way and allowing VOCs from biomass burning to further enhance O 3
production. Consistent with these changes in photochemical condition, the
averaged PO3 in northern Laos surges from 6.2 ppbv h-1 on rare-ﬁre days to
14.3 ppbv h-1 on peak-ﬁre days, and further reaches 17.6 ppbv h-1 on smoke-
laden days. These results provide r obust evidence that biomass burning
emissions lead to highly efﬁcient O₃ production in the core burning region
of northern Laos.

Evidently, due to the topographic con ﬁguration, the biomass burning
plumes from northern Indochina are uplifted and have limited opportunity
to intrude into the VOC-limited bo undary layer in downwind urban
regions, thus precluding the activation of the VOC → O3 pathway. How-
ever, this does not imply that precursor emissions from Indochina biomass
burning do not interact with anthropogenic emissions in downwind urban
areas. Previous studies have shown that downwind urban areas with
anthropogenic emissions establish a relatively high VOC background in the
lower free troposphere32, which, when encountered the inﬂux of pyrogenic
NOx emissions, inevitably ampli ﬁes O 3 production via the NO x → O3
pathway. Compared with extra-trop ical North America, the tropical
Indochina Peninsula and its downstream regions feature both higher VOC
background levels and stronger solar radiation. As a result, the additional
injection of substantial NOx from Indochina biomass burning into the NOx-
limited transport pathway acts upon these conditions, ultimately leading to
more efﬁcient photochemical O3 production.
The above analysis indicates that the NOx surge along the NOx-sen-
sitive transport pathway fuels efﬁcient O3 production in Indochina biomass
burning plumes. Figure 6 presents the excess concentrations of O3 (ΔO3)
and their normalized values (NEC_O3) along the transport axis on peak-ﬁre
days and the following two days, relative to rare-ﬁre days. Extremely high
ΔO3 ( > 60 ppbv) remains con ﬁned in the airshed over northern Laos,
indicating immediate and high-level O3 production in the near-source areas.
Subsequently,ΔO3 decreases rapidly during the plume’s transport over the
Annamite Range due to the dilution e ffects, but recovers gradually after
passing over Hanoi and eventually forms a secondary peak of ΔO3 (~3 0
ppbv) over the GBA region. This recovery of ΔO3 is consistent with ozo-
nesonde observations at Hanoi (ΔO3 = 28.9 ppbv, Fig.1d) and Hong Kong
(ΔO3 = 32.2 ppbv, Fig. 1e), indicating the additional formation of O 3
resulting from the interaction between Indochina biomass burning emis-
sions and downwind urban anthropogenic emissions during transport. In
contrast to ΔO3, NEC_O3 allows us to examine the O 3 f o r m a t i o no rl o s s
processes without confounding inﬂuence of dilution. The NEC_O 3 con-
centrations increase rapidly after the smoke plume passes over Hanoi and
then peak over the GBA region. On peak- ﬁre days, the peak NEC_O 3
concentration exceeds 120 ppbv over the GBA region, which is nearly twice
as high as the NEC_O3 concentration over the biomass burning area. On the
following two days, as the peak‑ﬁre emissions are transported downstream
and mixed with detrained urban an thropogenic emissions, the peak
NEC_O3 concentrations in the downwind region rise steadily day by day,
eventually reaching values above 150ppbv at more eastern locations over
the GBA region on the smoke-laden days. These NEC_O3 results suggest
signiﬁcant in-transit O3 formation as Indochina biomass burning emissions
interact with detrained anthropogenic emissions in downwind urban areas,
which well explains the secondary peak of ΔO3 over the GBA region.

Overall, a combined analysis ofΔO3 and NEC_O3 along the smoke trans-
port axis demonstrates the efﬁcient O3 production both over the burning
area and in the downwind aloft. In the NOx‑sensitive downwind aloft, the
maintenance of ef ﬁcient O 3 production is contingent upon the NO x
enhancement driven by smoke plume transport and its synergistic mixing
with detrained urban anthropogeni c emissions. Given the multifaceted
impacts of elevated O 3 levels on climate, air quality, and atmospheric
chemistry, our ﬁndings underscore the urgency of implementing biomass
burning prevention measures in the Indochina Peninsula.
Our study also has several limitati ons. First, the limited number of
ozonesonde observations at Hanoi and Taipei somewhat complicates the
robustness of cross-site comparisons.Nevertheless, multi-faceted compar-
isons, including comparisons between ﬁre season and adjacent months,
between smoke-laden and smoke-free days duringﬁre season, and between
ozonesonde measurements and EAC4 chemical reanalysis datasets, con-
sistently corroborate the relative magnitude of LFT O3 contributions from
Indochina biomass burning across different ozonesonde sites. Second, the
quantiﬁcation of the Indochina biomass b urning contribution to tropo-
spheric O3 enhancements is likely conservative, as it relies on a“rare-ﬁre”
baseline rather than a“no-ﬁre” baseline. From an observational perspective,
it is dif ﬁcult to identify days with no biom ass burning activity across the
entire Indochina Peninsula during the ﬁre season. Third, the selection of
FNR threshold introduces potential uncertainties in OFS identiﬁcation. We
adopted a moderate FNR value of 4 as the upper bound of the transitional
regime, and the derived OFS regimes show reasonable consistency with
previous observations conducted in the GBA region. As a cautionary
measure, we conducted two additional tests using commonly used and
recently proposed upper bounds, i.e., 2 from Duncan et al36. and 6 from Jin
et al12. Cross-comparisons reveal that the OFS regimes derived from all three
FNR thresholds consistently indicate a NO ₓ-limited regime across most
smoke transport pathways (Figures. S7and S9). This implies that the choice
of the FNR threshold in this study does not fundamentally alter our core
conclusions. Finally, the lack of validation of EAC4 against O 3 precursor
observations may introduce some uncertainties in the characterization of
O3-precursor chemistry. However, the EAC4 product has been well vali-
dated against multi-site ozones onde observations, which may help
constrain its uncertainties. Despite these limitations, this work provided the
ﬁrst comprehensive assessment of O3 production chemistry within Indo-
china biomass burning plumes, from n ear-source to downwind regions,
offering useful insights for both local and regional O3 mitigation strategies.
Methods
Ozonesonde dataset
The ozonesonde data used in this study comprise the publicly available
observations from four downwind sites of the Indochina Peninsula —
namely Hanoi, Hong Kong, Taipei, and Pengchiayu (Fig.7a). The ozone-
sonde data at Hanoi are available in the SHADOZ (Southern Hemisphere
ADditional OZonesondes) archive https://tropo.gsfc.nasa.gov/shadoz/,
while these at other three sites are available in the WOUDC (World Ozone
and Ultraviolet Radiation Data Centre) archive https://woudc.org/en/.
These ozonesondes are conducted at 14:00 local time, weekly at Hong Kong,
and once or twice a month at other sites. Nearly all of the aforementioned
sites have captured individual cases of tropospheric O 3 enhancement
induced by Indochina biomass burning using ozonesonde
observations29,37,38.F i g u r e7b provides a summary of the ozonesonde
observations at these four sites duringthe study period. Hong Kong, located
in the Greater Bay Area (GBA) of South China, exhibits the highest ozo-
nesonde frequency, with a total of 1,081 soundings, 199 of which were
conducted during the Indochina ﬁre season (March –April). In contrast,
ozonesondes at Taipei and Pengchiayu are relatively sparse in time, with a
total of 183 soundings. Given their geographical proximity, ozonesonde data
from these two stations are combined for subsequent analyses.

A spatiotemporal fusion framework is applied to integrate the OMI
and TROPOMI products. We ﬁrst extract the overla pping observational
data from the two sensors and examine their consistency. For variables that
exhibit pronounced biases—and given that TROPOMI offers superior data
quality relative to OMI31,43—we use the overlapping TROPOMI observa-
tions as a baseline to derive linear re gression functions relating the two
products44. Once the linear regression relationship is established, we correct
all OMI data, including those outside the overlapping period. Then, we
integrate the TROPOMI observation into the corrected OMI data by taking
the mean value at overlapping grid cells and inserting the TROPOMI value
at non-overlapping grid cells, thereby accomplishing cross-satellite data

EAC4 chemical reanalysis
The Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service’s Atmospheric Compo-
sition Reanalysis 4 (EAC4), the fourthgeneration ECMWF global reanalysis
of atmospheric composition, is used to characterize the three-dimensional
O3 and its precursors (NO2 and CH2O) within Indochina biomass burning
plumes. EAC4 adopts an extended version of the Carbon Bond 2005 che-
mical mechanism with anthropogenic and natural emission inputs, and also
assimilates satellite observations of O3,N O2, and other species with a 4D-
Var method45. The EAC4 Global Fire Assimilation System utilizes satellite
observations of ﬁre radiative power to estima te the pyrogenic emissions
from biomass burning and vegetationﬁres46. To evaluate whether EAC4 can
accurately represent atmospheric composition in Indochina biomass
burning plumes, tropospheric O 3 concentrations from EAC4 products
(06:00 UTC) are compared to ﬁre-season ozonesonde measurements at
Hanoi, Hong Kong, Taipei and Pengch iayu (Figure. S3). The validation
results conﬁrm an overall high correlation (R = 0.76) between EAC4 rea-
nalysis and ozonesonde observations in the troposphere, with a mean
absolute bias (MAB) below 13 ppbv. The signiﬁcant overestimation of O3 by
EAC4 in the atmospheric boundary layer is most likely attributable to model
deﬁciencies in representing loss processes and emission strength. However,
in the lower free troposphere, wher e long-range transport of Indochina
biomass burning plumes occurs, EAC4shows well agreement with ozone-
sondes (R = 0.69, MAB = 10.63 ppbv). Overall, these validations demon-
strated the capability of EAC4 products to capture the free-tropospheric O3
variability during the Indochinaﬁre season.
Identiﬁcation of ozone formation sensitivity
Tropospheric O3 formation exhibits a nonlinear response to its precursors
and can be broadly categorized into three sensitivity regimes: VOC-limited,
NOx-limited and transitional35,36. We diagnose ozone formation sensitivity
(OFS) using the ambient concentration ratio of two indicator species —
CH2Ot oN O2 (hereafter referred to as FNR)—which serves as a proxy for
VOCs and NOx directly involved in O3-NOx-VOC chemistry. The FNR is a
widely adopted indicator of OFS in both satellite-based and model-based
diagnostics30,35,47–50. Early studies suggested that when FNR exceeds 2, ozone
formation is limited by NOx availability; when FNR falls below 1, it is limited
by VOCs; and values between 1 and 2 indicate sensitivity to both
precursors36. However, recent studies have shown that the upper bound of
the transitional regime can be as high as 435,51, and occasionally exceeds this
value (e.g., reaching 6) within wildﬁre plumes12. Given the lack of available
surface O3 observations over the Indochina Peninsula, we are unable to
establish local FNR thresholds for thisbiomass burning study. We therefore
use the moderate FNR thresholds 1–4 from these previous studies for our
OFS identiﬁcation, and also test alternative thresholds (1 –2a n d1 –6) to
evaluate the sensitivity of our results (Figures. S7 and S8).
