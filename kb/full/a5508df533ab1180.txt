As the climate warms, fire and water stress are becoming more frequent and intense, making interactions between them more likely. While controlled field experiments in forests suggested that low-severity burns can influence drought responses, these findings may not apply to fire-prone savannas and shrublands, where large, variable-intensity fires and recurrent water stress dominate. Therefore, landscape-scale effects of fire on ecosystem responses to water stress in these savannas and shrublands remain poorly understood. Here, we focused on the North Australian Tropical Transect (NATT), where fire and water-stress events occur frequently across a 1,600 mm rainfall gradient spanning mesic woody savannas to semi-arid grasslands, and arid shrublands. Water-stress events were classified as fire-affected when fire occurred either during the water-stress period or within a 12-month antecedent window, and as fire-free otherwise. Ecosystem responses were quantified using satellite-derived gross primary productivity (GPP) from the Global OCO-2 Solar-Induced Fluorescence (GOSIF) product. Resistance was defined as the proportional change in GPP during the stress event relative to a multi-year climatological baseline, while resilience was defined as the relative recovery of GPP one year after the event compared with the same baseline. After accounting for water-stress severity and duration, fire-affected events showed lower resistance (−0.02 to −0.03) and resilience (−0.04 to −0.10) than fire-free events (p < 0.001), with resilience showing the greater reduction. Spatially, fire-associated declines were greatest in highly flammable hummock grasslands and arid shrublands, while woody savannas showed comparatively smaller responses. Late dry-season fires were consistently associated with larger immediate productivity declines and slower post-stress recovery than early dry-season burns. These findings extend previous savanna fire studies beyond site-scale and single-disturbance approaches by assessing ecosystem resistance and resilience under interacting fire–water stress conditions across multiple hydroclimatic zones and vegetation types.
