Abstract: Candidatus Accumulibacter is a key genus of polyphosphate-accumulating 16 
organisms (PAOs) found in laboratory- and full-scale wastewater treatment systems, 17 
mediating enhanced biological phosphorus removal (EBPR). However, little is known 18 
about their ability to resist phage infection. We conducted a systematic analysis of the 19 
occurrence and characteristics of clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic 20 
repeats and associated proteins (CRISPR-Cas) systems and prophages in diverse Ca. 21 
Accumulibacter taxa (43 in total, including 10 newly recovered genomes). Fourty 22 
complete CRISPR loci were identified in 28 genomes, falling into 6 subtypes. The 23 
occurrence and distribution of CRISPR-Cas systems did not follow a vertical 24 
evolutionary relationship. Phylogenetic analyses of the cas genes and direct repeats 25 
(DRs) suggested that the CRISPR-Cas systems were likely acquired via horizontal 26 
gene transfer, with acquisition rates higher than those of speciation, rendering 27 
different Ca. Accumulibacter distinct adaptivity to phage predations. 2448 spacers 28 
were identified, 67 of them matched to known phages. Major differences were 29 
observed among the numbers of spacers for different Ca. Accumulibacter, showing 30 
unique phages that could be resisted by different members. A comparison of the 31 
spacers in genomes having the same cas gene but from distinct geographical locations 32 
indicated that habitat isolation may have resulted in the acquisition of different 33 
spacers by different Ca. Accumulibacter. Metagenomic analysis allowed the 34 
identification of 26 viral contigs (18 are Caudovirales members) in 6 metagenomic 35 
datasets from three lab-scale enrichment reactors, matching to 73 spacers in 10 Ca. 36 
Accumulibacter genomes in these reactors, showing the specific immunity of these Ca. 37 
Accumulibacter. Metatranscriptomic analyses showed the activity of the CRISPR-Cas 38

1. Introduction 51 
Phosphorus (P) is a pollutant in surface waters and also a non-renewable resource 52 
(Oehmen et al., 2007). Enhanced biological phosphorous removal (EBPR) is a widely 53 
applied process in municipal wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) (Chen et al., 2022; 54 
Mino et al., 1998); it requires polyphosphate accumulating organisms (PAOs) capable 55 
of releasing P under anaerobic, and taking up more P than normally required for 56 
metabolism and growth, under aerobic conditions (He and McMahon, 2011; Lu et al., 57 
2021; Zhang et al., 2022). Candidatus Accumulibacter represents a key group of 58 
PAOs in lab- and full-scale EBPR systems (Martín et al., 2006; Petriglieri et al., 2022; 59 
Qiu et al., 2020; Singleton et al., 2022; Srinivasan et al., 2021; Tomás-Martínez et al., 60 
2021). Ensuring the viability of Ca. Accumulibacter is crucial for reliable P removal 61 
in WWTPs. However, even in well-controlled operating conditions, EBPR systems 62 
frequently experience deterioration or failure (Ong et al., 2014; Thomas et al., 2003). 63 
The overgrowth of glycogen accumulating organisms (GAOs) was commonly 64 
considered as a key cause, which competing with PAOs for carbon sources (such as 65 
volatile fatty acids, VFAs) without contributing to P removal (Kolakovic et al., 2021; 66 
McIlroy et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2021). However, GAOs might not well-explain 67 
EBPR deterioration in a variety of systems(Gu et al., 2008; Law et al., 2016; Nielsen 68 
et al., 2019). Even in lab-scale systems, non-GAO induced EBPR failure was 69 
commonly observed. In a lab-scale EBPR system operated at elevated temperate, a 70 
sudden drop in the EBPR activity (P release and uptake decreased from 40 to 0 mg/L 71 
within a day) was observed. The relative abundances of both PAOs and GAOs 72 
decreased significantly, suggesting that the affected EBPR activity was not a result of 73

Phages are viruses specialized in infecting bacteria, which play a major role in 75 
shaping the microbial communities in natural and engineered systems (Fernández et 76 
al., 2018). In WWTPs, phages affect functional microbial communities by infection, 77 
gene transfer, and lysogenic conversion (Tamaki et al., 2012; Wu and Liu, 2009). A 78 
previous study suggested that phages in activated sludge number on the order of 10 8-79 
109 ml -1 (Chen et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021), that is, their density is 10-1000 times 80 
higher than in natural aquatic environments (10 4-108 ml-1) (Wommack and Colwell, 81 
2000). Such high occurrences of phages imply intensive interactions with activated 82 
sludge bacteria. Like other bacteria, Ca. Accumulibacter are susceptible to phage 83 
predation. In a study on a deteriorated laboratory-scale EBPR system, large quantities 84 
of lysed Ca. Accumulibacter cells were observed, and phages isolated from the 85 
supernatant were used to inoculate other sequencing batch reactors (SBRs), resulting 86 
in Ca. Accumulibacter cell lysis and a significant decline in P removal (Barr et al., 87 
2010). 88 
A key mechanism for bacteria to resist phage predation is the clustered regularly 89 
interspaced short palindromic repeats and their associated protein (CRISPR-Cas) 90 
system, consisting of a CRISPR array and a few cas genes (Barrangou et al., 2007). 91 
The immune process of the CRISPR-Cas system works in three stages: adaption, 92 
CRISPR RNA (crRNA) biogenesis, and interference (Fig. S1). At the adaption stage, 93 
the CRISPR-Cas system captures foreign nucleic acid (such as phage genetic material 94 
and plasmids) and integrates a fragment (about 30 base pairs) into the CRISPR array 95 
as spacers to provide infection memory. At the crRNA biogenesis stage, CRISPR 96

a conserved direct repeat (DR) and a variable spacer. In the infection stage, the 98 
interference machinery is guided by crRNA to identify and cleave foreign nucleic 99 
acids (Hille et al., 2018). This immunity mechanism is widely preserved in the 100 
bacterial and archaeal kingdoms (Barrangou et al., 2007). Phage predation is a threat 101 
to Ca. Accumulibacter and EBPR, and CRISPR-Cas systems are essential for Ca. 102 
Accumulibacter to resist phages (Flowers et al., 2013). However, little is known about 103 
this defense system in different lineage members of Ca. Accumulibacter. 104 
Apart from lysing the host cells immediately, phages have another infection mode, 105 
namely lysogeny. Under such circumstance, temperate phages integrate their genomes 106 
into the host bacteria chromosome and become prophages (Feiner et al., 2015), which 107 
replicate with their host and stay at an inactive state. They may be activated with or 108 
without external stimulations (such as UV light, chemicals, or temperature) (Barnhart 109 
et al., 1976; Shan et al., 2014) to enter the lytic cycle and kill their host. Prophages in 110 
Ca. Accumulibacter genomes are timebombs which might be triggered at times, 111 
threatening the reliability of EBPR. Limited information is available on prophages in 112 
existing Ca. Accumulibacter genomes. 113 
In this study, CRISPR-Cas systems and prophages in different Ca. Accumulibacter 114 
lineage members were systematically investigated to understand their capability in 115 
resisting phage predations. The objectives were to (i) understand the adaptivity of 116 
different lineage members of Ca. Accumulibacter to phage predictions; (ii) analyze 117 
the temporal and spatial variation of their ability in resisting phages; (iii) identify and 118 
characterize phages and prophages which are highly threatening to different Ca. 119

2.2. Metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analysis 144 
After DNA and RNA extraction, metagenomic and metatranscriptomic sequencing 145

low-quality reads and adapter sequences were removed using fastp (0.20.1) (Chen et 147 
al., 2018). High-quality reads were assembled to contigs using metaSPAdes (v.3.13.0) 148 
(Nurk et al., 2017). The contigs were binned into metagenome-assembled genomes 149

et al., 2019). Completeness and contamination of MAGs were evaluated by checkM 151

performed using the Genome Taxonomy Database Toolkit (GTDB-Tk v. 1.1.0) 153

6% were discarded. The relative abundance of each Ca. Accumulibacter were 155 
calculated using BBMap (v38.96) (Bushnell et al., 2017). All draft genomes were 156 
annotated using RAST (V2.0) (Brettin et al., 2015). Except for quality control by fastp, 157 
annotation by RAST and relative abundance calculation by BBMap, all analyses were 158 
processed on the KBase platform (Arkin et al., 2018). 159

adapter sequences and rRNA (Chen et al., 2018; Kopylova et al., 2012). Reads that 161

2.6. Spacer analysis in Ca. Accumulibacter 208 
Spacers record the history of phage infections and show the identity of phages unable 209 
to infect the host bacteria. Spacer comparison and visualization were done using 210 
CRISPRStudio (Dion et al., 2018). They were compared to the IMG/VR database 211 
using the online comparison function provided by the database for the identification 212 
of their corresponding phages. IMG/VR database provided the largest collection of 213 
viral sequences covering both cultivated viruses and uncultured ones identified from 214 
metagenomes (Roux et al., 2020). 215 
To analyze the relationships between Ca. Accumulibacter and phages in our SBR 216 
reactors, VirSorter (v2.2.3) (Guo et al., 2021) was used to identify viral contigs from 217 
the assembled metagenomic data which contained Ca. Accumulibacter. These viral 218 
contigs were used to build a phage genome dataset. The spacers in Ca. 219 
Accumulibacter genomes (retrieved from our SBRs) were compared to the phage 220 
genome dataset by BLASTn using the short-BLAST parameter (Altschul et al., 1997). 221 
The phage contigs which completely matched the spacers were compared to the 222 
IMG/VR database for taxonomic identification (Roux et al., 2020). 223

3.3. High Occurrence of CRISPR-Cas system in Ca. Accumulibacter 260 
Fourty CRISPR-Cas loci were found in 28 Ca. Accumulibacter genomes (Fig. 1B and 261 
Table S3). Fifteen genomes were not found to have a complete CRISPR-Cas system. 262 
These genomes are distributed in different clades (e.g., IA member UW8, IIC 263 
members SK-02 and SCUT-1, IIF members UBA6585 and SCELSE-1 (Qiu et al., 264 
2020), and IIH member SCELSE-7). The overall occurrence of CRISPR-Cas systems 265 
in these Ca. Accumulibacter genomes was 65.1%, which is similar to the discovery 266 
rate in our newly recovered genomes (70%, 7 out of 10 had CRISPR-Cas systems). 267 
The rate is higher than the estimated average occurrence of CRISPR-Cas systems in 268 
bacteria (42.3%) (Makarova et al., 2020). There is consensus that CRISPR-Cas 269 
systems provide vital immunity to prokaryotes (Hille and Charpentier, 2016). The 270

Known CRISPR-Cas systems to date are classified into 2 classes, 6 types and 33 280 
subtypes (Makarova et al., 2020). All CRISPR-Cas systems in Ca. Accumulibacter 281 
fall into Class 1, including 2 types (type I and type III) and 6 subtypes (3 type I-A, 6 282 
type I-C, 9 type I-E, 2 type I-F, 11 type III-A and 9 type III-B, Fig. 1B). Type I system 283 
primarily targets double-stranded DNA (Lin et al., 2020). Type III system has both 284 
RNA and single-stranded DNA cleavage activities, which works against both the 285 
transcripts and genomes of invaders (Jiang et al., 2016). Previous studies 286 
hypothesized that type III systems could effectively counteract propagation of single-287 
stranded RNA phage in E. coli (Tamulaitis et al., 2014). Overall, RNA phages are rare 288 
in the prokaryotic world (<10%) and spacers in type III system that match RNA 289 
viruses are yet to be identified (Koonin et al., 2015; Krupovic et al., 2011). 290 
Additionally, the mutation rates of RNA viruses are higher than those of DNA viruses 291 
(by three orders of magnitude), and maintaining long-term immunity against RNA 292 
viruses would require extremely rapid spacer renewal (Duffy, 2018). Of 28 Ca. 293

3.5. Phylogenetic analysis of the CRISPR-Cas system 342 
Phylogenetic analysis was performed based on the species tree of Ca. Accumulibacter 343 
(Fig. 1B). The distribution of CRISPR-Cas systems was not in line with the vertical 344 
evolutionary relationships among different Ca. Accumulibacter members. CRISPR 345 
loci from different subtypes scattered across the species tree (Fig. 1B). Even for 346 
closely related species, for instance, Clade IIC members SSA1 (obtained from an 347 
EBPR reactor in Singapore, Arumugam et al., 2019), SK-02 (obtained in an SBR in 348 
Queensland (Skennerton et al., 2015)) and UW6 (from an EBPR reactor in the 349 
University of Wisconsin, McDaniel et al., 2021) are closely related (ANI>99.1%) 350 
(Fig.1A). However, the occurrence and identity of the CRISPR-Cas systems in these 351 
strains showed significant differences. SSA1 harbors a type I-C CRISPR-Cas locus, 352 
and UW6 contains type I-A and type I-E CRISPR-Cas loci. No CRISPR-Cas system 353 
was found for SK-02. Similar cases were found for Clade IIF members SSB1 and 354 
SCELSE-1, clade IIA members UW1 and AALB, and Clade IA members BA93, 355 
UBA6658 and UW8. Overall, different members of the same clades were shown to 356 
possess different type CRISPR-Cas systems (Fig. 1B). 357

genomic streamlining theory (Lawrence et al., 2001), incomplete prophages typically 614 
occur with neutral or beneficial genes being retained and the genes which are essential 615 
for prophage activation being removed. The high numbers of putatively defective 616 
prophage sequences observed in these Ca. Accumulibacter genomes indicate rapid 617 
inactivation of prophages by Ca. Accumulibacter together with a slow decay of 618 
prophage genes (Brüssow et al., 2004). Incomplete prophages typically lack the 619 
ability to entry the lytic cycle and kill the host. Prophages in Ca. Accumulibacter 620 
genomes may also benefit cells, e.g., via the superinfection exclusion mechanism, 621 
where prophages tend to prevent subsequent phage infections of their host (Bondy-622 
Denomy and Davidson, 2014), like those in Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp. 623 
(Labrie et al., 2010). The capability of Ca. Accumulibacter to resist lytic phages may 624 
also be enhanced due to the expression of genes that increase fitness of the host cell 625 
by a process known as lysogenic conversion. However, since previous research 626 
showed that prophages in Ca. Accumulibacter could be induced with external stimuli 627 
(Motlagh et al., 2015), the presence of intact and questionable prophages in Ca. 628 
Accumulibacter represents a threat to them and hence EBPR stability. 629 
ANI analysis showed that prophages in Ca. Accumulibacter are highly diverse 630 
(ANI<70%, Fig. 6B). There are also closely related prophages (e.g., BA-93_ppg2, 631 
UBA6658_ppg1 and ACC005_ppg1, ANI=100%) whose host Ca. Accumulibacter 632 
genomes also shared close phylogenetic relationships (ANI > 98.9%, Fig. 6B), 633 
implying that these prophages might have been inherited from their latest ancestor. 634

3.9. Prophage gene integrity 635 
For intact and questionable prophages, their genes were classified into five modules as 636 
lysogeny, DNA replication, DNA packaging, tail morphogenesis, and host lysis (Fig. 637

On the contrary, the genes classified into the lysis module showed the lowest 640 
occurrence. It was identified only in 15 prophages, three of which harbored both the 641 
lysin and holin genes. Holins and lysins are viral enzymes that lead to host cell 642 
destruction for phage lease (Wang et al., 2000). The presence of these genes confers 643 
higher lysogeny (Feiner et al., 2015). Three of these prophages (i.e., SCELSE-644 
5IIH_ppg1, SCELSE-7IIH_ppg1, and DS2011_ppg1, encoding both holin and lysin 645 
genes) are readily inducible. The induction of prophages may lead to the extinction of 646 
host Ca. Accumulibacter cells and adversely affect the EBPR performance. Prophages 647 
which lack of holin and/or lysin genes are not necessarily harmless. For example, the 648 
Dickeya spp. specific phage vB_DsoM_LIMEstone1 and the Pectobacterium 649 
carotovorum specific phage PP1 lack the genes encoding holin and lysin, respectively, 650 
but both were shown to be highly infectious (Adriaenssens et al., 2012; Lee et al., 651 
2012). It is possible that some hypothetic protein genes may have encoded the holin 652 
and lysin. 653
