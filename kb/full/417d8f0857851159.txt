Abstract
This study assesses the aquifer characterization for small-scale irrigation in the 
semi-arid Northeastern Amhara Region, Ethiopia, focusing on the Kobo, Girana and 
Cheffa districts. The study involved the integrated techniques of geophysical survey 
using Vertical Electrical Sounding employing Schlumberger array, field check of 
existing hand-dug wells, hydrological studies and soil properties analysis. The results 
indicate substantial shallow groundwater potential, at Kobo, Girana, and Cheffa, where 
farmers have successfully excavated hand-dug wells. The borehole data indicates 
at Kobo, Girana, and Cheffa exhibit static water levels at depths of 8 m, 5 m, and 
10 m, with corresponding total drilled depths of 40 m, 13 m, and 32 m, respectively. 
The identified potential land was 862 ha of land suitable for shallow groundwater 
coverage, comprising 448 ha in Kobo, 200 ha in Girana, and 214 ha in Cheffa. 
However, there are significant gaps between potential and actual use with only 
220 ha and 150 ha irrigated in Kobo and Cheffa respectively. The pumping discharge 
data from 25 boreholes in Cheffa indicated a uniform yield of 16.6 L/sec with a 
total annual capacity of about 130,994 m 3. The cumulative discharge rates from the 
Kobo and Girana well field indicated the opportunity for substantial agricultural 
development. The optimized development of shallow groundwater could provide 
for up to three growing seasons annually, contributing significantly to food security 
and agricultural productivity. These results provide critical scientific evidence for 
policymakers, planners, and local communities to optimize groundwater utilization 
while ensuring long-term sustainability in this drought-prone region.

 1 Introduction
Groundwater is a critical resource for agricultural production, particularly in semi-arid 
regions where surface water is scarce and unreliable [ 1]. Globally, groundwater sup -
plies approximately 40–43% of irrigation water, supporting the agricultural production 
of billions of people living in water-scarce environments [ 2, 3]. In Ethiopia, agricul -
ture accounts for approximately 35% to GDP and 68.2% to employment and 90% of the 
Page 2 of 17
Mollaw et al. Discover Water (2026) 6:112 
country’s export earnings, making water availability a key factor in the nation’s eco -
nomic development [ 4]. In the semi-arid regions of northeastern Amhara, specifically 
in the Kobo, Girana, and Cheffa districts, agriculture is heavily reliant on groundwater 
resources to supplement rain-fed farming systems, which are threatened by increasing 
variability in precipitation due to climate change.
The reliance on shallow groundwater systems for irrigation has become increasingly 
important in North and South Wollo, Oromia administrative zones. Shallow groundwa -
ter sources are typically found within 35 m of the subsurface and are accessible through 
shallow wells and hand-dug systems, making them more feasible and cost-effective for 
smallholder farmers [5]. However, shallow groundwater resources in Ethiopia are poorly 
characterized and inadequately managed. While deep aquifers have been the focus of 
many studies and development projects, shallow groundwater resources remain under-
explored, which can potentially lead to unsustainable water use and environmental 
degradation [ 6]. A systematic assessment and characterization of shallow groundwa -
ter potential in the Kobo, Girana, and Cheffa districts is critical for optimizing irriga -
tion potential, ensuring sustainable use, and supporting agricultural productivity in the 
region. The aim of this study is to delineate accessible shallow groundwater aquifers for 
manual excavation through geophysical mapping, specifically electrical resistivity; and 
to promote sustainable, modern irrigation technologies that optimize water use dur -
ing dry periods. By focusing on user-friendly systems that provide reliable, on-demand 
water access, this research will make practical recommendations for local communities 
and strategic guidance for policymakers on enhancing water resource management. In 
Ethiopia’s Northeastern Amhara region, specifically in the districts of Kobo, Girana, 
and Cheffa, agriculture is the predominant livelihood, and irrigation is a vital compo -
nent for food production. Though, the recurring droughts and erratic rainfall patterns 
in this region necessitate the exploration of shallow groundwater resources to support 
irrigation.
This study aims to assess and characterize shallow groundwater potential in the Kobo, 
Girana, and Cheffa districts. The study focuses on identifying areas with significant 
groundwater potential and evaluating the suitability of these resources for irrigation, 
ensuring sustainable water management. The results of this study inform water resource 
managers, farmers, and policymakers on the best strategies for harnessing aquifer char -
acterization for sustainable irrigation, ultimately contributing to the region’s agricultural 
development and resilience to climate change.
2 Study area description
The study focuses on the North Wollo, South Wollo and Oromia administrative zones, 
especially the Kobo, Sirinka, Girana, Jari and Cheffa districts, in the northeastern part 
of the Amhara region, Ethiopia, geographically situated between latitudes 11°30 ′ and 
12°50′ N and longitudes 39°40 ′ and 40°45′ E. The area spans across a wide range of alti -
tudes, from around 1400 m above sea level in the lowland areas of Kobo to more than 
2500 m in the highlands of the Cheffa district (Fig.  1). This elevation gradient influences 
not only the climate but also the surface and subsurface hydrological processes that 
govern groundwater recharge and availability. This region, positioned within the upper 
reaches of the Awash River Basin, is characterized by diverse topographical, climatic, 
and geological features. These variations play a crucial role in determining groundwater

3 Materials and methods
3.1 Subsurface hydrogeological study
Geophysical investigation of the subsurface structure was studied by using ABEM Ter -
rameter instrument. This system is essential for acquiring critical subsurface structural 
information by deploying electrodes into the ground and measuring the electrical prop -
erties of the underlying geological formations [ 7, 8]. The instrument works on the basic 
principles of electrical resistivity tomography and induced polarization using to inject 
electrical current through the ground while measuring any resulting potential differ -
ences across electrodes. The electrical resistivity survey employs electrical sounding 
utilizing the Schlumberger setup with an ABEM Terrameter. The Schlumberger array 
used, with maximum current electrode separation of (AB/2)1000 m, electrodes are nor -
mally arranged along a straight line, with the potential electrode placed between the 
current electrodes. This configuration is mostly used subsurface information consider -
ing the approximate depth of investigation is about one-third to one-half of the current 
electrode spacing depending on subsurface conditions [ 9, 10]. Using the VES approach,

3.2 Soil properties
Soil texture, hydraulic conductivity, porosity, and moisture retention characteristics 
were determined through field surveys and laboratory analyses. These properties play a 
critical role in assessing the infiltration rates and groundwater recharge capacity of the 
soils in the study area. Soil samples were analyzed for texture, moisture content, per -
meability, and hydraulic conductivity using standard laboratory procedures. The results 
provided essential data for assessing soil-water relationships and infiltration rates.
3.3 Meteorological data
This study used a long-term meteorological dataset to examine the climate patterns of 
the Kobo, Habru, and Cheffa Boundary region. Daily and monthly records of precipi -
tation and temperature, spanning 35 years (1987–2021), were acquired from nine local 
stations of the Ethiopian National Meteorological Agency (ENMA). These stations were 
Woldia, Sirinka, Mersa, Hara, Dessie, Kombolcha, Cheffa, Majetie, and Kemesie which 
provided the fundamental information needed to describe the distribution of rainfall 
and temperature regimes in the region.
3.4 Irrigation potential
The Kobo, girana, and Cheffa districts have shallow groundwater resources that could 
be used to expand irrigated agriculture. To effectively harness this potential, a rigorous 
assessment and characterization of groundwater availability are essential. This work will 
generate a detailed agricultural suitability map focused on areas where aquifer charac -
terization can be feasibly and sustainably developed for irrigation. The analysis will spe -
cifically highlight zones with high natural recharge and reliable groundwater availability, 
thereby delivering a scientific and spatially explicit basis for informed irrigation planning 
and sustainable water resource management.
3.5 Characterization of the study area
3.5.1 Climatic characterstics of the study area
The climate in the study area varies from semi-arid in the lowlands to sub-humid in the 
highland regions. Rainfall is highly seasonal, with the majority of precipitation occur -
ring during the Kiremt season (June to September). Annual rainfall ranges from 700 mm 
in the drier, lower-lying areas of Kobo Girana to over 1200 mm in the higher altitudes 
of Cheffa. The variability in rainfall poses significant challenges for rain-fed agricul -
ture, making the role of groundwater crucial for irrigation, especially during the dry

Mollaw et al. Discover Water (2026) 6:112 
seasons. Temperature patterns also vary with altitude. In the lower areas, such as Kobo 
and Girana, mean temperatures range between 20 and 35 °C, while in the higher regions 
of Cheffa, temperatures are cooler, ranging from 10  to 25  °C. The high temperatures 
in the lowlands lead to higher evapotranspiration rates, increasing the need for effec -
tive groundwater management to supplement surface water resources for agricultural 
activities.
3.5.2 Rainfall distribution of the study area
The rainfall distribution of the three-study area (Habru, kobo and Cheffa Boundary) 
shows considerable spatial and temporal variability, influenced by its diverse topography 
and climatic zones. The three of the Boundary receives an average monthly rainfall rang-
ing from minimum 0 mm to maximum 450 mm, with significant differences observed 
between the dry and wet seasons. During the dry season, typically from November to 
February, rainfall is minimal or completely absent, resulting in values close to 0 mm (Fig. 
2).
3.5.3 Temperature
The temperature characteristics of the Habru, Dawa Chaffa, and Kobo boundary exhibit 
a significant variation due to the wide range of elevation, above sea level. In the lower 
elevations, particularly in the Kola climatic zones, temperatures are generally high, often 
exceeding 25 °C, and the climate is hot and dry for much of the year. As the elevation 
increases towards the midlands, which fall within the Wena Dega zone, the temperatures 
become moderate, typically ranging between 18 and 25 °C, creating favorable conditions 
for diverse agricultural activities. In the highland areas, corresponding to the Dega zone,

3.5.4 Topography
The topography of the study area is characterized by a combination of flat plains, roll -
ing hills, and mountainous terrains. The lowland plains of Kobo Girana, known for their 
relatively gentle slopes, provide favorable conditions for shallow groundwater accumu -
lation. On the other hand, the hilly and mountainous regions of Mersa and Cheffa are 
defined by steeper slopes, which result in higher runoff and reduced groundwater infil -
tration. However, these regions serve as important recharge zones for groundwater due 
to the presence of fractured rocks and alluvial deposits that enhance infiltration. Several 
seasonal rivers and streams, including the Hormate, Borkena and Cheleka Rivers, tra -
verse the study area, contributing to the surface water hydrology and potential ground -
water recharge. These water bodies are vital for both agriculture and livestock, but they

3.5.5 Geomorphology
The geological formations in the studied area vary greatly and have a considerable 
impact on the distribution and availability of groundwater resources. Volcanic rocks, 
alluvial deposits, and sedimentary strata comprise the region’s foundation. Quaternary 
alluvial deposits dominate in the lower-lying regions of Kobo and Girana areas creat -
ing ideal conditions for shallow groundwater deposition [ 12]. This region is tectonically 
active, with normal faults and marginal grabens that shape drainage and groundwater 
storage. The geomorphology consists of Quaternary alluvium interfingering with frac -
tured volcanic rocks, making the valley an important groundwater storage area. This 
pattern creates a sharp seasonality in water availability and agricultural production.
3.5.6 Groundwater recharge
The geology and ground water potential assessment at Girana and Kobo valley have 
been determined by the Commission for Sustainable Agriculture and Environmental 
Rehabilitation in Amhara (Co-SAERAR) in 1996 through 1999 and review and appraisal 
of Hydrogeological studies by Geo-Engineering Service in 2002/3. The study made pre -
liminary estimation of annual recharge capacity of the Kobo and Gerana sub basins to 
be 119 and 27 MCM, respectively [ 13]. In 2015 hydrogeological study of the Kobo Val -
ley, irrigation currently uses about 30,600 cubic meters of groundwater per day and the 
aquifer receives an average of 122.9 million cubic meters of natural recharge each year 
[12] and [ 14]. A comparison of these numbers shows that the rate of recharge is much 
higher than the rate of abstraction at the moment, indicating a sustainable water balance 
for current irrigation techniques. The river network with the headwaters of the Borkena 
River in the Kutaber highlands, function as gaining streams, predominantly fed to 
groundwater recharge and also the rivers near Harbu and Dessie are classified as losing 
streams, contributing to aquifer recharge. Based on the hydrological assessment of the 
Cheffa Valley indicates an estimated groundwater recharge rate of 370 mm per year [15].
4 Result and discussion
4.1 Geological characteristics
A geophysical evaluation using electrical resistivity method for groundwater investiga -
tion was carried out which involved the utilization of Vertical Electrical Sounding (VES) 
technique with Schlumberger array system [ 16]. The basic principle involves injecting 
current into the ground and measuring the resulting voltage to calculate resistivity [ 17]. 
The sub-surface geologic materials have different nominal resistivity, different materials 
have different resistivity, so by measuring this, we can infer subsurface structures [ 18]. 
Vertical Electrical Sounding (VES) was used to assess subsurface hydrogeology at five 
study sites: Kobo Abourie Kebele, Habru Geredo and Girana Kebele, Hyke Jari Agricul -
tural Trial Site, and Dawa Cheffa Gobeya Kebele. The method effectively defined critical

4.1.1 Kobo
In kobo abourie kebele, the results of the VES survey show a progressive sequence that 
progresses from unconsolidated surficial deposits to a consolidated bedrock unit. A 
clayey topsoil layer that is 0.508 m thick at the beginning of the profile is followed by 
a sandy layer that is 0.488  m thick. The first two layers extend to a cumulative depth 
of 0.996 m (Fig.  5). Below this; the water table horizon is identified at a depth of about 
4.9 m by a saturated zone that is 3.91 m thick. A noticeable high-conductivity layer that 
is 3.74 m thick and extends to 8.64 m further characterizes this aquifer (Table 1).
A notable 8.86-meter stratum of coarse sand and gravel (17.5-meter total depth) that 
is considered as a main aquifer unit follows the sequence’s transition. A highly con -
ductive layer extends from 17.5 to 35.3 m depth, indicating that fruitful hydrogeologi -
cal conditions may continue down to 35.3 m. At the end a thick, 17.3-meter sequence 
of consolidated sandstone, forming the basal bedrock unit from 35.3 to 52.6 m depth. 
These results suggest that manually excavated wells may successfully access groundwater 
between approximately 8 and 28 m depth., the depth greater than 35 m will be effective 
for intended purpose.

4.2 Shallow ground water resource availability
The analysis of pumping discharge data from the Chefa borehole system reveals a high 
degree of consistency across the 25 sampled boreholes, with a predominant discharge 
rate of 16.6  L per second (l/s). The aggregate pumping capacity for the system were 
415 L/s, equivalent to an annual yield of approximately 130,994 cubic meters (Table  6). 
This cumulative discharge emphasizes the significant and reliable irrigation potential 
afforded by the borehole infrastructure within the Cheffa district.
Based on hydrogeological data from the Habru (Girana) hand-dug well field, each of 
the three boreholes demonstrates a sustainable yield of 4  L/s. This corresponds to an 
estimated annual water volume of between 28.8 and 57.6 thousand cubic meters (m³) 
per well. Collectively, the well field provides a combined discharge of 12  L/s, yielding 
an annual water supply of approximately 115,200 m³. The available data indicate a sub -
stantial groundwater potential within the Girana area. The yield is sufficient to support 
planned irrigation activities across a significant command area, confirming the viability 
of the resource for agricultural development but the farmers currently use for domestic 
needs and small-scale gardening.
The randomly sampling of 31 Hand-dug Well Data, all 31 wells show a pumping dis -
charge of 16.6 L/s each, with the cumulative pumping discharge summing up to 514.6 L/s 
(108882.2 m3/yr) (Table 8). This indicates a strong irrigation potential from the dug-well 
system in Kobo, providing a reliable and uniform discharge capacity across the wells.

5.2 Recommendation
Prioritization will be emphasis for groundwater development in Kobo and Girana, where 
thick, high-yield aquifers (coarse sand/gravel and weathered zones) occur at accessible 
depths (8–28 and 21–39 m, respectively), supporting sustainable irrigation abstraction. 
In Gerado, shallow aquifers (< 20 m) are perched and insufficient for irrigation, deeper 
layers are dominated by low-permeability clay or weathered basalt. In Jari, cobble/boul -
der deposits obstruct manual drilling, focus should instead be on exploring fractured 
basalt zones for potential deeper yields. Standardize borehole construction to target the 
most conductive saturated zones identified in VES profiles (e.g., layers 3–4 in Kobo, lay -
ers 5–6 in Girana). Maintain pumping rates at ≤ 16.6  L/s to avoid over-extraction and 
ensure long-term sustainability. Continuous monitoring wells and water-level loggers at 
key sites (Kobo, Girana, Cheffa) is needed to track dynamic water levels, salinity trends, 
and seasonal fluctuations, enabling adaptive management and early warning of deple -
tion. Encourage farmer cooperatives for shared well operation and maintenance, espe -
cially in high-yield zones, to optimize water use efficiency and reduce individual drilling 
risks. Use resistivity-derived lithological maps to guide land-use planning, aligning 
crop selection and irrigation scheduling with aquifer recharge capacity and soil mois -
ture retention characteristics. A targeted capacity-building program is needed to help 
farmers switch from traditional hand-dug methods to contemporary, light appropriate 
low-cost mechanized drilling technologies in order to advance sustainable irrigation 
practices.
