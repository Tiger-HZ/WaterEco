In recent years, the growing consumer demand for venison has prompted a
strategic shift in the sika deer industry,transitioning from traditional antler-
oriented practices toward meat-focused production systems4.C o n c u r r e n t l y ,
sika deer meat has entered high-end food markets globally. In addition,
regions such as New Zealand, Europe, and the United States have estab-

luation, and product marketing 5. These developments underscore the
urgent need for systematic research on venison to support industry
advancement and the comprehensive elucidation of meat quality
characteristics.
However, research on deer meat quality remains sparse, particularly at
the molecular level. To date, most studies have focused on macroscopic or
physicochemical parameters, with limited application of high-throughput
omics approaches. For example, a recent study by Jin et al. provided a
comprehensive analysis of the physicochemical and nutritional properties of
meat from twelve sika deer cut parts, highlighting site-speciﬁc differences in
pH, tenderness, amino acid content, and fatty acid composition4. However,
such studies lack insight into the molecular regulatory mechanisms driving
these differences. In recent years, high-throughput omics approaches—
including transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics —have been
widely employed to elucidate meat quality traits in major livestock species
such as cattle, pigs, and poultry6–8. For instance, a 2024 study on beef cattle
undergoing compensatory growth employed RNA-Seq to identify 57 dif-
ferentially expressed genes and 31 enriched pathways related to pH, cooking
loss, and fat content, highlighting the complement–coagulation–ﬁbrinolysis
system as a key regulatory network9. In pigs, a proteomic analysis used data-
independent acquisition mass spectrometry to investigate the effects of
chilling rate on pork quality, pinpointing several proteins involved in cut
structure and energy metabolism10. Additionally, a metabolomic study of
Jingyuan chickens identiﬁed 4643 differential metabolites across age groups,
revealing links between oxidative phosphorylation,β-alanine metabolism,
and cut lipid deposition with changes in pH₄₅min and color traits11.D e s p i t e
these advances in other livestock, comparable single-omics studies on sika
deer are still absent, and integrated multi-omics investigations in deer meat
have not yet been conducted. This gap underscores the need for a

Proteomic proﬁles and functional insights
A total of 5367 proteins were detected (Supplementary Fig. 2A), with
approximately 98% (5271) shared across all cuts (Fig.3A), indicating a high
overlap in protein types among different tissues. Based on this, differential
expression analysis was conducted by comparing each cuts against the
others, revealing differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in each: BB
(DEPs = 40), GM (DEPs = 56), IM (DEPs = 49), LD (DEPs = 60), QF
(DEPs = 128), and T (DEPs = 63) (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Data 4).
Functional enrichment showed region-speciﬁc proteomic patterns asso-
ciated with cuts quality (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Data 3). LD-speciﬁc
proteins were enriched in transcriptional regulation and arginine/proline
metabolism pathways, indicating act ive protein synthesis and turnover,
which may support tenderness and functional integrity 20. In contrast,
downregulated proteins in LD were associated with lipid metabolism and
the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, su ggesting a lower capacity for fat
deposition and stress adaptation21. QF was enriched in glycolysis and pyr-

Regarding sex differences (Supplementary Fig. 2B and Supplementary
Data 3), male samples exhibited upregulation in cAMP signaling and
morphine addiction pathways, suggesting more dynamic responses in

and ABC transporters, indicating enhanced capacity for protein synthesis
and metabolic stability26. In terms of seasonal differences (Supplementary
Fig. 2C and Supplementary Data 3), spring samples showed enrichment in
transcriptional regulation and virus-related pathways, suggesting higher
metabolic and renewal activity. In contrast, winter samples were enriched in
carbon ﬁxation, galactose metabolism, and starch/sucrose metabolism

mulation, which may enhance ﬂavor and nutritional quality (Fig. 3Ea n d

sexes, and seasons highlight functional variations in metabolic regulation,

Multi-omics integration reveals key regulatory hubs
To explore multi-level regulatory mechanisms, we constructed a cross-
omics interaction network based on WGCNA module correlations and
identiﬁed several signi ﬁcantly associated module pairs across tran-
scriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic levels (Fig. 6A). Among them,
ME4_metab and ME8_protein exhibited strong negative correlation and
were both highly associated with the IM region, suggesting that this cuts area
features lipid-driven and endocrine-regulated activity, while having lower
energy and structural metabolism—traits consistent with soft texture and
rich ﬂavor. Moreover, ME3_protein and ME2_metab formed two inte-
grated regulatory chains with ME6_trans and ME7_trans
(ME6_trans–ME3_protein-ME2_metab and ME7_trans –ME3_protein-
ME2_metab). ME6_trans was enrichedin oxidative phosphorylation and
glycolysis (Fig. 6B), while ME7_trans focused on carbon metabolism and
lipid biosynthesis (Fig.6C), indicating their roles in energy supply, cutsﬁber
formation, and fat deposition.
We further constructed a tri-omics regulatory network (Fig.6D) and
identiﬁed key hub molecules. Transcriptomic hub genes like COQ9 and
NDUFA8 (energy metabolism) were highly expressed in LD, BB, and GM,
whereas ITGA6 and WWC1 (linked to structural integrity and lipid meta-
bolism) were enriched in IM and QF, reﬂecting their roles in fat accumu-
lation and cuts quality formation (Fig. 6E). In the proteomic level, TBB1,
TNNC1, TNNT1,a n dMYL10 emerged as key hub proteins, showing high
expression in IM and T —cuts regions known for higher fat content and
tender texture—and lower levels in the ﬁrmer BB and QF regions. TBB1
contributes to cytoskeletal integrity, TNNC1 and TNNT1 regulate slow-
twitch ﬁber contraction, and MYL10 inﬂuences cuts ﬂexibility and lipid
metabolism. Their elevated expressionmay support the softness, elasticity,
and marbling features of IM and T, reinforcing their superior tenderness
and ﬂavor (Fig.6F). At the metabolite level, lipid oxidation products such as
Prostaglandin G2 and (±)11-HETE were downregulated in IM and T,
potentially contributing to tenderness and ﬂavor. Conversely, 19(R)-
hydroxy PGE2 was elevated in QF, likely supporting rapid metabolism and
ﬁrmer texture (Fig.6G).
In summary, our multi-omics WGCNA integration identiﬁed a set of
key regulatory modules and hub molecules associated with cuts region-
speciﬁc traits. In particular, the two core interaction chains —
ME6_trans–ME3_protein-ME2_metab and ME7_trans –ME3_protein-
ME2_metab—highlight the coordinated regulation of lipid metabolism,
energy supply, and tissue structure, representing majorﬁndings of this study
and offering valuable targets for meat quality improvement and molecular
breeding.

Different muscle cuts exhibited varying degrees of molecular variation
across the transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic datasets. At the
transcriptomic level, IM showed rela tively more differentially expressed
genes, including ADIPOQ and PLIN1, which are associated with lipid
metabolism and intramuscular fat deposition 17,18. Several extracellular
matrix-related genes, including COL11A2, COL12A1, COL22A1, COMP,
FMOD,a n dTHBS4, were also relatively highly expressed in IM. These genes
have been reported to participate in extracellular matrix organization and
structural maintenance in skeletal muscle 19. Pathways including PPAR
signaling, ECM–receptor interaction, and TGF-β signaling were also enri-
ched among different muscle regions. Previous studies have linked PPAR
signaling to lipid metabolism and intramuscular fat deposition, whereas
ECM-related pathways may contribute to tissue structure and tenderness-
associated properties13,19. At the proteomic level, QF was mainly enriched in
glycolysis and pyruvate metabolism pathways, which may inﬂuence muscle
energy metabolism and meat quality characteristics22. In contrast, IM and
GM were associated with lysosomal d egradation and glycosaminoglycan
metabolism, pathways potentially re lated to extracellular matrix main-
tenance and tissue properties 23,24. In addition, proteins such as TNNC1,
TNNT1,a n dMYL10 showed relatively high abundance in IM and T, con-
sistent with their roles in muscle contraction and slow-twitch ﬁber char-
acteristics. Metabolomic analysis identi ﬁed fatty acyls,
glycerophospholipids, and sterol lipids as major differential metabolite
classes among muscle regions 27,28. IM contained relatively high levels of
metabolites such as tiglic acid, oleamide, and propionylcarnitine, which may
be associated with lipid metabolism andﬂavor-related characteristics28.
Cross-omics integration identiﬁed several correlated transcriptomic,
proteomic, and metabolomic modules associated with regional differences
among muscle cuts. Among these, ME3_protein and ME8_protein were
enriched in pathways including glycolysis, amino acid biosynthesis, cytos-
keletal regulation, and PPAR signaling. Previous studies have linked these
processes to tenderness-related traits and intramuscular fat deposition29,30.
Metabolomic modules ME2 and ME4 displayed opposite association pat-
terns between QF and IM/T, suggesting differences in energy metabolism
and lipid-associated processes among muscle regions31,32. Two correlated
module chains, ME6_trans –ME3_protein–ME2_metab and
ME7_trans–ME3_protein–ME2_metab, connected transcriptomic, pro-
teomic, and metabolomic features a ssociated with muscle structure and
metabolism. In addition, hub molecules includingCOQ9, NDUFA8, ITGA6,
TNNC1,a n dTNNT1 displayed region-speciﬁc expression patterns. Finally,
RT–qPCR and Western blot analyses of genes associated with lipid
deposition, extracellular matrix-related characteristics, and muscle con-
traction features (FMOD, THBS4, PLIN1, TNNC1,a n d TNNT1)f u r t h e r
supported the expression patterns observed in the multi-omics analyses.
Several limitations should also be considered. First, this study mainly
focused on molecular proﬁling and did not include direct, detailed histo-
logical analyses of meat quality traits. Second, the relationships identiﬁed by
WGCNA were based on correlation analyses and cannot fully demonstrate
causal regulation among genes, proteins, and metabolites. Although
RT–qPCR and Western blot analyses supported several of the observed
expression patterns, validation was limited to selected targets and repre-
sentative muscle regions.
In conclusion, this study provides an integrative characterization of
molecular differences across six maj or muscle cuts in farmed sika deer.
Integrated transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analyses revealed
region-dependent molecular differences among muscle regions, with
intercostal and trapezius muscles showing coordinated molecular features
related to lipid metabolism, extracellular matrix organization, muscle con-
traction, and energy utilization. Cross-omics integration further identiﬁed
two correlated module chains, ME6_trans–ME3_protein–ME2_metab and
ME7_trans–ME3_protein–ME2_metab, linking transcriptional variation to
structural and contractile protein modules and downstream metabolic
characteristics. The region-dependent expression patterns of FMOD,
THBS4, PLIN1, TNNC1,a n d TNNT1 were further supported at the tran-
script and protein levels by RT–qPCR and Western blot analyses, providing
independent validation of selected multi-omics ﬁndings. These ﬁndings
provide candidate molecular features associated with variation among sika
deer muscle cuts and establish a reference framework for future studies
integrating direct meat quality measurements and functional validation.
Methods
Sample collection
A total of 72 cuts were obtained from 12 healthy adult sika deer (6 males and
6 females), all raised under standardized feeding and management condi-
tions at Shuangyang Deer Quality Breed Breeding Co., Ltd. (Changchun,
China). All animals were selected with comparable developmental status
and body condition and were maintai ned under standardized intensive
housing conditions with routine health monitoring to minimize biological
heterogeneity. The sample size was determined to ensure sufﬁcient statis-
tical power while adhering to the principles of animal welfare. No animals or
samples were excluded from the analysis. Due to the seasonal reproductive
characteristics of sika deer, estrus generally occurs in autumn, and par-
turition is concentrated mainly between May and June each year. All ani-
mals in the spring group were slaughtered on April 1, 2024, at 22–23 months
of age, whereas all animals in the winter group were slaughtered on
December 23, 2024, at 29–30 months of age. Therefore, when sika deer from
the same birth cohort are slaughtered in different seasons, a difference of
about 6 months in biological age is unavoidable. Within each group, all
animals were slaughtered on the same day under uniform procedures to
maintain consistency in their physiological condition, slaughter handling,
and postmortem treatment. Prior to sample collection, all deer were
transported to the Shilu Deer Industry Group Co., Ltd. in Shuangyang
District, Changchun, Jilin Province, where slaughtering was performed
under uniform protocols. Six distinct cuts—longissimus dorsi (LD), inter-
costal muscles(IM), trapezius (T), b iceps brachii (BB), gluteus maximus
(GM), and quadriceps femoris (QF)—were dissected postmortem. Detailed
sample information is provided in Supplementary Data 1. According to the
speciﬁc requirements of transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic
analyses, tissue samples were appropriately processed and preserved,
including storage in RNA-later solution and/or immediate snap-freezing in
liquid nitrogen. All procedures in volving animals were reviewed and
approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Northeast
Forestry University, China (A pproval No. 20220409). This study was
reported in accordance with the ARR IVE guidelines (Animal Research:
Reporting of In Vivo Experiments).
Transcriptomic sequencing and data analysis
Total RNA was extracted from cuts tissue samples collected from different
anatomical regions of sika deer using the Trizol method and quantiﬁed with
the NanoDrop® 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA). Genomic DNA
was removed with RNase-free DNase I and the mRNA libraries were
constructed using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina)
with 5 μg of input RNA. Fragmentation, reverse transcription, adapter
ligation, and ampliﬁcation were conducted following the manufacturer’s
protocol. A 150 bp insert size was selected using AMPure XP beads
(Beckman Coulter), and library quality was validated on the Agilent Bioa-
nalyzer 2100 system. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq
6000 platform.
Raw reads wereﬁltered using FASTP (v0.20.1)33,34 to remove adapters
and low-quality sequences. Clean reads were aligned to the sika deer
reference genome (GCA_040085125.1])12 using HISAT2(v2.0.4)35. Gene-
level counts were generated with HTSeq36, and expression levels were cal-
c u l a t e di nF P K M( f r a g m e n t sp e rk i l o b ase of transcript per million mapped
reads) using the formula: FPKM = (read count × 10 ⁹)/(total mapped
reads × gene length).

Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the
DESeq237 (v1.42.0) based on read count data obtained from RNA-seq. To
ensure statistical robustness and to avoidpotential pseudoreplication arising
from multiple muscle samples collected from the same animal, sex, sam-
pling season, and individual animal identity were included as covariates in
the design formula. For each cuts type , pairwise comparisons against the
remaining groups were performed, an d differentially expressed genes
(DEGs) were deﬁned using the threshold of adjusted p value (padj) <0.05
and absolute log₂ fold-change (log2FC) > 1. For dimensionality reduction,
transcript per million (TPM) values were calculated by normalizing FPKM
values across genes within each sample38. The top 2000 most variable genes
were selected for principal component analysis (PCA) to assess global
expression patterns across samples.
Proteomic proﬁling and data analysis
Cuts tissue samples from different a natomical regions of sika deer were
ground in liquid nitrogen and lysed using SDT buffer supplemented with
DTT39. After ultrasonication on ice and thermal denaturation at 95 °C,
samples were centrifuged, and supernatants were alkylated with IAM in the
dark. Proteins were precipitated with pre-cooled acetone and washed with
cold acetone before being re-dissolved in dissolution buffer. Protein con-
centrations were measured using a Bradford assay, and protein integrity was
assessed via SDS-PAGE. For digestion, proteins were denatured in 8 M urea
with 100 mM TEAB (pH 8.5), and trypsin was added for a two-step
digestion at 37 °C. After acidi ﬁcation, peptides were desalted using C18
columns and dried under vacuum. Dried peptides were reconstituted,
centrifuged, and subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis using a Vanquish™ Neo
UHPLC system coupled to a Thermo Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer.
Peptides were separated on a PepMap ™ Neo C18 column with a linear
gradient of acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid. DIA acquisition was
performed with 300 variable windows (m/z 380–980) and resolution set-
tings of 240,000 (MS1) and 80,000 (MS2).
Raw DIA data were analyzed using DIA-NN software(v2.2.0) in
DirectDIA mode40. A project-speciﬁc protein database was used for spectral
matching, with iRT peptides for reten tion time calibration. Search para-
meters included 10 ppm precursor mass tolerance and 0.02 Da fragment
tolerance. Cysteine residues were carbamidomethylated, and methionine
oxidation and N-terminal modiﬁcations were considered. Up to two missed
cleavages were allowed. Peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) wereﬁltered to
retain only those with ≥99% conﬁdence, and false discovery rate (FDR)
thresholds were set at 1% for both peptides and proteins.
We performed principal component analysis to reduce dimensionality
and visualize the distribution of proteomic expression proﬁles across dif-
ferent cuts regions. Protein abundance data were standardized usingz-score
transformation to ensure comparability across samples. Subsequently, two-
sample t-tests were conducted to identify proteins with signiﬁcant abun-
dance differences between each cuts region and all others. FC was calculated
as the ratio of mean expression levelsbetween groups, providing a quanti-
tative measure of differential expression.
Metabolomic proﬁling and data analysis
Approximately 100 mg of cuts tissue from different anatomical regions of
sika deer was ground in liquid nitrogen and extracted with prechilled 80%
methanol by vortexing. The homogenates were incubated on ice for 5 min
and centrifuged at 15,000 ×g, 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatant was diluted
with LC-MS grade water to a ﬁnal methanol concentration of 53%, then
centrifuged again under the same condi tions. The resulting supernatants
were transferred to autosampler vials for LC-MS/MS analysis41.C h r o m a -
tographic separation was performed on a Hypersil Gold C18 column
(100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9μm) using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher
Scientiﬁc), coupled with a Q Exactive ™ HF or HF-X Orbitrap mass spec-
trometer (Thermo Fisher Scienti ﬁc). A 12-min linear gradient of 0.1%
formic acid in water (eluent A) and methanol (eluent B) was applied at a
ﬂow rate of 0.2 mL/min. Mass spectrometry was operated in both positive
and negative ion modes, with a spray voltage of 3.5 kV, capillary
temperature of 320 °C, and sheath/auxiliary gas rates of 35 and 10 L/min,
respectively.
Raw MS data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.3
(Thermo Fisher) for peak detection, alignment, and quantiﬁcation. Main
parameters included a 5 ppm mass tolerance, 30% signal intensity tolerance,
and normalization to total ion intensi ty. Metabolites were annotated by
matching MS/MS spectra against mzCloud, mzVault, and MassList data-
bases. Quality controlﬁltering excluded compounds with a relative standard
deviation >30% in QC samples. Identiﬁed metabolites were annotated using
KEGG42, and LIPIDMaps43 databases.
Metabolite proﬁling data were normalized and analyzed through
Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) using the mixOmics
(v6.26.0)44 in R to identify cuts-speciﬁc metabolic signatures. The PLS-DA
model was used to evaluate the contribution of each metabolite to the
discrimination between cuts types. Metabolites with Variable Importance in
Projection (VIP) scores >1 were selected as discriminant features. Differ-
ential abundance analysis wasconducted with limma (v3.58.1)15,a p p l y i n g
thresholds of 1.5- FC andp value < 0.05, while controlling for sex, sampling
season, and individual animal identity as covariates.
KEGG pathway enrichment analysis
KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using KOBAS (v2.0)45,
where all differentially expressed genes were ﬁrst annotated to KEGG
Orthology (KO)42 terms using Bos taurus (bta) as the reference species.
Subsequently, enrichment testing via hypergeometric distribution using the
R package clusterProﬁler (v4.19.4) to identify signiﬁcantly overrepresented
biological pathways (adjustedp value < 0.05).
WGCNA-based module identiﬁcation
WGCNA was applied to transcriptom i c ,p r o t e o m i c ,a n dm e t a b o l o m i c
datasets to identify co-expressed mo dules and explore their associations
with cuts regions. WGCNA package (v1.72.5) was applied, we ﬁrst pre-
processed the data and removed ou tlier samples. The optimal soft-
thresholding power was selected using pickSoft-Threshold to construct a
scale-free network. An adjacency matrix was generated and transformed
into a topological overlap matrix (TOM), followed by hierarchical clustering
and dynamic tree cut to identify discrete modules. Each module was sum-
marized by its module eigengene. We then assessed the correlation between
module eigengenes and cuts parts, identifying trait-related modules, which
were further analyzed for KEGG pathway enrichment. Finally, we calculated
pairwise correlations between module eigengenes across the three omics
levels. Module pairs with |r|>0 . 5a n dp < 0.05 were selected to construct a
multi-omics regulatory network, highlighting cross-omics functional links
related to cuts characteristics.
RT-qPCR
RT–qPCR was used to quantify the expression of key genes (FMOD,
TNNC1, TNNT1, THBS4, and PLIN1) across six muscle regions, with three
biological replicates per group. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol
reagent (Takara, Japan), and reverse transcription was conducted using the
PrimeScript™ FAST RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan)
according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative PCR was carried
out using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) on a SLAN-96S
Real-Time PCR system (Hongshi Medical Technology, China). Each
sample was analyzed with three technical replicates. RPLP0 was used as the
internal reference gene for normalization. Relative gene expression levels
were calculated using the 2 −ΔΔCt method after quality control, where ΔCt
represents the difference between target and reference genes, andΔΔCt is
deﬁned as the difference between experimental and control groups. Primer
sequences were designed using Primer3 and are listed in Supplemen-
tary Data 5.
