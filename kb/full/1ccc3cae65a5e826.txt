Abstract 19 
 20 
 We examined flow alteration-ecology relationships in benthic macroinvertebrate, fish, 21 
and crayfish assemblages in Ozark Highland streams, USA, over two years with contrasting 22 
environmental conditions, a drought year (2012) and a flood year (2013). We hypothesized that: 23 
 
2 
 
1) there would be temporal variation in flow alteration-ecology relationships between the two 24 
years, 2) flow alteration-ecology relationships would be stronger during the drought year vs the 25 
flood year, and 3) fish assemblages would show the strongest relationships with flow alteration. 26 
We used a quantitative richest-targeted habitat (RTH) method and a qualitative multi-habitat 27 
(QMH) method to collect macroinvertebrates at 16 USGS gaged sites during both years. We 28 
used backpack electrofishing to sample fish and crayfish at 17 sites in 2012 and 11 sites in 2013. 29 
We used redundancy analysis to relate biological response metrics, including richness, diversity, 30 
density, and community-based metrics, to flow alteration. We found temporal variation in flow 31 
alteration-ecology relationships for all taxa, and that relationships differed greatly between 32 
assemblages. We found that relationships were stronger for macroinvertebrates during the 33 
drought year but not for other assemblages, and that fish assemblage relationships were not 34 
stronger than the invertebrate taxa. Magnitude of average flow, frequency of high flow, 35 
magnitude of high flow, and duration of high flow were the most important categories of flow 36 
alteration metrics across taxa. Alteration of high and average flows was more important than 37 
alteration of low flows. Of 32 important flow alteration metrics across years and assemblages, 38 
19 were significantly altered relative to expected values. Ecological responses differed 39 
substantially between drought and flood years, and this is likely to be exacerbated with predicted 40 
climate change scenarios. Differences in flow alteration-ecology relationships among taxonomic 41 
groups and temporal variation in relationships illustrate that a complex suite of variables should 42 
be considered for effective conservation of stream communities related to flow alteration. 43

Introduction 45 
 The natural flow regime paradigm posits that the ecological integrity of rivers depends on 46 
their natural dynamic character (Poff et al., 1997), and that traditional approaches to managing 47 
streams by simply focusing on minimum low flows may be inadequate to protect these 48 
ecosystems and their biota (Bunn and Arthington, 2002; Poff et al., 2010). A concept related to 49 
the natural flow regime is the environmental flow regime; the key difference is that 50 
environmental flow regimes allow for some degree of hydrologic alteration in an attempt to 51 
balance the needs of humans as well as stream ecosystems, while still resulting in the same 52 
patterns and ecological outcomes as the natural flow regime (Poff et al. 1997, Bunn and 53 
Arthington 2002, Poff et al. 2010). One of the great challenges in the implementation of the 54 
environmental flows (e-flows) approach to management and restoration is accounting for natural 55 
variability and complexity among different types of streams, even those within the same 56 
geographic region (Arthington et al., 2006; Kennard et al. 2010; Poff et al. 2010). 57 
Natural streamflow regimes are threatened worldwide by a host of anthropogenic factors, 58 
including construction of dams and diversion structures, groundwater withdrawals from aquifers, 59 
and other hydromorphological alterations (Sondergaard and Jeppesen 2007, Carlisle et al., 2010). 60 
Additionally, extreme climate events are expected to increase as a result of global climatic 61 
change, including many events that directly impact lotic ecosystems, such as increases in drought 62 
frequency, duration, and intensity in many regions of the world (Beniston et al., 2007; Beche et 63 
al., 2009), including in the focal region of this study, where these phenomena have already had 64 
consequences for rare and imperiled aquatic species (Magoulick and Lynch 2015). The potential 65 
interactive effects of natural and anthropogenic stressors such as drought, climate change, and 66 
human water use on ecosystems highlight the need for increased understanding of each stressor 67 
 
4 
 
(Christensen et al., 2006; Beche et al., 2009). For example, water withdrawals during dry years 68 
can reduce habitat connectivity and result in critical flow reductions (Beche et al., 2009). The 69 
maintenance of natural hydrologic regimes can also provide resistance to species invasion (Closs 70 
and Lake, 1996; Caiola et al., 2014), another pervasive world-wide phenomenon in freshwater 71 
habitats, often facilitated by anthropogenic alteration of flow regimes (Bunn and Arthington, 72 
2002). For example, naturally flashy streams or rivers typified by frequent or rapid onset of high 73 
flows can prevent the establishment of non-native fish species that lack behavioral adaptations to 74 
rapid onset of flows (Meffe, 1984; Poff et al., 2010) or have a vulnerable juvenile stage present 75 
during periods of peak flows (Fausch et al., 2001; Poff et al., 2010). 76 
Quantifying flow alteration, the degree of variation away from the natural flow regime, is 77 
a crucial step in environmental-flows based management approaches such as the ELOHA 78 
framework (Poff et al., 2010; Kendy et al., 2012; Gillespie et al., 2014, McManamay & 79 
Frimpong, 2015; King et al., 2015, Sengupta et al., 2018). While there is strong evidence that 80 
flow alteration generally negatively affects biodiversity as well as ecosystem function (Bunn and 81 
Arthington, 2002; Harris and Heathwaite, 2011; Warfe et al., 2014), there are challenges to 82 
establishing transferable relationships between flow alteration and ecological response (Poff and 83 
Zimmerman, 2010). Crucial steps in the ELOHA process include regional flow regime 84 
classification and the quantification of flow alteration; these steps are often made difficult by 85 
lack of hydrological data due to the somewhat sparse nature of stream gages, which are often 86 
placed only on larger order stream segments and may not represent all stream types in an area 87 
(Zimmerman et al., 2018). Determining quantifiable relationships between hydrologic alteration 88 
and biological data is not only of great interest in informing management decisions relating to 89 
issues of water conservation and restoration (McManamay et al., 2014), but could potentially 90 
 
5 
 
also be a critical tool in the assessment of the possible impacts of climate change on stream 91 
ecosystems and organisms (Xenopolous et al., 2005; Farjad et al., 2015). 92 
 The objective of this study was to examine flow alteration-biological response 93 
relationships for fish, crayfish, and benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages in the Ozark 94 
Highlands. We hypothesized that: 1) there would be temporal variation in flow alteration-95 
ecology relationships between the two years (drought year versus flood year), 2) flow alteration-96 
ecology relationships would be stronger during the drought year vs the flood year, and 3) fish 97 
assemblages would show the strongest relationships with flow alteration. 98 
We hypothesized that the potential interactive effects between the dual stressors of 99 
drought and flow alteration would lead to stronger relationships during the drought year (Acuna 100 
et al. 2005, Beche et al., 2009, Bunn and Arthington 2002, Dodds et al. 2004, Poff and Allan 101 
1995, Lynch et al. 2018). We hypothesized that fish would be more strongly impacted by 102 
drought than the other groups due to the ability of benthic macroinvertebrates and crayfish to 103 
utilize the hyporheic zone as a refuge (DiStefano et al., 2009; Wood, et al. 2010; Stubbington 104 
2012) or to utilize drought-coping life history strategies ranging from aestivation (Wickson et al., 105 
2012) and desiccation-resistant eggs (Pallares et al., 2016) to overland escape (Chester et al., 106 
2014) or shifts in timing of emergence (Stenroth et al., 2010). To address our objectives, we 107 
conducted aquatic community sampling at 18 sites in Groundwater Flashy streams in the Ozark 108 
Highlands over two years and used redundancy analysis (RDA) to relate biological response 109 
variables to metrics of flow alteration, including magnitude, frequency, duration, timing, and rate 110 
of change. 111 
Study Area 112 
 
6 
 
The Ozark Highlands ecoregion of southern Missouri, northern Arkansas, and northeast 113 
Oklahoma, USA (Omernik and Griffith, 2014), is heavily affected by a suite of anthropogenic 114 
impacts, including rapid development of urban areas and agricultural practices that affect water 115 
quality (Petersen et al., 2005; Haggard 2010; Scott et al., 2011), expansion of natural gas 116 
extraction (Johnson et al., 2015), displacement of native fauna due to the spread of invasive 117 
species (Magoulick and DiStefano, 2007; Larson et al., 2009), and direct hydrologic alteration of 118 
streams via construction of reservoirs and dams (TNC-OEAT, 2003). This region is home to a 119 
diverse assortment of freshwater habitats and aquatic species, including endemic fish, crayfish, 120 
mussels, macroinvertebrates, and herpetofauna (TNC-OEAT, 2003). Understanding the impacts 121 
of hydrologic alteration could be a crucial step in the formulation of guidelines for protection and 122 
restoration of stream ecosystems in the region. 123 
Methods 124 
Site Selection 125 
 Sampling was conducted at 18 sites with USGS stream gages over two summer field 126 
seasons (May-July) during 2012 and 2013 in northwest Arkansas, southwest Missouri, and 127 
northeast Oklahoma (Figure 1). Precipitation and flow conditions contrasted strongly between 128 
the two years. In summer 2012 the study area experienced an extreme drought, as measured on 129 
the Palmer Drought Severity Index (Palmer, 1965), while sustained higher than normal 130 
precipitation resulted in summer flooding at most sites during 2013 (NOAA, 2015). To facilitate 131 
biological comparisons, site selection was limited to a single ecoregion, the Ozark Highlands, a 132 
single physiographic region, the Springfield Plateau, and a single flow regime, Groundwater 133 
Flashy streams, based on a classification of Ozark-Ouachita Interior Highland streams into seven 134 
 
7 
 
different hydrologic flow regimes (Leasure et al., 2016). Streams selected ranged in drainage 135 
area from 16 to 542 km2. 136 
 Macroinvertebrate collections were taken at 16 sites that were the same in both years 137 
(Figure 1). Due to extreme differences in sampling conditions between the two years (drought in 138 
2012 versus extensive flooding in 2013), we were unable to resample seven of the largest sites 139 
from the first field season for fish and crayfish during the second season, but did add one 140 
additional site. Seventeen sites were sampled for fish and crayfish in 2012 and 11 in 2013, with 141 
10 overlapping sites between the two years (Figure 1). 142 
Hydrologic variable and flow alteration estimation 143 
We identified 64 USGS gaged streams in our study area in least-disturbed reference 144 
condition based on a composite hydrologic disturbance index (Falcone et al. 2010) using water 145 
withdrawals, density of major dams, change in dam storage between 1950 and 2009), percent 146 
canals in the watershed, water discharge locations, road density, and land cover fragmentation 147 
(Leasure et al. 2016). All streams selected in least-disturbed condition had an index less than the 148 
median of all gaged streams in the study area (Leasure et al. 2016). Using these streams, we 149 
developed a set of random forest models to predict 171 flow metrics (Olden and Poff 2003). Full 150 
models were built initially that included 282 predictor variables describing climate, geology, 151 
soils, topography, groundwater and landscape characteristics within reference watersheds 152 
(Appendix A). Importance of each variable was assessed using the default method of the 153 
randomForest R package (Liaw and Wiener 2002) that is based on how much prediction error 154 
increases when each variable is permuted while others are left the same. A benefit of using 155 
random forest models for this approach is that they are not sensitive to the number of variables at 156 
 
8 
 
each node or the number of trees (Liaw and Wiener 2002). A reduced model was built for each 157 
flow metric that included only the 30 most important predictor variables. 158 
Data were collected at all 208 USGS gages in the Interior Highlands for any predictor 159 
variable selected for at least one of the reduced random forest models. The reduced random 160 
forest models were used to predict values of each flow metric expected under natural conditions, 161 
as well as the distribution of expected values. The expected value for each flow metric under 162 
natural conditions was taken as the median of the predicted distribution. 163 
Flow metrics were calculated for every complete 15 year period within the daily flow 164 
records of 18 gages used in this study. Flow alteration was calculated as: 165

Aquatic community assessment 175 
 
9 
 
 Benthic macroinvertebrates, fish, and crayfish were sampled at each site. Reaches were 176 
defined by the presence of at least three discrete units of riffles, runs, and pools, and a qualitative 177 
attempt was made to ensure that sampling reaches were as comparable as possible between sites. 178 
Sampling was stratified by habitat to include three units each of riffles, runs and pools for a total 179 
of nine units per reach. Total area of reaches ranged from 140 – 957 m2 and units averaged 8.3 180 
m (SD = 3.2) in length across all habitat types. Habitat units were located at least 100 m from 181 
road crossings to avoid the hydrologic influence of man-made structures that could affect stream 182 
habitat (Barbour et al., 1999). 183 
Benthic macroinvertebrate samples were typically collected at sites a few hours prior to 184 
fish and crayfish sampling. Macroinvertebrates were sampled using two different, 185 
complementary methods at each site, a semi-quantitative richest-targeted habitat (RTH) method 186 
and a qualitative multiple habitat (QMH) method, both developed for the U.S. Geological Survey 187 
National Water Quality Assessment (NAWQA) program (Moulton et al., 2002). Quantitative 188 
RTH collections were taken in riffles using a 500-µm mesh Slack sampler (50 cm × 30 cm) 189 
equipped with a 0.25 m2 area PVC frame attached to the upstream end of the sampler (Moulton 190 
et al., 2002). The Slack sampler was positioned immediately downstream of the chosen quadrat 191 
perpendicular to the direction of flow. Large cobble and woody debris were lightly brushed, 192 
inspected for any remaining invertebrates, and then removed from the sampling area. The 193 
sampling area was then agitated by disturbing the substrate upstream of the mouth of the 194 
sampler, allowing the dislodged invertebrates to flow into the trailing net. The nine discrete 195 
subsamples were composited into a 19-L plastic bucket for processing, which consisted of 196 
rinsing and removing large debris from the samples, followed by elutriation and sieving (with a 197 
500 µm sieve) in order to separate invertebrates and organic debris from inorganic material. 198 
 
10 
 
 The QMH method was used to document invertebrate taxa present in all habitat types 199 
throughout the reach (Moulton et al., 2002). Crew members assessed the entire reach to 200 
determine the number of different instream habitat types present and to estimate the proportion 201 
of each type. QMH samples were collected using a D-frame kicknet with 500-µm mesh. Each 202 
habitat type was sampled in proportion to total habitat area for a standardized time of one hour 203 
per reach. Samples were processed in the field as described for the RTH method. 204 
 In the laboratory, RTH and QMH samples were sorted on a square gridded subsampling 205 
frame of 25 5 × 5 cm squares using a fixed-count approach targeting a minimum of 300 206 
organisms (Barbour et al., 1999; Moulton et al., 2000). After pouring the sample into the frame 207 
and allowing it to settle evenly, an initial inspection was performed to remove large and rare 208 
organisms likely to be missed during subsampling. A grid square was randomly selected and all 209 
organisms present were removed from the grid and processed. Subsampling proceeded in this 210 
fashion until a minimum of 300 organisms were counted, with the square in which the 300th 211 
organism was counted also fully processed. Macroinvertebrates were identified to the lowest 212 
practical taxonomic level, generally family or genus. To estimate total numbers of organisms, a 213 
laboratory subsampling correction factor was used (Moulton et al., 2000) in which the total 214 
number of grids was divided by the number of grids sorted during subsampling and multiplied by 215 
the number of organisms subsampled. Large and rare organisms taken from the sample as a 216 
whole were added to these numbers without a correction factor. These numbers were then used 217 
to calculate invertebrate community response metrics. 218 
Fish and crayfish were collected using a Smith-Root Model LR-24 backpack- 219 
electrofishing unit which has been shown effective for fish and crayfish sampling in Ozark 220 
 
11 
 
streams (Rabenin et al., 1997; Dauwalter and Pert, 2003). Standard LR-24 settings for power 221 
output based on ambient stream conductivity were used. Prior to sampling, 1.6 cm2 mesh block-222 
nets were placed at the end of each habitat unit to prevent animals from escaping or biasing 223 
sampling data by moving from one unit to another. A four-person team conducted three 224 
upstream passes per habitat unit. Collections from all passes were kept in separate buckets until 225 
all passes were completed. Each pass was processed separately and all specimens were 226 
identified to species and released live back into the stream. 227 
Biological Response Metric Selection 228 
Biological response metrics were calculated for macroinvertebrate, fish, and crayfish 229 
assemblages (Table 1). A subset of metrics considered most ecologically relevant was chosen 230 
based on published relationships and best professional judgment. The five macroinvertebrate 231 
response metrics chosen were: abundance; taxa richness; Simpson's diversity; percent 232 
contribution of individuals belonging to Orders Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, and Trichoptera 233 
(EPT), taxa that are associated with undisturbed habitat and high water quality (Karr, 1991); and 234 
percent contribution of the family Chironomidae, considered a generally tolerant taxon predicted 235 
to increase in abundance with increasing levels of perturbation (Barbour et al., 1999). Response 236 
metrics calculated from RTH and QMH samples were analyzed separately. 237 
 For crayfish, the three response metrics chosen were: Simpson's diversity, total crayfish 238 
density (per volume sampled), and percent contribution of species designated as habitat 239 
generalists in an assessment of invasion risk of crayfish in the eastern United States (Larson and 240 
Olden 2010). These are represented in our dataset by two species, Faxonius neglectus neglectus 241 
 
12 
 
and Faxonius virilis. Species richness was not used as a community response metric for crayfish 242 
due to the generally low and relatively uniform richness across sites. 243 
 For fish, the five biological response metrics chosen were: species richness, Simpson's 244 
diversity, total fish density (per volume sampled), percent of total individuals belonging to 245 
Family Centrarchidae, and percent of total individuals belonging to species categorized as 246 
intolerant, i.e. sensitive to various environmental perturbation, in an Index of Biotic Integrity 247 
specifically developed for fish assemblages of the Ozark Highlands (Dauwalter et al., 2003). 248 
Percent Centrarchidae was chosen as a response metric because most Ozark centrarchids are 249 
ecologically tolerant habitat generalists (Dauwalter et al., 2003; Robison & Buchanan, 1988). 250 
 Data Analysis 251 
 Redundancy analysis (RDA) was used to evaluate flow alteration-ecology relationships 252 
separately for assemblages and sampling years. RDA is a canonical ordination procedure that 253 
examines relationships among response variables and predictor variables in multivariate space 254 
(ter Braak, 1995). Linear model RDA’s were appropriate because preliminary Detrended 255 
Correspondence Analyses (DCA) indicated that species gradient lengths were less than 1 256 
standard deviation (ter Braak, 1995). We used forward selection in CANOCO 4.5 (ter Braak and 257 
Smilauer, 2002) to select flow alteration variables that were related to response metrics. We 258 
limited the flow alteration variables to those with lambda ≥ 0.7 after entry into the model. 259 
Prior to RDA analysis, response variables were centered and standardized. Scaling of 260 
ordination scores was focused on inter-response variable correlations rather than inter-sample 261 
distances, and the response variable scores were standardized to prevent variables with large 262 
 
13 
 
variances from disproportionately influencing ordination diagrams (ter Braak and Smilauer, 263 
2002). Monte Carlo permutations were performed for each RDA to test the significance of the 264 
canonical axes together and were then performed for each RDA to determine the overall 265 
importance of remaining environmental variables in influencing response variables. Analyses of 266 
response variable-flow alteration relationships were performed separately for each year, 267 
taxonomic assemblage and sampling type (for macroinvertebrates). All significant hydrologic 268 
alteration metrics are listed and defined in Table 2. Percent variance explained in assemblage-269 
environment relationships was examined by comparing eigenvalues from RDA analysis in order 270 
to test our second and third hypotheses. 271

Discussion 367 
 Our hypothesis that there would be temporal variation in flow alteration-ecology 368 
relationships was supported. The complete overlap of sites for macroinvertebrate collections 369 
makes it possible to draw temporal comparisons in flow alteration-ecological response 370 
relationships between the two years for macroinvertebrate assemblages. The contrast between 371 
years was most pronounced in the RTH samples, which may be because they are collected only 372 
from riffles, the stream habitat most heavily affected by drought (Dekar and Magoulick, 2007; 373 
Chester and Robison, 2011). While we expected some differences between the years, it was still 374 
somewhat surprising to see no consistently important metrics between the two years in either 375 
RTH or QMH collections. In some cases, there were seemingly different relationships between 376 
response metrics. For example, RTH percent EPT was negatively related to alteration of TA3 377 
(seasonal predictability of flooding) in 2012, whereas percent EPT was positively related to 378 
alteration of TH2 (variability in Julian date of annual maximum) in 2013. However, in both 379 
cases percent EPT was reduced with altered flow timing (discussed further below). It appears 380 
that alteration of flow timing is important for percent EPT, but specific relationships vary 381 
temporally. 382 
Non-stationarity in environmental conditions can complicate our ability to formulate 383 
predictable flow-ecology relationships and pose challenges for the implementation of e-flows 384 
science (Poff et al., 2010; Rolls et al., 2012; Katz and Freeman, 2015; Poff 2017; Lynch et al. 385 
2018). As the hydrologic cycle is further altered by global climate change and the severity, 386 
duration and frequency of droughts increases (Masson-Delmotte et al., 2013, Farjad et al., 2015) 387 
it is crucial to incorporate strategies that realistically account for these phenomena when 388 
 
19 
 
implementing e-flows science into management decisions (Poff 2017). One such strategy is to 389 
focus on resilience, the maintenance of processes and relationships that are robust and able to 390 
maintain integrity despite anticipated changes in environmental conditions (Poff 2017; Mazor et 391 
al., 2018). 392 
Our hypotheses that flow alteration-ecology relationships would be stronger during the 393 
drought year and that fish assemblages would show the strongest flow-alteration relationships, 394 
had much less support, and showed year- and taxa-dependent caveats. Relationship strength, as 395 
indicated by cumulative percent variance explained in Axes 1 and 2 of the RDA’s, was greater 396 
during the drought year for benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages, but weaker for fish and 397 
crayfish, with the pattern reversed during the flood year. Fish assemblage relationships were 398 
stronger than macroinvertebrates and lower than crayfish during the flood year, but weaker than 399 
all other groups during the drought year. Overall, relationships were slightly stronger during the 400 
drought year than the flood year, and relationships were actually weaker in fish than in the 401 
macroinvertebrate assemblages. 402 
With respect to which categories of alteration metrics were most important, the 403 
prominence of magnitude and frequency is of particular interest given that regional e-flows 404 
studies have suggested that magnitude of flow is an important influence on aquatic communities 405 
(Monk et al., 2006; Armstrong et al., 2011; Kendy et al., 2012), while others have found that 406 
frequency of floods may be one of the most important determinants of community structure in 407 
streams (Dodds et al., 2004; Matthews et al., 2013; Matthews et al., 2014). Anthropogenic 408 
alteration of streamflow magnitudes is a widespread phenomenon; in an assessment of 2,888 409 
streamflow monitoring sites throughout the conterminous U.S., Carlisle et al. (2010) found that 410 
 
20 
 
streamflow magnitude was altered at 86% of assessed streams, and that diminished magnitudes 411 
were better predictors of biological integrity in both fish and macroinvertebrate assemblages than 412 
other physical and chemical covariates. Reduction in high flow frequencies has also been linked 413 
to a decrease in the ecological integrity of river systems (Ward and Stanford, 1995). The general 414 
trend in our study area in both magnitude and frequency metrics was towards reduction relative 415 
to expected values. 416 
 Compared to alteration of high and average flows, alteration of low flows appeared to be 417 
a considerably less important influence on biota in Groundwater Flashy streams in the Ozark 418 
Highlands; only two of 32 important metrics across years and assemblages were low flow 419 
related. Although both floods and droughts act as major hydrologic disturbances in stream 420 
ecosystems and can exert significant influence on biota (Lake, 2000), the alteration of low-flow 421 
hydrology has been relatively less studied than that of high flows (Rolls et al., 2012). In the 422 
present study, we focused on Groundwater Flashy streams, one of the most common flow 423 
regimes in the Ozark Highlands (Leasure et al., 2016), but it is likely that in other flow regimes, 424 
alteration of low flows may be more important. Different natural flow regimes within the same 425 
region may be more or less susceptible to particular forms of flow alteration, which is the reason 426 
that flow regime classification is a crucial step in the assessment of hydrologic alteration (Poff et 427 
al., 2010). The seven distinct flow regimes in the Ozark Highlands can be divided into three 428 
broad categories – groundwater, runoff, and intermittent streams (Leasure et al., 2016). Runoff 429 
and intermittent flow regimes are categorized by more frequent low flow spells and lower base 430 
flows than groundwater streams; it may be that low flow metrics play a greater role in the life 431 
history of biota in these streams and therefore alteration of those metrics would have greater 432 
impact. Poff (1992) suggested that perennial runoff and intermittent streams may be more 433 
 
21 
 
strongly affected by alteration of low flows and groundwater streams more affected by alteration 434 
of high flows; the latter at least appears to be reflected in the present study. 435 
 RTH macroinvertebrate assemblages differed in key ways from the others in this study. 436 
This was the only group in which magnitude was not the most important category of flow 437 
alteration metric. It was also the only assemblage in which no flow alteration category was 438 
clearly more prominent than the others. Predictability of flooding is thought to be critically 439 
important to macroinvertebrate assemblages. Fritz and Dodds (2005) found that streams with 440 
low flow predictability had consistently lower macroinvertebrate taxa richness than those with 441 
greater predictability. Alteration of the variability in high flow timing (TH2) was also related to 442 
both percent EPT taxa and abundance in 2013. Predictable timing of floods may be very 443 
important in aquatic macroinvertebrates that rely on life-history adaptations to avoid 444 
disturbances rather than escaping on a per-event basis, particularly taxa that require gill 445 
respiration as juveniles but have an aerial adult stage, e.g. EPT taxa (Lytle, 2008). In the present 446 
study, predictability of flooding (TA3) was altered towards higher predictability and was 447 
negatively related to all response variables in 2012. Likewise, alteration towards increased 448 
variability in high flow timing (TH2) was positively related to increased percent EPT taxa and 449 
abundance in 2013, but it is important to note that TH2 alteration ranges from strongly negative 450 
to slightly positive so percent EPT and abundance increased with less altered (i.e., more normal) 451 
TH2. Therefore, in both cases percent EPT was reduced with altered flow timing. It appears that 452 
alteration of flow timing, regardless of direction, may negatively influence RTH 453 
macroinvertebrate assemblages in these systems. 454 
 
22 
 
Unlike RTH assemblages, QMH assemblages showed a pattern consistent with fish and 455 
crayfish assemblages with respect to the prominence of magnitude alteration metrics. 456 
Interestingly, QMH assemblages show more of an affinity with fish than crayfish assemblages in 457 
terms of the importance of high flow frequency (FH). Relationships between response variables 458 
in the two years were more consistent in QMH than RTH samples. It is possible that the 459 
inclusion of pool and run habitats, which act as refuges for macroinvertebrates during summer 460 
drying (Chester and Robson, 2011), may have somewhat ameliorated the effects of drought in 461 
2012 in QMH compared to RTH samples. Temporal variation in relationships was also apparent 462 
in QMH assemblages, however, as no individual flow alteration metrics were significant in both 463 
years. General trends among QMH macroinvertebrate assemblages in the region include 464 
reduction of important metrics relating to magnitude and variability in average and high flows, as 465 
well as frequency and duration of high flows. These may have a variety of effects on QMH 466 
macroinvertebrate assemblages in the region; in a few cases, some trends may actually offset 467 
each other. For example, decreasing magnitude of average flows (MA12) may lead to a decrease 468 
in richness which could be somewhat ameliorated by the trend toward decreasing flood 469 
frequency (FH11) (Fig. 3). Examining synergies and indirect effects of flow alteration on 470 
ecosystem structure and function could be a fruitful avenue for future research. 471 
It should be noted that care should be taken in interpreting the flow alteration-ecology 472 
relationships because flow can be negatively or positively altered or unaltered. Therefore, 473 
negative alteration can be positively correlated with a response variable. For example, in Fig. 2 474 
the alteration of FH3 is positively related to Taxa Richness and negatively related to 475 
%Chironomidae, but FH3 is negatively altered and high FH3 values are near normal (i.e., 476 
 
23 
 
FH3=0). Therefore, Taxa Richness is greater and %Chironomidae is reduced when FH3 is near 477 
normal. 478 
 Magnitude alteration was the most important category in both crayfish and fish 479 
assemblages. The two strongly differ in one important way, however – the lack of any important 480 
frequency alteration metrics in crayfish assemblages. The ability of crayfish species in the 481 
region to more fully utilize the hyporheic zone during dry periods (DiStefano et al., 2009; Larson 482 
et al., 2009) may make them less dependent on frequent high flow events than fish assemblages; 483 
this may lessen the impact that alteration of flood frequency has on them. MA3, variability in 484 
daily flows, was a consistently important metric in crayfish assemblages in the region, as it was 485 
selected in both 2012 and 2013 despite a lack of overlap between sites. The relationship between 486 
alteration of flow variability and density was similar to that observed in fish assemblages in this 487 
study, i.e. the relationship between fish density and MA36 in 2012, and is also supported by 488 
previous studies of flow variability and fish density (Craven et al., 2010). 489 
 In fish assemblages, the association between richness and diversity and alteration of 490 
variability in both average flow magnitude (MA22 and MA36) and high flow frequency, (FH2) 491 
is supported by studies relating hydrologic variation to North American stream fishes (Ward, 492 
1998; Niu et al., 2012, but see McGarvey, 2014). The trend towards reduction of these metrics 493 
in our study area could be associated with an overall decline in richness and diversity of stream 494 
fishes in the region. While previous studies have suggested that aquatic biodiversity is often 495 
lower in modified or disturbed streams than in those with relatively intact natural flow regimes 496 
(Ward and Stanford, 1995; Gehrke et al., 1999), it has been an ongoing challenge for stream 497 
ecologists to unravel the direct effects of flow alteration from multiple associated stressors that 498 
 
24 
 
often accompany development in watersheds, e.g. land-use factors or declining water quality 499 
(Bunn and Arthington, 2002). Our study provides evidence that alteration of specific flow 500 
metrics can influence richness and diversity in stream biota. 501 
 Overall, our results show the importance of magnitude and high flow alteration to stream 502 
assemblages in these systems. However, patterns related to alteration of specific flow metrics 503 
between years or between taxa are less obvious. It is possible that redundancy between tested 504 
flow metrics used to explain similar ecological processes may be responsible for lack of patterns 505 
with specific flow metrics. Future research that builds on established flow alteration-biological 506 
response relationships in a way that specifically elucidates functional links would be worthwhile. 507 
 Another caveat to consider is that, while relationships appeared to vary between years, 508 
our ability to detect these relationships may have varied as well based on the very different 509 
sampling conditions between drought and flood years. Detection probability of freshwater fish 510 
can vary strongly between samples taken at different flow magnitudes, and this may influence 511 
inferences based on fish community-flow relationships (Pregler et al., 2015, Gwinn et al., 2016). 512 
Similar factors may affect detection probability in benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages 513 
(Meador et al., 2011; Wisniewski et al., 2013). Furthermore, while hydrology plays a major role 514 
in structuring aquatic assemblages, it is heavily interrelated to many other factors, including 515 
geomorphology, land-use, and water quality; the ecological effects of hydrologic alteration are 516 
best examined within the context of this suite of factors (Poff et al., 2006; McManamay and 517 
Frimpong, 2015, Lynch, et. al 2018). Finally, while we examined flow alteration-ecology 518 
relationships in a predominant flow regime (Groundwater Flashy streams) in the Ozark 519 
Highlands, these relationships may strongly differ in other flow regimes even within the same 520 
 
25 
 
ecoregion (Poff, 1992; Poff et al., 2010; Leasure et al., 2016). Future studies of flow alteration-521 
ecology relationships focused on other flow regimes would help to form a more complete picture 522 
of the impact of hydrologic alteration on stream communities. 523 
Conclusions 524 
Flow alteration appears to be an important influence on community structure in 525 
Groundwater Flashy streams in the Ozark Highlands. The most important categories of 526 
alteration influencing stream biota were MA, FH, MH, and DH. The fact that three of these 527 
categories were high flow-related suggests the overall importance of high flows as a determinant 528 
of community structure and composition in these systems. Of the 32 important metrics across 529 
years and assemblages, 19 were significantly altered relative to expected values. General 530 
patterns, such as the importance of magnitude and high flow alteration, were apparent across 531 
assemblages and may be useful to managers and stakeholders attempting to conserve freshwater 532 
ecosystems in the region. However, key differences between taxonomic groups, as well as 533 
temporal variation in relationships, suggest that a complex suite of flow metrics should be 534 
considered for effective conservation of stream communities related to flow alteration. 535 
Environmental flows concepts are increasingly finding traction in regions across the world 536 
(Belmar et al., 2011; Buchanan et al., 2013; Rolls and Arthington, 2014; O'Brien, et al., 2017; 537 
Zhang et al., 2017), but could be enhanced by a better understanding of complexity with respect 538 
to interactions between temporal variation, disturbance, and taxa-dependent response differences. 539
