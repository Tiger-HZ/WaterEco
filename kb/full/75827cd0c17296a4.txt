Development and Characterization of a Linear 
Cold Atmospheric Pressure Plasma Jet for 
Large-Area Surface Treatment 
 
Viktor Udachin*, Alexander Tillmanns, Christof Zickenheiner, Lienhard 
Wegewitz, Henning Wiche and Wolfgang Maus-Friedrichs 
Clausthal Center of Materials Technology, Clausthal University of Technology, Clausthal-Zellerfeld, 
Germany 
 
 
*E-mail: viktor.udachin@tu-clausthal.de

Accepted for publication xxxxxx

Abstract 
Cold atmospheric pressure plasma jets are widely used for surface treatment and plasma-assisted processing. However, 
simultaneous plasma transport and spatial scaling remain challenging for existing plasma jet concepts. In this work, a novel 
linear-geometry cold atmospheric pressure plasma jet concept based on a floating metallic mesh electrode integrated into a 3D-
printed nozzle is developed to enable spatially extended plasma generation across the slit width of 36 mm and beyond. The 
plasma source comprises a primary discharge, which is formed between a high-voltage electrode and a metal mesh inside a 
quartz tube, and a secondary plasma phase. This secondary phase is generated at the opposite end of the metal mesh and 
propagates through a slit nozzle. The system was investigated under two gas atmospheres (Ar/H2 and Ar) and different substrate 
configurations, including conductive and non-conductive surfaces. Plasma propagation behavior was analyzed using high-speed 
imaging, while optical emission spectroscopy was employed to identify reactive species in both the primary and secondary 
discharge regions. Electrical characterization was performed to assess discharge behavior under representative operating 
conditions. The results demonstrate that the developed linear plasma jet enables stable plasma propagation across the nozzle 
width. Operation in Ar/H 2 results in a more homogeneous plasma phase distributed across the entire width of the nozzle, 
whereas pure Ar leads to more localized discharge structures. The presence of hydrogen is expected to modify ionization 
kinetics, promoting more uniform plasma behavior. Furthermore, reactive species characteristic of both argon excitation and 
hydrogen-related processes were detected in the Ar/H2 plasma jet, indicating its suitability for applications requiring e.g., metal 
oxide reduction. Overall, the presented concept offers a promising approach to generating line-shaped plasma jets with 
enhanced spatial uniformity. This makes it particularly well-suited to large-area surface treatment applications, such as material 
modification and deoxidation processes. 
 
Keywords: non-thermal plasma, linear-geometry plasma, atmospheric pressure plasma jet, metal oxide reduction

considerable attention due to their ability to generate reactive 
species at ambient temperature and pressure and deliver them 
to a surface of interest [1–4]. This capability enables a wide 
range of applications, including materials and surface

processing (e.g., metal deoxidation and polymer modification) 
as well as plasma medicine [1,2,4–8]. 
Indeed, for many of these applications, the effectiveness of 
the plasma treatment depends strongly on the generation of 
reactive species and on their ability to be transported over a 
sufficient distance and distributed uniformly across the target 
surface. For example, in plasma medicine, treatment of 
complex or hard-to-reach features requires plasma reactive 
species that can be transported over extended distances while 
maintaining their activity [2,6,9]. Similarly, in materials 
processing and joining applications, such as high-frequency 
(HF) welding of longitudinal steel tubes, large surface areas 
(often extending several tens of centimetres upstream of the 
welding point) must be uniformly treated, shielded, and 
deoxidized prior to welding [10–13]. In such cases, plasma jet 
systems must not only provide sufficient spatial coverage but 
also exhibit high stability under demanding conditions, 
including elevated temperatures exceeding 1000 °C near the 
welding zone. These requirements highlight the need for 
plasma jet systems capable of stable plasma propagation and 
uniform spatial distribution of reactive species over extended 
treatment areas. 
With respect to plasma transport, several strategies have 
been investigated. A key factor is the choice of working gas, 
which strongly influences plasma propagation behavior. In the 
context of both plasma medicine and materials processing, 
helium- and argon-based plasma jets are among the most 
widely studied systems [4,6–9]. Helium-based plasma jets are 
known to propagate over longer distances due to their 
favorable ionization characteristics, including low breakdown 
voltage and efficient energy transfer processes [14–16]. In 
contrast, argon plasma jets typically exhibit shorter 
propagation lengths and more localised discharge behaviour 
[14,17]. However, they are often preferred due to the lower 
cost of argon compared to helium [17]. Adding hydrogen to 
argon further modifies the discharge characteristics, often 
promoting a transition from filamentary to more diffuse 
plasma modes [4,18]. H2 addition alters the ionization 
pathways and suppresses filamentation by influencing ion 
kinetics and electron energy distribution [4,18]. Such Ar/H 2 
APPJs typically contain reactive hydrogen species, including 
atomic hydrogen (H α), which is particularly beneficial for 
materials processing applications, such as metal oxide 
reduction processes or polymer surface modification [4,7,19]. 
In addition to gas composition, dedicated concepts for 
plasma transport have been developed. In particular, Kostov 
and co-workers introduced an approach based on the use of a 
floating (non-grounded) metallic wire, enabling plasma 
transport through dielectric tubes [20,21]. In such systems, a 
primary discharge is formed between the high-voltage 
electrode and the floating wire, while a secondary discharge is 
generated between the wire and the target surface. This 
concept enables propagation of the discharge potential 
through dielectric tubing, resulting in secondary plasma 
generation outside the primary discharge region, and has been 
mainly investigated for biomedical applications [9,20]. 
However, its application to materials processing and large-
area surface treatment remains limited. 
Besides transport, the spatial distribution of the plasma 
phase represents a second key challenge. Conventional plasma 
jets typically produce circular localized treatment regions, 
which are insufficient for applications requiring uniform 
processing of extended surfaces. This limitation is linked to 
the design of the plasma source, in particular electrode 
configurations and nozzle geometries [22–24]. 
Traditionally, APPJs are predominantly characterized by 
DBD-based cylindrical or coaxial geometries. Although these 
are effective for localised treatment, they can have restricted 
treatment areas (typically between mm 2 and cm²) and may 
exhibit filamentary behaviour, as well as presenting additional 
complexity in terms of electrode alignment and thermal 
management, which can affect scalability [4,7,22,24–27]. To 
increase the effective treatment area, multi-jet array 
configurations have been developed [28–32]. While such 
systems enable scaling, they can suffer from jet-to-jet 
interactions, leading to non-uniform plasma characteristics 
and spatial inhomogeneities in the treated region [31–34]. 
Recently, efforts have focused on the development of 
alternative nozzle geometries to improve spatial uniformity 
and extend the treatment area. For instance, conical plasma jet 
designs have been proposed to increase the treatment footprint 
while maintaining similar gas flow conditions, even enabling 
simultaneous treatment of opposing surfaces of materials 
[35,36]. Additionally, modular nozzle systems, such as those 
developed for kINPen-type plasma jet devices, aim to tailor 
gas flow and optimize plasma delivery for specific 
applications, particularly in plasma medicine [37]. 
Further development was directed towards the application 
of linear-nozzle plasma jet geometries, which may enable 
homogeneous treatment of extended surfaces in applications 
where large processing widths and uniformity are 
simultaneously required (e.g., metal joining [10,12] or 
biomedical decontamination applications [38]). Several 
approaches have been proposed to realize line-shaped plasma 
jet sources. For instance, diffuse coplanar surface barrier 
discharge (DCSBD)-based systems have been used to 
generate linear afterglow plasma jets operating in air and 
nitrogen atmospheres, forming a so-called “plasma curtain” 
with a width of up to approximately 5 cm at an optimal gas 
flow rate of approximately 30 L/min [39]. Such systems are 
characterized by low gas temperatures (typically below 55 °C) 
and operate in a filament-free diffuse mode, enabling highly 
uniform treatment of temperature-sensitive materials and 
extended width processing. The use of nitrogen as working gas 
allows for specific surface functionalization processes [39]. 
However, the width of such plasma jets is directly linked to

the dimensions and design of the DCSBD electrode system, 
which requires specifically manufactured electrode 
geometries and dielectric structures [39]. In addition, the 
reported afterglow plasma jet length in open air environment 
was limited to approximately 1.2 cm. Such relatively short 
propagation distances may restrict direct delivery of reactive 
plasma species in applications requiring larger stand-off 
distances. For example, operating temperatures exceeding 
1000 °C and notable magnetic fields can be found near metal 
welding zones in e.g., high-frequency welding [10,11], which 
may affect the stability and integration of such plasma jet 
system. Moreover, the proposed plasma jet system has not 
been investigated in other gas atmospheres, such as helium or 
argon-based plasmas, which are widely used in many 
applications. 
Another approach for increasing the treatment area of 
conventional cylindrical DBD-based plasma jets was 
investigated by Ge and co-workers [40]. In their work, an Ar-
based plasma jet was generated using a specially designed 
broadened quartz nozzle attached to a quartz tube with an 
outer diameter of 6 mm. The developed nozzle geometry 
enabled the formation of a more spatially extended plasma 
plume with dimensions of approximately 25 mm in width and 
7 mm in propagation length under optimized operating 
conditions. The system demonstrated promising 
characteristics for biomedical applications, particularly for 
large-area plasma treatment in cancer therapy. Nevertheless, 
as discussed previously, the relatively short propagation 
distance of the reactive plasma phase may limit direct plasma 
species delivery in applications requiring larger stand-off 
distances. 
Radio-frequency-driven plasma jets in Ar-based 
atmospheres (as Ar, Ar/O 2, Ar/N 2) with slit-type nozzle 
geometry have also been developed to extend the treatment 
area beyond that of conventional cylindrical jets [41]. While 
this design enables the formation of elongated plasma jet 
plumes, it is often prone to filamentation, especially in the 
presence of varying substrate geometries. This leads to non-
uniform distribution of reactive species and limits the 
reproducibility and effectiveness of surface treatment [41]. In 
particular, the sensitivity of such systems to substrate 
positioning and geometry highlights the difficulty of 
maintaining stable and homogeneous plasma conditions over 
extended widths. 
These observations demonstrate that, although linear 
plasma sources offer a promising route toward large-area 
plasma processing, achieving stable, uniform, and scalable 
plasma generation with extended propagation distances under 
different gas atmospheres remains a significant challenge. 
This highlights the need for linear-geometry plasma jet 
concepts that enable controlled spatial expansion of the 
plasma phase in both width and length while maintaining a 
relatively simple design, ensuring uniform reactive species 
delivery. 
In this work, a linear-geometry cold atmospheric plasma jet 
based on a floating metallic mesh electrode integrated into a 
3D-printed nozzle is presented. In contrast to previously 
reported plasma transport systems based on single floating 
wires by Kostov and colleagues [21], the present approach 
introduces, for the first time, a spatially distributed floating 
electrode in the form of a metallic mesh specifically designed 
for linear-geometry plasma generation. This design allows the 
discharge to be extended in a controlled manner along the 
entire slit width, which can be several tens of millimetres. As 
a result, the proposed configuration allows simultaneous 
realization of plasma transport and spatial scaling, leading to 
the generation of a line-shaped plasma jet. Such a system is 
particularly relevant for applications requiring treatment of 
extended surfaces, such as the deoxidation and conditioning 
of metallic substrates prior to industrial welding processes, as 
well as large-area polymer surface activation and wettability 
improvement. The study focuses on the design of the plasma 
jet source and the experimental investigation of plasma 
propagation behavior under two gas compositions (Ar/H 2 and 
Ar) and different substrate conditions.

2.1. Experimental Setup for Linear Plasma Phase 
Generation

nozzle configuration. The system consists of two main 
functional parts: a primary nozzle for the generation of the 
non-thermal plasma primary discharge and a secondary slit 
nozzle designed for the formation of a linear plasma jet 
suitable for treatment of large areas.

The primary plasma discharge is generated inside a primary 
quartz glass nozzle (tube) with an outer diameter of 8 mm and 
an inner diameter of 6 mm (Figure 1). This quartz tube serves 
simultaneously as the discharge chamber and as a gas flow 
guide. The desired gas atmosphere for plasma generation 
(pure Ar 5.0 as well as Ar/H 2 gas mixture, 2 vol.% of H 2, 
Westfalen AG, Münster, Germany) is supplied through a 
dedicated gas inlet connected to a plastic T-piece connector 
(Swagelok, USA) mounted at the top of the quartz tube. For 
all experiments, the working gas flow rate was maintained at 
25 L/min, as this was found to be the minimum flow rate that 
ensured plasma generation across the slit width. 
A high-voltage (HV) electrode is fixed in place along the 
central axis of the quartz tube using a specially printed 3D 
plastic holder in the T-piece connector. The HV electrode 
consists of a copper wire with a diameter of 1 mm, which is 
enclosed within a ceramic tube (dielectric) with an outer 
diameter of 3 mm and inner diameter of 1.6 mm. For this 
purpose, a one-end-closed ceramic tube typically used for 
thermocouples was employed (made out of Al 2O3, Kyocera 
Fineceramics Europe GmbH, Germany). The copper wire is 
mechanically fixed inside the ceramic tube and electrically 
connected to brass powder located at the closed end of the 
ceramic tube. This brass powder enhances the distribution of 
the electric field and promotes a more homogeneous discharge 
at the electrode tip (Figure 1) [42]. 
To initiate the primary non-thermal plasma discharge, a 
counter electrode is required. Following the approach 
previously reported by Kostov et al. [21], a ground-free 
metallic component was employed instead of a conventional 
grounded electrode. This counter component was positioned 
at a distance of approximately 1 mm from the HV electrode 
tip without direct electrical contact. 
A key novelty of the present design is the use of a metallic 
mesh as the floating counter electrode. In contrast to the single 
wire configurations used in previous studies by Kostov and 
colleagues as well as by other groups [6,9,20,21], a brass mesh 
with an opening size of 1.00 mm (distance between the wires) 
and a wire diameter of 0.30 mm (Fohrmann-Werkzeuge 
GmbH, Germany) was employed. The use of a metallic mesh 
enables controlled distribution of the floating component 
along the slit geometry and provides improved mechanical 
stability compared to individual wire configurations, which 
are difficult to position uniformly within elongated slit 
nozzles. In contrast, the mesh structure allows easier 
construction, improved reproducibility, and enhanced control 
of discharge distribution along the slit geometry (Figure 1). 
The brass mesh was mechanically integrated into a custom-
designed 3D-printed slit nozzle fabricated from polylactic acid 
(PLA). The corresponding CAD file is provided in the 
available dataset [43]. The slit nozzle opening features a 
rectangular form with dimensions of 36 mm in width and 
5 mm in length. The overall nozzle width and length are 
40 mm and 8 mm, respectively, with a total nozzle height of 
approximately 125 mm. Inside the nozzle, specially designed 
mechanical fixation regions allow secure placement of the 
mesh without slippage. Additional stabilization was achieved 
using small reusable adhesive pieces (Blu Tack, United 
Kingdom). 
The metallic mesh is shaped to conform to the internal 
geometry of the slit nozzle. Near the connection to the primary 
quartz tube, the mesh narrows, exposing only 4 parallel wires 
in the transition region. Toward the slit opening, the mesh 
expands to form approximately 35 parallel wires aligned along 
the longitudinal direction of the slit opening and distributed 
uniformly across its width (Figure 1). The mesh is positioned 
at a distance of approximately 4 mm from the nozzle exit 
opening. 
Photographs of the experimental setup and plasma jet 
operation are presented in Figure 2. 
In Fig. 2, the assembled plasma jet system is shown. The 
system consists of the primary quartz tube used for plasma 
generation and the attached 3D-printed PLA slit nozzle in red 
colour. The quartz tube is inserted into the upper part of the 
printed nozzle, which internally contains the metallic mesh 
acting as the floating counter electrode.

High-voltage pulsed excitation was provided by a pre-
commercial power supply (PlasmaGreen GmbH, Clausthal-
Zellerfeld, Germany), which first generates low-voltage 
pulses with adjustable amplitude, frequency, and duty cycle. 
These pulses are subsequently transformed into high-voltage 
pulses using a step-up transformer. The applied high-voltage 
amplitude, reported as the peak-to-peak high-voltage (kV pp), 
was varied depending on the experimental conditions (shown 
in Section 2.2. cf. Table 1), while the pulse repetition 
frequency and duty cycle remained constant at 4.0 kHz and 
95%, respectively, throughout the study. The duty cycle of the 
pulse was inverted, meaning that at a set value of 95%, the 
resulting pulse had an ON time of 5% and an OFF time equal 
to the remaining pulse period, depending on the set frequency. 
2.2. Experimental conditions and treated surfaces 
Plasma treatment experiments using two gas atmospheres 
(Ar/H2 and Ar) were performed on four different substrate 
configurations (see Figure 3 for a schematic presentation) (a) a 
borosilicate glass sample (1 mm thickness) placed directly on 
top of a grounded metallic steel substrate, (b) a grounded 
metallic steel plate, (c) a borosilicate glass sample, and (d) a 
piece of paper. The first configuration (a) was selected 
following the approach reported by Kostov and co-workers 
[21], who investigated plasma jets using a similar substrate 
arrangement. The additional configurations (b–d) were used 
to investigate the influence of substrate conductivity on 
plasma jet behavior and surface interaction. In particular, the 
non-conductive substrates (c) and (d) allow examination of 
how the plasma jet propagates from the nozzle toward 
electrically insulating surfaces. 
For configurations (a), (b), and (c), the distance between the 
slit nozzle opening and the substrate surface was fixed at 
12 mm. For configuration (d), a larger distance of 40 mm was 
intentionally selected to evaluate the propagation length of the 
generated plasma phase and to verify whether the observed 
discharge can be classified as a plasma jet. 
The applied high-voltage amplitudes for the respective 
configurations were selected to ensure plasma generation and 
visible propagation of the plasma phase along the slit opening. 
The corresponding voltage values are summarized in Table 1. 
It should be noted that the primary aim of this study was to 
demonstrate the feasibility of uniform plasma generation 
along the slit opening. The operating parameters were 
therefore selected based on conditions providing a clearly 
visible plasma phase. In particular, for the configurations (a) 
and (b), a visible homogeneous plasma jet in Ar/H 2 was 
observed at approximately 11 kV pp, while higher voltages 
(~12 – 13 kV pp) led to increased direct discharge interactions 
with the sample. In contrast, for pure Ar, a visible plasma 
phase was firstly obtained only at slightly higher voltages 
(~12 kVpp). 
For the non-conductive configurations (c, glass) and (d, 
paper), variations in the applied voltage were required. In 
configuration (c), a plasma phase extending along the slit 
opening was obtained at ~15 kV pp in an Ar/H 2 mixture, 
whereas only localised filamentary structures were observed 
in pure Ar. At the same time, increasing the voltage to 
~16 kVpp led to a more spatially extended discharge in the case 
of Ar. For configuration (d), higher voltages of approximately 
21 to 22 kV pp were required due to the larger plasma 
propagation distance. At these operating conditions, both gas 
atmospheres produced a visibly continuous plasma jet phase 
across the slit opening, although differences in the discharge

2.3. High-speed imaging 
High-speed imaging of the plasma jet was performed using 
a nanosecond-gated ICCD camera (4 Quik E series, Stanford 
Computer Optics, Berkeley, CA, USA). The optical system 
was equipped with a Linos MeVis-C objective (25 mm focal 
length, F1.6). 
Three temporal parameters were controlled during image 
acquisition: exposure time (ET), integration time (IT), and 
trigger delay. The exposure time defines the duration of the 
camera gate, i.e., the time window during which photons are 
detected for a single frame, enabling time-resolved 
observation of fast plasma dynamics. The integration time 
corresponds to the total acquisition time over multiple 
discharge cycles, allowing signal accumulation to improve 
image intensity under low-emission conditions. 
The exposure time was varied between 1.2 ns (minimal 
value) and 10.0 ns to resolve fast transient plasma structures, 
while the integration time was adjusted to 5 s to achieve a 
sufficient signal-to-noise ratio without overexposure. 
The trigger delay was defined relative to the beginning of 
each discharge cycle, allowing time-resolved imaging at 
specific phases of plasma evolution. Given the operating 
frequency of 4 kHz (corresponding to a discharge period of 
250 µs), images were recorded at defined delays after the onset 
of the voltage pulse (e.g., 7 µs, 8 µs, 9 µs), enabling 
visualization of the initiation and propagation of a plasma 
phase within each cycle. It is important to note that the 
position of the high-voltage signal relative to the trigger 
exhibited a timing jitter of approximately ±37 ns (standard 
deviation). 
2.4. Electrical characteristics 
For the electrical characterization of the slit plasma jet, 
configuration (a), i.e., a borosilicate glass sample placed on a 
grounded metallic substrate (see Figure 3), was selected. This 
configuration was chosen to allow a comparative study with 
the plasma transport concept reported by Kostov and co-
workers, who investigated plasma propagation using a single 
floating wire, including its interaction with a glass sample 
positioned on a grounded metal surface [21]. Accordingly, all 
electrical measurements were performed using this 
configuration. The corresponding measurement setup is 
schematically illustrated in Fig. 1. 
An InfiniiVision DSOX3014T oscilloscope (Keysight 
Technologies, USA) was used for signal acquisition. The 
applied voltage was measured directly at the copper wire of 
the high-voltage (HV) electrode (see Figure 1, HV Probe 1). 
HV measurements were primarily performed using a 
PHV4002-3-RO probe (PMK Mess- und 
Kommunikationstechnik GmbH, Germany) with a resistance 
of 100 MΩ and a capacitance of 3 pF. In addition, a PP-
HVP2739 probe (Testec Elektronik GmbH, Germany) with a 
resistance of 900 MΩ and a capacitance of 2 pF was applied. 
The PMK probe exhibits electrical characteristics comparable 
to those used in previous studies by Kostov and co-workers 
[21], whereas the Testec probe is applied in other publications 
on plasma diagnostics. Owing to its higher resistance and 
lower capacitance, the Testec probe introduces less 
perturbation to the plasma discharge, which will be further 
discussed in Section 3.2. 
The transient discharge current was measured using a 
1147B current probe (Keysight Technologies, USA), which 
was placed around the grounded return conductor. To 
determine the transferred charge, the voltage drop across a 
10 nF capacitor was recorded. The capacitor was connected 
between the treated sample and ground, as shown in Fig. 1. 
Based on these measurements, the plasma power was 
calculated using the Lissajous method. 
2.5. Optical emission spectroscopy 
A qualitative analysis of active species in the plasma phase 
was performed using optical emission spectroscopy (OES)

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Imaging of the generated plasma phases 
Figure 5 presents photographs of the secondary plasma 
phase propagating from the 3D-printed PLA linear nozzle 
toward the four substrate configurations introduced in 
Figure 3. Images obtained during operation in an Ar/H 2 
atmosphere are shown in Fig. 5a–d, while operation in pure Ar 
is presented in Fig. 5e–h. The photographs were acquired 
using a smartphone camera and are intended solely for 
qualitative visualization of the overall plasma appearance. The 
aperture remained constant at F/1.8, while the exposure time 
(ET) and ISO sensitivity were automatically adjusted by the 
camera depending on the scene brightness and therefore varied 
between the individual images. 
Based on the photographs, the plasma phase generated in 
the Ar/H 2 atmosphere appears more spatially homogeneous 
along the slit width compared with operation in pure Ar, where 
more pronounced filamentary discharge structures can be 
observed. This trend is visible for all investigated substrate 
configurations. Insight into these differences can be obtained 
from the work of Wu and co-workers [18], who observed that 
the addition of H 2 to Ar was associated with a change in 
plasma morphology from a predominantly filamentary 
appearance toward a more diffuse discharge mode. The 
authors attributed this behavior to modifications of the 
ionization kinetics and reaction pathways in the presence of 
hydrogen, which may influence the formation and spatial 
distribution of localized discharge channels [18]. 
For the grounded configurations, namely borosilicate glass 
placed on grounded metal (Fig. 5a, e) and the grounded metal 
plate (Fig. 5b, f), localized filaments are more frequently 
observed, particularly under pure Ar conditions. In contrast, 
for the non-conductive substrates such as glass (Fig. 5c, g) and 
paper (Fig. 5d, h), the propagating plasma effluent appears 
more uniformly distributed and exhibits fewer distinct 
filamentary structures. Generally, in the Ar/H2 atmosphere the 
plasma plume appears to extend more continuously across the 
entire width of the slit opening, suggesting a more uniform 
spatial distribution of the generated plasma phase. This 
observation is further investigated in the following section 
using high-speed imaging, which provides more detailed 
insight into the spatial evolution of the plasma jet propagation.

Indeed, the high-speed images reveal clear differences in 
the spatial appearance of the plasma phase depending on the 
gas atmosphere. In the Ar/H 2 atmosphere (see Fig. 6a), the

To further analyse the fine structure of the plasma phase, 
images with reduced exposure were examined approximately 
9–11 µs after the voltage pulse began. The corresponding 
insets in Fig. 6a and Fig. 6b reveal the presence of finer 
filamentary structures in both atmospheres. Nevertheless, the 
Ar/H2 plasma phase remains more uniformly distributed, 
whereas the plasma generated in pure Ar is characterized by a 
stronger filamentary nature and a more localized discharge 
behavior. The observed differences in plasma jet appearance 
between the Ar and Ar/H 2 atmospheres can generally be 
associated with changes in ionization processes, plasma 
kinetics, and energy transfer mechanisms induced by the 
addition of hydrogen. However, due to the limited optical 
resolution and light sensitivity of the applied imaging system, 
a detailed characterization of individual streamer structures 
was not possible. 
Insights into the observed behavior can be obtained from 
previous studies investigating Ar/H 2 plasma jets. As already 
discussed, Wu and co-workers [18] reported a transition from 
a predominantly filamentary to a more diffuse plasma 
appearance after the addition of hydrogen and attributed this 
behavior to modifications of the ionization dynamics. 
Hydrogen introduces additional reaction pathways and energy 
transfer processes that can alter the electron energy 
distribution and consequently affect discharge development 
[4,18,44]. 
At the same time, it should be noted that the reported effects 
of hydrogen addition on plasma morphology and discharge 
characteristics are not always consistent across different 
studies. While Wu and co-workers [18] observed a diffuse 
plasma mode for Ar/H 2 mixtures containing up to 3% H 2, 
Nascimento and co-workers [4] did not report a clear 
transition toward diffuse discharge behavior despite observing 
significant changes in plasma characteristics for hydrogen 
concentrations up to 3.5%. The authors suggested that the 
effect of hydrogen addition depends strongly on the plasma 
source configuration and operating conditions [4]. Similar 
discrepancies can also be found in the reported electrical and 
plasma parameters. For example, Nascimento and co-workers 
[4] reported an increase in electron density and discharge 
current after hydrogen addition, whereas Wu and co-workers 
[18] observed an opposite trend. Furthermore, Guo and 
colleagues [44] reported a decrease in discharge current while 
observing only minor changes in electron density. 
In the present study, the addition of hydrogen resulted in 
noticeable changes in both plasma appearance and electrical 
characteristics. As discussed in the electrical characterization 
section (see section 3.2.), the Ar/H 2 plasma jet exhibited 
higher initial current peaks and increased power compared to 
operation in pure Ar. Additional indirect information 
regarding the changes in the electron density was obtained 
from optical emission spectroscopy and is discussed in 
Section 3.3. Nevertheless, while the present observations are 
consistent with the assumption that hydrogen modifies the 
ionization kinetics, the exact mechanisms responsible for the 
observed changes in plasma morphology remain beyond the 
scope of the present work and require further investigation.

3.2. Electrical characteristics 
Electrical characterization was performed on the substrate 
configuration using borosilicate glass placed on the grounded 
metal plate. This configuration was selected following the 
approach reported by Kostov and co-workers, who performed 
electrical characterization using a similar glass-on-grounded-
metal substrate arrangement [21]. The distance between the 
nozzle outlet and the glass surface was 12 mm. The power 
supply used in the experiment generates short voltage pulses 
with an adjustable amplitude ranging from 0 to 30 V. These 
low-voltage pulses are converted into high-voltage pulses 
using a transformer, which excites the plasma jet setup. The 
frequency (4.0 kHz), duty cycle (95 %) and amplitude of the 
pulses in this experiment were kept constant as discussed in 
the Section 2.1. 
Excitation of the system with these high-voltage pulses 
causes voltage oscillations within the plasma jet circuit, which 
depend on the electrical properties of the setup. The voltage 
and current waveforms measured for an Ar/H 2 plasma jet are 
shown in Fig. 8(a). The amplitude of the low-voltage pulses 
was set to 9.0 V at the generator, resulting in a high-voltage 
waveform with a peak-to-peak amplitude of 11.1 kV pp. The 
voltage and current waveforms for an Ar plasma jet are 
presented in Fig. 8(b), where the low-voltage pulse amplitude 
was set to 9.2 V, yielding a 11.7 kV pp peak-to-peak high-
voltage waveform. Both voltage waveforms were measured at 
the top electrode using a PMK HV-probe, while the current 
was recorded by a current clamp placed around the system’s 
grounded return wire. 
The voltage waveforms of both plasma jet configurations 
exhibit periodic excitation pulses at a frequency of 4.0 kHz, 
corresponding to a pulse repetition period of approximately 
250 µs. In addition to the excitation pulses, damped 
oscillations of the electrical system can be observed. The 
measured oscillation half-periods were 81.9 µs for the Ar/H 2 
plasma jet and 83.0 µs for the Ar plasma jet, corresponding to 
oscillation frequencies of approximately 6.11 kHz and 
6.02 kHz, respectively (see Figure 8). 
Short ripples in the current waveforms indicate plasma 
discharges. For the Ar/H 2 plasma jet, pronounced current 
peaks are observed immediately after the excitation pulse 
(after 0 µs), followed by additional discharge activity 
predominantly during the first half-cycle of the voltage 
oscillation. In contrast, the current waveform of the pure Ar 
plasma jet exhibits a less pronounced current peak associated 
with the rising edge of the excitation pulse (after 0 µs). The 
subsequent current peaks, however, are of comparable 
magnitude to those observed in the Ar/H 2 plasma jet. 
Furthermore, additional discharge events can be identified at 
later times (> 70 µs), indicating that discharge activity in the 
Ar plasma jet is distributed over a larger fraction of the 
excitation period. This observation is in agreement with the 
optical characterization results (Fig. 6), which showed a more 
pronounced plasma emission and a higher density of visible 
discharge channels during the early stage of the discharge 
development (≥ 8 µs) for the Ar/H 2 plasma jet. At the same 
time, the high-speed imaging revealed more pronounced 
plasma emission at later delays (≥ 70 µs) for the Ar plasma jet, 
which is consistent with the additional current peaks observed 
in the electrical measurements. 
The voltage waveforms measured for the plasma jet with 
the other substrate configurations (metal plate, glass, and 
paper, as shown in Figure 3) are presented in the 
Supplementary Material as Figure S1 and exhibit the same 
characteristic waveform shape.

Interestingly, during the electrical characterization, it was 
observed that connecting a high-voltage (HV) probe to the 
plasma jet system influenced the behavior of the secondary 
discharge emerging from the slit nozzle. When the voltage was 
measured at the top electrode, the primary discharge between 
the ceramic-insulated HV electrode and the floating mesh 
remained visibly unaffected. In contrast, the plasma phase 
leaving the nozzle became less intense and less homogeneous. 
This effect was observed for both gas atmospheres and was 
more pronounced during operation in pure Ar. 
A more pronounced effect was observed when the HV 
probe was connected directly to the floating mesh. To take 
measurements at the mesh, a small access hole was made in 
the PLA slit nozzle. This allowed a copper wire, which was 
connected to the mesh, to be guided outside the nozzle for 
connection to the HV probe. Under these conditions, no 
secondary plasma discharge could be generated at the nozzle 
outlet, regardless of the gas atmosphere. A similar behavior 
was previously reported by Kostov and co-workers, who noted 
that the plasma jet at the end of the dielectric tube could not 
be ignited when the HV probe was connected to the floating 
copper wire [21]. This observation suggests that the floating-
electrode plasma jet is highly sensitive to perturbations of the 
electrical circuit. 
The observed behavior can be explained by the additional 
resistance and capacitance introduced by the HV probe, which 
modifies the electrical configuration of the system through a 
parallel connection to the oscilloscope. In conventional 
dielectric barrier discharge systems, such perturbations are 
typically negligible due to the comparatively large 
capacitances present in the reactor. In contrast, plasma jet 
systems employing floating electrodes appear to be 
considerably more sensitive to such changes, particularly 
when the floating electrode itself is contacted. While the 
primary discharge exhibits characteristics similar to a 
conventional DBD, the secondary discharge generated from 
the floating electrode is strongly influenced by the floating 
potential and the overall electrical impedance of the system. 
The corresponding voltage waveforms measured at the top 
electrode and the floating mesh are presented in Figures S2 
and S3 for the Ar/H2 and Ar plasma jets, respectively. In both 
cases, the voltage measured at the floating mesh is lower than 
that measured at the top electrode by approximately 5 kV pp 
peak-to-peak. A similar behaviour in the voltage drop between 
the powered electrode and the floating electrode was reported 
by Kostov and co-workers for plasma jet systems employing 
a single floating copper wire [21]. The observed voltage 
reduction, together with the sharp voltage transitions visible in 
the waveforms, can be attributed to the primary discharge 
occurring between the top electrode and the floating mesh, 
which acts as an electrical load within the system and results 
in energy dissipation before the potential is transferred to the 
secondary discharge region. 
It is important to mention that the measurements presented 
in this work were primarily performed using a PMK high-
voltage probe with a resistance of 100 MΩ and a capacitance 
of 3 pF, as it exhibits electrical characteristics comparable to 
those used in the previous study by Kostov and co-workers 
[21]. Additional experiments were conducted using a Testec 
probe with a higher resistance (900 MΩ) and lower 
capacitance (2 pF). As shown in Figure S4, the Testec probe 
resulted in a smaller perturbation of the plasma process when 
connected to the top electrode, yielding higher measured 
voltages and less pronounced changes in the secondary 
discharge. Nevertheless, even with this probe, some influence 
on the plasma behavior remained observable. When connected 
to the floating mesh, the secondary discharge again 
disappeared completely. These observations highlight the 
importance of careful probe selection when electrically 
characterizing plasma jet systems based on floating 
electrodes. 
3.3. Characterization of the reactive species 
A qualitative characterization of the reactive species 
present in the plasma phases was performed using optical 
emission spectroscopy (OES), with the corresponding results 
presented in Figure 10. The measurements were conducted 
using substrate configuration (a), i.e., a borosilicate glass 
sample placed on a grounded metallic substrate. First, 
Figures 10 (a) and 10 (b) present the OES spectra of the 
primary discharge generated inside the quartz tube between 
the high-voltage electrode and the floating metallic mesh for 
the Ar/H2 and Ar atmospheres, respectively. Both spectra were 
acquired using the same integration time of 42.290 ms. 
Both spectra are dominated by a group of characteristic 
emission lines in the wavelength range of approximately 696–
965 nm, which can be attributed to Ar I transitions [7,8,46,47]. 
These emissions can originate from electron impact excitation 
of argon atoms from the ground state to higher energy levels, 
followed by radiative relaxation. The presence of these lines 
indicates the formation of excited argon species as well as 
ionization processes occurring within the primary discharge. 
In the Ar/H2 plasma (Fig. 10a), an additional emission peak 
at approximately 656 nm is observed and assigned to the H α 
(Balmer) line [7,47–49]. This transition corresponds to the 
radiative decay of excited hydrogen atoms from the energy 
level n = 3 to n = 2 and is commonly used as an indicator of 
excited atomic hydrogen in plasma phases. Such hydrogen 
species are expected to be generated via dissociative excitation 
of molecular hydrogen and through electron impact excitation 
and ionization processes [48,49]. 
Furthermore, a broad emission feature in the spectral range 
of approximately 200–500 nm is detected in the Ar/H 2 
spectrum. Interestingly, a similar feature was reported by 
Nascimento and co-workers during the optical emission 
spectroscopy characterization of Ar/H 2 atmospheric-pressure

attributed to trace amounts of residual O₂ in the argon gas used

specifications, contains oxygen impurities at levels of ≤2 ppm. 
Interestingly, a corresponding emission feature at 
approximately 777 nm could not be clearly observed in the 
Ar/H2 spectrum. This difference may indicate that hydrogen 
participates in reactions with oxygen-containing species, 
potentially altering their concentration or excitation pathways 
in the plasma. 
The use of identical integration times allows a comparison 
of the overall emission intensity between the two gas 
atmospheres. Under these conditions, the characteristic 
emission lines in the Ar/H 2 plasma generally exhibit lower 
intensities than the emissions in pure Ar. For the primary 
discharge, this difference may reflect changes in the discharge 
characteristics and excitation kinetics resulting from the 
presence of hydrogen. However, a detailed interpretation of 
the mechanisms responsible for this difference would require 
further investigation using e.g., improved high-speed imaging 
conditions, such as higher-resolution optics and greater 
magnification, to better resolve the characteristics of the 
primary discharge.

The OES characterization of the secondary plasma phases 
is presented in Fig. 10 (c) and 10 (d) for the Ar/H 2 and Ar 
atmospheres, respectively. In both cases, the spectra were 
measured outside the slit-shaped nozzle in ambient air using 
an identical integration time of 1 s. Compared with the 
primary discharge, the emission spectra of the secondary 
plasma phase exhibit additional spectral features resulting 
from the interaction of the plasma with the surrounding 
atmosphere. 
For the Ar/H2 plasma jet (Fig. 10 (c)), the characteristic Ar 
emission lines and the H α emission at 656 nm, as well as the 
broad emission feature in the range of approximately 200–
500 nm, remain visible. In addition, several emission features 
appear in the wavelength range of approximately 300–450 nm. 
The dominant signals in this region can be attributed to 
nitrogen-related species, such as transitions of the N 2 second 
positive system and N 2+ first negative system [46,47,52]. 
Furthermore, a distinct peak at around 309 nm indicated the 
presence of OH radicals [46,50]. 
A similar general behavior is observed for the Ar plasma 
jet (Fig. 10 (d)), with the characteristic Ar I emission lines 
remaining clearly visible. As expected, no hydrogen-related 
emissions are detected. Instead, additional spectral features 
associated with air-related species are observed, including 
emission bands attributed to the N2 second positive system and 
N2+ first negative system, as well as emission from OH 
radicals and atomic oxygen [7,8,40,50,51]. 
The appearance of these additional emission features in 
both plasma jets demonstrates the interaction of the secondary 
plasma phase with the surrounding air. In particular, excitation 
and ionization of atmospheric components such as N2, O2, and 
water vapour contribute to the observed emission spectra. 
Such behaviour is typical for atmospheric-pressure plasma jets 
operated in open air and has been reported for different plasma 
jet systems [7,8,40,50,51]. 
At the same time, when comparing the spectra obtained 
using the same integration time of 1 s, the Ar/H 2 plasma jet 
exhibits a lower overall emission intensity than the 
corresponding Ar plasma jet (see Figures 10 (c) and (d)). This 
difference may be related to the more localized and intense 
discharge structures observed in pure Ar, as evident from the 
photographs and high-speed imaging (see Figures 5e and 7b, 
respectively). Such localized high-intensity structures can 
contribute to the collected optical emission and may therefore 
account for the higher overall emission intensity observed for 
the Ar plasma jet. 
Additionally, an interesting difference can be observed 
when comparing the Ar/H2 spectra obtained from the primary 
(Figure 10 (a)) and secondary (Figure 10 (c)) discharge 
regions. Despite the substantially longer integration time used 
for the secondary plasma phase (1 s) compared with the 
primary discharge (42.290 ms), the H α emission in the 
secondary plasma phase exhibits lower intensity and is 
accompanied by a higher background noise level. This 
difference can indicate that the secondary plasma phase 
produces weaker optical emission due to its distinct discharge 
and propagation conditions, while also suggesting that the 
applied integration time may not be optimal for resolving the 
Hα emission with a comparable signal quality. To further 
support the presence of H α emission in the secondary plasma 
phase for substrate configuration (a) (see Figure 3) and 
provide a clearer representation of this feature under more 
favorable measurement conditions, an additional spectrum 
was acquired using a longer integration time of 10 s. This 
spectrum, which provides an improved signal-to-noise ratio, 
is included in the Supplementary Material as Figure S5. 
To investigate the influence of the substrate configuration 
on the secondary plasma phase, additional OES measurements 
were performed for the four systems. Figure 11 presents the 
spectra obtained for the Ar/H2 plasma jet at an integration time 
of 1 s while propagating toward: (a) borosilicate glass on 
grounded metal (Gl/Me), (b) a grounded metal plate (Me), (c) 
borosilicate glass (Glass), and (d) paper. The spectrum for the 
Gl/Me configuration (Fig. 11) corresponds to the secondary 
plasma phase already characterized in Fig. 10c and is included 
here as a reference for comparison. 
All four configurations exhibit the characteristic emission 
features previously identified for the secondary Ar/H 2 plasma 
phase, including Ar-, N 2-related, and OH emission. The 
overall emission intensities are of a similar order of 
magnitude, with the highest intensity observed for Gl/Me and 
the lowest for Me. A more distinct difference is observed for 
the H α emission at approximately 656 nm: the feature is 
resolved for the Gl/Me and Me configurations, which include 
a grounded metallic component, but is not clearly detected for 
the isolated glass and paper substrates. The observed 
difference may be associated with the distinct plasma 
structures visualized in Sections 3.1 and 3.2, where localized 
discharge channels were more frequently observed for 
configurations containing a grounded metal, whereas the 
plasma propagating toward the electrically isolated substrates 
appeared more uniformly distributed. These differences in 
plasma structure may affect the local electric-field distribution 
and excitation processes and, consequently, the generation and 
excitation of atomic hydrogen. In addition, the broad emission 
feature between approximately 200 and 500 nm is observed 
for all four configurations, although with lower intensity for 
the isolated substrates.

integration time of 10 s. The corresponding spectra are 
provided in Supplements as Figure S6 and confirm the H α 
emission for the Gl/Me and Me configurations, while no 
clearly resolved Hα feature is observed for the glass and paper 
substrates. Overall, the substrate configuration appears to 
influence primarily the detectability of Hα rather than the types

4. Conclusions 
This work addressed two key challenges associated with 
atmospheric pressure plasma jets: the transfer of reactive 
plasma species beyond the primary discharge region, and the 
spatial expansion of the plasma phase to enable the treatment 
of larger surface areas. To this end, a novel slit-geometry 
plasma jet based on a floating metallic mesh integrated into a 
3D-printed nozzle was developed and investigated under Ar 
and Ar/H2 (2 vol.% H2) atmospheres using different substrate 
configurations. 
Unlike previously reported floating-wire systems, this 
concept combines plasma potential transfer with spatial 
scaling. This enables the generation of a line-shaped plasma 
phase, for example with a width of approximately 36 mm. 
High-speed imaging revealed that the gas atmosphere 
significantly impacts plasma morphology and discharge 
behaviour. Specifically, Ar/H 2 produces a more uniform 
discharge distribution across the slit width, whereas pure Ar 
results in a more localized plasma appearance. 
Optical emission spectroscopy (OES) confirmed the 
presence of reactive argon species in both atmospheres, as 
well as emission from atomic hydrogen species in the Ar/H 2 
plasma jet propagating toward substrate configurations 
containing grounded metallic surfaces. This indicates its 
potential for reduction-based processes, such as metallic 
surface deoxidation. Furthermore, the OES results provided 
indirect evidence of differences in plasma characteristics 
between the Ar and Ar/H 2 atmospheres, which may be 
associated with changes in electron density. 
At the same time, electrical characterization revealed clear 
differences in discharge behavior between the two 
atmospheres. Plasma power determined from the Lissajous 
analysis amounted to 0.85 W for the Ar/H 2 plasma jet and 
0.19 W for the Ar plasma jet under the selected operating 
conditions. The measurements further demonstrated the high 
sensitivity of the floating-electrode configuration to 
perturbations introduced by high-voltage probes, emphasizing 
the importance of careful electrical diagnostics of such plasma 
systems. 
From a broader perspective, the presented concept expands 
the design possibilities of atmospheric pressure plasma jets for 
large-area surface treatment. To further evaluate the 
scalability of the concept, an additional slit nozzle with an 
opening width of approximately 73 mm was fabricated and 
operated. Under comparable Ar/H 2 plasma conditions, a 
homogeneously distributed plasma phase could also be 
generated across this increased width (see Supplementary 
Information, Figure S8), demonstrating the potential of the 
proposed design for further spatial scaling. Such systems may 
be of interest for applications including metallic surface 
cleaning, deoxidation, polymer activation, and other plasma-
assisted manufacturing processes where treatment uniformity 
and scalability are required.

At the same time, several aspects require further 
investigation. In particular, the influence of hydrogen 
concentration on plasma morphology, the relationship 
between electrical discharge characteristics and plasma 
uniformity, as well as the transport and distribution of reactive 
species along the slit geometry remain open questions. 
Furthermore, optimization of the nozzle geometry and gas 
distribution to reduce the required gas flow rate while 
maintaining stable plasma generation represents an important 
direction for future work. In addition, optimization of the 
electrical excitation conditions and more detailed optical 
characterization using higher-resolution imaging systems will 
be necessary to further clarify plasma propagation 
mechanisms and evaluate the technological potential of the 
proposed system. 
Overall, the results demonstrate that the combination of a 
floating metallic mesh with a slit-nozzle geometry represents 
a promising strategy for coupling plasma transport with spatial 
scaling, thereby providing a foundation for the development 
of atmospheric pressure plasma jets for the uniform treatment 
of extended surfaces.
