Effect of graded levels of nitrogen application on yield
and nutrient uptake of maize
K Shivashankar1, MP Potdar2, Aishwarya Golshetti3, DP Biradar4
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/2618060X.2026.v9.i9l.6620
A BSTRACT
Among the world’s cereal crops, maize ranks third after rice and wheat, and its dual value as human
food and animal feed underlines its economic importance. Because maize is a heavy feeder, a balanced
dose of 150:65:65 kg N, P2O5 and K2O ha−1 is generally recommended under irrigation, although the
crop continues to respond favourably to nitrogen well beyond this level. Nitrogen, in particular, remains
the nutrient of greatest concern in maize production systems, being the primary driver of vegetative
growth and reproductive development. With this in view, a field trial was conducted at the Main
Agricultural Research Station, University of Agricultural Sciences, Dharwad, during kharif 2020 to ex-
amine how graded nitrogen doses affect the yield and nutrient uptake of maize. The trial, laid out in a
randomized complete block design, comprised nine treatments spanning 0 to 200 per cent of the recom-
mended dose of nitrogen (RDN) in steps of 25 per cent. The 200 per cent RDN treatment produced the
best yield-contributing traits, including cob length (17.6 cm), cob girth (4.70 cm), grains per row (35.5),
grains per cob (566.2), test weight (32.8 g) and grain yield per cob (163.1 g), all significantly ahead of
the remaining treatments. The same treatment also gave the highest grain yield (9627.6 kg ha−1), straw
yield (12243.8 kg ha−1), harvest index (44.0 %) and NPK uptake, although it did not differ significantly
from the 175 and 150 per cent RDN treatments.
Keywords: Nitrogen, Maize, Yield, Yield attributes, Nutrient uptake
Introduction
Globally, maize occupies third place among cereal crops in overall importance, trailing only rice and wheat,
with roughly 85 per cent of its area sown during the kharif season. For nearly 900 million people in low-in-
come households it serves as a preferred staple, contributing meaningfully to food security across develop-
ing economies. Nutritionally, maize grain comprises about 72 per cent starch, 10 per cent protein, 4.8 per
cent oil, 8.5 per cent fibre, 3.0 per cent sugar and 1.7 per cent ash (Huma et al., 2019) [1], and the crop is
equally valued as fodder and grain under both irrigated and rainfed conditions (Kumar et al., 2013) [2]. Its si-
multaneous role in human and livestock nutrition explains its widespread cultivation. Climate projections
suggest that despite warmer temperatures and increasingly erratic rainfall, maize output will need to rise by
at least 18 per cent by 2030 to satisfy demand driven by population growth and evolving diets (Anon, 2015)
[3]. At present the crop is grown worldwide on 206.3 million hectares, yielding 780 million tonnes at an av-
erage productivity of 3150 kg ha−1 (Anon, 2021) [4]. India accounts for 9.86 million hectares of this area and
31.51 million tonnes of production, while Karnataka, the country’s leading producer, cultivates 1.68 million
hectares at a productivity of 3092 kg ha−1, somewhat below the national figure of 3195 kg ha−1 (Anon,
2021) [4].
Being an exhaustive feeder, maize responds strongly to applied nutrients, which makes careful nutrient and
water management central to its productivity. A balanced dose of 150:65:65 kg N, P2O5 and K2O ha−1 is the
general recommendation for the irrigated crop. Among these nutrients, nitrogen holds particular significance
as it is a structural component of every amino acid and of chlorophyll, the pigment that drives photosynthe-
sis, and is consequently regarded as the nutrient with the single greatest bearing on grain yield. Nitrogen fer-
tilizer underpins food production for more than half the world’s population, even though its use efficiency
typically remains only 30 to 50 per cent. Because it is the principal nutrient governing crop growth and de-
velopment, nitrogen management continues to be a central concern in maize cultivation (Blumenthal et al.,
2008) [5]. A range of earlier studies has shown that nitrogen supply governs its own uptake as well as photo-
synthesis, leaf area development, the onset of senescence, kernel numbers and eventual yield potential
(Hanjanatti et al., 2021; Ciampitti et al., 2011) [7, 6]. Elevated nitrogen doses have likewise been associated
with greater plant height, thicker stems, larger leaf area and leaf area index, higher dry matter accumulation,
improved net assimilation ratio and better productivity (Cheema et al., 2010) [8]. Since nitrogen remains the
most limiting of all nutrients, ensuring its adequate supply alongside other nutrients is essential for maintain-
ing soil fertility and sustaining high crop productivity (Scharf et al., 2002) [9]. Keeping these considerations

Material and methods
The field experiment was conducted at the Main Agricultural
Research Station (MARS), University of Agricultural Sciences,
Dharwad, located in the Northern Transition Zone of Karnataka
(Zone-8) at 150 30’6” N latitude, 740 59’13.2” E longitude, at an ele-
vation of 678 m above mean sea level. A randomized complete block
design was used, comprising nine graded nitrogen levels — absolute
control together with 25, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150, 175 and 200 per cent
of the recommended dose of nitrogen — each replicated thrice. Urea,
the nitrogen source, was applied in four split doses in line with stan-
dard package-of-practice recommendations for the respective treat-
ments (Table 1). Pre-sowing analysis showed the experimental soil to
be neutral to slightly alkaline (pH 7.71) with normal electrical con-
ductivity (0.33 dS m−1), low organic carbon (0.47%), low available
nitrogen (259.3 kg N ha−1), medium available phosphorus (33.4 kg
P2O5 ha−1) and high available potassium (377.5 kg K2O ha−1). Data
generated during the study were compiled and statistically analysed
following the procedure described by Gomez and Gomez (1984), and
treatment means were compared using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test
(DMRT) based on the corresponding error mean sum of squares and
degrees of freedom.
Results and discussion
Yield attributes, yield and harvest index of maize as influ-
enced by graded levels of nitrogen
Graded nitrogen levels had a significant bearing on the yield at-
tributes, the individual yield components (cob, grain and straw) and
the harvest index of maize; the corresponding data are summarised in
Tables 2 and 3.
Progressive increases in nitrogen supply exerted a favourable influ-
ence on the yield attributes of maize. The greatest cob length
(17.6 cm), cob diameter (4.70 cm) and test weight (32.8 g) were ob-
tained under 200 per cent RDN, a treatment that remained statistically
on par with 175 per cent RDN (17.1 cm, 4.51 cm and 32.5 g, respec-
tively) and 150 per cent RDN (16.4 cm, 4.38 cm and 32.1 g, respec-
tively). Greater nitrogen availability and uptake at the higher doses
appears to have promoted dry matter synthesis and its preferential
movement towards reproductive structures, which likely explains this
pattern. This is consistent with Majid et al. (2017) [10], who recorded
significantly longer cobs with 345 kg N ha−1 than with 115 kg N
ha−1, and with Matusso et al. (2016) [11], who also reported a positive
association between cob length and other yield attributes as nitrogen
dose increased.
Shelling percentage (82.2 %) peaked under 200 per cent RDN, re-
maining statistically comparable to all other treatments except the ab-
solute control (72.2 %) and 25 per cent RDN (73.1 %). Likewise,
grains per row (35.5), grains per cob (566) and grain weight per cob
(163.1 g) reached their maximum under 200 per cent RDN, on par
with 175 per cent RDN (35.0, 561 and 161.6 g), 150 per cent RDN
(33.2, 515 and 148.3 g) and 125 per cent RDN (31.8, 496 and 142.8
g). This can largely be attributed to greater nitrogen uptake during the
vegetative phase, which enhanced translocation of carbohydrate re-
serves from source to sink, thereby raising overall growth and dry
matter production and, in turn, the number of rows per cob, grains per
cob and test weight. Together, more rows per cob and more grains per
row produced a higher grain count per cob, which combined with
heavier test weight to raise grain weight per cob. These findings agree
with those of Ramachandiran et al. (2016) [12], Ngosong et al. (2019)
[13] and Singh et al. (2021) [14] in maize, while Varshini et al. (2019)
[15] similarly observed higher grain rows per cob, grains per row and
grains per cob with 225 kg N ha−1 applied in four equal splits.
Cob yield was likewise significantly greater under 200 per cent RDN
(11150.7 kg ha−1) than under the remaining treatments, though it
stayed on par with 175 per cent RDN (10865.6 kg ha−1), 150 per cent
RDN (10576.6 kg ha−1) and 125 per cent RDN (9929.8 kg ha−1).
Grain yield (9627.6 kg ha−1) and straw yield (12243.8 kg ha−1) fol-
lowed the same pattern, peaking under 200 per cent RDN and remain-
ing statistically similar to 175 per cent RDN (9308.9 and 12090.1 kg
ha−1) and 150 per cent RDN (8935.2 and 11859.2 kg ha−1) (Fig. 1).
Harvest index was also highest under 200 per cent RDN (45.0 %), on
par with every treatment except the absolute control (37.3 %). These
gains can be attributed to greater nitrogen uptake and more vigorous
physiological activity under higher nitrogen doses, which supported
stronger vegetative development and, consequently, greater produc-
tion and translocation of photosynthates towards the reproductive
parts. As before, higher rows per cob and grains per row raised grain
numbers per cob, which together with heavier test weight enhanced
grain weight per cob, ultimately lifting straw and grain yield. These
outcomes are consistent with Kumar et al. (2021) [16], Hanjanatti et
al. (2021) [7] and Mohapatro et al. (2022) [17], and align with Sharma
et al. (2019) [18], who likewise reported higher cob yield with increas-
ing nitrogen rates.
Effect of graded levels of nitrogen on nutrient uptake at
harvest
Nitrogen uptake by grain (160 kg ha−1), straw (56.7 kg ha−1) and in
total (216.7 kg ha−1) at harvest was significantly greater under 200
per cent RDN than under the other treatments (Fig. 2), though compa-
rable with 175 per cent RDN (154.2, 55.6 and 209.8 kg ha−1) and 150
per cent RDN (145.2, 54.2 and 199.3 kg ha−1). Higher nitrogen doses
evidently sustained greater nitrogen availability throughout crop
growth, which likely drove this increase in uptake (Dobermann et al.,
2002) [19]; this, in turn, boosted metabolic activity and the accumula-
tion of dry matter and nutrients within it, ultimately raising both
biomass and grain yield (Hussain et al., 2005) [20]. Greater nitrogen
content in the plant coupled with higher biomass thus translated into
higher nitrogen uptake, a pattern also reported by Sunitha et al.
(2012) [21] in sweet corn and by Rawal et al. (2014) [22] in maize.
Phosphorus and potassium uptake followed a similar trend, with the
highest values for grain (30.5 and 73.3 kg ha−1), straw (33.9 and
113.1 kg ha−1) and total uptake (64.4 and 186.4 kg ha−1) recorded un-
der 200 per cent RDN, on par with 175 and 150 per cent RDN (Fig. 3
and 4). This can be explained by the positive interaction between ni-
trogen and the other two nutrients: as nitrogen application and uptake
increased, so did biomass along with the phosphorus and potassium
concentrations in grain and straw, ultimately raising total P and K up-
take. Similar findings have been reported by Yinkun et al. (2014) [23]
in rice, Mohanty et al. (2016) [24] in wheat, Suresh et al. (2017) [25]
in maize and Ali et al. (2019) [26] in wheat.
Conclusion
The present study indicates that applying 200 per cent RDN gives sig-
nificantly greater yield and yield attributes relative to the remaining
treatments, although the 175 and 150 per cent RDN treatments pro-
duced statistically comparable yields.
