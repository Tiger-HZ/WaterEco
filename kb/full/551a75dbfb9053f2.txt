Quantification of groundwater recharge (GWR) and groundwater-sustained baseflow is fundamental for understanding freshwater systems and for the sustainable management of both groundwater and surface-water resources. Baseflow, which dominates river discharge during dry periods, plays a key role in regulating water availability, nutrient cycling, and contaminant transport. However, estimation of groundwater recharge and thus the baseflow index (BFI) — defined as the fraction of streamflow sustained by groundwater contributions — remains highly uncertain due to methodological differences, data limitations, and spatial variability. To shed light on these uncertainties, we evaluate the performance of the WaterGAP model against independent BFI estimates based on observed streamflow (Xie et al. 2024). Two WaterGAP variants with different GWR algorithms are evaluated: the standard WaterGAP 2.2e GWR algorithm and an improved version proposed by Wan et al. (2024), which explicitly differentiates diffuse groundwater recharge in karst and non-karst regions based on the World Karst Aquifer Map and has been tuned using a large number of independent GWR recharge estimates. Each variant is driven by two climate forcings, GWSP-ERA5 and GWSP-W5E5. Xie et al. (2024) provide 12 BFI estimates for each of the 15,496 streamflow stations in their global dataset. For this study, their observation-based estimates were aggregated to BFIs at 5,461 global 0.5° grid cells for the period 1950–2019. A dedicated analysis was conducted for karst-dominated regions, defined as grid cells with more than 50% karst coverage, comprising 635 cells globally. For each grid cell, the BFI is calculated using two approaches: BFI_cell and BFI_soil. BFI_cell is defined as the ratio of total groundwater recharge (diffuse plus point recharge occurring below surface water bodies) to net cell runoff (runoff from land and from the surface water bodies inside the cell), whereas BFI_soil is calculated as the ratio of diffuse groundwater recharge to total runoff from land. Additionally, since GWR also affects water storage, we compared terrestrial water storage anomalies (TWSA) simulated by both model versions with TWSA estimates from the Center for Space Research (CSR) mascon solution, which is based on observations from the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor mission, GRACE Follow-On, across 148 large basins worldwide. Wan’s GWR algorithm generally improves BFI estimates compared to the standard WaterGAP 2.2e approach. For example, using GWSP-ERA5 climate forcing, BFI_cell values simulated with Wan’s GWR algorithm fell within the range of observation-based values for 47% of the cells, while this was true for only 30% with the standard WaterGAP 2.2e GWR algorithm. These performance values are similar for BFI_soil and the GWSP-W5E5 climate forcing. Surprisingly, the BFI fit did not improve in karst-dominated regions.
