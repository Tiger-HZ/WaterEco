Abstract The stratification of ice‐covered lakes is currently understood as either cryomictic (mixing near 0°C) or cryostratified (inverse stratification reaching 4°C). However, internal or external salt sources may alter these patterns, creating stable stratification with high under‐ice bottom water temperatures, a condition we term cryohalostratification, which may lead to anoxia and interference with spring mixing. We measured stratification and water quality over two winters in a rural Canadian Shield lake exposed to a highway. We found high under‐ice water temperatures (&gt; 4°C) and low dissolved oxygen (&lt; 2 mg L −1 ) because of increases in chloride and sodium.
