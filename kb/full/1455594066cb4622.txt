Abstract 21 
A groundwater -dependent ecosystem (GDE) can be a stream or wetland sustained by 22 
groundwater, a terrestrial ecosystem that relies on groundwater for root water uptake, or the 23 
biodiversity that lives within aquifers. These ecosystems perform hydrological, ecological , and 24 
climate functions, and are meaningful to people through recreational, spiritual, and cultural 25 
practices. Traditionally studied at the local scale, GDEs are largely overlooked in global -scale 26 
research programmes and lack adequate incorporation in global environmental and sustainability 27 
policy agendas, despite holding vast potential to enhance their outcomes. Several global GDE 28 
studies have recently emerged, revealing that GDEs are globally prevalent and increasingly under 29 
threat. This Review synthesises early insights from these studies and argues that this emerging 30 
picture of GDE presence must be complemented by a dynamic, process-based understanding of 31 
GDE responses under multiple pressures . We outline policy agendas and Earth system 32 
frameworks that could be strengthened by the inclusion of GDEs and identify open questions to 33 
guide research in support of this shift. 34

Groundwater-dependent ecosystem (GDE) scholarship emerged from multiple distinct 35 
disciplinary lineages, spanning the documentation of phreatophytes in arid regions,1 the study of 36 
groundwater and surface water interactions in rivers and the hyporheic zone ,2 and the 37 
development of groundwater ecology.3 Collectively, these advances established groundwater as 38 
a dynamic component of the critical zone (where rock, soil, water, air and biota interact to sustain 39 
terrestrial life) 4 that supports a wide variety of ecosystems and their functions ,5–7 including 40 
mediating heat flux es at the land surface, regulating streamflow and water temperature, and 41 
transporting solutes, carbon and nutrients. GDE science further illuminates groundwater not 42 
merely as an inert physical resource but as a living ecosystem that nurtures dependent species 43 
and facilitates life processes .8 Groundwater, together with other subterranean ecosystems, 44 
contributes to three-quarters of all classified ecosystem services.9 45 
 Ecological research involving groundwater has advanced alongside a broader embrace of 46 
interdisciplinarity in groundwater science.10–13 Such developments at the interface of groundwater 47 
hydrology and ecology have been highly symbiotic . For instance, phreatophyte identification 48 
provided boundary conditions used in the early development of regional groundwater flow 49 
theory.14,15 The term ‘groundwater-dependent ecosystem’16 offers an overarching categorisation 50 
through which to understand ecological interactions with groundwater . I ts introduction 51 
encouraged further collaboration among hydrology, ecology, and biology research and 52 
practitioner communities,17 and made significant inroads promoting the awareness and protection 53 
of these ecosystems.18,19 Many GDE advances have also been incentivised by sustainable water 54 
laws and policies in jurisdictions worldwide which have required an understanding of GDE 55 
locations and their ecological thresholds to inform water decisions.19 56 
After a century of groundwater-ecosystem science,1 and nearly three decades since GDEs 57 
were formally recognised,16 we propose that a meaningful continuation of GDE scholarship lies in 58 
pursuing a community-wide and policy-relevant global-scale research agenda on GDEs. Although 59 
typically studied at the local scale, advancing a global focus on GDEs is particularly meaningful 60 
because: (i) the emergence of continental to global scale hydrology and ecohydrology 20–22 61 
enables analysis at this scale ; and (ii) GDEs are profoundly linked to global change and its 62 
associated challenges , including biodiversity loss, climate change, and the pursuit of 63 
multidimensional sustainability goals ,23 which require global perspectives to be meaningfully 64 
addressed. 65 
Here, we (1) provide a synthesis of insights from the first global-scale GDE studies, from 66 
which we (2) argue that a more dynamic understanding of GDEs under global change is missing 67 
and needed. We then (3) situate GDEs as embedded within the critical zone and social-ecological 68 
systems, making ( 4) addressing policy -relevant knowledge gaps on processes linking global 69 
GDEs with biodiversity, climate, and sustainability goals a high -potential priority to guide an 70 
impactful future in global GDE science. 71

Box 1: What are GDEs and why study them at the global scale? 
Groundwater-dependent ecosystems (GDEs): An ecosystem that relies on groundwater for 
some or all of its freshwater needs (see legend of Figure 1). There are several types and 
classifications of GDEs6,24,25 that include terrestrial GDEs (land-based ecosystems such as fens 
and dryland woodlands whose vegetation or ecological processes depend on access to 
groundwater); aquatic GDEs (surface water ecosystems such as rivers, wetlands, and springs 
that are sustained by groundwater discharge); subterranean GDEs (subsurface ecosystems in 
aquifers and caves that depend on groundwater as their primary habitat ); and coastal and 
marine GDEs (estuarine and nearshore marine systems such as mangroves and salt marshes 
that rely on groundwater discharge). 
 
Why a global focus on GDEs? A global perspective on GDEs can be useful because: (1) 
understanding the distribution of GDEs and their pressures can help place environments within 
a global context, (2) systematic analysis of GDEs can enable prioritisation and knowledge 
transfer between regions, (3) GDEs link groundwater to biodiversity, climate, and sustainability 
processes and policy agendas that operate at the global scale, and (4) global studies help raise 
awareness about the distributed nature of groundwater and its ecological functi ons. Many of 
these purposes align with the general benefits of global scale groundwater science (see full list 
in ref.26). 
 GDEs are a n integrative concept that link s research topics with strong research 
programmes and traditions at the global scale. These include plant-groundwater interactions,27 
groundwater-surface water interactions,28 groundwater biodiversity,29 ecosystem services and 
nature's contribution s to people .5,30 Yet, t here remain few studies at the global scale that 
explicitly work within a GDE framework. Most GDE scholarship to date has been conducted at 
site-specific scales, although several states and countries have advanced GDE mapping and 
protection initiatives.31,32 Advancing global perspectives on GDEs opens distinct research 
questions that can complement and strengthen (but not supersede) insights produced at local, 
regional, and biome scales. 
GDEs are present across the globe in both humid and arid landscapes 72 
A handful of studies have generated first -order estimates of the distribution of global GDEs.33–35 73 
Here, we synthesise these first global mapping efforts and summarise GDE distributions across 74 
environmental gradients ( Figure 1 ). Existing studies have focused on mapping aquatic and 75 
terrestrial GDEs and vary in the granularity with which GDE types are specified, spatial resolution, 76 
and methods to detect or model GDE presence (see Supplementary Figure 1). 77

At our scale of comparison (~5 5 km grids at the equator), almost every grid cell whose 88 
land cover permits GDE occurrence contains at least some GDE fraction (Figure 1a). Although 89 
groundwater dependence is predominantly framed as a feature of arid landscapes, GDEs are in 90 
fact present in all biomes (Figure 1b). Indeed, aquatic GDEs are considerably more extensive 91 
than terrestrial GDEs globally (Figure 1c). This suggests it may be more useful to understand 92 
GDEs through their areal density (e.g., sparse versus dense) and landscape composition (i.e., 93 
what types of GDEs are present) rather than through a binary presence or absence classification. 94 
GDE extents generally decrease with aridity ( Supplementary Figure 3) as reduced 95 
precipitation excess limits recharge and deepens water tables. Terrestrial and aquatic GDE 96 
distributions generally follow the same density gradients (i.e., landscapes with extensive terrestrial 97 
GDEs often co -occur with landscapes with extensive aquatic GDEs), and this coupling is 98 
especially strong within Holdridge Life Zones (a bioclimatic ecosystem classification based on 99 
precipitation, temperature and evapotranspiration) 44 (Figure 1 d). Both aquatic and terrestrial 100 
GDEs are most extensive in low elevation, humid environments (e.g., tropical and temperate 101 
forests; Figure 1 e). Relative distributions of terrestrial and aquatic GDEs differ across 102 
environmental gradients and landforms (Figure 1f). For instance, a larger share of aquatic GDEs 103 
is present in plain landforms than of terrestrial GDEs, while mountain landforms hold a larger 104 
share of terrestrial GDEs than of aquatic GDEs. 105 
These distributions align with plausible process explanations . For instance, in 106 
mountainous landscapes with deep water tables, the water table often falls below the depth 107 
threshold for surface expression before it exceeds the reach of deep -rooted phreatophytes . 108 
Conversely, water table depth is often shallower in humid plains, which are often discharge zones 109 
of regional groundwater flow with high permeability geology that supports extensive groundwater-110 
surface water interactions and wetland formation. 111 
GDEs provide social and ecological functions across a range of landscapes, climates, and 112 
area densities. For instance, GDEs that are extensive across the landscape (e.g., large wetland 113 
complexes such as the Napahai Wetland in Figure 1b) perform functions such as regulating land 114 
surface heat fluxes, 45 supporting peat accumulation and anaerobic storage of soil organic 115 
carbon,46 and providing a continuous habitat for biota 47 such as species adapted for constant 116 
temperature. Conversely, GDEs that are sparsely located within a landscape perform functions 117 
such as buffering microclimates, acting as ecological habitat for specialist fauna and 118 
phreatophytes,48,49 and as refuge for pastoralists.50 These occur across a range of aridities, from 119 
hyper-arid oases (e.g., Blanche Cup Mound Springs, Australia; Figure 1b) and arid phreatophyte 120 
patches ( e.g., the Populus euphratica forests of the Upper Tarim River, China ; Figure 1b ) to 121 
seasonally dry Mediterranean landscapes where GDEs such as coastal woodlands (e.g., Quercus 122 
agrifolia in Marin County, USA; Figure 1b) support biodiversity hotspots. GDEs in arid climates 123 
are typically perceived as the stereotypical form and imagery used to depict GDEs.18 These GDEs 124 
are often named places, sacred sites, or sites of cultural and spiritual significanc e, such as the 125 
role of Mound Springs in the Dreaming stories of Indigenous Australians,51 and this visual identity 126 
and cultural significance aid in motivating the protection of these ecosystems, independent of their 127 
limited spatial extent. 128

A dynamic, process-based understanding of global GDEs under multiple pressures 129 
While existing studies provide benchmark estimates regarding the contemporary extent and 130 
composition of global GDEs, they are difficult to appraise without the context of a historical 131 
baseline. A major limitation of current global GDE studies is the omission of temporal dynamics. 132 
Multi-century global wetland extent reconstructions52 and estimates of potential natural vegetation 133 
in the absence of human activities53 offer useful reference initiatives to build on, yet there remain 134 
no global time series that cover the full range of GDE types. Developing such temporally resolved 135 
data is a necessary step to generate causal theories on GDE formation in landscape processes 136 
and to understand GDE responses under pressures from global change and human activity. 137 
Presently, process-based global hydrological models are being developed to represent 138 
surface expressions of groundwater 54 and groundwater-surface water interactions .55 However, 139 
using existing hydrological tools to model GDEs remains a difficult task. Groundwater models 140 
typically represent vegetation statically and are unable to represent two-way adaptive coupling, 141 
such as between rooting depths of plants and water table fluctuations.56 Dynamic global 142 
vegetation models represent some adaptive vegetation processes but poorly resolve groundwater 143 
dynamics. How to best approach embedding ecological knowledge of GDEs into global 144 
frameworks to support reliable models is an important priority for the discipline. Given that 145 
ecological thresholds for GDEs are specific to regional climate, flora, and habitat types, global 146 
GDE modelling efforts could consider aggregating region -specific models, such as has been 147 
argued as an opportunity for the broader global groundwater modelling community.57 148 
 Robust global GDE science will rest on the adoption of research practices which recognise 149 
that groundwater-ecosystem interactions occur within complex, open systems that exhibit path 150 
dependence, emergent behaviour, and potentially multiple stable system states. 13,58 Thus, 151 
opportunities for global GDE science include understanding how GDEs interconnect with Earth 152 
system processes such as atmospheric moisture recycling;59 mapping and tracking the extent and 153 
condition of GDEs worldwide; and identifying how changes in GDE conditions propagate to impact 154 
global social-ecological systems through altered functions and services such as climate buffering, 155 
nutrient retention, and pastoral refuge. Further, systematic global perspective s enable 156 
comparison of the severity of change across regions and can help identify priority areas for more 157 
localised investigation. 158 
Preliminary global studies have considered the risks of groundwater depletion 34,35 and 159 
deficiencies in area-based conservation measures.33,35 These evaluations show that over half of 160 
dryland GDEs are facing regional declines in groundwater storage , which can be attributed to 161 
both human and climate drivers ,35 and similarly that half of tropical GDEs, a third of arid GDEs, 162 
and a fifth of temperate GDEs are located in regions with at least moderate pumping -induced 163 
groundwater stress.34 However, depletion is only one of many pressures facing GDEs worldwide, 164 
which include groundwater warming, drought, land use change, groundwater pollution,60 and their 165 
combined impacts. Figure 2a-b highlights the occurrence of three primary pressures for which 166 
data are available: regional groundwater depletion ,61 land conversion pressure ,62 and 167 
groundwater warming under climate change .63 These pressures pose distinct risks to GDE s, 168 
which are summarised further below. 169

Groundwater depletion , which results from both natural variability and human -caused 189 
climate change and pumping,69 has extensively documented GDE impacts across scales. These 190 
include: reducing perennial stream networks 70, springs71, and their flow ; transgressing 191 
environmental flow needs;72 driving transitions in community composition and tree health once 192 
drawdown thresholds are surpassed ;56,73,74 and degrading and eliminating75 subsurface habitat 193 
through weakened exchange with surface water, which reduces food and oxygen for groundwater 194 
biota.76 During groundwater depletion, GDEs may remain relatively stable in extent and 195 
community composition until a threshold is crossed, such as a maximum rooting depth, a rate of 196 
change exceeding root zone adaptation rates, or a stream stage resulting in a change from a 197 
gaining to losing stream. Once crossed, groundwater -dependent vegetation may die off, soil 198 
desiccation may alter soil texture and organic matter and lead to compaction and subsidence, 199 
and invasive species may alter community composition. 19 Recovery processes are likely path 200 
dependent where water table recovery occurs on faster timescales than ecosystems can re -201 
establish their structure, composition, and function, particularly if groundwater depletion resulted 202 
in further changes in soil structure or hydraulic conductivity. In some cases, such as land 203 
subsidence resulting from irreversible aquifer compaction, ecological recovery may be impossible 204 
even if groundwater levels recover (Figure 2c). 205 
Land use change, which includes threats such as forestry and the drainage of peatlands, 206 
directly results in the loss and conversion of GDEs to other land cover types and carries further 207 
impacts through altered groundwater recharge regimes, 77 nutrient and contaminant loading, 208 
habitat fragmentation, and soil compaction. In the case of land cover change where GDEs were 209 
present, this represents a direct loss of GDE area. How this aggregates regionally is difficult to 210 
anticipate, as many drivers and response thresholds act asynchronously across large areas, and 211 
thus individual thresholds may not be discernible in aggregate regional responses. Legacy effects 212 
of landscape conversion such as entrenched invasive species and altered recharge pathways 213 
may sustain a degraded GDE condition after land use pressures are reduced. 214 
The evidence base of g roundwater warming impacts on GDEs is small, but initial work 215 
shows that faunal diversity decreases with warming temperatures78 as environments exceed the 216 
thermal tolerance of groundwater biota. Studies have revealed non -linear thermal tolerance 217 
mortality frequencies in groundwater crustaceans 79 and potential regional extinction of cold -218 
temperature specialist macroinvertebrates with warming groundwater temperatures.80 We do not 219 
hypothesise recovery curves for groundwater warming as reversal of temperature increases is 220 
unlikely over human timescales. Warmer groundwater will also likely impact riverine ecosystems 221 
sustained by groundwater discharge 81 by increasing microbial metabolic rates and altering 222 
nutrient cycling,82 and by eroding cool-water refugia.83 223

Incorporating GDEs can enhance global-scale science and policy agendas 224 
GDE science and policy are inherently interdisciplinary as they require the integrated 225 
consideration of geological, hydrological, biological, ecological, and social systems in diverse 226 
environments. A global GDE perspective can therefore offer new and needed contributions to 227 
several existing priority agendas in science and policy . Yet, GDEs remain largely absent from 228 
these agendas despite their potential to make each more robust. We highlight a subset of these 229 
opportunities below through prima facie comparisons of global datasets ( Figure 3 ), yet these 230 
comparisons do not substitute for rigorous analyses that we leave for future work. 231 
GDEs can further enhance our understanding of groundwater-climate interactions (Figure 232 
3a). Flux partitioning is a fundamental dynamic of the global water cycle ,84 and groundwater-233 
climate interactions are a core Earth system process .85 Globally, the mode of these interactions 234 
has been analytically modelled,86 and process-based models estimate these interactions based 235 
on root water uptake modules. 27 However, a direct comparison of terrestrial and aquatic GDE 236 
locations with analytically modelled groundwater -climate interactions reveal s many contiguous 237 
regions with extensive GDEs in areas estimated to have limited groundwater evapotranspiration 238 
potential.86 These regions (red areas in Figure 3a) may be starting points to investigate whether 239 
groundwater evapotranspiration is being under-accounted due to neglected consideration of GDE 240 
processes, or equally starting points to refine frameworks to assess groundwater -climate 241 
interactions through the incorporation of ecohydrogeological variables. 242 
GDEs can help enhance groundwater representation in Earth system change and 243 
resilience frameworks (Figure 3b) . Groundwater has long been a challenging Earth system 244 
element to include in Earth system resilience initiatives, such as the Planetary Boundaries87 and 245 
the safe and just Earth system boundaries .88 These challenges arise as groundwater is a 246 
fundamentally local resource89 with minimal mixing and connectivity across continental to global 247 
scales in comparison to other Earth system elements. In these frameworks, a simple accounting 248 
of groundwater use is insufficient to represent functional impacts on hydroclimatic and 249 
hydroecological regulation, and on nutrient transport .85 Misrepresenting groundwater dynamics 250 
may lead to counterproductive policy recommendations that are inconsistent with Earth system 251 
resilience, sustainability, and justice goals .90 Rather than aggregating groundwater stores or 252 
consumption fluxes, incorporating GDE condition as a response variable might offer a pragmatic 253 
way to better represent groundwater's hydroecological and hydroclimatic functions in these 254 
frameworks. For instance, the proportion of present-day GDE areas facing meteorological drought 255 
now exceeds a range of natural variability , characterised here for illustrative purposes using a 256 
1970–2000 reference period (Figure 3b). Yet incorporating GDEs into such frameworks depends 257 
not only on selecting the right control variable, but also on understanding the causal processes 258 
linking environmental controls to GDE condition, which, as argued above, remains poorly 259 
understood at the global scale. 260

inside protected areas are vulnerable to human impacts occurring outside of protection 263 
boundaries from exposure through lateral groundwater flow .33,35 Many ecosystems receive 264 
protections from dedicated international policy, such as through conventions or international 265 
assembly resolutions , some of which overlap with GDE types (the Ramsar Convention on 266 
Wetlands or the United Nations Environmental Assembly Resolution 4/16 on peatlands ). Yet, 267

GDE conservation can support global carbon policy (Figure 3d). There are many regions 276 
worldwide with high levels of soil organic carbon and extensive GDE presence (Figure 3d), 277 
including the Amazonian headwaters, coastal tropical western Africa, island southeast Asia, the 278 
Baltics, Siberia, and central boreal Canada. In many of these regions , the carbon is held in 279 
waterlogged organic soils sustained by groundwater levels. GDE conservation and management 280 
in these areas not only supports their hydrological, ecological, and social functions but doubles 281 
as a measure to prevent soil organic carbon mineralisation. Drained peatlands alone contribute 282 
5% of global anthropogenic carbon emissions from only 0.4% of land area.95 It may prove strategic 283 
to embed GDE protection within existing carbon policy initiatives since peatlands and wetlands (a 284 
subset of which are GDEs) are already recognised in international carbon accounting 285 
frameworks.96 Making this conceptual link between carbon-rich ecosystems and GDEs may 286 
generate broader awareness about the value of conserving and managing GDEs while 287 
simultaneously encouraging the integrated, landscape -scale considerations inherent to GDE 288 
management.94 289 
Given the widespread omission of GDEs from global policy agendas, advancing research 290 
questions tailored to connect GDE insights to existing policy priorities may prove an effective 291 
strategy for their broader recognition, protection, and inclusion in these agendas. Several open 292 
questions remain unexplored in the global GDE literature , which we list in Table 1 to stimulate 293 
their further consideration and uptake by the broader research community . Many of these 294 
directions may equally be pursued at finer scales, and aggregating regional assessments into 295 
global databases will likely prove useful given the heterogeneity of GDEs across diverse biomes. 296 
Yet, closing knowledge gaps at the global scale is necessary to more fully quantify GDE 297 
interactions with the Earth system, to incorporate GDEs into Earth system change frameworks 298 
and global-scale policy, and because certain phenomena, such as teleconnections and cross -299 
scale interactions, require an understanding of global processes to fully resolve. 300
