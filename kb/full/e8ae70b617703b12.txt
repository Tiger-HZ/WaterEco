Abstract
Protonic ceramic electrochemical cells (PCECs) are promising platforms for efficient energy con-
version, hydrogen production, and high-value chemical synthesis at intermediate temperatures.
However, large-scale deployment of PCECs remains constrained by the poor sinterability, high
fabrication cost, and long processing time associated with state-of-the-art proton-conducting
ceramic (PCC) electrolytes. Here, we report a time-efficient, ball-milling-free solid-state reaction
approach using nano-sized precursor particles (n-SSR) to synthesize Ba (Zr,Ce,Y ,Yb)O3−δ proton-
conducting electrolytes with enhanced manufacturability and performance. Compared with the
conventional solid-state reaction route using micro-sized precursors (µ-SSR), the n-SSR process
largely shortens powder fabrication time by eliminating ball-milling process while producing fine
electrolyte particles (200–600 nm) with superior sintering activity. As a result, PCECs fabricated
with n-SSR electrolytes exhibit lower densification temperature and markedly improved electro-
chemical performance, delivering a 32% higher peak power density in fuel cell mode and a 40%
higher electrolysis current density at 600 ◦C, primarily due to reduced ohmic resistance. Long-
term stability exceeding 500 h without measurable degradation is demonstrated. Importantly,
a techno-economic analysis reveals that the n-SSR process reduces total electrolyte fabrication
costs by 25%–29% by minimizing comminution, calcination time, and processing complexity.
Large-area (5 cm × 5 cm) single-cell fabrication further validates the scalability of this approach.
These results establish n-SSR as a practical and generalizable manufacturing strategy for high-
performance PCCs, addressing a key bottleneck for the large-scale deployment of protonic ceramic
energy technologies.
1.Introduction
Protonic ceramic electrochemical cells (PCECs) have emerged as a versatile class of energy-conversion
devices capable of operating efficiently at intermediate temperatures (400 ◦C–600 ◦C), offering lower
operating temperatures required by conventional oxide-ion-conducting solid oxide cells, which typically
operate above 700 ◦C. By leveraging fast proton transport in ceramic electrolytes, PCECs enable a wide
range of energy-conversion and chemical-processing applications [ 1, 2]. In fuel cell and water electrol-
ysis modes, PCECs can efficiently produce and utilize pure H 2, which can subsequently be converted
back to electricity in fuel cell mode [ 3–9]. As membrane reactors, they enable the synthesis of a wide

range of chemicals, including alkane activation, CO 2 conversion, production of light olefins, ammo-
nia, and aromatics [ 10–17]. Beyond energy and chemical manufacturing, PCECs can also be integrated
into the fusion energy fuel cycle operations, where the hydrogen isotopes deuterium (D) and tritium (T)
need to be processed efficiently at high temperatures, including tritium recovery, recycling, and isotope
separations [ 18–20].
The key to PCEC’s high performance and efficiency lies in their proton-conducting ceramic (PCC)
electrolyte [ 21–23]. State-of-the-art PCC electrolytes are Ba (Zr, Ce)O 3-based perovskite oxides,
such as BaZr 0.1Ce0.7Y0.1Yb0.1O3−δ (BZCYYb1711), BaZr 0.4Ce0.4Y0.1Yb0.1O3−δ (BZCYYb4411), and
BaZr0.8Y0.2O3−δ (BZY) [ 8, 24–28]. A major challenge limiting the performance and large-scale manu-
facturing of PCECs is the poor sinterability of Ba (Zr, Ce)O 3-based electrolytes, which usually require
relatively high sintering temperatures of >1450 ◦C for BZCYYb and >1500 ◦C for BZY to achieve full
densification [ 29, 30]. In the fabrication of hydrogen electrode supported half-cell (e.g. NiO + BZCYYb
| BZCYYb), the high sintering temperature leads to NiO particle growth and Ba evaporation [ 31]. The
growth of NiO particles significantly hinders the gas transport and reduces the number of triple phase
boundaries in hydrogen electrode, leading to higher polarization resistance. The volatilization of Ba from
Ba (Zr, Ce)O 3-based electrolytes disrupts the stoichiometry and cause the segregation of Y 2O3/Yb2O3,
which consequently diminish the electrolyte’s protonic conductivity and affect its chemical stability [ 31].
Approaches to improve sinterability and lower sintering temperature of Ba (Zr, Ce)O 3-based electrolytes
powders include using sintering aids (e.g. NiO, ZnO, and CuO) and modifying electrolyte powder fab-
rication processes to improve the particle surface energies [ 32]. The introduction of sintering aids has
been shown to impact conductivities and transference numbers due to the generation of low melting
point secondary phase impurity. For example, the introduction of Ni in BZY leads to the formation of
BaY2NiO5 transient liquid phase, which not only decreases the bulk conductivity of BZY but also can
be aggregated at the grain boundaries, increasing grain boundary resistance and negatively affecting the
mechanical strength and fracture toughness [ 33, 34]. T o avoid compromising cell performance and sta-
bility, producing finer powders provides an attractive alternative due to their larger specific surface area
and reduced sintering energy activation, which allows a lower sintering temperature for densification
without introducing secondary phases [ 35, 36]. Additionally, the improved sinterability promotes grain
growth and can significantly improve electrolyte conductivity because of the reduced grain boundary
resistance at the same sintering temperature [ 24, 37, 38].
Fabricating small and homogeneous particle size electrolyte powder ( <1 µm) can be accomplished
using common synthesis methods, such as the wet-chemistry method (e.g. sol–gel, combustion method)
or the solid-state reaction method involving high-energy ball milling, but there are challenges associ-
ated with applying these methods. The wet-chemistry method is a suitable approach to synthesize pure
and fine electrolyte powders [ 39, 40]. However, it involves the use of costly metal nitrate precursors, par-
ticularly rare earth element nitrates, making it economically impractical for large-scale production [ 30,
41]. Thus, the wet-chemistry method is primarily suitable for laboratory-scale research and development
rather than large-scale applications. The high-energy ball milling solid-state reaction method offers a
synthetic method with significantly reduced production costs by using cheaper metal oxides/carbonates
as precursors, which is feasible for Ba (Zr, Ce)O 3-based electrolytes powder fabrication on an industrial-
scale. Given that solid-state reaction rates are highly dependent on particle sizes and their interactions, it
typically requires a high-energy ball milling step to reduce precursor powder particle sizes [ 42]. However,
the particle size reduction by ball-milling typically remains within the micron size range, making it dif-
ficult and time-consuming to achieve submicron size. Using hard material milling jars, like alumina, zir-
conia, agate, and stainless-steel, is more effective, but impurities will be introduced into electrolyte due
to the abrasion between the hard milling media (usually yttrium stabilized zirconia milling balls) and
the milling jars from high-speed collisions. T o avoid impurities, soft-walled polymer milling jars are cho-
sen as an alternative [ 43]. However, the ball milling process with polymer milling jars is time-consuming
(usually >24 h) to achieve an effective particle size reduction because of the insufficient dynamic colli-
sion between the milling media and the milling jar’s soft walls. For example, the synthesis of BZCYYb
electrolytes using micro-sized zirconia powder as a precursor requires >48 h of ball milling to reduce
particle size, including >24 h for grinding precursors and an additional >24 h for grinding the reacted
electrolyte powder. Thus, addressing the challenges of scale-up feasibility, quality control, and cost con-
trol, including balancing the time-consuming and material cost, is crucial in finer powder fabrication.
In this work, we report a modified solid-state reaction strategy utilizing nano-sized precursor par-
ticles (n-SSR) to address these manufacturing challenges. By eliminating high-energy ball milling and
improving precursor mixing at the nanoscale, the n-SSR approach significantly reduces processing time
while yielding proton-conducting electrolyte powders with smaller particle size and superior sinterability.

2.Experimental
2.1.Electrolytepowderssynthesis
2.1.1. µ-SSR: traditional ball-milling solid-state reaction method with micro-sized precursor particles
µ-BZCYYb was synthesized following the flowchart shown in figure 1(a). The weighing of precursors for
∼200 g BZCYYb electrolyte powder fabrication is shown in table S1. First, 500 ml ethanol was added to
the micro-sized precursors including BaCO 3 (Alfa Aesar, 99.8%, ∼1 µm), CeO 2 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%,
∼5 µm), ZrO 2 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99%, ∼5 µm), Y 2O3 (Alfa Aesar, 99.9%, ∼10 µm), Yb 2O3 (Alfa Aesar,
99.9%, ∼10 µm) with the resulting mixture transferred into a polyurethane milling jar with 10 mm zir-
conia milling-media. The ball-milling process was carried out for 24 h at 300 rpm (performed in inter-
vals with 25 min milling and 5 min rest). Next, the milled precursor mixture was transferred into an
open dish, then placed on a hot plate at 100 ◦C for ∼6 h to evaporate the ethanol. The dried precursors
were pressed into pellets (around 10 MPa) and fired in a furnace at 1100 ◦C (ramping rate: 3 oC min −1)
for 10 h (in total of ∼24 h for the firing procedure). Finally, the pellets were ball-milled and dried into
powders with a median particle diameter (D50) less than 1 µm. Additional calcination temperatures of
1200 ◦C and 1300 ◦C were used for XRD analysis.
2.1.2. N-SRR: ball-milling-free solid-state reaction method with n-SSR
The electrolyte powders n-BZCYYb1711 and n-BZCYYb4411 using n-SSR were synthesized following the
flowchart shown in figure 1(b). The weighing of precursors for the fabrication of ∼200 g n-BZCYYb
electrolyte powder is shown in table S1. First, micro-sized BaCO 3 (Alfa Aesar, 99.8%, ∼1 µm) with
stoichiometric amounts of nano-sized ZrO 2 (US Research Nanomaterials, 99 +%, ∼40 nm), CeO 2 (US
Research Nanomaterials, 99.97%, ∼50 nm), Y 2O3 (US Research Nanomaterials, 99.99%, 30–45 nm), and
Yb2O3 (Nanostructured & Amorphous Materials, 99.9%, 10–80 nm) were weighted and then transferred
into a glass beaker filled with 500 ml ethanol. After mixing by sonicating along with stirring for 1 h, the
beaker was heated on a hot plate for ∼6 h at 100 ◦C to evaporate the ethanol. The dried powder was
pressed into pellets at ∼10 MPa and fired in a furnace at 1100 ◦C for 10 h, with a total firing proce-
dure time of ∼24 h including heating and cooling (ramping rate: 3 ◦C min −1). After cooling to room
temperature, the electrolyte pellet was ground into powders for analysis and use. Additional calcination
temperatures of 1200 ◦C and 1300 ◦C were used for XRD analysis.
2.2.Oxygenelectrodesynthesis
PrNi0.7Co0.3O3−δ (PNC73) oxygen electrode was selected as the oxygen electrode due to its relatively low
thermal and chemical expansion [ 44]. The PNC73 powder was synthesized using a combustion method
following our previous work [ 27]. The corresponding precursor weights for ∼5 g PNC73 fabrication are
shown in table S1. Pr(NO 3)3 · 6H2O (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), Ni(NO 3)2 · 6H2O (98%, Sigma-Aldrich),
and Co(NO 3)2 · 6H2O (98%, Sigma-Aldrich) were dissolved in 200 ml distilled water. Glycine (99%,
Thermo Scientific) and citric acid (99 +%, Thermo Scientific), in a ratio of 2:1, were then added to the
nitrite solution. The solution was heated up to 400 ◦C in air, followed by combustion to form ashes. The
resulting ash was then calcined at 1000 ◦C (ramping rate: 3 ◦C min −1) in air for 4 h to obtain PNC73
powders.
2.3.Cellfabrication
Hydrogen electrode-supported cells with configuration of NiO-BZCYYb1711 | BZCYYb1711 |
PNC73 and NiO-BZCYYb4411 | BZCYYb4411 | PNC73 were fabricated by a tape-casting process fol-
lowing our reported work [ 28, 45, 46], which is detailed described in Supporting Information.

2.5.Electrochemicalperformancemeasurement
The electrochemical performances of the PCECs were collected using an electrochemical workstation
(Solartron 1400) in a homemade testing fixture. Ceramic sealant (Ceramabond 552) was used to seal the
PCECs. Before testing, the hydrogen electrode side was purged with dilute hydrogen ( ∼20%) to reduce
the NiO in the hydrogen electrode. After the NiO was fully reduced to Ni and a stable open circuit volt-
age (OCV) was reached, the gas condition was switched to the different testing mode. In fuel cell mode,
the PNC73 oxygen electrode was exposed to air (40 ml min −1), and the hydrogen electrode was exposed
to H 2 with 3% steam (20 ml min −1). At each testing temperature (500 ◦C, 550 ◦C, and 600 ◦C), before
collecting the results, a stable OCV was observed. In electrolysis mode, the feedstock was pure H 2 for the
hydrogen electrode and humidified air (30% steam) for the steam electrode. Electrochemical impedance
spectroscopy (EIS) was carried out in the fuel cell mode gas condition, with air supplied to the oxygen
electrode and wet hydrogen (3% H 2O) supplied to the hydrogen electrode. Measurements were per-
formed over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz with an AC amplitude of 20 mV at the OCV . The
long-term operation of the PCEC in fuel cell mode was conducted at 600 ◦C under 0.75 V , with air sup-
plied to the oxygen electrode and wet hydrogen (3% H 2O) supplied to the hydrogen electrode.

4.Conclusion
W e have developed a ball-milling-free solid-state reaction process based on n-SSR for the efficient
synthesis of PCC electrolytes. Compared with conventional solid-state routes using micro-sized pre-
cursors, the n-SSR approach significantly reduces fabrication time while producing finer electrolyte
powders with enhanced sintering activity. This improved sinterability enables dense electrolyte mem-
branes to be achieved at lower firing temperatures, mitigating common fabrication challenges such as
barium volatilization and electrode microstructural degradation. PCECs fabricated with n-SSR elec-
trolytes exhibit substantially improved electrochemical performance in both fuel cell and electroly-
sis modes, primarily due to reduced ohmic resistance associated with enhanced electrolyte densifica-
tion and grain growth. Long-term stable operation exceeding 500 h and successful fabrication of large-
area (5 cm × 5 cm) single cells further demonstrate the robustness and scalability of this approach.
Importantly, techno-economic analysis reveals that the n-SSR process reduces total electrolyte fabrication
costs by up to 29% by minimizing comminution, calcination duration, and processing complexity. This
work establishes n-SSR as a practical and generalizable manufacturing strategy for high-performance

PCCs. By addressing critical challenges in cost, processing time, and scalability, the proposed approach
provides a viable pathway toward the large-scale deployment of protonic ceramic electrochemical tech-
nologies for sustainable energy conversion and hydrogen-based systems. Future efforts integrating this
approach with advanced shaping, co-sintering, and roll-to-roll–compatible processing techniques could
further accelerate the scalable deployment of ceramic electrochemical devices in hydrogen, electricity, and
chemical energy systems.
