Abstract 14 
The occurrence of cyanobacteria and microcystin is highly dynamic in natural environments and 15 
poses one of the biggest challenges to water resources management. While a number of drivers 16 
are known to be responsible for the occurrence of cyanobacterial blooms, the drivers of 17 
microcystins production are not adequately known. This study aims to quantify the effects of the 18 
changes in the structures of phytoplankton and cyanobacterial communities on the dynamics of 19 
microcystin production under highly variable nutrient concentration. In our study, nutrient 20 
variability could explain 64% of the variability in microcystin production. When changes in the 21 
fractions of non-cyanobacteria versus cyanobacteria genera were additionally included, 80% of 22 
the variability in microcystin production could be explained; under high nutrient concentrations, 23 
non-cyanobacterial phytoplankton groups were dominant over cyanobacteria and cyanobacteria 24 
produced more toxins. In contrast, changes in the cyanobacterial community structures could 25 
only explain a further 4% of the dynamics of microcystin production. As such, the dominance of 26 
non-cyanobacterial groups appears to be a useful factor to explain microcystin occurrence in 27 
1 
 
 
addition to traditionally used factors such as absolute cyanobacterial cell numbers, especially 28 
when the nutrient regime is taken into account. This information could help to further refine the 29 
risk assessment frameworks which are currently used to manage the risk posed by cyanobacterial 30 
blooms. 31

Introduction 36 
An adequate understanding on the dynamics of microcystin production will lead to a better 37 
management of cyanobacterial blooms and reduce their impact on water resources. While the 38 
occurrence of algal blooms is considered to be a significant threat to water safety and security around 39 
the world, the presence of high level of cyanotoxins, such as microcystins, significantly increase the 40 
risk to the environment and the public. As such, it is critical to understand the key drivers behind the 41 
toxin production during a bloom situation. 42 
The dynamics of microcystin production in a bloom depends on the toxin production on a cellular 43 
level, caused by changes in environmental factors such as nutrients and light (Chorus and Bartram 44 
1999, Van de Waal et al. 2009, Zurawell et al. 2005), and has been shown to be associated with the 45 
changes in the planktonic communities’ structure, in particular phytoplankton populations, including 46 
cyanobacterial as well as non-cyanobacterial species (Holland and Kinnear 2013, Joung et al. 2011, 47 
Pflugmacher 2002, Yang et al. 2014), and with changes in the ratio of toxic to non-toxic genotypes 48 
(Janse et al. 2005, Kardinaal et al. 2007). To date, many causation factors leading to changes in the 49 
community structure of phytoplankton populations and the development of cyanobacterial blooms 50 
have been proposed and debated (Dadheech et al. 2014, Macías et al. 2008, O'Neil et al. 2012, Paerl 51 
and Otten 2013). 52 
2 
 
 
Allelopathy has been suggested as a key mechanism in determining the community structure and the 53 
dynamics of phytoplankton populations (Holland and Kinnear 2013, Macías et al. 2008). The theory of 54 
allelopathy suggests that the ability of certain phytoplankton groups to dominate the community may 55 
be enhanced by the production of toxic or non-toxic compounds (Camacho 2008, Graneli and Hansen 56 
2006, Li and Li 2011, O'Neil et al. 2012). This theory has been used in an attempt to explain the 57 
ecological role of microcystin production in cyanobacteria; however, this has not been extensively 58 
supported by experimental data (Babica et al. 2006, Berry et al. 2008, Jang et al. 2006). As such, 59 
cyanobacteria species could increase their competitive advantage through the production of 60 
microcystin (Huisman and Hulot 2005), especially under certain conditions of nutrient limitation 61 
(Graneli et al. 2008). Phytoplankton exposure to microcystin has been suggested to be associated with 62 
growth inhibition and deterioration of photosynthesis and enzymatic activities in some non-63 
cyanobacteria species (Babica et al. 2006, O'Neil et al. 2012). Consequently, it can be hypothesized 64 
that the dynamics of microcystin production is related to the inter-species interaction between 65 
cyanobacteria and other phytoplankton species, interaction within cyanobacterial communities, as well 66 
as the direct effects of environmental factors on phytoplankton. While the concept of allelopathy is 67 
hard to prove in situ, as the effect of the chemical on the receiving organism is alleviated by the effects 68 
of other environmental factors, an increase in the production of toxins by cyanobacteria in the presence 69 
of a larger fraction of non-cyanobacterial genera might be an indirect indicator for allelopathy. 70 
Many studies have been focusing on the effect of nutrients on microcystin production (Chorus and 71 
Bartram 1999, Orihel et al. 2012, Zurawell et al. 2005); while a limited number of studies have also 72 
investigated the combined effects of phytoplankton community structure and cyanobacterial species 73 
composition on microcystin production (Engström-Öst et al. 2011, Kardinaal et al. 2007, Millie et al. 74 
2009), none has studied this in relation to changes in nutrient levels. This information is essential and 75 
would enable managers to assess the potential toxicity of cyanobacterial blooms from the structure of 76 
the phytoplankton community and cyanobacterial composition under different environmental 77 
conditions. The present study aims to quantify the effects of the changes in the structure of 78 
3 
 
 
phytoplankton communities, including cyanobacterial species composition, on the dynamics of 79 
microcystin production under highly variable nutrient regimes. It is anticipated that nutrient dynamics, 80 
specifically phosphorus, nitrogen and iron, alone are not good predictors of cyanotoxin production and 81 
that key changes in the phytoplankton communities, especially a dominance of non-toxin producing 82 
classes, such as cholorophytes or diatoms, may be responsible for triggering an allopathetic response 83 
leading to a higher toxin production. 84

Materials and Methods 86 
Study area 87 
This study was carried out in Jackadder Lake (31°54′30″S, 115°47′36″E), Bibra Lake (32°5′25″S, 88 
115°49′16″E) and Yangebup Lake (32°6′56″S, 115°49′33″E) located on the Swan Coastal Plain, 89 
Western Australia. These lakes are shallow with mean depth of 2.1 m, 1.1 m, and 2.5 m in Jackadder 90 
Lake, Bibra Lake and Yangebup Lake, respectively. Jackadder Lake is surrounded by 6.6 ha of 91 
parkland (Arnold 1990, Woodward 2008), while Bibra Lake is surrounded by urban areas and a golf 92 
course. Yangebup Lake is surrounded by residential, agriculture and industrial areas. Jackadder Lake 93 
and Yangebup Lake are permanent lakes, while Bibra Lake is subject to seasonal drying. Prolonged 94 
stable thermal stratification is usually prevented in these lakes during summer due to continuous or 95 
intermittent wind mixing that creates a homogeneous environment throughout the water column 96 
(Arnold and Oldham 1997, Davis et al. 1993). These lakes were selected due to levels of 97 
cyanobacterial biomass and microcystin concentration reported in earlier extensive sampling 98 
(Reichwaldt et al. 2013, Sinang et al. 2013). These lakes represent systems with low, medium and high 99 
cyanobacterial biomass and microcystin concentration. Mean cyanobacterial biomass in an earlier 100 
study was reported as 28 µg chl-a L-1 in Jackadder Lake, 108 µg chl-a L-1 in Bibra Lake, and 80 µg 101 
chl-a L-1 in Yangebup Lake. Mean cellular microcystin concentrations (mg g-1 cyanobacterial dry 102 
mass) was 4.8 mg g-1 in Jackadder Lake, 35 mg g-1 in Bibra Lake and 1.7 mg g-1 in Yangebup Lake 103 
(Sinang et al. 2013). 104

Sampling 106 
The study lakes were sampled twice a month between January and March 2010. Samples were 107 
collected from three points on each lake on every sampling occasion. Samples were taken from waist-108 
deep water at the same point on every sampling occasion. Bibra Lake dried up in late February; 109 
therefore no samples were taken from this lake in March. 110 
Grab water samples were taken from 15 cm below the surface and stored immediately in a cool 111 
container. Parameters analysed from these samples were total phosphorus (TP), total dissolved 112 
phosphorus (TDP), total iron (TFe), total dissolved iron (TDFe), total nitrogen (TN), total dissolved 113 
nitrogen (TDN), ammonium (NH4+), cyanobacterial biomass, total phytoplankton biomass, 114 
intracellular and extracellular microcystin fractions, and cellular microcystin concentration. Samples 115 
for dissolved nutrients analyses were pre-filtered with a 0.45µm syringe filter (Acrodisc, HT Tuffryn) 116 
before storing at -20°C. Water samples for phytoplankton enumeration were preserved with Lugol’s 117 
iodine (1 % final concentration). 118 
 119 
Nutrient analyses and phytoplankton biomass 120 
TP and TDP concentrations were analyzed using the ascorbic acid method, while TFe and TDFe 121 
concentrations were analyzed with the Phenanthroline method, according to standard methods (APHA 122 
1998). TN, TDN, and NH4+ were analyzed at the South Coast Nutrients Analysis Laboratory (Albany, 123 
Western Australia) with the standard colorimetric methods on a segmented flow auto-analyser 124 
(Alpkem, Wilsonville, OR, USA). Cyanobacterial and total phytoplankton chlorophyll-a were 125 
measured with a benchtop version of a FluoroProbe (bbe Moldaenke, Germany), which is capable of 126 
identifying up to four spectral algal groups based on their specific excitation spectrum of chlorophyll 127 
fluorescence (Beutler et al. 2002, Ghadouani and Smith 2005). The biomass of each group is given in 128 
equivalent µg chl-a L-1. Fluoroprobe measurements of total chlorophyll-a gave a good correlation 129 
5 
 
 
against samples extracted according to standard methods (APHA 1998) (linear regression analysis: R2 130 
= 0.94, N = 32, P < 0.05) (Sinang, S.C., unpublished data). 131 
Water samples were filtered through pre-combusted and pre-weighed 47mm GF/C filter papers to 132 
collect the phytoplankton biomass and to separate intracellular from the dissolved microcystin fraction. 133 
Filter papers containing particulate organic matter and phytoplankton biomass were dried at 60°C and 134 
re-weighed after 24 hours to obtain the dry biomass (Chorus and Bartram 1999). These filter papers 135 
were then moistened with Milli-Q water and stored at -20°C until intracellular microcystin extraction. 136 
As we were interested in the actual toxic potential of cyanobacteria, cyanobacterial dry mass was 137 
calculated from the total dry mass by adjusting it to the percentage of cyanobacterial biomass 138 
measured with the FluoroProbe. 139 
Cyanobacteria were identified and enumerated to the taxonomic level of genera with 10-50 mL water 140 
samples fixed in Lugol’s solution using an inverse microscope according to Utermöhl (Utermöhl 141 
1958). A minimum of 200 cells or colonies of the most abundant cyanobacteria were counted, and the 142 
size of 20 individual cells or colonies of each occurring genera was measured by using an inverse 143 
microscope. Cell densities were converted to biovolume by means of geometric figures that best 144 
approximated the shape of individual taxa (Hillebrand et al. 1999). The biovolume of each 145 
cyanobacterial genera was then used to calculate the biovolume fraction of the respective 146 
cyanobacterial genera in the total cyanobacterial community. 147 
 148 
Microcystin extraction and quantification 149 
Filters were freeze-thawed twice to break the cells prior to methanol extraction (Lawton et al. 1994). 150 
Each filter was placed into a separate centrifuge tube and 5mL 75 % methanol-water (v/v) was added. 151 
Filters were sonicated on ice for 25 minutes, followed by gentle shaking for another 25 minutes. The 152 
extracts were then centrifuged at 3750 rpm for 10 minutes at room temperature. Extracts were 153 
carefully transferred into conical flasks, and two more extractions were done per filter. All three 154 
extracts (75 % methanol) were pooled and diluted with Milli-Q to 20 % methanol. Intracellular 155 
6 
 
 
microcystin extracts and the pre-filtered water containing dissolved microcystin were subjected to 156 
solid phase extraction (SPE) (Waters Oasis HLB) as a clean-up step and for concentrating the 157 
microcystins. Sample loading speed to the SPE was adjusted to <10 mL per minute. After the samples 158 
had passed through, each cartridge was rinsed with 10 mL 10, 20 and 30 % methanol-water (v/v), 159 
before microcystin was eluted with 100 % methanol + 0.1 % trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) into 6mL glass 160 
vials and samples were evaporated to dryness under a mild nitrogen stream in a 40°C water bath. 161 
Finally, samples were re-dissolved in 30 % acetonitrile and analysed with HPLC-PDA systems 162 
(Waters, Alliance 2695). 163 
HPLC analysis was carried out by using the Alliance 2695 (Waters, Australia) with a PDA detector 164 
(1.2nm resolution) and an Atlantis T3 3µm column (4.6 x 150mm i.d). Mobile phases used were 165 
acetonitrile + 0.05 % v/v trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and Milli-Q water + 0.05 % v/v TFA. Microcystin 166 
peak separation was achieved by using a linear gradient a flow rate of 0.6 mL min-1 (ISO 2005). 167 
Column temperature was maintained at 37.5 ± 2.5 °C. The limit of detection per microcystin peak was 168 
1.12ng. Microcystin was identified by PDA spectra at 238nm. Commercially available microcystin-LR 169 
standard (Sapphire Bioscience, Australia; purity ≥ 95 %) was used to quantify microcystin 170 
concentrations in all samples. 171

Statistical analyses 173 
Redundancy analysis (RDA) (Legendre and Legendre 1998) was carried out in R language to quantify 174 
the effects of nutrients, and the structure of phytoplankton and cyanobacterial community on 175 
microcystin production. The RDA analysis was carried out using the combined data from three lakes 176 
as we emphasize on finding the general trend rather than individual lake pattern. Explanatory variables 177 
used in the RDA analysis were nutrient concentrations in the water column, the fraction of different 178 
phytoplankton groups of total phytoplankton biomass, and the fraction of different cyanobacterial 179 
genera of total cyanobacterial community. Response variables used in the RDA analysis were cellular 180 
microcystin concentration and total microcystin concentration in the water column. The canonical 181 
7 
 
 
relationship was computed with standardized explanatory and response variables and tested for the 182 
level of significant after 999 permutations. In all analyses, results were considered significant at P < 183 
0.05. 184

The phytoplankton composition in Jackadder, Bibra and Yangebup Lakes changed during the study 211 
period (Fig. 2). Phytoplankton composition varied on a temporal basis within lakes and spatially 212 
between lakes. In Jackadder Lake, cyanobacteria were dominant (68%) from January to early 213 
February. In late February, the phytoplankton community shifted to mainly chlorophytes and diatoms, 214 
and cyanobacteria only accounted for 7% of the community. Then in early March, cyanobacteria 215 
started to increase and contributed to 67% of the total phytoplankton biomass by the end of March. In 216 
Bibra Lake, chlorophytes dominated with the biomass up to 66%, while cyanobacteria only contributed 217 
27% to the total phytoplankton biomass in January. Cyanobacteria contributed up to 49% of the total 218 
phytoplankton biomass in early February, and the dominant phytoplankton changed to chlorophytes in 219 
late February. In Yangebup Lake, cyanobacteria were dominant in January and cyanobacteria 220 
contributed up to 67 % to the total phytoplankton biomass. In February, cyanobacterial fraction in the 221 
total phytoplankton biomass decreased from 64 to 17%, and chlorophytes took over the major fraction 222 
of total phytoplankton biomass in late February. In early March, cyanobacteria contributed 48% to the 223 
total phytoplankton biomass in Yangebup Lake, and the fraction decreased to 32% in late March. 224

Correlations between nutrients, phytoplankton and cyanobacterial community structure and 264 
microcystin production 265 
 266 
RDA analysis showed that the dynamics of microcystin production in the studied lakes were 267 
significantly correlated to nutrient concentrations in the water column. Nutrients explained 64% of 268 
variation in cellular microcystin concentration (Fig. 6). Cellular microcystin concentration was closely 269 
correlated to iron concentration, while total microcystin concentration in the water was closely 270 
correlated to phosphorus and nitrogen availability in the systems. Inclusion of cyanobacterial 271 
composition as an explanatory variable in addition to nutrients increased the percentage explained 272 
from 64% to 68% (P <0.05), suggesting that nutrients were more important as a factor influencing the 273 
dynamics of cellular microcystin production than the cyanobacterial composition (data not shown). In 274 
contrast, including the phytoplankton community as an explanatory variable in addition to nutrients, 275 
80% of variability in microcystin production could be explained (P <0.05) (Fig. 7). The combination 276 
of the phytoplankton community and nutrients as explanatory variables increased the ability to predict 277 
the dynamics of microcystin production by 16%, indicating the significance of both variables on 278 
microcystin production. As shown in the RDA biplot (Fig. 7), high microcystin production correlated 279 
to high nutrient concentrations in the presence of a high fraction of chlorophytes and diatoms and a 280 
low fraction of cyanobacteria in the phytoplankton community. In addition, higher cellular and total 281 
microcystin concentrations were associated with lower fractions of cyanobacteria present in the 282 
systems (Fig. 8). There was a significant, but very weak correlation between total cyanobacterial 283 
biomass and total microcystin concentration in the water (R2 = 0.175; P <0.05; y = 0.99 + 0.05*x). 284

Discussion 286 
Changes in water levels during dry months can have strong effects on nutrient dynamics, which 287 
subsequently could affect the structure of phytoplankton communities in lakes (Coops et al. 2003, 288 
Jeppesen et al. 2015). As such, this study has taken into account both nutrient and phytoplankton 289 
dynamics to understand the dynamics of microcystin production. The results of our study suggested 290 
that high microcystin production in the studied lakes was significantly correlated to high nutrient 291 
concentration and low TN:TP. Our results agree with previous studies, which have reported that 292 
increased phosphorus, nitrogen and iron concentrations could enhance microcystin production (Albay 293 
et al. 2005, Jiang et al. 2008, Scott et al. 2014), and that higher microcystin concentrations can be 294 
found at lower TN:TP ratios (Orihel et al. 2012). The observed close positive relationships between 295 
microcystin production with phosphorus and nitrogen are potentially due to the high cyanobacterial 296 
cellular requirement of phosphorus for microcystin biosynthesis and biomass production (Vezie et al. 297 
2002). In addition, a positive correlation between iron and microcystin production may be related to an 298 
earlier suggestion that microcystin production involves enzyme synthetase, in which the enzyme’s 299 
activity is controlled by the amount of iron present (Utkilen and Gjolme 1995). 300 
In addition to the possible direct effect of nutrients on the dynamic of microcystin production, previous 301 
studies have also suggested that nutrients might indirectly regulate microcystin production through 302 
their effects on cyanobacterial abundance and its distribution (Dai et al. 2008, Millie et al. 2009, Wu et 303 
al. 2014). Unlike chlorophytes and diatoms, a high cyanobacterial fraction was pointing in the opposite 304 
direction to high nutrient availability (Fig. 7). This result indicates that high nutrient concentrations in 305 
our systems did not favor the dominance of cyanobacterial genera. Instead, high phosphorus, nitrogen 306 
and iron concentrations in the water column may have triggered the dominance of chlorophytes and 307 
diatoms, due to their ability to grow faster than cyanobacteria (Huisman et al. 2005, Jensen et al. 1994, 308 
Reynolds et al. 2006). 309 
Microcystin production is complex and various environmental factors including nutrients have been 310 
shown to have direct effects on the dynamics of microcystin production (Rinta-Kanto et al. 2009). Our 311 
12 
 
 
study suggests that the dominance of non-cyanobacterial groups is an additional factor to be 312 
considered and that changes in the structure of the phytoplankton community may have a large effect 313 
on microcystin production. Changes in the structure of the phytoplankton community explained up to 314 
16% of the variability in microcystin production. Moreover, cyanobacteria tend to increase microcystin 315 
production in the presence of a high fraction of other phytoplankton groups. This result supports the 316 
theory of allelopathic interactions in cyanobacteria through the higher production of toxin microcystin 317 
with an increasing number of competitors (Holland and Kinnear 2013, Huisman and Hulot 2005, 318 
Rzymski et al. 2014). Earlier studies on allelopathy suggest that cyanobacteria may be capable of react 319 
to the presence of competitors by increasing microcystin production stimulated by the presence of 320 
extracellular products released by competitors such as chlorophytes (Bittencourt-Oliveira et al. 2014, 321 
Kearns and Hunter 2000, Pinheiro et al. 2013). In addition to the effects of changes in the structure of 322 
phytoplankton community, the dynamics of microcystin production could also be related to the 323 
succession of toxic and non-toxic cyanobacterial genotypes (Janse et al. 2005, Kardinaal et al. 2007, 324 
O'Neil et al. 2012), which was not analyzed in this study. 325 
Based on the available cyanobacterial genera composition data, our results suggest that the changes in 326 
the structure of the cyanobacterial community did not affect the dynamics of microcystin production to 327 
a large extent. This result indicates that the succession of Planktothrix spp., Anabaena spp., Nodularia 328 
sp., Anabaenopsis spp., and Microcystis spp. in our lakes may not be related to changes in microcystin 329 
production in response to cyanobacterial competitors, and, as such would not support the theory of 330 
allelopathic interaction between different cyanobacteria genera (Paerl and Otten 2013, Švercel 2013). 331 
Rather, the succession of different cyanobacteria genera may be related to different cyanobacterial 332 
genera benefitting from different concentrations of phosphorus, nitrogen and iron species (Chorus and 333 
Bartram 1999, Huisman and Hulot 2005). Drastic changes to the structure of cyanobacterial 334 
communities are possible even with only slight changes in the cyanobacterial total biomass (Soares et 335 
al. 2009). It was suggested that this could be due to the short doubling times of cyanobacteria in 336 
13 
 
 
addition to different cyanobacterial genera occupying different niches, which develop due to temporal 337 
changes in the water’s physicochemical properties (Reynolds 1998). 338 
As an example, Microcystis spp. are known to be capable to store high amounts of phosphorus and to 339 
use phosphorus very efficiently (Baldia et al. 2007), while Planktothrix spp. has a lower affinity 340 
towards phosphorus (Istvánovics et al. 2002). In addition, Nagai et al. (2007), has suggested that 341 
Planktothrix agardhii requires an iron concentration 30 times higher compared to Microcystis 342 
aeruginosa to achieve the same growth rate. This indicates that cyanobacteria may react differently to 343 
environmental conditions created by other genera, which could lead to drastic changes in the structure 344 
of the cyanobacterial community over short periods of time (Beversdorf 2013). As an example, the 345 
higher iron requirement of Planktothrix spp. might reduce the iron availability in the water column, 346 
and it would thus become favorable for Microcystis spp. in the system. Therefore, the succession of 347 
different cyanobacteria genera is a driven by a complex interaction of environmental conditions, 348 
including the availability of nutrients, and allelopathic interactions through cyanotoxins or other 349 
metabolites (Švercel 2013). 350

Conclusions 352 
Our results indicate that the changes in the structure of the phytoplankton community and changes in 353 
nutrient concentrations are important factors that determine the dynamics of microcystin production. 354 
Under high nutrient concentration, non-cyanobacterial phytoplankton groups were dominant over 355 
cyanobacteria, and cyanobacteria produced more microcystin. Our results might therefore support the 356 
theory of allelopathic interactions between cyanobacteria and non-cyanobacterial competitors. We 357 
conclude that eutrophication could lead to the occurrence of more toxic cyanobacterial blooms directly 358 
through increased nutrient concentrations, and indirectly through changes in the phytoplankton 359 
community leading to increased toxin production within the cyanobacterial cells. 360 
Our results also suggest that changes in cyanobacterial community composition may not be of major 361 
importance for the dynamics of microcystin production. In relation to the microcystin risk assessment 362 
14 
 
 
during toxic cyanobacterial blooms, our results therefore suggest to consider the fraction of total 363 
cyanobacteria in addition to the absolute cyanobacterial biomass, which is currently the basis of 364 
existing WHO alert levels for microcystin risk assessment (Churro et al. 2012). Our conclusion is 365 
drawn based on the quantification of total biomass for each cyanobacterial genera without 366 
differentiating between microcystin producing strains and non-producing strains. The quantification of 367 
toxic and non-toxic cyanobacteria strains would have allowed for a deeper understanding of the 368 
microcystin dynamics and the dominance within the cyanobacteria community and we therefore 369 
suggest to include molecular characterisation of cyanobacterial communities in future studies to get a 370 
clearer picture of the role of strain dynamics on microcystin production, which would help to develop a 371 
more assertive microcystin risk assessment. 372 
Additionally, a direct relationship between the structure of the phytoplankton/cyanobacteria 373 
community and microcystin production is likely to be established. Such a relationship illustrates the 374 
possibility of using the cyanobacterial fraction in the phytoplankton community as an ecological 375 
indicator to infer the level of microcystin production or risk of microcystin contamination in shallow 376 
water bodies. The relationship observed in this study was in agreement with the theory of allelopathic 377 
interaction between cyanobacteria and non-cyanobacteria. As direct microcystin quantification is not 378 
regularly conducted in water quality monitoring, the observation of high nutrient concentrations in the 379 
presence of potentially toxic cyanobacterial species, together with the dominance of non-380 
cyanobacterial phytoplankton groups over cyanobacteria could be used as an indication that toxin 381 
production per cell is high and should prompt direct microcystin quantification. Even so, we propose 382 
further testing of this relationship with a larger number of lakes, including verification for single lakes, 383 
to prove its application for lake management. 384
