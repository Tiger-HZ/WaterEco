Pesticide exposure risks and seasonal variation in honey bee‑collected 
pollen across agricultural landscapes in Latvia

Abstract
Honey bees (Apis mellifera) are widely used as bioindicators of environmental contamination and bee-collected pollen reflects 
pesticide exposure in agricultural landscapes. This study evaluated pesticide residues in honey bee-collected pollen from six 
Latvian apiaries during 2024 and 2025 flowering seasons, and assessed toxicological risk using the Pollen Hazard Quotient 
(PHQ). In total, 15 active substances were detected in 2024 and 19 in 2025. Fungicides were the most frequently detected 
pesticide class in both years, accounting for 51% of detections in 2024 and 54% in 2025, followed by herbicides (38% and 
24%) and insecticides (11% and 22%). The highest concentrations were recorded for glyphosate, fluazifop-P-butyl, boscalid, 
azoxystrobin, acetamiprid, and tebuconazole. Seasonal variation was evident, with higher residue concentrations and PHQ 
values mainly from mid-May to early July, coinciding with crop flowering and pesticide application periods. Median PHQ 
values remained below the threshold (PHQ < 50), however several samples in 2025 exceeded this level, with a maximum 
PHQ of 1173.91 driven by cypermethrin. No consistent descriptive pattern was observed between PHQ values and the 
proportion of agricultural land or nectar producing crops. This suggests that landscape composition alone did not explain 
differences in pesticide exposure among the studied apiaries. The results indicate that pesticide exposure is influenced by 
temporal variation, pesticide toxicity, and local factors such as crop management and foraging behaviour, rather than land-
scape composition alone. Honey bee-collected pollen proved to be a suitable tool for monitoring pesticide exposure. It also 
allowed the identification of periods of increased risk in agricultural environments.
Keywords Apis mellifera · Pesticide residues · Pollinator exposure · Agricultural landscapes · Pollen hazard quotient
Introduction
Global agricultural production increasingly relies on insect 
pollination due to the expansion of pollinator dependent 
crops (Aizen et al. 2019). A similar trend can be observed 
in Latvia, where the cultivated area of oilseed rape has 
remained consistently high in recent years (145.1 thousand 
ha), while the area of legume crops has expanded by 84% 
since 2020 (Official Statistics Portal of Latvia 2026). How -
ever, bee populations continue to decline due to multiple 
environmental stressors associated with agricultural inten-
sification (Winfree 2010; Lima et al. 2022).
Pesticide exposure and limited floral resources are among 
the main stressors affecting managed and wild bee popula-
tions. Mass flowering crops may increase food availability 
for pollinators, but can also increase exposure to pesticide 
residues. This interaction remains insufficiently studied 
(Goulson et al. 2015; Mallick et al. 2023). This is particu -
larly relevant for oilseed rape (Brassica napus), where foliar 
fertilization and pesticide application can affect flower visit-
ing bees, including honey bees (Apis mellifera), bumble bees 
(Bombus spp.), and solitary bee species (Viik 2012).

Honey bees and their products are widely recognised 
as effective bioindicators of environmental contamination, 
including heavy metals and persistent organic pollutants, 
such as pesticides (Bargańska et al. 2016). Bee-collected 
pollen is particularly informative for pesticide exposure 
studies because it reflects material collected by foraging bees 
from flowering plants. It can also be linked to both pesticide 
residues and botanical composition. Compared with wax or 
bee bread, freshly collected pollen provides a more tem-
porally resolved indication of recent exposure. Nectar can 
also indicate pesticide exposure, but it does not provide the 
same direct link with pollen botanical composition. Recent 
studies have shown that pesticide residues are also present 
in the pollen and nectar of wildflowers. This may increase 
pollinator exposure to pesticides, sometimes at concentra-
tions higher than those detected in cultivated crops (Botías 
et al. 2015).
Most pesticides are applied during cultivation of agricul-
tural crops. Bees are primarily exposed to these substances 
near farmland, particularly during crop flowering periods 
(Calatayud-Vernich et al. 2019). In Latvia, the flowering 
period for several major agricultural crops occurs between 
May and June and coincides with intensive pesticide use. 
Previous studies have linked neonicotinoid seed treatments 
in oilseed rape to negative effects on wild bee populations, 
highlighting the importance of assessing pollinator expo-
sure to pesticides (Woodcock et al. 2016). Despite increasing 
attention to pesticide exposure in pollinators, information 
on pesticide contamination in honey bee-collected pollen 
in relation to landscape composition remains limited in the 
Baltic region. This region is particularly relevant because 
the flowering period of major crops, including oilseed rape 
and legumes, overlaps with intensive pesticide use, while 
regional long term monitoring data remain scarce.
This study follows earlier research initiated and pub-
lished in 2024 (Ozols et al. 2024), and extends the dataset to 
include the 2024 and 2025 sampling seasons. By combining 
seasonal pesticide residue data, PHQ-based risk screening, 
and landscape composition analysis, this study provides a 
more detailed assessment of pollinator exposure under Baltic 
agricultural conditions. Our main objective was to further 
assess pesticide contamination in honey bees collected pol-
len, and to evaluate the potential risks to bee health in rela-
tion to surrounding landscape composition. To achieve this 
objective, we focused on four main tasks: (1) identifying 
pesticide residues in honey bee-collected pollen samples; 
(2) examining the seasonal dynamics of residue occurrence; 
(3) screening and evaluating the potential impact of these 
residues on bee health; and (4) investigating the relationship 
between landscape structure, expressed as the proportion of 
agricultural land, and pesticide related risks to bees. Two 
hypotheses were formulated. We hypothesised that PHQ val-
ues would increase with increasing agricultural land cover 
and that pesticide occurrence would peak during the main 
flowering period from May to June.
Materials and methods
Study area and landscape assessment
Six commercial apiaries representing different regions 
of Latvia were selected for this study (Fig.  1), and pollen 
samples were collected from these sites in seasons 2024 
and 2025. Three of the selected apiaries were located in 
Zemgale region, which represents the most intensive 
agricultural production in Latvia: GPS coordinates for 
Jelgava town (56°39′ 46.4″ N, 23°45′ 14.2″ E), Platone 
parish (56°30′ 47.5″ N, 23°41′ 03.8″ E), and Vecauce par -
ish (56°28′ 03.2″ N, 22°53′ 16.4″ E). The remaining three 
apiaries were situated in the Vidzeme, Kurzeme, and Lat-
gale: Lube parish (57°26′23.3″ N, 22°35′33.9″ E), Ledurga 
parish (57°17′ 33.1″ N, 24°49′07.0″ E), and Ambeli parish 
(56°05′19.9″ N, 26°53′47.4″ E).
Landscape composition was assessed within a 3-km 
radius around each apiary. This radius was selected to rep-
resent the main foraging area of honey bees under typical 
agricultural conditions. Although honey bees can forage 
longer distances, including within six- to 8-km radius, forag-
ing activity is generally concentrated closer to the hive when 
floral resources are available (Beekman and Ratnieks 2000). 
Spatial processing and landscape classification were per -
formed using QGIS (version 4.0. “Norrköping”). Landscape 
variables were treated as continuous variables and expressed 
as the proportion (%) of each land use class within the 3-km 
radius around each apiary. Detailed information on the 
biotopes surrounding the study sites is provided in Online 
Resource 1. Agricultural land data were obtained from the 
Rural Support Service of Latvia, forest cover data from the 
State Forest Registry, and urban area estimates from the 
Dynamic World dataset (Brown et al. 2022). Mean values 
for the study years 2024 and 2025 were extracted from the 
dataset, and the total area classified under the “built-up 
area” was calculated. In this study, the category “built-up 
area” was referred to as “urban”, additional data are given in 
Online Resource 2. As pesticide residues were expected to 
occur primarily within agricultural areas, all forested land, 
natural and semi-natural grasslands (originally classified 
under agricultural land), as well as waterbodies and simi -
lar habitats, were grouped into a category “semi-natural”. 
Areas that did not fall within the agricultural, urban, or forest 
classes were also assigned to the “semi-natural” category. 
Consequently, three main landscape classes were defined: 
Semi-natural, Urban and Agriculture. Each site was clas -
sified to a dominant landscape category when a single land 
use class exceeded 50% of the surrounding area. Dominant

categories were used only for descriptive site classification. 
In Jelgava town, semi natural and urban land uses occurred 
in comparable proportions because of the extensive green 
infrastructure, including parks, public green areas and tree 
covered spaces. Nevertheless, the site was classified as urban 
due to the location of the apiary, even though “build up” land 
represented only 44% of the area.
Pollen collection and palynological analysis
Pollen sampling was conducted from 15 May to 20 August 
in 2024 and from 5 May to 10 August in 2025. Depending on 
weather conditions and pollen availability, collections took 
place every second day or at longer intervals, using pollen 
traps installed at the apiaries. Samples affected by rain were 
excluded, and damaged material was removed after each 
collection. The pollen traps were then cleaned with a 70% 
ethanol solution before the next sampling event. At each api-
ary, pollen was collected from two marked hives equipped 
with pollen traps. Two hives per apiary were used to reduce 
colony level variability while maintaining a consistent sam-
pling design and limiting disturbance to the colonies. Pollen 
collected from two hives was pooled into one daily sample. 
A target sample mass of approximately 100 g was collected 
when sufficient pollen was available. This target mass was 
used to standardise sample handling, although the actual 
amount depended on pollen availability during each collec-
tion. Each sample was labelled with the apiary name and 
date of collection. After fieldwork, the samples were placed 
in a cooler container with ice elements for temporary stor -
age and transported to the laboratory. Then, they were stored 
frozen at –18 ℃ until analysis.
In both study years, the sampling season was divided 
into seven two-week periods. In 2024, the sampling periods 
were as follows: 15–28 May (period 1), 29 May–11 June 
(period 2), 12–25 June (period 3), 26 June–9 July (period 
4), 10–23 July (period 5), 24 July–6 August (period 6), and 
7–20 August (period 7). In 2025, the periods were slightly 
adjusted: 5–18 May (period 1), 19 May–1 June (period 2), 
2–15 June (period 3), 16–29 June (period 4), 30 June–13 
July (period 5), 14–27 July (period 6), and 28 July–10 
August (period 7). All daily samples from the same apiary 
and period were combined into one composite two-week 
sample. This resulted in one composite sample per apiary for 
each two-week sampling period. No additional independent 
field replicates were collected within the same apiary and 
period. In total, 41 pollen samples were analysed for 2024—
seven from each apiary, except for period 1 at the Ambeli

The botanical composition of pollen samples was ana-
lysed at the laboratory of the Institute of Plant Protec-
tion Research “Agrihorts”. A modified light microscopy 
approach was applied (Jones and Bryant 1998; Halbritter 
et al. 2018). Pollen identification and counting were car -
ried out using images captured under a light microscope 
at 400× magnification. For each composite sample, three 
replicate palynological analyses were conducted. In each 
replicate, 8–10 images were evaluated, with approximately 
10–40 pollen grains per image. This resulted in more than 
500 identified pollen grains per sample.
Pesticide residue analysis in pollen samples
The chemical analysis of pollen samples was performed by 
Bilacon GmbH, a QS-recognised and DAkkS-accredited 
analytical laboratory (Berlin, Germany). According to the 
laboratory reports, pesticide residues and related compounds 
were analysed using validated combi methods PV-SA-085 
and PV-SA-343 Teil A. Method PV-SA-085 included multi 
residue screening by liquid chromatography tandem mass 
spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), gas chromatography tandem 
mass spectrometry (GC-MS/MS) and GC-NCl. Method 
PV-SA-343 Teil A was applied for the analysis of glyphosate 
and AMPA by LC-MS/MS.
Each pollen sample was analysed under controlled labo-
ratory conditions. The analytical scope for method PV-SA-
085, supplied by laboratory, included the target pesticide 
list together with reporting limits, limits of quantification 
(LOQ), limits of detection (LOD), and the analytical plat-
form used for each compound (Online resource 3). The 
scope document listed 793 active substances and related 
compounds. The reporting limit for most substances was 
0.01 mg kg−1.
The laboratory confirmed that the methods are accred-
ited under ISO/IEC 17025 and are performed according to 
SANTE requirements. Measurement uncertainty (U) was 
reported by the laboratory where applicable. According to 
the laboratory decision rule, expanded measurement uncer-
tainty was calculated using a coverage factor k = 2, corre-
sponding to a confidence level of approximately 95% The 
laboratory also confirmed that recovery values were within 
the 70 to 130% range. Full internal validation reports, 
including compound specific recovery data, matrix effects, 
internal standards, calibration ranges and precision data, 
were not provided to the authors because they were consid-
ered sensitive laboratory information.
According to the laboratory reports, GC-NCl was used 
for selected GC-based determinations. NCl refers to nega-
tive chemical ionisation rather than to the detector itself. 
The exact mass spectrometric detector configuration for 
GC-NCl was not further specified in the reports available 
to the authors.
Pollen Hazard Quotient as an indicator of pollen 
toxicity
The Pollen Hazard Quotient (PHQ) was applied as a screen-
ing indicator of potential pollen toxicity to honey bees (Végh 
et al. 2022; Carlson et al. 2022). PHQ was calculated as the 
ratio between the detected pesticide concentration in pollen 
(ppb; µg kg−1) and the corresponding oral or contact LD50 
for honeybees (Friedle et al. 2021). LD50 values for honey 
bees (Apis spp.) were obtained from the Pesticide Proper -
ties DataBase, using entries based on EU regulatory and 
evaluation data published by the European Commission and 
European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) (European Food 
Safety Authority 2026). Only entries classified as verified 
data used for regulatory purposes were used when available. 
Acute oral and contact LD50 values for honey bees, reported 
as µg bee−1, were applied depending on data availability. The 
 LD50 endpoint applied for each active substance is provided 
in Online Resource 6. The screening approach assumes a 
maximum daily intake of up to 9.5 mg of bee bread per nurse 
bee (Crailsheim et al. 1992). PHQ values were interpreted as 
screening categories of potential risk: values below 50 were 
considered low risk, values between 50 and 500 moderate 
risk, and values above 500 high risk (Stoner and Eitzer 2013; 
Böhme et al. 2018).
All cultivated crops within the surrounded 3-km land -
scapes were grouped according to pesticide application 
intensity, based on the latest national data for Latvia (Official 
Statistics Portal of Latvia 2025). Four pesticide use inten-
sity categories were used: no use (no pesticides applied), 
low (< 0.5 kg a.s. ha−1) (a.s.—active substance), moderate 
(0.5–1.0 kg a.s. ha−1), and high (> 1.0 kg a.s. ha−1) as shown 
in Online Resource 4. These categories were then used to 
examine how pesticide input levels related to both total agri-
cultural land cover and the proportion of nectar producing 
crops within the study landscapes. For this analysis, nectar 
plants were defined as cultivated crops visited by honey bees 
for both nectar and pollen collection. Given that honey bees 
pollinate many “key” crops, for example, oilseed rape, field 
beans, and apple orchards (Geslin et al. 2017; Barda et al. 
2024; Reilly et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2024), we therefore 
15939Environmental Science and Pollution Research (2026) 33:15935–15950 
examined whether the proportion of these crops within the 
landscape affected values of PHQ across apiary locations.
Descriptive analyses were performed in R version 4.4.0 
(R Core Team, 2024). Due to the limited number of study 
sites and high variability in PHQ values, no linear regres-
sion model was applied to evaluate the relationship between 
landscape composition and PHQ values. Instead, cumula-
tive PHQ values were summarised descriptively by apiary, 
sampling period and study year. Relationships between PHQ 
values and the proportion of agricultural land, nectar pro -
ducing crops and pesticide use intensity categories within 
a radius of 3 km around each apiary were evaluated using 
graphical comparison.
Results and discussion
Active substances detected and quantified in pollen 
samples
In pollen samples collected during study year 2024, 15 pes-
ticide active substances were detected and quantified. The 
detected residues were dominated by fungicides, which 
accounted for eight active substances. Among these, boscalid 
and azoxystrobin were recorded most often, occurring in six 
samples (14.6%) and four samples (9.8%), respectively. Her-
bicides represented the second most common group, with 
six active substances identified, of which glyphosate and 
clopyralid occurred most frequently, each being detected in 
six samples (14.6%). Only one insecticide, acetamiprid, was 
detected, occurring in five samples (12.2%). The detected 
substances were consistent with crop protection practices in 
the main agricultural crops surrounding the apiaries, includ-
ing oilseed rape, legumes such as field beans and peas, cere-
als and orchard crops. In 2025, pollen samples contained 
residues of 19 active substances. In comparison to 2024, 
herbicides formed the largest group, again comprising eight 
active substances, with glyphosate detected most frequently, 
occurring in six samples (15.0%). This was followed by 
seven fungicides, among which azoxystrobin, occurring in 
11 samples (27.5%), while boscalid and tebuconazole were 
each detected in nine samples (22.5%), respectively. In addi-
tion, four insecticides were detected, with acetamiprid show-
ing the highest occurrence among this group, being detected 
in 10 samples (25.0%) (see Table 1).
Previous investigators have consistently reported fun-
gicides as dominant residues in pollen. For example, a 
study in Slovakia and Germany by Sabo et. al. (Sabo et al. 
2024) reported maximum pollen concentrations of 40.1 
µg kg−1 for boscalid and 520.61 µg kg−1 for azoxystrobin. 
Meanwhile a Polish monitoring study documented even 
higher concentrations, reaching 150 µg kg−1 for boscalid 
and 2900 µg kg−1 for azoxystrobin (Swiatly-Blaszkiewicz 
et al. 2025). This pattern was consistent with our results, 
where fungicides were the most diverse pesticide group 
in 2024 and remained among the most frequently detected 
substances in 2025. Boscalid and azoxystrobin reached 
maximum concentrations of 610 and 480 µg kg−1 in 2024 
and 360 and 76 µg kg−1 in 2025, respectively.
Tebuconazole was also frequently detected in 2025 
and reached 240 µg kg−1, indicating continued fungicide 
input during the flowering season. The higher tebucona -
zole level observed in 2025 may be related to the timing of 
triazole fungicide applications in surrounding crop fields, 
particularly oilseed rape, cereals, or orchard crops. Resi-
due occurrence in pollen may also have been influenced 
by bee foraging on flowering crops or adjacent flowering 
vegetation during the same period. Meanwhile, in Sweden, 
Nicholson et. al. (Nicholson et al. 2024) reported infre-
quent detections of tebuconazole, with concentrations 
reaching 227 µg kg−1. Higher concentrations have also 
been reported from Romania, where tebuconazole levels 
in bee pollen reached 120 µg kg−1 (Bodescu et al. 2025).
Herbicides were also an important group, particularly 
glyphosate and clopyralid in 2024. Glyphosate reached 
840 µg kg−1 in 2024 and 327 µg kg−1 in 2025, which was 
higher than values reported in several European pollen 
studies (Kaila et al. 2022; Jesús et al. 2024). These ele-
vated values may reflect differences in sampling timing, 
local herbicide use practices, and the analytical approach 
used for highly polar compounds. The highest glyphosate 
concentrations occurred during periods when bees were 
actively collecting pollen in agricultural landscapes. This 
suggests that local pesticide application timing may have 
contributed to observed levels. In Latvia, high glypho-
sate concentrations have also been reported in bee related 
matrices, including honey and bee bread (Lace et al. 
2026). However, notably higher substance levels have been 
reported in other bee related matrices from Latvia, where 
concentrations in honey and bee bread reached 1400 µg 
 kg−1 and 1900 µg kg−1, respectively. Our findings indicate 
that glyphosate contamination of bee products may be rel-
evant under local agricultural conditions.
Fluazifop-P-butyl, an herbicide, was detected once in 
pollen samples in 2024 and three times in 2025. In both 
study years, this compound reached the highest concen -
tration among all detected substances, measuring 660 
µg kg−1 in 2024 and 1800 µg kg−1 in 2025. These levels 
exceeded the EU recommended MRL of 50 µg kg−1 for 
bee products. Available literature indicates a wide range 
of reported fluazifop-P-butyl concentrations in bee pollen. 
For example, substantially higher levels were detected in 
pollen samples from southern Germany, where Böhme et. 
al. (Böhme et al. 2018) reported concentrations reaching 
6831.3 µg kg−1. Fluazifop-P-butyl concentrations detected 
in this study were higher than most values reported in

Europe, but remained within the broad range published 
in previous studies.

in 2025, occurring in 10 samples and reaching a maxi-
mum concentration of 200 µg kg−1. The relatively fre-
quent occurrence of acetamiprid may reflect its use against 
aphids and other plant sucking pests in several crop sys-
tems surrounding the apiaries. Its systemic properties may 
also facilitate residue transfer into pollen when applica-
tions occur close to flowering. In 2024, detections were 
less frequent, however, the maximum concentration was 
higher, reaching 350 µg kg−1. Lower maximum concen-
trations of acetamiprid in bee pollen have been reported 
elsewhere in Europe, including 34.45 µg kg−1 reported 
by Carrera et. al. (Carrera et al. 2024). In contrast, higher 
concentrations have been reported outside EU, for exam-
ple, a study conducted in Kenya by Koech et. al. (Koech

The concentrations we found are consistent with ranges 
reported in previous investigations, although substantial 
variation among regions is evident. Such variation likely 
reflects differences in sampling design, timing, and analyti-
cal methods; therefore, comparisons between studies should 
be interpreted cautiously.
One pollen sample collected in 2025 contained herbicide 
terbutryn, a substance banned for use in EU. This compound 
was detected only once and exclusively at the Jelgava town 
apiary. As terbutryn was not detected in earlier sampling 
years, its occurrence cannot be explained by current agri-
cultural pesticide use. Possible sources of exposure include 
its application as a biocidal additive in outdoor construction 
materials or its use for aquatic algae control. Terbutryn has 
been reported to leach from biocide treated façade coatings 
and paints into runoff and wastewater systems (Bollmann 
et al. 2016). The presence of a wastewater treatment plant 
in Jelgava town within the foraging radius of the apiary may 
represent a potential pathway for this contamination.
Seasonal variation and landscape influence 
on pollen contamination
In both study years, the number of pesticide active sub-
stances detected above the limit of detection varied nota-
bly (between 0 and 7 detections) among sites and sampling 
periods (Fig. 2). In 2024, pesticide detections were generally 
concentrated in the early sampling periods, with several sites 
showing high numbers of detected substances during the 
first half of the season. We observed the highest diversity 
in Vecauce parish, where multiple active substances were 
detected in more than one sampling period, including the 
highest single period counts. In comparison, apiaries located 
in Ambeli parish showed low detection frequencies, with 
pesticide residues absent or detected only occasionally dur-
ing the sampling season.
A different seasonal pattern was observed in 2025 in 
both timing and intensity. Pesticide detections were more 
evenly distributed across sampling periods, and several sites 
showed increased numbers of detected substances later in 
the season. Again, Vecauce parish showed the highest over-
all frequency of detections, with multiple periods exceeding 
those observed in other sites. Platone and Lube parishes also 
displayed increased detection frequencies during mid and 
late sampling periods, indicating prolonged pesticide pres-
ence in pollen. In comparison, Jelgava town and Ambeli 
parish maintained relatively low numbers of detected sub-
stances across most periods.
The results indicate that pesticide occurrence in pollen 
was more temporally extended in 2025 than 2024. Sites 
surrounded by intensively managed agricultural landscapes 
consistently showed higher frequencies of detected active 
substances, whereas locations with a greater proportion of 
semi-natural or urban land cover exhibited lower and more 
irregular detection patterns.
The highest total residue concentration was recorded 
during the second (20.05.–02.06.) sampling period in 2024. 
This peak was caused mainly by samples from Vecauce 
parish (Fig. 3). During this period, pollen composition was 
dominated by Brassicaceae family, indicating intensive for-
aging on flowering crucifer crops, including winter oilseed 
rape, which represents a major pollen source for honey bees 
early in the season (Ouvrard and Jacquemart 2018; Poonam 
et al. 2022), additional data are given in Online Resource 5. 
Elevated residue concentrations were also recorded in the 
third sampling period (03.06.–16.06.), mainly in samples 
from Platone and Vecauce parishes. At this time, Fabaceae 
pollen was predominant, suggesting foraging on legumi-
nous crops such as field beans and peas, which are known 
to provide high quality pollen resources (Paulino et al. 2022; 
Chau and Rehan 2024). A further increase in total residue 
concentration occurred during the sixth sampling period 
(15.07.–28.07.), largely influenced by samples from Pla-
tone parish. In this period, pollen originated mainly from 
Chenopodiaceae flowering weeds. These plants are recog-
nised as an important pollen source for honey bee colonies 
in agricultural landscapes during late summer (Engel and 
Maricle 2021).
In 2025, elevated residue levels were recorded during the 
second (19.05.–01.06.) and third (02.06.–15.06.) sampling 
periods. These values were mainly driven by samples from 
Lube and Ledurga parishes. During this time, pollen compo-
sition was dominated by crucifers and rose family plants, for 
example, apple orchards, plums, and pears. Increased residue 
levels were also observed during the sixth (14.07.–27.07.) 
and seventh (28.07.–10.08.) sampling periods. This increase 
was associated with samples from Vecauce parish. Pol-
len collected during these periods originated mainly from 
Fabaceae and Brassicaceae species, including cultivated 
crucifers such as summer oilseed rape, which covered 136 
ha within the apiary foraging area.

periods. However, higher proportions of these pollen types 
did not always correspond to the highest pesticide residue 
concentrations. For example, high Brassicaceae pollen was 
recorded in Ledurga and Lube parishes during the second 
sampling period, whereas Vecauce parish showed high 
residue concentrations despite a lower proportion of Bras-
sicaceae pollen. In Jelgava, a high proportion of Fabaceae

Descriptive comparison showed that pesticide residue 
occurrence varied more clearly among sampling periods 
than among landscape categories. Higher residue concen-
trations were mainly observed from late May to mid-June 
in 2024 and during early and late season periods in 2025. 
These peaks coincided with periods when major flowering 
crops or other pollen sources were available to honey bees.
The comparison with crop area and pollen botanical com-
position did not show a consistent pattern across all apiaries. 
High proportions of Brassicaceae, Fabaceae, or Rosaceae 
pollen did not always correspond to the highest pesticide 
residue concentrations. Similarly, apiaries surrounded by 
larger areas of agricultural land did not consistently show 
higher residue levels.
Temporal factors, including crop flowering period and 
pesticide application timing, were more important for 
explaining pesticide occurrence in pollen than landscape 
composition alone. The systemic properties of some active 
substances may also facilitate residue transfer into pollen, 
which can weaken the direct link between mapped crop area 
and measured residues. Bee foraging plasticity may further 
contribute to this pattern, because colonies can shift between 
crop, non-crop, urban, and more distant floral resources 
depending on availability.
Pollen hazard quotient as an indicator of honey bee 
exposure
Cumulative PHQ values were calculated for each sampling 
period to estimate potential pesticide exposure of honey 
bees, as shown in Online Resource 6. Overall, exposure 
risk was generally low, but it was characterised by short-
term peaks in specific apiaries and sampling periods. In 
both study years, median PHQ values remained below the 
biologically relevant threshold of 50 in all sampling periods. 
However, several individual samples in 2025 exceeded this 
threshold, and one sample exceeded the high risk screening 
level of 500 (Fig. 4).
In 2024, PHQ values were generally low throughout the 
vegetation season. Median PHQ values ranged from 0.15 to 
2.70, and no sampling period exceeded the screening thresh-
old of 50. The highest median value (2.70) was recorded 
during the sixth period (15.07.–28.07). The highest cumula-
tive PHQ value was 42.16 and was recorded in Vecauce par-
ish during the fourth sampling period (17.06.–30.06.). This 
indicated a localised exposure event rather than consistent 
regional trend. Higher values were also observed in Vecauce 
parish during the second and third sampling periods, but all 
2024 samples remained within the low risk category.
The PHQ results showed that toxicity, expressed by the 
 LD50 values, had a stronger influence on risk screening than 
concentration alone. Compounds with low LD50 values con-
tributed disproportionately to cumulative PHQ, even when 
detected at moderate concentrations. For example, acetami-
prid was the main contributor to cumulative PHQ in 2024 
because of its relatively low contact LD50. In contrast, fre -
quently detected fungicides and herbicides generally con-
tributed less to PHQ because they had higher LD50 values.
In 2025, PHQ values remained low in most samples, but 
several short-term exposure peaks were observed. Ledurga 
parish exceeded the threshold of 50 during the second and 
third sampling periods, mainly due to flupyradifurone.

Jelgava town also exceeded this threshold during the third 
sampling period, driven by terbutryn. The highest cumula-
tive PHQ value 1173.91 was recorded in Vecauce parish dur-
ing the fifth sampling period and was driven almost entirely 
by cypermethrin. This example illustrates the importance of 
pesticide toxicity in PHQ interpretation: cypermethrin was 
detected at a moderate concentration, but its very low con-
tact LD50 resulted in a high PHQ value. Despite these peaks, 
median PHQ values during the same periods remained low. 
This indicates that elevated exposure was episodic and site 
specific rather than widespread across all apiaries.
PHQ is useful as a screening indicator, but it does not 
fully capture all biologically relevant risks. The calcula-
tion is based on acute LD50 values and does not account for 
chronic exposure, sublethal effects or interactions among 
pesticide mixtures. Mixture effects were not included in the 
PHQ calculation. Therefore, PHQ values may underestimate 
real exposure risk, especially when bees are exposed repeat-
edly or to multiple compounds at the same time. Potential 
interactions among pesticide groups also cannot be excluded.
Overall, toxicological pressure at the colony level was 
low in 2024 and mostly low in 2025. However, several epi-
sodic exposure peaks occurred between mid-May and early 
July. These short-term peaks may be biologically relevant 
because they coincided with active brood rearing and inten-
sive foraging periods.
Effect of landscape composition on PHQ values
We hypothesised that apiaries located in landscapes with 
a higher proportion of agricultural land would have higher 
cumulative PHQ values. However, graphical comparison 
did not show a consistent pattern across study sites or 
years (Fig. 5). Apiaries with higher agricultural land cover 
did not consistently have higher cumulative PHQ values 
than apiaries with lower agricultural land cover. Therefore, 
the hypothesis that higher PHQ values would increase with 
increasing agricultural land cover was not supported by the 
descriptive comparison.
In some cases, higher PHQ values were observed in 
more intensively cultivated agricultural landscapes, par -
ticularly in Vecauce parish. However, this should be inter -
preted only as a weak tendency, because the pattern was 
not consistent across all apiaries. For example, Platone 
parish had the highest proportion of agricultural land, but 
did not show the highest cumulative PHQ values. In con-
trast, some sites with lower agricultural land cover still 
showed measurable or elevated PHQ values (Fig.  5).
The absence of a consistent relationship may reflect 
the strong influence of temporal and local factors; pes-
ticide exposure in honey bee-collected pollen can vary 
throughout the flowering season depending on pesticide 
application timing, crop phenology and the availability of 
attractive flowering resources (Böhme et al. 2018; Friedle 
et al. 2021; Kast et al. 2024). This is consistent with the 
present results, where PHQ peaks were episodic and site 
specific rather than consistently associated with the overall 
proportion of agricultural land.
The analysis of nectar producing crops and pesticide use 
intensity also indicated that crop presence alone did not pre-
dict exposure (Figs. 6 and 7). Apiaries surrounded by larger 
areas of nectar producing crop or crops classified under 
higher pesticide use intensity did not consistently show 
higher PHQ values. Conversely, measurable PHQ values

Several factors may explain the absence of a consistent 
relationship between landscape composition and PHQ val-

pesticide applications in relation to crop flowering and bee 
foraging activity. Honey bees may selectively forage on par-
ticular flowering resources rather than using all available 
crops equally, and their foraging choices may shift depend-
ing on local availability and attractiveness (Sabo et al. 2024). 
In addition, broad landscape categories may be too coarse to 
capture field level pesticide use, crop phenology and short-
term exposure events.
Landscape composition provided useful context for inter-
preting pesticide exposure, but it did not explain PHQ pat-
terns on its own. The observed variation was more likely 
driven by a combination of local pesticide application tim-
ing, crop flowering, pesticide toxicity and honey bee forag-
ing behaviour. This interpretation is consistent with previous 
studies showing that surrounding land use or crop composi-
tion alone does not fully explain pesticide exposure in honey 
bee-collected pollen, because contamination also depends 
on local pesticide application practices and foraging con-
ditions (Koech et al. 2023; Nicholson et al. 2024). There-
fore, the apparent association between intensive agricultural 
landscapes and higher PHQ values should be interpreted 
as a possible tendency rather than a statistically supported 
conclusion.
Conclusions
This study showed that honey bee-collected pollen contained 
multiple pesticide residues across agricultural landscapes in 
Latvia, but the overall exposure risk was generally low. Fun-
gicides were the most frequently detected pesticide group, 
whereas herbicides produced some of the highest residue 
concentrations. However, PHQ values were driven mainly 
by pesticide toxicity rather than concentration alone, with 
highly toxic insecticides contributing disproportionately to 
risk screening.
In both study years, median PHQ values remained below 
the biologically relevant threshold of 50. Nevertheless, 
several short-term exposure peaks were recorded in 2025, 
including one high-risk PHQ value driven by cypermethrin. 
These results indicate that pesticide exposure in pollen was 
not continuous or uniform, but episodic and site specific.
Graphical comparison did not show a consistent 
descriptive pattern between PHQ values and the propor -
tion of agricultural land, nectar producing crops or specific 
crop types around the apiaries. Landscape composition 
and crop distribution provided useful context for identify -
ing potential exposure sources, but they did not explain 
PHQ variation on their own. Local pesticide application 
timing, crop flowering period, active substance toxicity
