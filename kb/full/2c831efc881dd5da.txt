Interface-driven electrocatalytic activity of NiFe-LDH on 
MnO2-modified SWCNT hierarchical nanostructures for 
overall water splitting
N. Subha · A. Ravi Sankar

Abstract
Developing cost-effective bifunctional electrocatalysts is important for sustainable and clean energy production. 
Herein, a hierarchical NiFe layered double hydroxide integrated with MnO 2 nanorods and single-walled car -
bon nanotubes (NiFe-LDH@MnO2/SWCNT) was synthesized to enhance bifunctional catalytic activity toward 
oxygen evolution reaction (OER) and hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). The synergistic coupling between 
Ni and Fe promotes high-valent Ni active sites that facilitate OH⁻ adsorption and accelerate OER kinetics, 
whereas synergistic adsorption of hydrogen and hydroxyl intermediates facilitates improved HER performance. 
The incorporation of multivalent MnO2 introduces redox-active centers that enhance electron transfer and inter-
mediate formation, whereas the conductive SWCNT framework provides rapid charge transport and increased 
electrochemically active surface area. Among the synthesized catalysts, NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/SWCNT exhibits 
superior electrocatalytic performance, with low overpotentials of 206 mV for OER and 198 mV for HER at 
10 mA cm-2 with a small OER Tafel slope of 33 mV dec -1. Comparative analyses demonstrate that the catalyst 
outperforms several previously reported NiFe-LDH and MnO 2-based systems, exhibiting faster reaction kinet -
ics and improved efficiency. The enhanced activity achieved from hierarchical architecture, improved electro -
lyte diffusion, and strong interfacial electronic coupling. These results reveal that NiFe-LDH@MnO 2/SWCNT 
is a promising electrocatalyst for efficient overall water splitting.
Keywords NiFe-LDH · MnO 2 · SWCNT · HER · OER · Water splitting
Introduction
Water electrolysis via electrocatalysis is emerging as a leading method for producing hydrogen (H2) and oxygen 
(O2) from water, offering a clean, sustainable pathway for energy production. As an environmentally friendly 
technology, it avoids greenhouse gas emissions and offers a viable alternative to fossil-fuel–based energy sources. 
Moreover, its scalability makes it attractive for industrial applications 1,2. The minimum theoretical cell voltage 
required for water splitting is 1.23 V , corresponding to a Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) of 237.1 kJ mol -1. In 
practice, the required voltage exceeds this value due to sluggish kinetics of the hydrogen evolution reaction

(HER) and the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), which significantly reduce energy conversion efficiency. 
Reducing the overpotential associated with these reactions is therefore critical, and the development of efficient 
electrocatalysts plays a central role in improving overall performance. While noble-metal-based catalysts, such as 
Pt (for HER) and Ru/Ir oxides (for OER), remain the benchmarks, their high cost and limited availability hinder 
their widespread deployment. As a result, considerable research has focused on discovering cost-effective and 
durable alternatives3. Among these, transition–metal–based materials, including phosphides4, sulfides5, oxides6, 
nitrides1,7, and hydroxides8, have shown great potential for enhancing both HER and OER activities.
Layered double hydroxides (LDHs) have attracted considerable interest as bifunctional catalysts for both HER 
and OER 9. In these two-dimensional materials, transition-metal cations occupy octahedral sites within MO 6, 
while charge-compensating anions reside between stacked layers. LDHs composed of Fe, Co, and Ni are particu-
larly effective under alkaline conditions: incorporation of trace Fe into Ni(OH) 2 markedly improves OER activ-
ity by enhancing interfacial conductivity 10,11. Among them, NiFe-LDH stands out due to its unique electronic 
structure, large specific surface area, and tunable interlayer chemistry, which together confer excellent catalytic 
performance. Indeed, the identity of the intercalated anion can modulate the electron density at active sites and 
thus strongly influence OER kinetics12–14. In particular, Reevesite-type NiFe-LDH, a naturally occurring hydro-
talcite-phase mineral, showed enhanced OER activity compared to conventional NiFe-LDH. This improvement 
is attributed to potential-induced contraction of the M–O bond at Ni and Fe centers, which optimizes the adsorp-
tion energies of oxygen intermediates and lowers overpotentials. Moreover, the strong electronic interactions 
between Ni and Fe facilitate efficient redox transitions and accelerate charge-transfer processes15.
The intrinsic activity of NiFe-LDH can be further boosted through hybridization with conductive carbon nano-
materials such as carbon nanotubes, graphitic carbon nitride, and graphene 16,17. These carbonaceous scaffolds 
(i) provide rapid electron pathways, (ii) supply additional catalytic sites, (iii) suppress nanosheet agglomeration, 
and (iv) facilitate mass transport of reactants and products18,19. For example, coupling NiFe-LDH with graphene 
oxide yields a highly percolated network that accelerates both charge transfer and mass diffusion 20. Likewise, 
single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT)–In2O3 heterostructures promote electron transfer through the metallic 
nanotube backbone while In 2O3 enhances the adsorption of water and reaction intermediates 21. Uniform wrap-
ping of MnO2 around multiwalled CNTs has been shown to increase catalytic activity dramatically, and SWCNTs 
themselves afford high conductivity, a large surface area, and favorable hydrogen-adsorption energetics 22–24. 
MnO2 is an earth-abundant material, making it an attractive co-catalyst. Mixed-valent Mn4+/Mn3+ centres facili-
tate rapid redox cycling, significantly enhancing the electrocatalytic performance of OER and HER. Furthermore, 
the unique crystallographic structure of MnO2, arising from the arrangement of MnO6 octahedra, improves elec-
trocatalytic efficiency25,26. These structural features translate into low charge-transfer resistance and strong O 2 
adsorption, resulting in superior OER performance for MnO2 relative to many other first-row oxides MnO2 com-
pared with many conventional first-row transition-metal oxides27,28. Incorporating Mn into NiFe-LDH has been 
shown to modulate its electronic structure and catalytic behavior further. Mn doping introduces hybrid energy 
levels between the Ni 3d and O 2p orbitals, effectively narrowing the band gap and improving electrical conduc-
tivity. This modification also reduces the Gibbs free energy required for the adsorption and conversion of oxygen 
intermediates, thereby facilitating the Ni2+ → Ni3+/Ni4+ transition, which is important for efficient OER kinetics. 
Moreover, Mn incorporation optimizes the electron density distribution around Ni and Fe centers, enhancing 
their ability to adsorb and activate reaction intermediates and thereby improving the overall catalytic efficiency 
of NiFe-LDH-based catalysts29,30.
Despite extensive studies on NiFe-LDH-based electrocatalysts, achieving low overpotentials and rapid OER 
kinetics remains a key challenge. Previously reported systems such as Ni 2Fe1-LDH/rGO/NF (228 mV , 37.9 mV 
dec− 1)31, NiMoO4/NiFe-LDH/rGO (270 mV , 76.2 mV dec− 1)32, and NiFe-LDH/Ti3C2Tx–rGO (~ 235 mV , ~ 40 
mV dec− 1)33 highlight the need for further enhancement in catalytic efficiency. The present NiFe-LDH integrated 
with SWCNT and MnO 2 exhibited a lower Tafel slope (~ 33 mV dec − 1) and reduced overpotential, indicating 
faster kinetics and improved charge-transfer efficiency. This enhanced performance is attributed to the synergistic

Experimental section
Materials and chemicals
All chemicals used, such as ferric nitrate nonahydrate, nickel (II) nitrate hexahydrate, and urea, were obtained 
from SRL, India. Super black and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich, while 
single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) and potassium permanganate were supplied by Nano Research Ele -
ments and Merck, respectively. Deionized water purified through a Milli-Q system was used in all procedures. 
All chemicals were of high purity and used without further purification, with specifications as provided by the 
respective suppliers.
Synthesis of NiFe-LDH
A solution was prepared by dissolving 1 mmol of nickel nitrate hexahydrate and 0.5 mmol of ferric nitrate 
nonahydrate in a mixture of 50 mL of water and 30 mL of ethanol. To this, 4 mmol of urea was introduced, and 
the mixture was stirred until fully mixed. The homogeneous solution was poured into a stainless steel autoclave 
lined with Teflon and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 120 °C for 16 h. The resulting solid was separated 
by filtration, thoroughly washed with both deionized water and ethanol, and then dried under vacuum at 80 °C.
Synthesis of MnO2/SWCNT
A dispersion of 20 mg of SWCNTs was prepared in 100 mL of deionized water via ultrasonication. To this 
homogeneous suspension, 200 mg of KMnO4 was added, and the mixture was stirred continuously for 1 h. Sub-
sequently, concentrated hydrochloric acid was slowly introduced to reduce the permanganate ions, and stirring 
was continued for an additional hour. The resulting mixture was transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless steel auto-
clave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 140 °C for 1 h. After naturally cooling to room temperature, the 
product was washed thoroughly with deionized water and ethanol to remove residual reactants and impurities, 
followed by drying at 70 °C.
Synthesis of NiFe-LDH@MnO2/SWCNT
MnO2/SWCNT (1, 3, 5 mg) was dispersed in a 1:1 mixture of DI water and ethanol for 1 h, and then the NiFe-
LDH synthesis procedure was followed for the synthesis of NiFe-LDH@MnO 2/SWCNT. The 1, 3, and 5 mg 
MnO2/SWCNT samples with NiFe-LDH are named NiFe-LDH@1-MnO 2/SWCNT, NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/
SWCNT, and NiFe-LDH@5-MnO2/SWCNT, respectively. The schematic representation of the synthesis proce-
dure is given in Fig. 1.

Material characterization
The samples were characterized using various techniques, including XRD on a PANalytical EMPYREAN instru-
ment, SEM on a Carl Zeiss EVO 10 microscope, HR-TEM on a JEOL JEM-2100 Plus microscope, and XPS on 
a ThermoScientific K-Alpha instrument.
Electrochemical measurements
The electrochemical tests were performed using a Squidstat™ Plus electrochemical workstation in a three-elec -
trode setup with Hg/HgO reference electrode and graphite rod counter electrode. The graphite rod counter elec -
trode was used to avoid possible interference from Pt-based counter electrodes during HER measurements, while 
Hg/HgO was selected as a pH-compatible reference electrode for alkaline electrolyte, providing a stable refer -
ence potential in 1.0 M KOH34,35. The working electrode was prepared by coating a nickel foam piece (1 cm x1 
cm) with a catalyst slurry, comprising the catalyst, carbon black, and PVDF in a 9:0.5:0.5 mass ratio, and drying 
it in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h. The deposited coating mass was approximately 3.5 mg on a 1 cm x 1 cm 
electrode, corresponding to a total loading of ∼3.5 mg cm− 2. Carbon black was used as a conductive additive to 
improve electron transport within the catalyst layer, while PVDF was used as a binder to enhance the adhesion of 
the catalyst on the nickel foam substrate. Control experiments showed that direct coating of the catalyst on nickel 
foam without binder led to poor adhesion and delamination during electrochemical testing. For comparison, bare 
nickel foam and PVDF/CB-coated nickel foam were also evaluated as control electrodes under identical condi -
tions, and the corresponding LSV curves are provided in Fig. S1 and Fig. S2. The electrochemical tests were 
conducted in 1.0 M KOH solution, involving linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) at a scan rate of 5 mV s − 1 and 
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The results were referenced to the reversible hydrogen electrode 
(RHE) using the equation: ERHE = EHg/HgO + 0.059 pH + 0.098. Both the LSV curves and chronopotentiometric 
stability measurements were corrected for solution resistance using iR compensation.

Results and discussion
Structural and morphological analysis of NiFe-LDH@MnO2/SWCNT nanocomposite
XRD analysis revealed the crystal structure of the electrocatalysts, as shown in Fig. 2a and b. The diffraction 
peaks at 11.7°, 19.5°, 34.4°, 39°, and 46.6° for NiFe-LDH and NiFe-LDH@MnO 2/SWCNT correspond to the 
Reevesite crystal phase, indicating the formation of a (Ni, Fe) hydrotalcite structure with carbonate anions in 
the interlayer space. This confirms the formation of NiFe-LDH with carbonate interlayer anions36. The observed 
low-angle reflection at 11.7° corresponds to the basal reflection of the layered hydroxide structure. While the 
relative peak intensities differ slightly from the standard reference pattern (JCPDS No. 26-1286), such varia -
tions are attributed to stacking disorder and imperfect layer alignment, which are commonly observed in layered 
hydroxide materials37. Furthermore, the successful in-situ growth of MnO 2 on SWCNT in MnO 2/SWCNT was 
verified by the appearance of MnO 2 peaks at 22.3°, 34.7°, 37°, 43°, 56.7°, and 62.5° (JCPDS No. 14–0644) in 
Fig. 2b. The peaks corresponding to MnO2 are not distinguishable in the NiFe-LDH@MnO2/SWCNT composite, 
likely due to its low concentration. Therefore, the presence of MnO2 was confirmed by further characterization.
The morphology of the synthesized materials was studied using HR-TEM and SEM analysis. As shown in 
Fig. 3a, the synthesized NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/SWCNT exhibits a flower-like architecture comprising ultrathin 
nanosheets with expanded interlayer spacing. Notably, Fig. 3a (inset) also reveals separated sheets, indicating 
the presence of both stacked and individual nanosheets. The hierarchical nanosheet structures were developed 
via a two-step process: an initial oxidation reaction followed by crystallization. The nanosheets are stacked into 
a flower-like structure to reduce surface energy. The hierarchical, flower-like morphology of NiFe-LDH provides 
a greater number of surface-active sites, thereby enhancing adsorption and electrochemical performance38. When 
NiFe-LDH is grown in situ on MnO2/SWCNT, the hydrolysis of urea releases OH⁻ ions that react with Ni2+ and 
Fe3+ ions to nucleate into nanosheets on the MnO2/SWCNT surface. As the reaction progresses, these nanosheets 
stack and form a flower-like structure. The SWCNT serves as a conducting network that connects nanosheets and 
microflowers. MnO2 nanorods are evenly distributed between the layers, as inferred from Fig. 3b39. The micro-
graphs of NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT composite further confirmed the sheet-like morphology of NiFe-LDH. 
The images reveal the formation of defect-rich hexagonal nanosheets that interconnect and stack upon each other. 
The characteristic hexagonal shape of LDH is evident in Fig. 3c and d, where MnO2 and SWCNT are embedded 
between the NiFe-LDH sheets. Figure 3e shows the particle size distribution curve, indicating an average MnO2

The Tafel slopes (Fig. 5b) were evaluated to investigate the electrocatalytic reaction kinetics using the equation 
η = b log j + a, where η is the overpotential, b is the Tafel slope, j is the current density, and a is the Tafel constant. 
This analysis provides insight into the rate-determining step and efficiency of the catalyst. The Tafel slope was 
evaluated following the standard recommendations for electrocatalytic water-splitting analysis reported by Anan-
tharaj et al.45. The NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT (33 mV dec− 1) possessed a smaller Tafel slope when compared 
to other catalysts NiFe-LDH (62 mV dec− 1), NiFe-LDH@1-MnO2/SWCNT (46 mV dec− 1), and NiFe-LDH@5-
MnO2/SWCNT (41 mV dec− 1). This implies rapid electron transfer and intrinsic activity of the hybrid structure 
in NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT. The observed Tafel slope of less than 40 mV dec− 1 suggests an efficient four-
electron transfer process. Additionally, the oxidation of hydroxide species, oxygen-oxygen bond formation, and 
oxygen evolution co-occur. The electrocatalyst with closely spaced active sites will exhibit a similar mechanism, 
as shown in Fig. 5c46. The chronopotentiometry technique (Fig. 5d) was applied to assess the stability of NiFe-
LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT. The NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT was tested for 48 h at a constant current density of 
50 mA cm− 2. The NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT showed excellent stability of about 98.04%. The observed post-
OER morphology in Fig. 5e and f indicates electrochemical reconstruction of NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/SWCNT. 
This morphological changes can be attributed to OER-induced surface reconstruction and structural degradation 
of the NiFe-LDH-based composite under alkaline medium. During OER, the surface and edge sites of NiFe-LDH 
actively participate in the reaction, and continuous interaction with OH⁻ species can induce structural changes 
within the layered framework, leading to partial dissolution and reconstruction of the catalyst during prolonged 
OER operation47.
The HER performance is also high for NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/SWCNT when compared to NiFe-LDH, NiFe-
LDH@1-MnO2/SWCNT, and NiFe-LDH@5-MnO2/SWCNT, as shown in Fig. 6a. From the result, it is evident 
that the hetero-interface formed between NiFe-LDH, MnO 2, and SWCNT enhanced the HER performance of 
the modified catalysts. The synergistic contribution of these components to HER activity is further confirmed 
in Fig. S2. Specifically, NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/SWCNT exhibits the highest activity, achieving 10 mA cm − 2 at 
an overpotential of 198 mV . The overpotentials required for NiFe-LDH, NiFe-LDH@1-MnO 2/SWCNT, and 
NiFe-LDH@5-MnO2/SWCNT to reach the current density of 10 mA cm − 2 are 248, 225, and 216 mV , respec-
tively. Tafel values (Fig. 6b) for NiFe-LDH (127 mV dec − 1), NiFe-LDH@1-MnO2/SWCNT (121 mV dec − 1), 
NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT (119 mV dec − 1), NiFe-LDH@5-MnO2/SWCNT (121 mV dec − 1), indicate that 
NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT follows the V olmer-Heyrovsky mechanism in alkaline medium. Chronopotenti-
ometry at 50 mA cm− 2 for 48 h (Fig. 6c) was used to assess the stability of NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/SWCNT. The 
NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT exhibited remarkable stability of approximately 98%. The SEM image (Fig. 6d)

the electrode surface. Although slight surface roughening and particle aggregation are observed after prolonged 
HER operation, the overall structure remains well retained without severe collapse or detachment. This indicates 
good morphological stability of the catalyst during HER in alkaline electrolyte.

performance than the others due to efficient charge transport and increased utilization of active sites. Having 
exhibited remarkable OER and HER activities, NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/SWCNT was subsequently employed as 
a bifunctional catalyst for overall water splitting. The overall water-splitting performance was evaluated in a 
two-electrode beaker-type cell containing 1.0 M KOH electrolyte, where NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT-coated 
electrodes were used as both the anode and cathode. The corresponding full-cell LSV polarization curve of 
NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT||NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT is shown in Fig. 7f. The NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/
SWCNT||NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT system achieved cell voltage (Vcell) of 1.66 V at 10 mA cm− 2. The sta-
bility of the full cell was evaluated via chronopotentiometry at 50 mA cm− 2 for 48 h in 1.0 M KOH electrolyte in 
beaker-type cell. The NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT||NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT showed exceptional stabil-
ity of about 98.63% after 48 h of continuous reaction.
Comparison of synthesized catalyst
Table 1 presents the electrocatalytic activity of the synthesized electrocatalysts. The synthesized NiFe-LDH 
composites have shown excellent OER performance and reasonable HER performance. The 3d transition metal 
hydroxide is more active in an alkaline medium. In an alkaline medium, Fe 3+ can induce a high-valency state in 
Ni, facilitating bonding with OH⁻ and promoting water dissociation during the OER. The selective adsorption 
of OH− on Ni-sites forms OH–Niδ+ intermediates, then decomposes to form O2. The synergy between Ni and Fe 
species facilitates water dissociation for HER, with hydrogen adsorption on Ni sites and OH − adsorption on Fe 
sites from Had–Niδ+ and FeOOH intermediates. Additionally, the presence of Fe inhibits the self-oxidation of Ni 
and promotes the dissociation of water into H2 during the HER process49,50. The dispersion of MnO2 nanorods on 
NiFe-LDH and SWCNT increases the electrochemically active sites and conductivity of the composite material, 
NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT. This synergistic combination enhances the inherent OER and HER activity of 
NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT51,52.
A comparative analysis of the electrocatalytic performance of NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/SWCNT with other 
reported NiFe-LDH composites reveals its superior OER activity, as evident from Fig. 8a. MnO 2 played an 
important role in enhancing the OER performance of the NiFe-LDH. Since MnO 2 enables reversible transitions 
between the Mn 2+, Mn 3+, and Mn 4+ oxidation states, it promotes OER. Additionally, the Mn n+ increases the 
electron transfer from the electrolyte and forms OOH intermediates, which subsequently undergo deprotonation. 
Also, the presence of Mn3+ ions in MnO2 enhances the adsorption of OH− and accelerates the reaction intermedi-
ates. A comparative analysis of MnO 2-based composites for OER revealed that NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/SWCNT 
outperforms other materials, as evident from Fig. 8b. Notably, the Tafel slope of NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT 
(33 mV/dec) is less than that of other MnO 2-based materials, including CoTe/MnO 2/BN (81 mV/dec) 53, Ni/
MnO2/CC (45 mV/dec)54, and MnO2 anchored N–Ti3C2 MXene (73.2 mV/dec)55. This superior performance is 
attributed to the MnO2 nanorods grown on conductive SWCNTs, which enhance the high-conductivity pathway, 
facilitating high electrolyte diffusion into the LDH layers and thereby reducing the liquid sealing effect. The 
hierarchical structure of NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/SWCNT composite further enhances the electrocatalytic surface 
area and high electrical conductivity. Although the HER activity of NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT is lower when

Conclusion
To summarize, this study successfully synthesized the hierarchical NiFe-LDH with intercalated CO 3
2− and its 
composites via a simple hydrothermal method. The morphological studies of the synthesized electrocatalysts 
were investigated using SEM and HR-TEM analysis. The results revealed that NiFe-LDH exhibited nanosheet 
and micro-flower morphologies. Furthermore, the in-situ growth of NiFe-LDH on MnO 2/SWCNT led to the 
uniform dispersion of MnO 2 nanorods and effective integration of SWCNT within the NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/
SWCNT composite. Electrochemical studies revealed that the optimized NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/SWCNT exhib-
ited low overpotentials of 206 and 198 mV at 10 mA cm− 2 for OER and HER, respectively. Additionally, the stud-
ies revealed that the heterojunction formed between NiFe-LDH, MnO2, and SWCNT enhanced the conductivity 
and mass-transfer properties of NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/SWCNT. Notably, the NiFe-LDH@3-MnO 2/SWCNT 
showed enhanced OER performance compared to HER, which can be attributed to the role of MnO2 in enhancing 
the OER performance of NiFe-LDH. MnO 2 facilitates a reversible transition among the Mn 2+, Mn3+, and Mn4+ 
oxidation states, catalyzing the OER and achieving better performance than most reported NiFe-LDH-based 
electrocatalysts. Moreover, the fabricated two-electrode system retained approximately 98.63% of its initial per-
formance after 48 h of continuous operation. The enhanced electrocatalytic activity is attributed to the hierarchi-
cal morphology and hetero-interfaces formed between NiFe-LDH, MnO2, and SWCNT. Our work suggests that 
the NiFe-LDH@3-MnO2/SWCNT can be a cost-effective and efficient catalyst for water-splitting applications.
