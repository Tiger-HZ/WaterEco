Key Summary Points 
Ocular surface disease (OSD) is highly preva-
lent in patients with glaucoma receiving 
long-term topical therapy and may adversely 
affect the success of glaucoma filtration sur-
gery.
Chronic exposure to preservative-containing 
glaucoma medications promotes ocular 
surface inflammation and tissue changes that 
can impair wound healing and increase the 
risk of bleb failure.
This narrative review synthesizes current evi-
dence and proposes five practical, evidence-
informed recommendations for optimizing 
the ocular surface before, during, and after 
glaucoma filtration surgery.
Key strategies include identifying and treat-
ing OSD, minimizing preservative exposure, 
controlling perioperative inflammation, 
employing meticulous surgical techniques, 
and preserving long-term bleb function 
through postoperative ocular surface man-
agement.
Integrating these recommendations into 
routine clinical practice may help optimize 
conditions for bleb survival, support surgical 
outcomes, and facilitate long-term manage-
ment of patients undergoing glaucoma filtra-
tion surgery.
INTRODUCTION
Ocular surface disease (OSD) is a spectrum of 
conditions that affect various tissues including 
the cornea, conjunctiva, eyelids, and lid mar -
gins, leading to dysfunction of the ocular struc-
tures responsible for tear film production. This 
dysfunction causes tear film hyperosmolarity 
and instability, leading to a self-perpetuating 
cycle of ocular surface inflammation and con-
current damage. The use of chronic preserved 
topical ophthalmic formulations is a signifi-
cant risk factor for developing OSD that esca-
lates with increasing number of medications 
[1]. In chronic conditions such as glaucoma, 
which require long-term topical therapy, the 
prevalence of OSD increases with age, severity 
of disease, number of eyedrops, and the dura -
tion of topical therapy [1 , 2]. As intraocular 
pressure (IOP) is the only modifiable risk fac-
tor in the management of glaucoma, patients 
with moderate-to-severe glaucoma often have 
an increased burden of medications due to mul-
tidrop therapy, increasing the risk of OSD [2 ]. 
For example, 38% of patients treated with one 
drop/day of preserved antiglaucoma medication 
had moderate-to-severe OSD, which increased to 
54% and 71% when treated with 2 drops and ≥ 
3 drops, respectively [2].
In recent years, there has been increasing 
emphasis on minimizing cumulative exposure 
of the ocular surface to preservatives and active 
compounds throughout the natural history 
of glaucoma. The LIGHT trial and subsequent 
studies have established selective laser trabecu-
loplasty (SLT) as an effective first-line treatment 
capable of providing durable IOP control while 
delaying or avoiding the need for chronic topi-
cal therapy [3 , 4]. Beyond its role as a primary 
intervention, SLT can also serve as a treatment-
sparing strategy, allowing reduction in the num-
ber of topical medications in patients already 
receiving multiple agents or in those experienc-
ing OSD related to chronic drop use [3 , 5]. In 
the post-LIGHT era, early adoption of SLT repre-
sents an important strategy for preserving ocular 
surface integrity and reducing the risk of drop-
induced OSD, with potential benefits for future 
surgical outcomes. [6]
Although preventing vision loss and main-
taining quality of life are the primary goals of 
glaucoma management, concomitant OSD has 
a significant deleterious effect on quality of life 
and reduces adherence, resulting in faster dis-
ease progression [7], and higher chances of sur-
gical intervention [8 ]. OSD is therefore a major 
barrier to effective glaucoma management, com-
promising long-term outcomes.
Glaucoma filtration surgery is generally 
reserved for patients with glaucoma that is inad-
equately controlled despite maximal tolerated 
medical and/or laser therapy, including those 
with advanced disease in whom a sufficiently 
low target IOP cannot be achieved medically,

in accordance with European Glaucoma Soci-
ety guidelines [9 ]. Such patients have typically 
been using multiple antiglaucoma drops for a 
long duration of time, which may compromise 
outcomes of subconjunctival glaucoma surgery. 
Toxicity due to chronic, long-term topical glau-
coma therapy results in alterations to the ocu-
lar surface, including inflammation, epithelial 
damage and tear film instability changes which 
maybe subclinical [10, 11]. These alterations 
can precipitate more aggressive wound healing 
responses following surgery, obstructing aque-
ous humor (AH) outflow [10]. Previous studies 
have shown patients with OSD have poorer out-
comes after glaucoma filtering surgery [1 , 12]. 
Additionally, a higher preoperative daily dose of 
the preservative benzalkonium chloride (BAK) 
(e.g., more daily topical medications) resulted in 
a shorter time to surgical failure in patients who 
underwent trabeculectomy [13]. Furthermore, 
if topical antiglaucoma medications need to be 
reintroduced following surgery, for example in 
a partially functioning bleb, there is a risk that 
further drop-induced inflammation may convert 
a partially successful operation to a failed filter. 
However preoperative topical steroid use can 
increase success rates [14, 15].
Although there is abundant literature high-
lighting the importance of managing OSD prior 
to glaucoma surgery, there is little examining 
the ocular surface management throughout the 
peri-operative stages. In this narrative review we 
present five tips to optimize the success of fil-
tration surgery for glaucoma by managing OSD 
preoperatively, intraoperatively, and postoper-
atively [11, 16, 17]. This article is intended to 
provide practical, evidence-informed guidance 
recommendations for clinicians. Relevant publi-
cations were identified through targeted searches 
of the published literature and selected based 
on their relevance to the topics discussed. As a 
narrative review, no formal systematic search 
strategy or quality assessment was undertaken.
Before outlining these recommendations, it is 
useful to review key principles of subconjuncti-
val glaucoma surgery.
Glaucoma Surgery Outcome: The Filtering 
Bleb
Subconjunctival glaucoma surgery relies on 
enhancing drainage of AH by creating a fistula 
from the anterior chamber into the subconjunc-
tival/sub-Tenon’s space that creates an area of 
elevated conjunctiva called a bleb. A function -
ing bleb is fundamental to the success of tradi-
tional filtering surgery such as trabeculectomy 
and glaucoma drainage device implantation 
and newer techniques such as new bleb form-
ing procedures. The success of filtering surgery 
relies on controlling the postoperative wound 
healing response. Inadequate control of pre-
existing ocular surface inflammation or surgi-
cally induced inflammation can lead to clot 
formation, angiogenesis and fibrosis resulting 
in bleb failure.
Studies using in  vivo optical coherence 
tomography (OCT) imaging of the anterior seg-
ment indicate that successful blebs are diffuse 
and present various structural characteristics 
such as an inhomogeneous architecture with 
a thick wall, a suprascleral fluid space of low 
reflectivity, as well as an adequate bleb height 
[18]. Ocular tissue fibrosis at the bleb site may 
decrease egress of AH or cause a complete block-
age of outflow, resulting in poor IOP control 
leading to surgical failure. Insights using imag -
ing have shown that a functioning bleb involves 
a compromise between physiologic wound heal-
ing and the scleral outflow of AH to maintain 
target IOP [19–21].
Studies using in vivo confocal microscopy 
indicate that normal functioning bleb anatomy 
involves various structural changes includ -
ing the formation of numerous intraepithelial 
microcysts, widely spaced subepithelial con-
nective tissue and minimal vascularity [19– 21]. 
These microcysts are formed by transconjunc -
tival outflow of AH. It has even been suggested 
that these microcysts may correspond to gob -
let cells that contain AH [22]. In addition, high 
preoperative conjunctival goblet cell density has 
also been correlated to successful filtering sur-
gery and good IOP control [ 23, 24]. However, 
BAK decreases conjunctival goblet cells sur-
vival and consequently, patients treated with

BAK-containing eyedrops have a reduced preop-
erative goblet cell density [25]. Hence, pre-exist-
ing inflammation from preservative-containing 
multidrop glaucoma therapy is a risk factor for 
surgical failure.
Advances in imaging are providing novel 
methods of evaluating the ocular surface and 
associated structures before and after glau-
coma surgery. For example, in vivo confocal 
microscopy allows quantification of goblet cell 
density in the conjunctiva. Optical coherence 
tomography-angiography also provides peri-
operative conjunctival vascularity [26], while 
conventional anterior segment optical coher-
ence tomography can be used to provide quan-
titative measures of bleb morphology, includ-
ing bleb height, fluid cavity height, bleb wall 
thickness and microcyst density. These metrics 
can be assessed over the postoperative period to 
determine changes in bleb architecture that may 
indicate bleb failure and warrant intervention 
[18].
Emerging evidence highlights the pivotal role 
of the conjunctival lymphatic system in main-
taining filtration bleb function by providing an 
auxiliary outflow route for AH [27]. Experimen-
tal and clinical imaging studies demonstrate 
organized lymphatic channels around func-
tioning blebs, whereas reduced or disorganized 
lymphatic patterns are associated with surgical 
failure [20, 28]. Early postoperative lymphangio-
genesis may aid aqueous absorption and pressure 
control, while excessive fibrosis can compromise 
bleb drainage [29, 30]. Advances in anterior seg-
ment OCT and in vivo confocal microscopy 
now permit visualization and quantification of 
conjunctival lymphatic vessels, which may offer 
potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for 
optimizing bleb survival [31].
Protecting and Preparing the Ocular Surface 
Before Glaucoma Surgery
TIP 1: Look for Ocular Surface Disease 
Preoperatively
Assessment for ocular surface disease should 
be a key element of the glaucoma examina -
tion at all stages of disease management. This 
is particularly important before considering 
glaucoma surgery and is likely to influence the 
choice of procedure, timing and preparation 
required prior to surgical intervention. OSD is 
highly prevalent in individuals with glaucoma, 
with approximately 50% of patients with glau-
coma using topical therapy reporting symptoms 
of OSD [1]. The prevalence is likely to be higher 
in the surgical population as subconjunctival 
surgery tends to be reserved for cases where 
topical medications have failed, and many of 
those requiring surgery have been using multi -
ple eye drops for a long duration. Surgery may 
also be considered as an alternative to eye drops 
in patients with iatrogenic drop-induced or 
drop-exacerbated OSD [25]. It is imperative that 
the evaluation of OSD includes assessment of 
symptoms and signs, as there may be a discon-
nect between perceived symptoms and clinical 
appearance. Recommendations from DEWS III 
propose screening for OSD using a standardized 
patient questionnaire followed by clinical eval-
uation, including assessment of non-invasive 
breakup time, osmolarity and ocular surface 
staining with fluorescein [32, 33]. Question-
naires include the ocular surface disease index 
(OSDI), however the OSDI lacks specificity in 
detecting OSD in patients with glaucoma due to 
its inclusion of questions related to visual func-
tion, which may be affected by glaucoma rather 
than OSD [ 34]. The more specific FAST® ques-
tionnaire, specially designed for patients with 
glaucoma, can also be used in clinical practice 
to reliably detect patients at risk of OSD [35]. 
Signs such as conjunctival hyperemia and eyelid 
redness are suggestive of ocular inflammation 
[32, 33, 35]. Corneal and conjunctival staining 
are beneficial for determining disease severity 
in severe dry eye disease (DED) that is often pre-
sent in patients undergoing glaucoma filtration 
surgery [32].
TIP 2: Optimize the Ocular Surface Prior 
to Surgery
Steps should be taken to remove iatrogenic 
causes of ocular surface inflammation, which 
may compromise surgical success. Measures 
include switching to unpreserved topical medi-
cations and decreasing the number of eyedrops.

This is especially important if the patient is using 
topical medications that are known to be harm-
ful to the ocular surface such as brimonidine [36, 
37]. Although preservatives are considered the 
main contributors to OSD through cumulative 
dose-dependent toxicity, active ingredients such 
as brimonidine may induce distinct ocular sur-
face changes, including delayed hypersensitivity 
reactions, which require discontinuation of the 
causative agent rather than simply reducing pre-
servative exposure [36]. Where feasible, surgeons 
can elect to halt all topical therapy and switch 
to oral acetazolamide before surgery and special 
consideration should also be given to minimiz-
ing preservative exposure from time of diagnosis 
in those likely to need glaucoma surgery.
Chronic ocular surface inflammation is 
thought to promote subconjunctival fibroblast 
activation and profibrotic signaling, thereby 
increasing the risk of postoperative bleb fibrosis 
and surgical failure [29].
BAK is the most common preservative in eye 
drops, however, it causes damage to the ocular 
surface and the trabecular meshwork through 
dose- and time-dependent toxicity [23]. Higher 
exposure to BAK-containing ophthalmic for-
mulations has been associated with earlier fail-
ure of trabeculectomy and decreased success of 
glaucoma surgery [13]. Halting the use of pre-
servative-containing medications can improve 
the ocular surface in about 2 weeks due to the 
decreased inflammation and, perhaps, increased 
goblet cell density [25, 38]. Halting all preserved 
medications may not be feasible; hence, mini-
mizing the load of preserved medications is still 
beneficial as it decreases the cumulative load of 
preservative that is cytotoxic to the cornea and 
conjunctiva. For example, the threshold concen-
tration of BAK at which cytotoxicity occurs is 
0.005%, and topical eyedrops typically contain 
BAK concentrations of 0.01–0.02% [39].
OSD such as DED and blepharitis are 
extremely common among patients with glau -
coma, and efforts should be made to treat these 
conditions pre-operatively. Ideally ocular sur-
face inflammation should be controlled at least 
1 month prior to surgery [12]. The pre-opera-
tive use of preservative-free topical lubricants is 
beneficial for addressing dry eye thus decreas-
ing pre-existing inflammation [11]. Given the 
role of inflammation in surgical failure, the use 
of preservative-free topical lubricants, concur-
rently with topical glaucoma medications, is 
recommended [40]. Notably, topical lubricants 
containing BAK decrease goblet cell viability, 
which is a factor of filtering surgery failure [38, 
40, 41].
Inflammation can be controlled with topical 
fluorometholone (FML) or low-dose hydrocorti-
sone, as these agents confer a lower likelihood 
of inducing an IOP spike or cataract compared 
to more potent topical corticosteroids [14, 15, 
42, 43]. In selected patients with moderate-to-
severe ocular surface disease undergoing bleb-
forming glaucoma surgery, a short preoperative 
course of low-potency, preferably preservative-
free topical corticosteroids may be considered 
[44]. Preoperative steroids reduce conjunctival 
inflammatory cell density and may decrease 
the need for postoperative bleb interventions, 
although evidence demonstrating improved 
long-term surgical success remains limited [14, 
15, 31].
Alternately, or adjunctively, topical non-
steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or 
cyclosporine can be initiated to suppress ocu-
lar surface inflammation [11, 25]. Where fea-
sible, preservative-free ophthalmic formula-
tions should be prioritized. Treatment selection 
should also consider patient preference, ease of 
administration, local availability, and access to 
therapy to support long-term adherence.
Blepharitis contributes to tear-film instability, 
ocular surface inflammation, and increased risk 
of bleb-related infection after trabeculectomy. 
Patients, particularly those with moderate-to-
severe disease, should undergo preoperative 
management with daily warm compresses and 
eyelid hygiene. When indicated, antibiotic ther-
apy may be added, with topical antibiotics (e.g., 
erythromycin, bacitracin ointment, or azithro-
mycin) recommended for anterior blepharitis, 
while oral tetracyclines or macrolides may be 
considered because of their additional anti-
inflammatory effects [45].
In conclusion, preoperative optimization of 
the ocular surface through reduced preservative 
exposure and control of inflammation promotes 
wound healing, decreases complications, and 
improves long-term surgical success.

Management of the Ocular Surface During 
Glaucoma Surgery
Trauma due to surgery results in inflammation 
that is mediated by a cascade of events involv-
ing clotting and the release of hormones (e.g., 
prostaglandins), cytokines and growth factors 
(e.g., fibroblast growth factor), which attracts 
components of the immune system (e.g., neu -
trophils, macrophages) to the surgical site [46, 
47]. Subsequent migration of fibroblasts to the 
surgical site during tissue repair and remodeling, 
results in fibrosis and scar formation [46].
Treating dry eye and blepharitis before surgery 
ensures a healthier conjunctiva and improves 
the likelihood of long-term surgical success.
TIP 3: Respect the Conjunctiva During Surgery
Intraoperatively the surgeon can mitigate 
inflammation by controlling bleeding, meticu-
lous technique, gentle handling of tissue and 
limiting interventions [48]. Minimally invasive 
bleb surgery (MIBS), including PreserFlo ® or 
 Xen®, may be good alternatives as these proce-
dure are less invasive than traditional glaucoma 
surgery and have some advantages over tradi -
tional filtration surgery, including less tissue 
manipulation and shorter surgery times [36]. 
However, less invasive techniques tend to cre-
ate a smaller fistula and do not allow the same 
room for bleb manipulation and postoperative 
titration of IOP, for example, through the use of 
releasable sutures or laser suture-lysis. Studies are 
required to determine whether these features of 
MIBS [49], may decrease inflammation and the 
risk of postoperative fibrosis.
Gentle surgical technique with minimal con-
junctival trauma, and the use of MIBS when fea-
sible, help reduce postoperative inflammation, 
preservative conjunctival integrity, and promote 
faster recovery.
TIP 4: Control Cell Proliferation to Prevent 
Fibrosis
Antimitotic agents such as 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) 
and mitomycin C (MMC) are primarily used 
intra- and postoperatively during glaucoma 
filtration surgery to inhibit fibroblast activity 
and reduce the risk of postoperative fibrosis. In 
selected high-risk cases, preoperative modifica-
tion of topical therapy with short-term topi-
cal corticosteroids has been shown to reverse 
adverse conjunctival changes induced by 
chronic antiglaucoma medications and may 
improve surgical outcomes [31, 50]. Although 
5-FU has been routinely used during filtration 
surgery, this antimitotic is toxic to the cornea 
and conjunctival epithelium in high doses 
which can cause vision threatening complica-
tions [47, 51]. More commonly, MMC is now 
used during glaucoma surgery for prevent-
ing fibrosis. Comparisons of MMC to 5-FU in 
glaucoma surgery indicate less epitheliopathy 
and lower risk of postoperative corneal dam-
age with MMC [52]. Higher concentrations of 
MMC and application over a larger area of the 
surgical site have been associated with reduced 
postoperative fibrosis after filtration surgery, 
although the magnitude of benefit appears to 
depend on baseline scarring risk, concentration 
range, and surgical context [53]. In contempo-
rary practice, lower MMC concentrations (e.g., 
0.02%) may be insufficient in eyes at increased 
risk of scarring, while higher concentrations 
(e.g., 0.04%) are more commonly employed 
in such cases and have been associated with 
improved surgical success. Such dosing may 
therefore be appropriate for selected high-risk 
patients, including those with a preoperative 
diagnosis of ocular surface disease undergoing 
filtration surgery. However, increasing concen-
tration or extending contact time also increases 
the cytotoxic effects of MMC and carries the 
risks of corneal decompensation, blebitis and 
endophthalmitis [46, 51].
In addition to its effects on the corneal and 
conjunctival epithelium, MMC may also dam-
age the meibomian glands. It is well known to 
be toxic to ocular surface tissues, with potential 
adverse effects on adjacent eyelid structures [16, 
54]. These alterations can exacerbate evaporative 
dry eye and destabilize the tear film, worsening 
the ocular surface postoperatively, especially in 
patients with pre-existing OSD [16]. Therefore, 
minimizing MMC exposure and using the low-
est effective dose and contact time is advisable 
Ophthalmol Ther 
in those with compromised meibomian gland 
function or dry eye disease.
Intraoperative injection of MMC addresses 
some of these issues and has some advantages 
over soaked sponges, including delivery of a 
standardized dose to the surgical site, no risk of 
retained sponge fragments and less surgical time 
[55]. Notably, the dose of MMC that is deliv -
ered to the ocular tissues remains unknown 
with soaked sponges. Additionally, the use of 
soaked sponges prolongs surgery time as they are 
applied intraoperatively up to 5 minutes [56]. 
Some have postulated that perioperative MMC 
injection may be more appropriate in patients 
with pre-existing OSD [57]. Perioperative MMC 
injection (0.002–0.02%) has become more com-
mon over time for trabeculectomy and glau-
coma drainage devices [46, 49, 58]. Perioperative 
MMC injection is also reported for implantation 
of some newer bleb forming devices [49]. More 
recently, the use of anti-vascular endothelial 
growth factor (anti-VEGF) during filtration sur-
gery has also been investigated. Less bleb vascu-
larity has been reported with subconjunctival 
delivery of anti-VEGF agents compared to MMC, 
and adjunctive use of anti-VEGF and MMC may 
have complementary effects on modulating 
wound healing [59]. A prospective randomized 
double-blinded clinical trial has shown that 
intracameral bevacizumab improves success 
rates and reduces needling rates after trabeculec-
tomy [60].
Preserve the Bleb
TIP 5: Preserve the Bleb and Ocular Surface
Long-term preservation of the bleb and opti-
mal outcomes after glaucoma filtration surgery 
require lifelong follow-up to detect and man -
age complications early. Evidence indicates a 
dynamic interaction between the ocular surface 
and filtration bleb, where postoperative inflam-
mation and ocular surface disease can accelerate 
fibrosis and bleb failure [16]. Routine ocular sur-
face evaluation and treatment with preservative-
free lubricants, anti-inflammatory, or immu -
nomodulatory agents can help stabilize the tear 
film, reduce inflammation, and maintain bleb 
function [61, 62]. Minimizing toxic exposures 
and managing meibomian gland dysfunction 
(MGD) are equally important. Careful, individ-
ualized use of antimetabolites such as MMC or 
5-FU, at the lowest effective dose and with deliv-
ery tailored to patient risk, can further prevent 
fibrosis, and support long-term surgical success.
Postoperative follow-up should include assess-
ment of both bleb morphology and ocular sur-
face health. Intensive postoperative corticoster-
oid use limits the inflammatory response and 
reduces fibrosis of the bleb. Interventions such 
as scleral flap suture adjustment, laser suture-
lysis, bleb massage and needling can be used to 
titrate flow and counter fibrosis. Longer term 
preservative-free steroid therapy can be pre-
scribed to further limit late-stage bleb fibrosis. 
Postoperatively, subconjunctival MMC or 5-FU 
injections may also be used to further limit the 
wound healing response.
If the desired IOP target is not achieved and 
topical medications must be reintroduced, pre-
servative-free preparations are highly recom -
mended to minimize the risk of inciting further 
inflammation postoperatively.
Postoperative evaluation of the eyelids and 
the ocular surface is essential, as eyelid margin 
disease, particularly blepharitis and MGD, can 
exacerbate inflammation, destabilize the tear 
film, and compromise bleb function [63]. Rou-
tine management of these conditions should be 
part of postoperative care [16]. Measures such 
as lid hygiene, warm compresses, and targeted 
MGD therapy help maintain ocular surface sta-
bility and patient comfort [64].
Preservative-free formulations are an impor-
tant adjunct in postoperative care following ocu-
lar surgery, helping to maintain ocular surface 
integrity, reduce friction with the filtering bleb, 
and alleviate discomfort [65]. They are particu-
larly beneficial in eyes with pre-existing ocular 
surface disease from long-term antiglaucoma 
therapy [66]. The choice of lubricant should 
be guided by the predominant ocular surface 
abnormality [67, 68]. Preservative-free formu-
lations are essential to avoid epithelial toxicity 
and inflammation that may compromise bleb 
function.
Long-term success of filtration surgery relies 
on vigilant postoperative care and preservation

of ocular surface health through consistent, pre-
servative-free management of inflammation and 
tear film stability.
DISCUSSION
This article presents a structured, evidence-
informed framework for managing ocular sur-
face disease across the perioperative stages of 
glaucoma surgery. In contrast to prior reviews 
that have focused primarily on preoperative 
optimization, it consolidates recent findings 
on imaging biomarkers, lymphatic modulation, 
and preservative minimization within a unified 
approach to support long-term surgical success.
Controlling inflammation both pre- and post-
operatively is essential for reducing fibrosis and 
encouraging long-term bleb survival. Inflamma-
tion of the ocular surface is, however, often pre-
sent pre-operatively and is associated with OSD. 
We propose guidance for pre-operative, intra-
operative and postoperative measures to reduce 
the risk of failure of filtration surgery due to OSD 
(Fig. 1). Managing OSD concurrently with each 
phase of glaucoma management is fundamental 
for long-term success.
Most of the evidence supporting ocular 
surface optimization derives from anterior 
bleb-forming procedures, such as trabeculec-
tomy, and extrapolation of these strategies to 
posterior bleb-forming surgeries, including 
tube shunts, should be interpreted with cau-
tion given differences in surgical anatomy and 
wound-healing dynamics. In addition, evidence 
remains limited for minimally invasive bleb-
forming procedures and diverse patient popula-
tions, and further studies are needed to establish 
the generalizability of these recommendations.
One issue that remains incompletely 
addressed is the optimal timing of surgical 
intervention, which often depends on surgeon 
experience and patient-specific factors. Though 
surgery exposes the patient to a risk of compli-
cations, there is also a risk associated with con-
servative or medical treatment, including the 
risk of glaucoma progression. In the absence 
of formal recommendations, timing should be 
individualized based on IOP control, rate of dis-
ease progression, and ocular surface condition, 
as earlier intervention with selected procedures 
has been shown in some studies to improve IOP, 
ocular surface health, and quality of life com-
pared with delayed intervention [69]. Further 
studies are needed to determine whether the 
safety and efficacy profiles of these techniques 
support earlier intervention before the onset of 
significant inflammation or OSD.

It should also be acknowledged that preop-
erative ocular surface optimization is unlikely 
to completely reverse the chronic conjunctival 
and Tenon’s capsule changes induced by long-
term exposure to preserved topical therapy [11]. 
While reducing active inflammation before sur-
gery may improve the perioperative ocular sur-
face environment and facilitate postoperative bleb 
management, these observations further support 
minimizing preservative exposure throughout the 
course of glaucoma management rather than rely-
ing solely on preoperative intervention.
CONCLUSIONS
Multiple strategies can improve the likelihood of 
achieving a long-term functioning bleb, including 
reducing pre- and postoperative exposure to pre-
servatives in glaucoma medications and control-
ling inflammation and cell proliferation to limit 
fibrosis. Prospective studies evaluating the impact 
of interventions for OSD management on surgical 
outcomes remain limited. While individual pri-
mary studies have explored specific interventions, 
such as topical steroid use prior to glaucoma filtra-
tion surgery, heterogeneity in medication expo-
sure, surgical techniques, outcome measures, and 
preoperative protocols precludes definitive con-
clusions [4]. Consequently, much of the available 
evidence is derived from reviews rather than pri-
mary studies. Therefore, the recommendations 
presented in this review should be considered 
evidence-informed and intended to support clini-
cal decision-making in areas where high-quality 
prospective evidence remains limited. There is a 
clear need for consensus-based recommendations 
to standardize the identification and management 
of OSD before, during and after glaucoma surgery. 
This framework underscores the importance of 
ocular surface preservation as a continuous pro-
cess before, during, and after surgery to maximize 
bleb survival and long-term vision outcomes.
