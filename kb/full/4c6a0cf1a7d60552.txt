Abstract 23 
The water intake of a drinking water treatment plant (DWTP) in Central Italy was monitored over six 24 
bloom seasons for cyanotoxin severity , which supplies drinking water from an oligo-mesotrophic 25 
lake with microcystin levels up to 10.3 μg/L. The historical data showed that the water temperature 26 
did not show extreme/large seasonal variation and it was not correlated either with cyanobacterial 27 
growth or microcystin concentration. Among all parameters, the cyanobacteria growth was negatively 28 
correlated with humidity and manganese and positively correlated with atmospheric temperature. No 29 
significant correlation was found between microcystin concentration and the climatic parameters. 30 
Polymer(chitosan)-enhanced microfiltration (PEMF) and ultrafiltration (PEUF) were further tested 31 
as an alternative microcystin removal approach from dense cyanobacteria-rich flows. The dominant 32 
cyanobacteria in the water intake, Planktothrix rubescens , was isolated and enriched to simulate 33 
cyanobacterial blooms in the lake . The PEMF and PEUF were separately applied to enriched P. 34 
rubescens culture (PC) (microcystin = 1.236 µg/L) as well as to the sand filter backwash water 35 
(SFBW) of the DWTP where microcystin concentration was higher than 12 µg/L . The overall 36 
microcystin removal rates from the final effluent of PC (always <0.15 µg/L) were between 90.1 -37 
94.7% and 89.5-95.4% using 4 and 20 mg chitosan/L, respectively. Meanwhile, after the PEMF and 38 
PEUF of SFBW, the final effluent contained only 0.099 and 0.057 µg microcystin/L with an overall 39 
removal >99%. The presented results are the first from the application of chitosan to remove P. 40 
rubescens as well as the implementation of PEMF and PEUF on SFBW to remove cyanobacteria l 41 
cells and associated toxins. 42 
Keywords: chitosan; cyanobacterial blooms; drinking water treatment; microcystin; Planktothrix 43 
rubescens; polymer enhanced ultrafiltration 44

1. Introduction 49 
Harmful algal blooms (HABs) are becoming an aquatic stressor of great concern since the 50 
anthropogenic perturbations (e.g. climate change and eutrophication) increase continuously in coastal 51 
areas (Griffith and Gobler, 2020). Simply, biotoxins that are produced by many HAB species, called 52 
phycotoxins, can bioaccumulate in the tissues of bivalve shellfish in time and, when consumed by 53 
humans, can cause severe/fatal shellfish poisoning syndromes. Phycotoxins are also a serious threat 54 
to marine organisms including marine mammals, fish, sea birds, invertebrates , etc. (Huisman et al., 55 
2018). Furthermore, the presence of toxins from cyanoHABs in drinking water reservoirs may 56 
represent serious health risks for the human population (Almuhtaram et al., 2018). The World Health 57 
Organization (WHO) set a guideline limit of 1 µg/L for the presence of microcystin -LR, one of the 58 
most harmful cyanotoxins known in drinking and recreational waters that inhibits protein phosphatase 59 
and causes liver failure and hepatic haemorrhage (WHO, 2020). Moreover, dissolved microcystin can 60 
be persistent in water for a long period without any change in its chemical structure, which makes it 61 
hard to degrade in natural ecosystems (He et al., 2021). 62 
Planktothrix rubescens is one of the most frequently-detected cyanobacteria species in Europe with 63 
microcystins being the most frequent cyanotoxins in freshwaters (Bogialli et al., 2013). P. rubescens 64 
is a red-colored, filamentous cyanobacterium with gas vesicles, which can adjust its buoyancy and 65 
position in the water column to obtain its preferred low light environment. This metalimnic species 66 
can adapt to low underwater light climate, and usually forms deep -water maxima in the stratified 67 
lakes of temperate latitudes (Gallina et al., 2017) . This has implications for planning monitoring 68 
activities, especially in the case of drinking water supplies, with offtakes typically at depths of some 69 
meters (Manganelli et al., 2016). 70 
The over-enrichment of nutrients in watersheds is one of the main drivers of cyanoHABs. Additional 71 
drivers include stratification and associated changes in temperature, light as well as changes in the 72 
frequency and timing of extreme weather events (Bertani et al., 2017). While forecasting the effects 73 
of climate change is a challenge, the high probability that future climatic conditions will favor bloom 74 
4 
 
formation poses an added challenge to develop effective mitigation strategies that consider both 75 
nutrient and climate drivers (Gobler, 2020). At this point, understanding long-term trends is important 76 
to inform forecasting efforts and to reduce uncertainty estimates while developing effective water and 77 
cyanoHAB management strategies and ensuring water safety (Urquhart et al., 2017). 78 
In princip le, d rinking water treatment remove s cyanobacteria in the final effluent without 79 
compromising cell integrity in order to simultaneously eliminate intracellular and extracellular toxins 80 
(Merel et al., 2013). However, when dense cyanobacterial colonies enter a drinking water treatment 81 
plant (DWTP), they can block filters and accumulate in these units due to the particulate load on the 82 
flocculation and clarification that can further cause the free toxin to pass through the system 83 
(Ghernaout et al., 2010) . In fact, algal cell accumulation (over 1 x 10 5 cells/mL) in clarifier sludge 84 
and filter backwash samples were reported (Almuhtaram et al., 2018) with various cyanotoxin species 85 
at varying concentrations. 86 
Conventional coagulation processes are effective to remove cyanobacteria cells but do not remove 87 
cyanotoxins efficiently, especially extracellular ones (Sorlini et al., 2013) . Most of the DWTPs use 88 
conventional adsorbents such as powdered activated carbon (PAC) (Dixon et al., 2011) or coagulants 89 
such as polyaluminum chloride (PACl) (Sun et al., 2013) that both achieved high removal efficiencies 90 
for cyanobacteria cells, and even microcystins (Ho et al., 2011). However, in order to obtain complete 91 
cell removal, high dosages of coagulants must be added, which in return increase operational costs, 92 
produce high volumes of inorganic metallic sludges. Hence, the application of organic polymers as 93 
coagulants may be a more feasible and sustainable solution. Chitosan is considered as a new 94 
generation coagulant (Jin et al., 2017) since it is abundant, non-toxic, biodegradable, renewable, and 95 
ecologically sustainable as well as highly effective in removing algae from water (Xu et al., 2013) . 96 
Chitosan can be easily derived from shellfish and crustaceans through alkaline deacetylation of chitin 97 
(Mucci et al., 2020) . Its characteristics such as efficient charge neutralization and bridging effects 98 
give good coagulation/flocculation properties (Yang et al., 2016) , thus it has been widely used in 99 
water and wastewater treatment in recent years (Miranda et al., 2017). 100 
5 
 
Coagulation and sedimentation processes are typically followed by rapid sand filtration in 101 
conventional DWTPs. No remarkable impact has been reported for the sand filter process to remove 102 
cyanobacteria and /or cyanotoxins (He et al., 2016) . Moreover, the major concern for filtration 103 
underlies the possible disruption of cells that accumulate in the filter media, and the subsequent 104 
release of toxins via the effluent and/or backwashing . Almuhtaram et al. (2018) reported that o ver 105 
70% of filter backwash samples contain ed cyanobacteria cell concentration greater than that of raw 106 
water, an order of magnitude higher than any other processes in DWTPs. Most of the time, sand filter 107 
backwash water (SFBW) is directly discharged or subjected to a sedimentation basin to recycle the 108 
water to the plant (Shafiquzzaman et al., 2018), which may not remove cyanotoxins. 109 
Membrane processes (e.g., microfiltration ( MF), ultrafiltration (UF) , and nanofiltration (NF) are 110 
particularly efficient to remove cyanobacteria and cyanotoxins (Merel et al., 2013). Specifically, UF 111 
was reported to remove intracellular cyanotoxins higher than 98% and NF to remove extracellular 112 
toxins in the range of 96 -99% (Gijsbertsen-Abrahamse et al., 2006) . However, direct membrane 113 
application to high algae concentration brings challenges such as fouling due to algal cells and algae-114 
derived organic matters (Qu et al., 2012) and/or cell rejection that causes an increase in extracellular 115 
toxins in the permeate due to cell breakage during filtration (Liu et al., 2017) . Herein, the use of 116 
hybrid systems with enhanced coagulation has a high potential to remove both intra- and extracellular 117 
cyanotoxins and to reduce membrane fouling (Dixon et al., 2011; Yan et al., 2017) . Polymer-118 
enhanced ultrafiltration (PEUF) is effective and even sustainable in this sense , while it helps to 119 
remove foulants before filtration. In comparison to other membrane technologies, PEUF has the 120 
capability of separating low molecular weight compounds and a high permeability for solvent 121 
transport at relatively low transmembrane pressures, which is desirable from an energy and capital 122 
cost perspective (Huang and Feng, 2019). 123 
A multi -barrier approach by developing a monitoring system to facilitate effective treatment is 124 
therefore highly applicable to minimize the effects of cyanotoxins in water bodies (He et al., 2016). 125 
This study reports the characteristics of cyanobacterial bloom and microcystin production through 126 
6 
 
monitoring six bloom seasons in the water intake of a DWTP in Central Italy that supplies drinking 127 
water from an oligo-mesotrophic lake. Whereas the plant can efficiently remove cyanotoxins from 128 
the final effluent in the current configuration even in the bloom periods, we proposed PEUF and 129 
polymer-enhanced MF (PEMF) as alternative and more sustainable treatment strategies that can also 130 
be applied in case of potential toxin accumulation in treatment units. Although chitosan-aided 131 
coagulation/flocculation has been reported in a number of studies , to the best of our knowledge, no 132 
study has evaluated the removal of P. rubescens cells via chitosan. Moreover, we reported the 133 
findings obtained from both PEMF and PEUF on SFBW for the first time. Hence, the aims of this 134 
study can be listed as follow: i) to monitor and assess critical cyanobacterial blooms and microcystin 135 
variations in the DWTP of Lake Castreccioni; ii) to determine any correlation between cyanobacterial 136 
bloom/cyanotoxin production and environmental parameters; iii) to evaluate any potential cyanotoxin 137 
accumulation within the DWTP even in the absence of a bloom event; iv) to test and optimize PEUF 138 
to remove cyanobacterial cells and microcystin in comparison to PEMF ; v) to perform PEUF and 139 
PEMF configurations to SFBW to mitigate the release cyanotoxins via these flows into water bodies. 140 
2. Materials and methods 141 
2.1.Site and plant description 142 
Lake Castreccioni is in the municipality of Cingoli in the Marche Region, Italy, located across the 143 
Musone River near Monte San Vicino, about 70 km far from the coast. The biggest artificial lake in 144 
the region, Castreccioni covers about 2.4 km2 and reaches depths of 16.8 m. The volume of the lake 145 
at the maximum altitude is approximately 50 million cubic meters. The DWTP (managed by 146 
Acquambiente Marche S.r.l.) is in the district of Castreccioni and responsible for drinking water for 147 
the member municipalities as shown in the e-Supplementary file. The plant supplies water for a total 148 
of about 65,000 inhabitants in the winter and about 95,000 in the summer with a maximum capacity 149 
of 500 L/s. The process steps include pre-disinfection with ozone (pre-ozonation), clariflocculation, 150 
sand filtration, ozonation, activated carbon filtration, accumulation tanks, and final disinfection with 151 
chlorine dioxide. 152 
7 
 
2.2.Water intake monitoring 153 
The water intake of the DWTP in Castreccioni was systematically monitored over six bloom seasons 154 
from January 201 4 to November 2019. The water level of the lake varied between 332 -343 m sea 155 
level during the monitoring period (e-Supplementary file). The physical-chemical parameters were 156 
monitored at monthly intervals ; while total coliform, E. coli, cyanobacteria, and microcystin were 157 
measured 4 -5 times a month. All physical -chemical parameters were measured according to the 158 
Standard Methods of the American Public Health Association (APHA, 2012) . E. coli and total 159 
coliforms were determined using the membrane filtration method explained in Standard Methods 160 
9221B (APHA, 2012). Cyanobacteria was measured according to a protocol described elsewhere 161 
(Lucentini and Ottaviani, 2011). Briefly, 1 L water sample taken from the water intake was fixed in 162 
Lugol's solution and an aliquot of 25 mL was sedimented and observed after 24 h in the Utermöhl 163 
chamber under an inverted microscope for the quantitative determination of the cyanobacteria 164 
species. Cell dimensions were calculated using a transmitted light optical microscope with an 165 
immersion objective. Microcystin analysis was done via enzyme -linked immunosorbent assay 166 
(ELISA) as described in Section 2.6. The historical meteorological data of Cingoli were obtained as 167 
the average monthly data from a national website for the monitoring period. 168 
2.3.Cyanobacteria isolation and cultivation 169 
The isolation of single filaments of the cyanobacteria P. rubescens was made from raw water taken 170 
from the water intake of the Castreccioni DWTP on 15 November 2019 (water temperature = 10 °C, 171 
pH = 7.9, turbidity = 0.23 NTU), following the capillary pipette method (Hoshaw and Rosowski, 172 
1973) under an inverted microscope (Zeiss Axiovert 135) equipped with phase contrast, at 200x 173 
magnification. After an initial growth in microplates, cells were cultured at 21 ± 0.1 °C under a 12:12 174 
h L:D photoperiod and an irradiance di 90 -100 µmol m -2s-1, in BG11 medium. For the molecular 175 
identification, genomic DNA was extracted from the cells collected by centrifugation at 4,000×g for 176 
20 min from a 30 mL monoclonal culture of P. rubescens (strain PRMC1119) in logarithmic growth 177 
phase using the CTAB (N-cetyl-N,N,N-trimethylammoniumbromide) method (Richards et al., 2001). 178 
8 
 
Extracted DNA was amplified by Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) using a SimpliAmpTM Thermal 179 
Cycler with the primers CYA371F and CYA783R (Janse et al., 2003) targeting the 16S rRNA gene. 180 
The PCR conditions were 94 °C for 4 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 sec, annealing at 181 
60 °C for 45 sec, and elongation at 72 °C for 1 min, followed by a further elongation at 72 °C for 5 182 
min. PCR products were visualized and quantified in 1.5% agarose gels stained with GelRedTM 183 
(Biotium, Hayward, CA, USA) using Low DNA Mass Ladder (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) as a 184 
reference, and visualized under UV light. PCR products with expected lengths and yields were 185 
purified and directly Sanger -sequenced by Macrogen Europe (Amsterdam, The Netherlands). The 186 
sequence was compared to the NCBI database by BLAST search with default settings ( see e-187 
Supplementary file). The culture was harvested at the final cell yield up to 105 cells/mL and further 188 
diluted to 10 4 cells/mL with the lake water of Castreccioni to simulate dense cyanobacteria period 189 
according to the historical data. The lake water was pre -filtered through a 0.45 µm fiber membrane 190 
to remove any background algae before dilution. 191

2.5.PEMF and PEUF experiments 207 
Chitosan powder from crab shells was obtained from Sigma –Aldrich Company Ltd. and used as the 208 
coagulant in the experiments. A chitosan solution of 1 mg/mL was prepared according to the protocol 209 
described elsewhere (Divakaran and Pillai, 2002) . The experimental setup is depicted in the e-210 
Supplementary file. The operating parameters (i.e. coagulant dose, rapid mixing velocity, and time) 211 
for the PEMF and PEUF experiments were initially optimized in 28 experimental systems. Briefly, 212 
chitosan was added to 500 mL of P.rubescens culture (104 cells/mL) at the concentrations of 1, 2, 3, 213 
10 
 
4, 5, 10 , and 20 mg/L, and different rapid mixing conditions were tested for each concentration as 214 
follows: 100 rpm at 3 min, 150 rpm at 2 min, 200 rpm at 1.5 min and 250 rpm at 1.2 min. Then, the 215 
suspensions were slowly mixed at the velocity of 40 rpm for 20 min and finally left for sedimentation 216 
for 30 min. The supernatants were carefully collected and subjected to MF (fiber membrane filters - 217 
0.45 µm) and UF (Merck Mi llipore AmiconTM stirred cells – 10 kDa) separately. The clarification 218 
efficiency (CE) of the supernatants after coagulation , MF, and UF were further determined via the 219 
measurement of the optical density at 750 nm (OD750) with an ONDA UV-20 spectrophotometer. The 220 
CE was then calculated by CE = (1 – ODs/ODf) x 100% (Xu et al., 2013), where ODf is the OD of 221 
the feed sample, ODs is the OD of the supernatant after coagulation-flocculation-sedimentation, MF, 222 
or UF. Further, the PEMF and PEUF experiments were conducted at the pre -determined optimal 223 
conditions using two different cases: cyanobacterial culture (104 cells/mL) and SFBW. In addition to 224 
the chitosan dose of 4 mg/L, a high concentration of 20 mg/L was also tested to evaluate any possible 225 
enhanced removal s of cyanobacteria cells and microcystin . In parallel, the effectiveness of the 226 
PEMF/PEUF was experimentally compared to that of conventionally used MF and UF, where the P. 227 
rubescens culture and SFBW were directly filtered without prior coagulation. After each test, the 228 
samples were collected for microcystin analysis. The samples after PEUF and UF were also checked 229 
to ensure any microbiological contamination in the final effluents using the membrane filtration 230 
method. All experiments were carried out in duplicate and mean values are presented. 231 
2.6.Microcystin analysis 232 
Water samples of 10 mL were conditioned at room temperature, mixed by vortex for 60 s , and 233 
subjected to ultrasonication (180 W, 40 kHz DU-65, Argolab, Carpi, Italy) for 30 min for cell lysing 234 
(Foss and Aubel, 2015) . The ELISA was performed by the commercial Microcystins -ADDA-235 
Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (Abraxis, Product No. 520011, Lot.No. 19GO113) following 236 
the manufacturer’s instructions. All reagents, such as microtiter plate (12 x 8 strips) coated with an 237 
analog of microcystins conjugated to protein, ready -made Standards solutions at 0, 0.15, 0.40, 1.0, 238 
2.0, 5.0 µg/L, Control at 0.75 ± 0.185 µg/L, Sample Diluent, Antibody solution, Anti -sheep-HRP 239 
11 
 
conjugate solution, Wash buffer (5x) concentrate, a substrate solution (color solution), and Stop 240 
solution, were provided by the manufacturer. The absorbances were read at 450 nm in a Multiskan 241 
FC, Labsystems microtiter plate reader (Thermofisher). Microcystin concentrations were calculated 242 
by interpolation using the standard curve constructed by plotting the %B/B0 for each standard versus 243 
the corresponding microcystins concentration (0, 0.15, 0.40, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0 µg/L). The coefficient of 244 
variations (CVs) for standards was always ˂ 10%; for samples always ˂ 15%. Recovery for control 245 
standard (QCS, 0.75± 0.185 µg/L), were always between 75 % and 125%. A final estimate for 246 
microcystins content was obtained and calculated as the mean of at least two subsamples (i.e. two 247 
replicates per subsample). 248 
2.7. Statistical analysis 249 
Correlations between environmental parameters and cyanobacterial blooms/microcystin 250 
concentrations during the monitoring period were determined by Pearson’s correlation method. The 251 
significant differences were determined at the p < 0.001, p < 0.0 5, and p < 0.1 level of significance. 252 
Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to investigate the contribution of each considered 253 
variable. All analyses and visualizations were done in R ver. 3.6.3. 254

The sample taken from the water intake of the plant for the experimental tests contained 0.099 µg/L 383

4. Discussion 436 
According to the historical data, the cyanobacteria proliferated multiple times throughout the year in 437 
Lake Castreccioni reaching a bloom usually in spring-summer periods. Furthermore, there were some 438 
periods when the microcystin concentrations reached up to 10 µg/L with respect to the dense 439 
cyanobacteria occurrence and the average concentration ranged from 0.6 µg/L to 3.81 µg/L , which 440 
often exceeded the limit set by WHO (1 µg/L). Our results are in line with the previous study carried 441 
out by Manganelli and co-authors (2016), where P. rubescens was found to be dominant in another 442 
meso-eutrophic lake in Central Italy (Vico Lake) with 0.5-5 µg/L of microcystin in the drinking water 443 
collection point. The authors also found no relevant correlation between P. rubescens density nor 444 
toxicity and total phosphorus, phosphate , and inorganic nitrogen . In another Italian drinking water 445 
basin, high levels of cyanobacteria (up to 160 × 10 6 cells/L) and microcystin (28.4 μg/L) were 446 
recorded over 28 months of monitoring , which was mostly represented by P. rubescens (Bogialli et 447 
al., 2013). A discharge of microcystin-contaminated freshwater into a coastal environment primarily 448 
originated from P. rubescens, has also been reported in the Adriatic coast, Italy (Rita et al., 2014). It 449 
is among the predominant cyanobacterial species in many Italian regions (Funari et al., 2017) . P. 450 
rubescens produces mainly variants of the hepatotoxin microcystin, known to be associated with 451 
oncological and endocrinological risks . At high levels of exposure (representative of acute 452 
intoxication), microcystin produces a cascade of events (e.g. cytoskeleton alterations, lipid 453 
peroxidation, oxidative stress, apoptosis), while at low doses (typical of long -term exposure), 454 
phosphatases inhibition induces cellular proliferation and hepatic hypertrophy (Funari et al., 2008). 455 
Lake Castreccioni is characterized by relatively low nutrient values (oligo-mesotrophic lake), which 456 
is typical for most Italian lakes (Rogora et al., 2018). DO is one of the key parameter indications for 457 
a healthy aquatic ecosystem and 4 to 6 mg/L of DO indicates a good water quality (Avvannavar and 458 
Shrihari, 2008). The oxygen levels in Lake Castreccioni also pointed a healthy ecosystem. According 459 
to the data obtained in our study during the monitoring period, there w ere not any notable changes 460 
either in phosphorous nor in nitrate concentration (except in several cases) in the water intake which 461 
22 
 
resulted to have no significant interaction between the cyanobacterial growth as well as microcystin 462 
concentration and nutrient concentration. Carvalho et al. (2011) also found that the nutrient 463 
concentrations were not the key drivers for cyanobacteria growth. Our results also reflect those of 464 
Zamyadi et al. (2012) who could not determine any correlation between the physico -chemical 465 
parameters such as pH, turbidity, dissolved oxygen, conductivity, and microcystin concentration. 466 
Like other organisms, cyanobacteria also require trace metals for their growth especially as cofactors 467 
in electron transfer apparatus ( e.g. cytochromes, plastocyanin) in photosynthesis, and a sufficient 468 
amount of trace metals are necessary . Despite manganese is required for the metabolic functions in 469 
the organism, it is mostly found at high levels in aquatic environments and is not considered as a 470 
limiting factor (Facey et al., 2019). Contrarily, high levels can be toxic for cyanobacteria which results 471 
in a reduction of growth rates (Rueter and Petersen, 1987) . In fact, we found a significant negative 472 
correlation between cyanobacteria abundance and manganese concentration. In accordance with our 473 
findings, Giles et al. (2016) also recorded an increased manganese concentration during the bloom 474 
collapse period. Although the blooms were correlated with atmospheric temperature in our study, we 475 
could not determine any correlation between the algal bloom and water temperature. 476 
P. rubescens is stenoterm species, its optimal growth temperature is 12 °C (Walsby et al., 2005), and 477 
Lake Castreccioni provides a favorable temperature for its growth. Many factors have a role in the 478 
phytoplankton succession such as ecological requirements and competition between species (Hadas 479 
et al., 2002) and P. rubescens is superior in competition with other phytoplankton species thanks to 480 
regulating its buoyancy (Akçaalan et al., 2014) . When the environmental conditions are not 481 
convenient for their growth, they can migrate to the appropriate depth and benefit from the conditions 482 
and proliferate. A strong correlation between the cyanobacteria abundance and m icrocystin 483 
concentration was inherently found in our study , which is also introduced by t he WHO. Since the 484 
toxin analysis is quite costly and cannot be performed regularly, the WHO proposed two threshold 485 
levels for cyanobacteria density (Merel et al., 2013). Our correlation analysis also revealed that within 486 
all climatic parameters, cyanobacteria density was only in relation to humidity and atmospheric 487 
23 
 
temperature. In a study carried out by Zhang et al. (2016), significant correlations were found for 488 
algal bloom area, wind speed, and air pressure. On the other hand, similar to our study, they could 489 
not determine any correlation between algal growth and precipitation, also the main contributors in 490 
the lake were atmospheric temperature, air pressure, and wind speed. 491 
There are many studies (summarized in the e-Supplementary file) on the removal of various 492 
cyanobacteria species and/or cyanotoxins via coagulation using chitosan (Pei et al., 2014; Xu et al., 493 
2013) as well as other coagulants (Sun et al., 2013) . Whereas this approach is common, with and 494 
without membrane processes, it was mainly used to remove M. aeruginosa and not P. rubescens. 495 
Moreover, depending on the quantity and quality of the cyanotoxins, membrane technologies have 496 
been reported with high removal rates on cyanobacteria and/or cyanotoxins (Campinas and Rosa, 497 
2010; Gijsbertsen-Abrahamse et al., 2006; Sorlini et al., 2013). The most common type is UF, while 498 
MF was not often applied and compared to UF , as we demonstrated in our study. Finally, either 499 
enriched pure culture or real lake water was used in these studies and SFBW was not tested so far. 500 
Sorlini et al. (2013) obtained high removals of cyanobacteria ( Anabaena lemmermannii) (98%) and 501 
total algae (99%) using a pilot -scale MF and further reported severe fouling that caused a rapid 502 
increase in the transmembrane pressure. In addition, UF has also been used to treat SFBW to enable 503 
safe reuse in a DWTP (Reissmann and Uhl, 2006). Regarding membrane processes, such as UF that 504 
shows superb separation properties while treating dense -cyanobacteria water of lakes or reservoirs, 505 
they still face challenges due to membrane fouling and poor rejection of soluble organics (Liu et al., 506 
2017). A combination of coagulation and membrane processes (e.g. PEMF, PEUF) can effectively 507 
remove cyanobacteria and associated cyanotoxins as proposed and confirmed with the results of this 508 
study while alleviating the fouling on the membranes (Yan et al., 2017) . At this point, the 509 
determination of proper coagulant dosage is an important factor controlling membrane fouling in the 510 
membrane process of algal -rich water as highlighted by Park and colleagues (2019) that tested the 511 
combination of PACl-coagulation and MF. 512 
24 
 
Our results remarked that there was an apparent difference in the microcystin concentrations in both 513 
effluents, PC, and SFBW, after the coagulation at 4 and 20 mg chitosan/L. Although 4 mg chitosan/L 514 
was comparatively more efficient to remove microcystin in PC, a high concentration of microcystin, 515 
as well as the more complex nature of SFBW, required a higher dosage of chitosan. After the 516 
coagulation-flocculation-clarification steps, 20 mg chitosan/L achieved relatively 0.73 and 4.69-fold 517 
higher microcystin removal rates in PC and SFBW compared to 4 mg/L, respectively. The difference 518 
in the increment of removal efficiency between these two water matrices can be due to the better 519 
formation of flocs via the suspended matter in SFBW . In the study of Jin et al. (2017), chitosan 520 
quaternary ammonium salt (HTTC) was used to coagulate Microcystis aeruginosa cells in different 521 
coagulation conditions. The authors found complete removal of algal cells at the HTCC dosage of 522 
1.5 mg/L, rapid mixing for 0.5 min at 5.04 g, and slow mixing for 30 min at 0.20 g. On the contrary, 523 
chitosan was found to be effective in the removal of Microcystis spp. but not Cylindrospermopsis 524 
spp. (Miranda et al., 2017). This finding points out the importance of algal species and the selection 525 
of relevant coagulants. 526 
Furthermore, there was not a remarkable difference between PEMF and PEUF in terms of overall 527 
microcystin removal and clarification efficiencies. In all conditions, the microcystin concentration in 528 
the final effluents was < 0.15 µg/L. A slight increase in microcystin concentration was even observed 529 
in the final PEUF effluents in PC samples. This can be contributed to high transmembrane pressure 530 
that may have caused a slight release of microcystin (Gijsbertsen-Abrahamse et al., 2006) . 531 
Meanwhile, direct MF and UF applications without prior coagulation resulted in poorer removal 532 
efficiencies. Although PEMF and PEUF resulted in similar removal efficiencies, the superiority of 533 
UF over MF in terms of microbial elimination should also be noted. PAC-aided coagulation and UF 534 
were investigated by (Dixon et al., 2011) with above 90% removal of intracellular and extracellular 535 
cyanobacterial toxins. The authors highlighted the importance of PAC -coagulation on cell removal 536 
and improving the flux of the UF. Similarly, up to 94% of microcystin was effectively removed when 537 
the coagulation/flocculation and PAC systems were combined with UF membranes, while 538 
25 
 
coagulation/flocculation alone could not lead to an appreciable removal of microcystin (Şengül et al., 539 
2018). Eventually, the adaptation and optimization of such hybrid systems are crucial since their 540 
performance is governed by multiple mechanisms. 541 
Although polymer-enhanced membrane treatment is a promising approach for the removal of 542 
cyanobacteria and cyanotoxins from drinking water , most studies on PEUF were carried out on a 543 
small scale to date . The biggest obstacle in membrane-based systems is high capital expenditure 544 
(CAPEX) as well as a high operational expenditure (OPEX) for membrane replacements due to 545 
fouling. For instance, a pilot test was conducted by Lermontov et al. (2014) to make a comparative 546 
cost assessment of conventional water treatment (with coagulation, flocculation, settling, filtration 547 
steps followed by chlorine for disinfection) and UF unit. Even though UF comprises a higher CAPEX, 548 
its net present value shows a lower cost than the conventional option. When considering the land 549 
required and its cost for each solution, the UF solution indicates an equivalent or even more 550 
financially feasible option, since the UF -based DWTP requires eight times less land than a 551 
conventional DWTP. Hybrid systems that include polymer-aided coagulation/flocculation before UF 552 
can ease the membrane fouling and thus help to decrease OPE X in the long term. However, water-553 
soluble polymers added to the feed solution to carry out PEUF can often cause severe foulant in some 554 
cases (Huang and Feng, 2019) . From the coagulant poi nt of view, the unit price of chitosan is still 555 
much higher than that of traditional inorganic flocculants (Yang et al., 2016), but its environmental 556 
benefits can encompass the high operational cost. Farid et al. (2013) indicated that flocculant chitosan 557 
for water treatment on the industrial scale can be provided with the price of about $1.5/kg, while 558 
calculated the cost of coagulation /flocculation process of microalgae around $0.0246/ (kg dry 559 
biomass) including (nano)chitosan and acetic acid. Hence, novel and simplified techniques can be 560 
developed to improve the functionality and to decrease the cost of chitosan-based flocculants (Yang 561 
et al., 2016). 562 
5. Conclusion 563 
26 
 
The historical data of the water intake supplied from Lake Castreccioni revealed unconventional 564 
water quality (manganese) and climatic (humidity and atmospheric temperature) correlations with the 565 
dense cyanobacteria periods while no correlation was found with conventional indicators such as 566 
nutrients and water temperature. This can lead to alternative choices of parameters for future decision 567 
support systems to control cyanotoxins in the drinking water supply chain and put different 568 
management/treatment options in place if needed. In addition to the occurrence of microcystin in the 569 
water intake of the DWTP, SFBW was found to be another critical flow (microcysin > 12 µg/L). Such 570 
intermediate waste flows during drinking water treatment often exhibit a high level of cyanotoxin 571 
accumulation and therefore should not be neglected and thoroughly treated before being dischar ged 572 
into water bodies or recirculated within the plant . The overall PEMF and PEUF test results enabled 573 
to reach the concentration of microcystin for national drinking water standard s and also indicated 574 
great removal efficiencies (>99%) to eliminate microcystin in the final effluent of the SFBW. 575 
Although PEMF can be applied for water flows with low concentrations of microcystin , PEUF can 576 
be more effective at higher concentrations and/or on more complex water matrices li ke SFBW. In 577 
addition, higher chitosan dosage (i.e. 20 mg/L) performed better in terms of microcystin removal in 578 
both water flows, while it did not have a major e ffect on the overall removal performance of the 579 
further membrane units. Thus, the required chitosan dosage may also vary with respect to water 580 
matrix and/or initial microcystin concentration. The superior removal efficiency of PEUF systems as 581 
well as their help to alleviate membrane fouling highlights the cruciality of multiple barrier 582 
approaches for removing cyanotoxins from drinking water. However, further research is needed to 583 
test bench- and/or pilot-scale PEUF units in continuous operation and to understand the relationship 584 
between cyanobacterial removal and possible re -release of cyanotoxins as well as the fouling 585 
mechanism of membranes before these results can be generalized. 586
