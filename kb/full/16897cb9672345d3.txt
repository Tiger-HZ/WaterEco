Abstract
Increasing urbanization and industrialization of the Visakhapatnam region have brought domestic sewage and industrial wastewater discharge into the
coastal ocean. This study tries to look at the quantitative abundance of the indicator and pathogenic bacteria and their antibiotic susceptibility. As part of this
study, both surface and subsurface water samples were collected from ten different regions (147 stations; 294 samples) which included 12 industrial
discharge points, surrounding stations and two harbours from the coast of Pydibheemavaram to Tuni. Physicochemical parameters like salinity, temperature,
uorescence, pH, total suspended matter, nutrients, chlorophyll-a, and dissolved oxygen of the water showed a difference from one region to other. We noticed
the presence of indicator (Escherichia coli and Enterococcus faecalis) and pathogenic (Aeromonas hydrophila, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis,
Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella and Shigella, Vibrio cholera and Vibrio parahaemolyticus) bacteria among the samples. Waters from the near harbour
and Visakhapatnam steel plant showed lower bacterial load where there is no direct input from industries to the coastal water. Samples collected during the
industrial discharge period had a higher bacterial load including E. coli. Enteric bacteria were found in higher numbers at most of the stations with some
isolates being resistant to multiple antibiotics with higher antibiotic resistance (AR) and multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) indexes compared with the other
coastal water habitats in the Bay of Bengal. The occurrence of these bacteria above the standard limits and with multiple antibiotic resistance in the study
region may pose a potential threat to the local inhabitants and can create an alarming situation in the coastal waters in the study region.
Introduction
India has a coastline of 7,516.6 km including the mainland which occupies 6100 km and the rest is shared by the island union territories. Forty percent of the
total population lives within 100 km of the coast (National cyclone risk mitigation project (NCRMP)). According to a census by the Center for International
Earth Science Information Network (CIESIN), there are more than 500 individuals per square km in a 10 m low elevation coastal zone along most of the east
coast of India. Industries and thermal power plants prefer coastal cities due to the availability of water for cooling/dilution and the easy discharge of wastes.
The presence and activities of harbours add to the increasing pollution and diseases along with the coastal population. India has a major contribution of
discharge from pharmaceutical companies (Larsson, 2008). A report by Reddy and Kostenzer (2011) indicates that the East coastal population of India is
more vulnerable and at higher risk of contracting diseases.
The state of Andhra Pradesh along the East coast of India has various major and minor ports. Two of them are situated in the Visakhapatnam district
(Visakhapatnam port and Gangavaram port). The major port Visakhapatnam is the second largest port in India based on the volume of cargo handled.
Visakhapatnam has emerged as a major industrial center in recent years producing a wide range of goods including steel, metal, polymers, fertilizers,
pharmaceutical products, heavy engineering equipment, shipbuilding facilities, textiles, etc. Therefore, a lot of industrial wastes are discharged into the coastal
ocean. Studies have reported that treated/untreated sewage and urban run-off carry disease-causing bacteria that impair the coastal waters the most in
comparison to other determinants (Naidu & Olaniran, 2014; Pandey et al., 2014).
Water is considered one of the major carriers of various pathogenic bacteria. According to a report by Gleick (2002), mortality by water-related diseases is
more than 5 million people in a year. Among the waterborne diseases, cholera alone is responsible for 21000 to 143000 deaths worldwide and 1.3 to 4 million
cases which are caused by the pathogenic bacterium Vibrio cholerae (Ali et al., 2015). The counts of these bacteria depend on various factors like rainfall
leading to high land runoff, discharge of industrial euents by point sources, anthropogenic activities on the coastline and harbours, and non-point sources
like agricultural and urban land run-off. Heterotrophic bacteria varied according to the volume of freshwater inux from different rivers along the East coast of
India when the samples from coastal waters from these regions were analyzed (Prasad et al., 2015). Wastewater from different sources brings
chemical/organic contaminants, pesticides, hormones, pharmaceutical discharge, etc. Studies from coastal water, mariculture sites (Chen et al., 2017; Zou et
al., 2011) and wastewater treatment, pharmaceutical plants (Obayiuwana et al., 2018) have shown the presence of both antibiotic residues and antibiotic-
resistant bacteria. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria helps in their pathogenicity making them multidrug-resistant (sometimes known as a superbug) and
dicult to treat diseases that are caused by them.
Several studies by Clark et al. (2003), and Lakshmi et al. (2019) showed coastal waters of Visakhapatnam city have a high pollution index in terms of different
indicator organisms such as total coliforms, faecal coliforms, protozoan cysts, etc. The city’s shing harbour and shipyard are mostly targeted for
microbiological pollution studies which showed the abundance of selected pathogenic bacteria is higher compared to other beaches (Babu et al., 2014; Sailaja
et al., 2013). A recent study by Khandeparker et al. (2020) in shing harbour water compared these bacterial numbers which showed Escherichia coli numbers
were higher during the southwest monsoon period. Similarly, Sudha Rani et al. (2018) studied the seasonal differences in bacterial numbers in offshore
Visakhapatnam waters suggesting heterotrophic bacterial counts were high during the upwelling period and monsoon. All of these studies have focused on
the waters near the harbour and coastal waters of the main city. The current study targets the coastal waters of the Visakhapatnam and Vizianagaram
districts where major industrial discharge points were spread around a 150 km coastal area. There are certain inlets into the ocean where industrial euents
are released. The Central Pollution Control Board has set up some norms for these waters which are treated before their release at different point sources. As
part of the quality monitoring, different physical, chemical and microbiological parameters are measured in these coastal waters. The study is carried out to
nd out whether the bacterial abundance is varying across the coast and if the physicochemical parameters have any role to play in shaping the community.
The study also tries to nd out the variation in the antibiotic resistance potential and presence of multidrug resistance in the bacteria isolated from different
discharge points.
Materials And Methods
Study Area
Page 3/21
Bay of Bengal lies between latitude 5° N to 22° N and longitude between 80° E to 94° E. The Bay is semi-enclosed and surrounded by India, Bangladesh,
Myanmar, the Andaman, and the Nicobar Islands. The Bay receives a huge amount of freshwater inux (1.6 × 1012 m3 per year) along with sediment from the
Brahmaputra, Cauvery, Ganges, Godavari, Irrawaddy, Krishna, and Pennar rivers (Subramanian, 1993; Varkey et al., 1996). The population living in this region is
one-third of the world. Industries in this region are a major part of the growth, development and economy of the countries. Visakhapatnam – Guntur industrial
region falls under the 8 major industrial regions of India with many industries related to maritime, petroleum chemicals, pharmaceuticals, etc. The presence of
a major port and several minor ports add to the industrial activities of this region. In this study, the coastline from Pydibhimabaram to Tuni in the district of
Visakhapatnam was chosen. This region covers ten major sampling locations including treated euent discharge points from industries and harbours (Fig.
1). The details of the study sites, industries, type of discharge and the number of stations sampled from each station are listed in Table 1. Except for 12 major
discharge points, nearby stations varying in numbers from 9 to 24 surrounding the discharge points are also sampled. A total of 147 stations were sampled as
part of this study. All these stations are between 0.5 to 6.6 km from the coast. For easier understanding, the stations are broadly divided into three groups;
north stations (NS), middle stations (MS) and south stations (SS) based on their geographic location and time of collection. The north stations (NS) included
PBM and TPM, the middle stations (MS) included CHP, VSH, GPH and VSP and the south stations (SS) included TVM, PDM, NKP and TUN.
Sample collection and analyses
Samples were collected during two time periods. During the 1st phase of sampling (8th to 11th Dec 2018), Water samples from all the stations from CHP, VSH,
GPH, and VSP were collected. In the 2nd phase of sampling (24th Dec 2018 to 1st Jan 2019) water samples from the rest of the stations were collected. The
second phase of sampling was carried out so that water samples can be collected from the stations that included industrial discharge points during their
respective discharge period. Niskin bottle (10 litres) was used for a sample collection from 147 stations along the coast of the study area. Both surface (below
0.5 m from the surface) and bottom (above 1 m from the bottom) water samples were collected from each station and xed/preserved accordingly for
downstream analyses.
Physicochemical parameters
Vertical proles of pressure, temperature (°C), and salinity (ppt) were measured using a portable conductivity, temperature, and depth (CTD) proler (SBE 19
plus; Sea-Bird Electronics, USA). First, bubble-free water is collected for dissolved oxygen (DO) and immediately xed with Winkler’s reagents which were later
analyzed following the Winkler titration method of Carritt and Carpenter (1966). Water samples were collected in cleaned plastic bottles for nutrient analysis
and xed by adding mercury chloride (for ammonia, nitrate, nitrite, and phosphate). The concentrations of nutrients were measured following standard
spectrophotometric procedures (Grasshoff et al., 1983). A 2-L water sample was ltered through a GF/F lter (0.5 μ m pore size, Whatman). Chlorophyll-a on
the lter was rst extracted with 90% acetone at 4 °C in the dark for 12 h and then spectrouorometrically analyzed. Samples collected in acid-washed glass
bottles were analyzed for pH by potentiometric method (Dickson et al., 2007) using 835 Titrando (Metrohm, Switzerland). For total suspended matter (TSM), 1-
2 l of water samples were ltered through 0.22 µm lter paper (MCE, 47 mm, Millipore) and they were dried and weighed and calculated in grams per litre. 
Bacteriological parameters
About 100 ml of sample was taken into a pre-sterilized screw-capped bottle for bacterial analysis from both surface and bottom water from the Niskin water
sampler’s nozzle. All samples were collected after rinsing the sterile collected bottle with precautions required for microbiological analysis while wearing
gloves. Samples were kept in an ice box for transportation to the lab where it is analyzed soon after or kept at 4 °C whenever necessary to stunt bacterial
growth (APHA, 2017; Ramaiah et al., 2004). The bacteriological examinations were carried out following Ramaiah et al. (2004) and Prasad et al. (2015) for the
enumeration of heterotrophic, indicator, and few pathogenic bacteria. A 100 µl of the sample was taken and spread on various plates and incubated at 25 °C
for 24-48 hours. Sample dilution (10 times) was performed whenever the plate became overcrowded and replated. All the samples were plated in replicates.
The number of colonies was then counted using a colony counter. The specic colonies (unique to the organism of interest) on the respective agar media
(HIMEDIA) were quantied and the results were expressed in colony-forming units (CFU ml−1) after averaging out from the replicate plates according to
Nagvenkar and Ramaiah (2009). Different selective, isolation and differential media were used for the specic bacterial groups. Heterotrophic bacterial counts
were determined on R2A agar, enumeration of coliforms on MacConkey agar, Aeromonas hydrophila on Aeromonas Isolation agar, Enterococcus faecalis,
Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumonia, Proteus mirabilis, Staphylococcus aureus on Universal differential medium, Pseudomonas sp. like Pseudomonas
aeruginosa on Cetrimide agar, Salmonella spp. and Shigella spp. on Xylose-Lysine-Deoxycholate (XLD) agar, and Vibrio spp. like Vibrio cholerae, Vibrio
uvialis, Vibrio parahaemolyticus and Vibrio vulnicus on thiosulphate citrate bile salts sucrose (TCBS) agar. 
Antibiogram
From respective agar plates, presumptive pathogenic bacterial colonies were isolated by the streak plate method. A total of 46 isolates from respective agar
plates were transferred to 1 ml of Mueller Hinton broth and left to grow overnight. The concentrations of the cultures were adjusted to 0.5 McFarland (OD of
0.1 at 600 nm) before plating. Mueller Hinton agar plates were prepared and fresh cultures from the tubes were spread evenly on them by using sterilized
cotton swabs covering the whole surface. A total of 21 antibiotic discs used for testing sensitivity/resistance of Enterobacteriaceae isolates as per CLSI
guidelines, are cefuroxime (CXM), cefoxitin (CX), ceftazidime (CAZ), ceftriaxone (CTR), cefoperazone (CPZ), cefotaxime (CTX), cefepime (CPM), piperacillin
(PI), piperacillin/tazobactam (PIT), ampicillin/sulbactam (A/S), nalidixic acid (NA), noroxacin (NX), ciprooxacin (CIP), gentamicin (GEN), amikacin (AK),
imipenem (IPM), meropenem (MRP), azithromycin (AZM), tetracycline (TE), aztreonam (AT), and chloramphenicol (C). These antibiotic discs were placed on
MH agar plates and left overnight to grow. The next day, the diameter of the inhibition zones was measured using a scale specically for this purpose. Among
them, 11 antibiotics are considered for Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates as per CLSI guidelines in M100 (CLSI, 2017).
The resulting inhibition zones were compared with the inhibition zone diameter range given in the current CLSI (clinical and laboratory standards institute)
guidelines that determine the inhibition zone diameter breakpoint where the bacterial strain is resistant, intermediate or susceptible to the given antibiotic

which follows Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method (Bauer et al., 1996). For the Enterobacteriaceae family, 21 and Pseudomonas, 11 antibiotics were taken for
testing. The antibacterial resistance (AR) index for these samples based on the isolates was determined. 
AR index = y/nx
Where, y = Number of resistant microbes in the sample
             n = population size, 
             x = total number of antibiotics used.
For isolates resistant to 3 or more antibiotics multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index was calculated. 
MAR index = a/b
Where a = No. of antibiotics, the isolate is resistant to, b = No. of antibiotics, the isolate is exposed to/tested against.
Statistical analyses
To compare the means of the parameters and whether they are varying signicantly spatially, pair-wise statistical analyses were performed between NS, MS
and SS combining both values in surface and sub-surface water samples. For physicochemical parameters, a parametric Tukey’s HSD (honestly signicant
difference) test was performed. For bacteriological parameters, non-parametric Dunn’s post hoc test is carried out. Principal component analysis (PCA) was
performed to infer the effect of physical (Temperature, Salinity, PAR, and Fluorescence) and chemical parameters (Dissolved oxygen, Chlorophyll-a, Total
suspended matter, pH, Ammonium, Nitrate, Nitrite, and Phosphate) on bacteriological parameters (Heterotrophic bacterial count, total coliforms, Escherichia
coli, Enterococcus faecalis, Klebsiella pneumonia, Aeromonas hydrophila, Proteus mirabilis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella, Shigella, Vibrio
cholera, and Vibrio parahaemolyticus) for surface samples and bottom samples separately. All the statistical analyses were performed using PAST 4.03
software.
Results
Physicochemical parameters
The range of all physicochemical parameters in both surface and bottom waters was summarized in Table 2 and also shown in Figs. 2 and 3. The VSH and
GPH stations were deeper compared to all other stations and they are litter far from the coast as well (Fig. 3). The mean temperature for the NS and SS groups
varied between 24 to 25 °C whereas the temperature of the MS group was 1°C higher in both surface and subsurface waters (Table 2, Figs. 2 and 3). The
temperature in SS was found to be a little lower as compared to NS. In MS, the subsurface water temperature was more than the surface as compared to NS
and SS where the temperature in the water column is more or less constant. Salinity showed a contrasting trend as opposed to temperature. The salinity of
MS was 1 ppt lower than SS and NS. The subsurface salinity of MS was 1 to 3 ppt higher than the surface water. Lower pH was found in MS compared to SS
and NS (Table 2, Figs. 2 and 3). Higher pH values were observed in TVM, PDM, and TUN (Table 2, Figs. 2 and 3). Stations of TVM showed a little higher
chlorophyll-a as compared to others (Table 2, Figs. 2 and 3). TSM was found higher in MS compared to SS and NS (Table 2, Figs. 2 and 3) and is higher in
both surface and bottom waters. Dissolved oxygen (DO) concentration was found a little higher on the surface than in the subsurface water. DO is less in both
surface and bottom waters in the MS, especially in VSH (Table 2, Figs. 2 and 3). Subsurface waters showed higher concentrations of nitrate, nitrite and
phosphate as compared to surface waters and ammonia concentration didn’t show much difference throughout. Nitrite concentration showed a higher trend
towards SS (Table 2, Figs. 2 and 3). Ammonia concentration was higher in TUN in both surface and bottom waters (Table 2, Figs. 2 and 3).
Heterotrophic, indicator and pathogenic bacteria
The range of all bacterial counts (CFU/ml) in both surface and bottom waters was summarized in Table 3 and the trend is shown in Figs. 4 and 5. Surface
water HBC was found to be higher in CHP stations followed by VSP, NKP, GPH, and TVM stations, lowest was found in VSH stations (Table 3, Fig. 4). Similarly,
CHP stations had the highest HBC in subsurface water (Table 3, Fig. 5). HBC of subsurface waters was more compared to surface in most of the stations.
Surface water total coliforms (TC) counts were highest in PDM stations and lowest in the MS group except for CHP. Subsurface TC was highest in TVM
stations (Table 3, Fig. 5). Escherichia coli (EC)showed a different pattern, surface EC numbers were found to be higher in the NS group and 2 stations of the
SS group (PDM, NKP). Similarly, PBM, TPM, and PDM stations had higher subsurface EC counts along with CHP stations (Fig. 5). VSH, GPH, and VSP stations
had the lowest EC numbers. Enterococcus feacalis (EF) was present only in stations of PBM, TPM, and CHP in both surface and subsurface waters (Table 3,
Figs. 4 and 5). Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP) was present in relatively higher numbers in PBM and TUN stations (Table 3, Figs. 4 and 5). KP counts in other
stations are found to be low. Proteus mirabilis (PM) was found to be present in higher numbers in TUN stations followed by VSH, GPH, and VSP (Table 3, Fig.
4) in surface waters. In subsurface waters, they were high in PBM (Table 3, Fig. 5). Though the counts of Staphylococcus aureus (SA) were very less across all
the stations some signatures were found in stations of PBM, TPM, CHP, VSP, and TVM (Table 3, Figs. 4 and 5). Aeromonas hydrophila (AH) was found in
surface and subsurface waters NS and SS group and was very less in MS (Table 3, Figs. 4 and 5). Vibrio cholera (VC) and Vibrio parahaemolyticus (VP) were
mostly found in the subsurface and surface waters of NS group stations (Table 3, Figs. 4 and 5). VP was additionally found in CHP and some stations of NKP
(Table 3, Figs. 4 and 5). Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) was found in most of the stations in higher numbers as compared to other pathogenic bacteria (Table
3, Figs. 4 and 5). Their numbers were highest on the surface waters of TPM and GPH followed by CHP and PBM stations. In the subsurface water, PA counts
were highest in TVM followed by CHP, TPM, and TVM (Table 3, Figs. 4 and 5).

Among the 28 isolates belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family, only 3 were sensitive to all of the antibiotics, and among the 18 Pseudomonas isolates, 5
were sensitive to all of the antibiotics. Out of all the 46 isolates, 38 were resistant to at least 1 antibiotic used in the study (Tables 6 and 7). Isolates from the
Enterobacteriaceae family showed resistance to more antibiotics than Pseudomonas (Tables 6 and 7). The antibiotics to which many of
the Enterobacteriaceae isolates were resistant are aztreonam (24), cefuroxime (12), cefotaxime (12), ceftazidime (14), cefepime (12) and azithromycin (11).
No Enterobacteriaceae isolates were resistant to chloramphenicol, noroxacin and tetracycline. Among the Enterobacteriaceae isolates, 85% and among
Pseudomonas, 66% were resistant to aztreonam, followed by ceftazidime (50%, 27%) and cefepime (42%, 22%) for Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonas
respectively (Fig. 6). In the case of Enterobacteriaceae isolates, 42.8%, 42.8% and 39.2% were found to be resistant to cefuroxime, cefotaxime and
azithromycin respectively (Fig. 6). Similar inference can be drawn in terms of AR indexes for each antibiotic with aztreonam having an AR index of 0.04,
followed by ceftazidime (0.023), cefuroxime (0.02), cefotaxime (0.02) and cefepime (0.02) (Table 6). Isolates belonging to the genera Proteus and Escherichia
showed the most resistance to the antibiotics tested (Table 6). Aztreonam had the highest AR index of 0.06 among the Pseudomonas isolates followed by
0.025 for ceftazidime and 0.02 for cefepime (Table 7). Among 28 isolates of the Enterobacteriaceae family, 13 had a MAR index more than or equal to 0.2 and
among Pseudomonas, the number was 4 among the 18 isolates (Table 8).
Statistical analyses
Temperature, Fluorescence, pH and DO concentrations showed signicant variation in their means among the 3 stations when compared pairwise (p-value
<0.05) at a 95% condence interval (Tukey's HSD test) (Table 4). The salinity of the SS group showed a signicant difference when compared to NS and MS
groups. Nutrients didn’t show signicant variation among the 3 groups except for phosphate. The MS group was found to be very different from the other two
groups in terms of values of different physicochemical parameters. Dunn’s post hoc test for the non-parametric distribution of bacteriological parameters
suggested that there is a signicant difference between the 3 groups (Table 5). Aeromonas hydrophila, Salmonella Shigella-like organisms, and Klebsiella
pneumoniae numbers showed a signicant difference in all the three groups as inferred by the pair-wise test at a 95 % signicance level. The test suggested
NS group is different from other groups in terms of various bacterial counts. 
In the surface sample PCA (Fig. 7), there are two major clusters. The rst cluster included stations of the MS group along the direction of higher temperature
and TSM load and opposite to salinity. The other cluster contained mostly the SS group (TVM, PDM, NKP) and one from the NS group (TPM) in the direction
of higher salinity, pH, uorescence, chlorophyll- a, and TC. PBM was separate from others and showed positive variation with dissolved oxygen concentration,
EC, EF, AH, KP, VC and VP. The rst two components explained the 22.82 % variation in the data (PC1: 13.61 %, PC2: 9.21 %) in the PCA. 
In the bottom samples, a similar grouping is seen (Fig. 8) but it is not that prominent as compared to the former arrangement in the case of surface samples.
Again, MS group stations are clustered together with CHP a little far from the main group. The grouping was in the direction of temperature, TSM and salinity.
The second cluster was found to be present in the middle with the SS group (PDM, TUN, NKP) except TVM. TVM was present in the direction of TC, PA, and SS
counts. Station PDM fell in the direction of EC< EF, VC and PM separated from other stations. The rst two components in this PCA explained a 23.99 %
variation in the data (PC1: 14.63 %, PC2: 9.36 %). 
Discussion
Effect of rainfall on physicochemical parameters
A temperature difference of 2 °C in the MS group station surface waters as compared to other groups can be attributed to the difference in the time of
collection. Water samples from these places were collected during 1st phase of sampling whereas other samples were collected during the 2nd phase. The
water temperature corresponds to the air temperature recorded during these times which was in the range of 23 to 30 °C during early December and decreased
to the range of 17 to 27 °C during late December. The lower salinity observed on this particular stretch of coast that included the MS group is attributed to
rainfall near these places before the period of sampling (Supplementary Fig. 1). Except for these variations normal trend of salinity towards the southern
station is increasing because the northern Bay of Bengal is directly affected by freshwater inux from rivers that drain into the ocean. During December, East
India Coastal Current (EICC) is southwards (Shankar et al., 1996) which brings a gradual change in salinity which increases downwards to the South. The
stations which have a 3-4 ppt increase in salinity from surface to subsurface waters of VSH, GPH, and VSP, have a depth of 25 m or more whereas in other
stations where salinity has a maximum 1 ppt difference between the surface and subsurface waters have depths under 15 m. A little lower pH of these
stations can also be attributed to the rainfall as rainwater has a lower pH. These particular stations also had 2 to 2.5 times higher TSM as compared to other
stations which corresponds to lower chlorophyll-a content of these stations, as higher TSM can reduce the availability of sunlight thereby reducing
phytoplankton abundance (Sarma et al., 2010; Sudha Rani et al., 2018). This is supported by the PCA (Figs. 7 and 8) where total suspended matter is grouped
with stations VSH, GPH, and VSP. Among these stations, the highest TSM load of GPH stations may depend on the port activity and dredging caused by the
port authority, shing nets and trawling. Nutrients like ammonium, nitrate, nitrite, and phosphate didn’t show much variation. Principal component analysis of
both surface and bottom waters shows the signicant effect of rainfall on the stations where two different groups are formed, one including the MS group
with higher temperature and low salinity, and another with other stations with lower temperature and higher salinity conditions (Figs. 7 and 8). 
Spatial distribution of bacterial numbers
Sample properties of all the stations showed variation in two groups based on the time of collection which in turn affects the physical and chemical properties
of the water. During this sampling, changes in physical parameters are much more prominent than chemical. Waters of MS group stations were warmer, less
saline, and less alkaline as compared to other stations from which water samples were collected 13 days later. Heterotrophic bacteria are the ones that use
organic matter for energy production; their high number in most of the samples indicates their presence is not affected much by the physical property change
of the water column. While heterotrophic bacterial counts and total coliform counts are useful in the prediction of the microbial processes that are going on in
the water column, specic bacteria have been used as indicators of heavy metal pollution, faecal contamination, wastewater pollution, and oil contamination
Page 6/21
(Sumampouw & Risjani, 2014). As enteric and faecal origin bacteria like Escherichia coli and Enterococcus feacalis have a narrow host range of humans and
other warm-blooded animals, they are very useful in determining the source of contamination and determining the extent of anthropogenic pollution (Brandt et
al., 2017; Wheeler et al., 2002). Indicator bacteria like E. coli and E. feacalis counts showed a particular pattern in this study. Both were highest in PBM. E.
feacalis was found in higher numbers in NS regions in the stations of TPM and CHP and was mostly absent thereafter except in very few stations. E. coli
numbers were high in similar stations as E. feacalis along with PDM and were present in fewer numbers in other stations and were very less in VSH and GPH
stations. All these stations (PBM, TPM, CHP, PDM, and TVM) are located near the coast where small villages including shing villages are present. The
sampling location PBM is close to the biggest village among others. PCA of both surface and bottom water also indicates PBM stations have a higher
abundance of E. coli, E. feacalis, Aeromonas hydrophila, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Vibrio cholerae and Vibrio parahaemolyticus as compared to others (Figs. 7
and 8). The presence of villages can affect the health of coastal water negatively. Land run-off into the ocean from these places can be affected by human
and animal faeces via domestic sewage, open defecation, and water from animal sheds. This water can harbour various enteric and pathogenic bacteria. Less
commonly used indicator bacteria like K. pneumoniae were also found in VSH and GPH along with northern stations. Another pathogenic bacterium Proteus
mirabilis showed a reverse pattern in its presence. They were found in most of the stations of the MS group (VSH, GPH and VSP) and bottom waters of very
few of the other stations. Though these waters have very less of E. coli and E. feacalis and are present offshore, the presence of enteric bacteria like P.
mirabilis shows potential faecal contamination from the port region. The presence of opportunistic pathogenic bacteria like Pseudomonas aeruginosa in all of
the stations in high abundance shows their adaptability to spatial and temporal variations of different parameters which have been indicated by their genome
study (Silby et al., 2011). Fish pathogenic bacteria like A. hydrophila counts were higher in bottom waters and didn’t show much variation spatially. Vibrio
groups that included V. cholerae and V. parahaemolyticus were mostly present in the northern stations which included PBM, TPM, and also CHP. A study by
Prasanthan et al. (2011) from the Kerala coast has shown that V. cholerae and V. parahaemolyticus have a positive correlation with each other similar to our
study where these two are co-present in the stations. Increased counts of E. coli, A. hydrophila, and P. mirabilis in the bottom waters of most of the stations
indicate mixing activity near-bottom sediment and their mode of respiration as these bacteria are facultative anaerobes that can divide niches according to the
availability of oxygen. Bacterial abundance data from Visakhapatnam harbour on 24th Dec 2019 (unpublished) showed very high heterotrophic bacterial
counts, total coliform counts, E. coli, E. feacalis, A. hydrophila, and, P. aeruginosa counts as compared to the data in this study for the same sampling points.
The difference between 24 days during sampling and rainfall during earlier days of sampling was found to be effective in controlling these bacterial numbers.
Additionally, bacterial abundance data directly from the discharge waters of PBM, CHP, TVM and NKP (from the industry before discharge into the sea)
showed consistency with our data from the corresponding discharge points in the sea (Supplementary Table 1). 
A study from a marine coastal zone, in Italy, found that the counts of E. coli, Aeromonas spp. and Vibrio are in the maximum range of 102 CFU/100 ml of
sample (Maugeri et al., 2004). This bacterial range is found to be lower as compared to the data from the present study. A study by Mudryk et al. (2014) from
the coastal Baltic Sea showed the counts of Aeromonas to be higher as compared to Pseudomonas and E. coli, whereas in the present study the highest
numbers were seen for Pseudomonas counts. When studies from the Indian coast are concerned regarding indicator and pathogenic bacteria, the East Coast
(off Chennai) showed a higher abundance of total viable counts, and total coliforms as compared to the West Coast of India (off Kerala) (Sudhanandh et al.,
2012; Vignesh et al., 2012). A study from Chennai coastal water showed TVC/heterotrophic bacterial numbers to be 10 times higher than this study from the
coast of Andhra Pradesh (Vignesh et al., 2012). There have been many similar studies from Visakhapatnam coastal waters (mostly beaches and
Visakhapatnam harbour) which show the water of this region with high pollution index concerning indicator bacteria (Clark et al., 2003; Khandeparker et al.,
2020, Kumar et al., 2017). The current study gives a comparative study of how these bacteria are distributed along the coast from industrial discharge sites as
well as from harbours. 
Occurrence of antibiotic-resistant bacterial isolates
Several of the Enterobacteriaceae isolates were resistant to antibiotics azithromycin, aztreonam, cefuroxime, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, and cefepime. Except
for azithromycin, other antibiotics mentioned have β -lactam rings in their structure. Many bacteria target these β -lactam rings and hydrolyze these antibiotics
as part of their antibiotic resistance mechanism by producing β -lactamase (Paterson & Bonomo, 2005). Bacteria with extended-spectrum β -lactamase (ESBL)
activity from Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonas can confer resistance against most of the β -lactam antibiotics such as penicillin, cephalosporins and
aztreonam (Pang et al., 2019; Paterson & Bonomo, 2005). Resistance of many isolates to cefepime which is a 4th generation cephalosporin is concerning.
Azithromycin which belongs to the macrolide class of antibiotics was shown resistant by 39% of isolates belonging to Enterobacteriaceae. Surprisingly, only
one Pseudomonas isolate showed resistance to an old broad-spectrum antibiotic like tetracycline, same result was seen in the case of another antibiotic
chloramphenicol. Very few of these isolates showed resistance to ampicillin/sulbactam, gentamicin, and ciprooxacin. The carbapenem class of antibiotics
used in the study including imipenem and meropenem also showed resistance by a very few isolates. Carbapenems are generally used as last resort
antibiotics, which are not frequently administered to help prevent antibiotic resistance against them. In our study, the common antibiotics which were shown
resistance by the maximum of the isolates belonged to the class cephalosporins. Farooqui et al. (2019) found out from medical audit data that  β -lactam
cephalosporins had the highest prescription rate followed by  β -lactam penicillin. As the study suggests 23 out of 46 isolates (50%) showed a MAR index of
more than 0.2. A MAR index greater than 0.2 suggests contamination from a source with higher use of antibiotics (Ayandele et al., 2020). There are various
sources from which antibiotic-resistant bacteria can be originated. These bacteria can enter the natural environment by various means from human faeces,
live-stock manure slurry, agricultural land runoff, sewage water, wild bird, and animals, and, even from pharmaceutical industry discharge during the
manufacturing process of antibiotics (Wellington et al., 2013). A study by Obayiuwana et al. (2018) pointed out the fact that pharmaceutical wastewater
discharge can act as a hot spot for genetic determinants for antibiotic resistance in bacteria. In the current study, the presence of antibiotic-resistant isolates
can be attributed to the widespread open defecation practices near the coastal villages. As there are several pharmaceutical companies’ discharge points
along the study area, they can act as a determinant too. These antibiotic susceptibility data in terms of AR and MAR indexes, as compared to the data
obtained from isolates from different port regions along the east coast of India showed a huge variation (Unpublished data). Data collected from the port
regions belong to two seasons monsoon (August 2019) and pre-monsoon (June 2020). If the percentage of bacterial isolates with a MAR index of more than
0.2 were considered, the current study has a higher (0.46) percentage value as compared to 0.36 % and 0.26 % of the above-mentioned sampling times
Page 7/21
(Supplementary Table 2). The higher values of the indexes in the present study can be attributed to the closeness of the sampling sites to human settlements
and the sampling time, that is December when dilution by fresh water is at the lowest.
Antibiotic susceptibility test from Enterobacteriaceae family from Veraval, West Coast of India showed 80 % resistance to the Ampicillin (Maloo et al., 2014),
and in the present study, it is less than 10 % for the same antibiotic. The same study also reported no resistance to antibiotics of the cephalosporin family
whereas, in the present study, 10-50 % resistance is seen for different antibiotics of the same family. Discharge from antibiotic manufacturing/pharmaceutical
sites gives rise to the development and dissemination of multidrug-resistant bacteria (González -Plaza et al., 2019; Hubeny et al., 2021). Sewage
contamination also affects marine life including sh as studied by Al-Bahry et al. (2009) when antibiotic tests were performed on bacteria isolated from sh.
So, the presence of multi-drug resistant bacteria on the coast as part of this study gives a glimpse of the risk factors that this region faces. Moreover, the
counts of indicator bacteria are more than the standard limit in the study region (Enterococcus feacalis more than 35 CFU/100 ml set in recreational water).
The coliform counts also far exceeded the faecal coliform limit set by the Central pollution control board, India i.e., 100 CFU/100 ml.
Conclusion
Physical forcing controlling the heterotrophic, indicator and pathogenic bacterial abundance in the Bay of Bengal is well studied. Indicator (Escherichia
coli and Enterococcus faecalis) and pathogenic (Aeromonas hydrophila, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella and
Shigella, Vibrio cholera and Vibrio parahaemolyticus) bacteria are found to be present in high numbers in the study region. Counts of E. faecalis were found to
be 103 to 104 times high in Pydibhimavaram and Tammayyapalem stations as compared to the standard for marine recreational water. The difference in the
bacterial counts in the current study was found to be dependent on the presence of coastal villages and industrial discharge points. Antibiotic susceptibility
test of the isolates also revealed multidrug-resistant bacteria in these waters which are of great concern. Continuous monitoring of these bacteria, their
resistance potential and presence of antibiotic residues, their dissemination, persistence in natural waters, and, their source determination can lead to a clearer
understanding of this grave situation.
