Abstract 
Microplastic (MP) pollution in freshwater ecosystems is a growing environmental 
concern, particularly in the fragile Himalayan River systems. This study investigated 
the occurrence, abundance, and characteristics of MPs in water and sediment samples 
collected from the River Uhl, originating from the Thamsar Glacier in the Dhauladhar 
range, Himachal Pradesh, India. Sampling was conducted at three sites representing 
varying degrees of anthropogenic influence and analysed through microscopic exami-
nation and Fourier-Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy analysis. From each site, 
n=3 water samples (1 L each) and sediment samples (1 kg each) were collected. MP 
concentrations were expressed as particles L−1 for water and particles Kg−1 (dry weight) 
for sediment. MP abundance showed clear spatial variability, with lower concentrations 
at the relatively pristine site (50–100 particles L−1 or Kg−1) and comparatively higher lev-
els at downstream sites (100–500 particles L−1 or Kg−1). Fragmented MP was the domi-
nant morphotype in both water (83.16%) and sediment (87.61%), followed by fibres 
and films. Colour analysis revealed a predominance of colourless and blue particles 
in both matrices. FT-IR spectroscopy confirmed the presence of common polymer 
types and revealed site-specific spectral variations, indicating differences in polymer 
composition and degradation states influenced by local environmental conditions. The 
detection of MP even at sites with limited human activity underscores its widespread 
distribution and potential for transport. These findings underscore the need for con-
tinuous monitoring and targeted management strategies to mitigate MP pollution 
and protect freshwater ecosystems in the Himalayan region.

1 Introduction
Plastics are long polymers of carbon and hydrogen, along with oxygen, nitrogen, chlo -
rine, and sulphur. Some of the most commonly and frequently used polymers in our 
everyday life are Low-density polyethylene (LDPE), Polyvinyl chloride (PVC), High-
density polyethylene (HDPE), Polypropylene (PP), and Polyethylene terephthalate 
(PET). LDPE is known for its flexibility and impact resistance, HDPE for its strength, 
PVC for its versatility, PET for its clarity, and PP for its heat resistance. These are used 
in the manufacturing of bottles, bags, clothing, packaging, paints, and PCPs [41]. 
Properties such as low manufacturing cost, water resistance, high strength-to-weight 
ratio, and high thermal and electric insulation properties mark its high production 
[13, 25].
Plastic had an annual production of nearly 445.25 million metric tons in 2025 and is 
expected to reach 516.18 million metric tons in 2035 [54]. Accordingly, over 380 million 
tonnes of plastic waste are produced globally, with India producing 9.4 million tonnes 
[42]. Consequently, India ranks 4th in production (9%) and consumes only 5% of total 
plastic [1, 42].
Such a huge production has aggravated the problem of plastic pollution. These have 
now infiltrated even the most isolated corners of the Earth, including the polar regions. 
In recent years, research into the presence of plastic waste in the Arctic and Antarc -
tic oceans has significantly increased, leading to the unequivocal conclusion that it is a 
tangible, pressing, and significant problem [9, 29]. In the environment, the accumulated 
plastic may undergo fragmentation due to surface abrasion, UV degradation, and chem -
ical weathering. These particles are classified based on size  as mega plastics, macro -
plastics, mesoplastics, and microplastics [56]. Microplastics (MPs) and nanoplastics 
(NPs) are among the most reported emerging toxicants [8]. MPs are defined as artificial

polymers in size ranging between 0.001 and 5  mm, while NPs  are artificial polymers 
with sizes ranging between 1 and 1000 nm [22]. 
MPs exert a significant impact on soil fauna as they alter soil structure, disrupt nutri -
ent cycling, and influence microbial communities [60]. Understanding and examining 
the influence of MPs on soil ecosystem health is crucial for sustainable ecosystem man -
agement [66]. Terrestrial MPs contamination can indirectly affect marine MPs pollution 
through surface runoff, groundwater leaching, and organismal transfer [45]. This inter -
connected pathway of pollution underscores the necessity for comprehensive manage -
ment strategies that address MPs pollution in both terrestrial and aquatic environments 
[15].
MPs particles in water bodies impact aquatic invertebrates and fish by disrupting feed-
ing behaviour, fertility, and organ functions [6, 68]. The extent of harm varies based on 
particle size, dose, and exposure duration. Consequently, MPs present a serious concern 
for human health, through inhalation, dermal contact, contaminated food and water 
sources. Inside the body, MPs can trigger oxidative stress, DNA mutilation, organ mal -
function, metabolic disorders, immune reactions, neurotoxic effects, and reproductive 
and developmental toxicity in humans [51]. Epidemiological data also hint at a possible 
association between MPs exposure and several chronic diseases [11, 37].
Recent studies have demonstrated that MP contamination is not restricted to urban or 
coastal environments but is increasingly reported from remote, high-altitude, and gla -
cier-fed Himalayan ecosystems. MPs have been detected in Himalayan rivers, lakes, and 
cryospheric compartments, highlighting the influence of long-range atmospheric trans -
port, increasing tourism, dam-regulated hydrology, and inadequate waste management 
in mountain regions [3, 33].
Despite the emerging evidence, baseline data on the abundance, composition, and spa-
tial distribution of MPs in glacier-fed river systems of the western Himalaya, particu -
larly across coupled water and sediment matrices, remain extremely limited. This lack of 
region-specific information constrains a comprehensive understanding of MP sources, 
transport pathways, and ecological risks in these fragile mountain ecosystems.
The study hypothesised that before estimating the toxic impacts of MPs, it is essential 
to estimate their presence in the water and sediment of the aquatic system. So, the pre -
sent study aimed to (i) assess the levels and morphological types and chemical nature of 
MPs contamination in water and sediment samples from the River Uhl basin through 
standard methodologies, and (ii) investigate the spatial distribution along different sites 
of the River Uhl. The findings from the present study will help in implementing effective 
management strategies, which are imperative for tackling the longstanding ramifications 
of MPs pollution on aquatic ecosystems [65].
2 Materials and methods
2.1 Study area
The study was conducted in the Kangra district of Himachal Pradesh, focusing on the 
River Uhl, originating from the Thamsar Glacier in the Dhauladhar range of the Himala-
yas (31°43′52.4″N, 76°51′15.9″E) (Fig.  1). River Uhl serves as a crucial water source for 
surrounding communities and is used for hydroelectric power generation. Furthermore, 
Page 4 of 15Kumar et al. Discover Environment (2026) 4:523 
the region attracts tourists seeking solace in its natural splendour, with activities such as 
trekking, angling, and eco-tourism gaining popularity.
Three distinct sites were selected for water and sediment sampling for the presence 
of MPs along the River Uhl. The first site  (Site I) was Kukar gundha (32.111916° Lat. 
and 76.760869° Long.) situated approximately 3 kms away from the last village in the 
area, minimizing human intervention and ensuring a more pristine, contamination-free 
environment for sampling. The second site  (Site II), Zero Point (32.046732° Lat. and 
76.833532° Long.), was  located 14 kms downstream from the first site, where numer -
ous small tributaries join the main river, and agricultural lands line the riverbanks. This 
location provides insight into the potential impact of agriculture and domestic activities 
on water and sediment quality. Finally, the third site (Site III) was Barot (32.037697° Lat. 
and 76.844332° Long.) positioned at the dam constructed for storing water for a hydro -
electric project.
2.2 Sample collection and preparation (water and sediment)
Sampling of water and sediment (n=3) was made in November, 2024, from three distinct 
sites of River Uhl as detailed in Fig. 1.
2.2.1 Water sample preparation
At each sampling site, three independent water samples were collected using pre-
cleaned glass bottles (1 L each), following the plastic contamination avoidance protocol 
recommended by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) [36]. 
Samples were collected from the side of the river at a depth of 0.5 m below the surface of 
the water [59]. To ensure sample homogeneity, collected water samples (1 L per jar),total 
3 L per site were gently shaken before laboratory processing.

Individual samples were filtered sequentially through stainless-steel sieves of 
325 µm and 100 µm mesh size to remove large debris while retaining MP within the 
defined size range (100 µm–5 mm) [32]. Particles retained were carefully recovered 
into a clean glass beaker, and volume was adjusted to 100 mL using Milli-Q water to 
ensure efficient chemical digestion [4 ]. Organic matter removal was performed using 
a modified Fenton’s reagent method by adding 20 mL of H2O2 (30%), followed by gen -
tle heating at 60 °C for 30 min until visible organic material was completely digested. 
This temperature was selected to minimize potential thermal alteration of MP parti -
cles while ensuring effective oxidation.
For the density separation, the sample was added with NaCl (0.5 M) and heated at 
75 °C for a minimum of 30 min until complete dissolution of the salt to facilitate salt 
dissolution without compromising polymer integrity [36]. The suspension was trans -
ferred to a density separator and allowed to settle for 24 h. The supernatant was sub -
sequently filtered through a glass microfiber filter (11 µm pore size).
2.2.2 Sediment sample preparation
Three independent sediment samples (1 kg each) were collected from each site from 
depth of 15 cm using stainless steel shovels, wrapped in aluminium foil, and trans -
ported to the laboratory [14, 21]. Collected sediment samples were air-dried at ambi -
ent room temperature to remove moisture and establish a dry-weight basis for MPs 
analysis. After drying, samples were gently homogenized and sieved through stain -
less-steel meshes (1–5 mm) to remove large debris and macroplastics.
For MP extraction, a 50  g subsample was taken from each 1  kg sediment batch 
(three subsamples per site) to minimize contamination risk and ensure uniform 
chemical processing. Each subsample was transferred to a clean glass beaker and 
mixed with 20 mL of distilled water, followed by mechanical stirring at 2200 rpm for 
15 min to disaggregate sediment particles [21].
Density separation was performed by adding 20  mL of saturated NaCl solution 
(5  M) followed by 20  mL of ZnCl2 solution (5  M) to increase the solution density 
and facilitate the flotation of MPs with varying polymer densities. The mixture was 
allowed to settle overnight (≈ 24 h) to enable clear phase separation.
The supernatant was carefully decanted and filtered using Whatman filter paper 
(CAT No. 42) into a clean glass beaker. Organic matter digestion was conducted by 
adding 30% H2O2, followed by incubation at 60 °C for 24 h in an oven to ensure effec -
tive oxidation while minimizing thermal damage to MPs [32]. Following digestion, the 
samples were re-filtered using Whatman CAT No. 42 filter paper for visualization.
2.2.3 MPs visualization
The retained suspected particles on a glass microfiber filter (for water) and a What -
man CAT No. 42 filter paper (for sediment) were visually examined under a MAG -
NUS CH201 compound microscope equipped with a digital camera at 10× and 
40× magnification. Suspected MPs were manually counted and categorized based 
on shape and colour. A fibre resembles thin threads and is often coloured, while 
Page 6 of 15Kumar et al. Discover Environment (2026) 4:523 
fragments are pieces of plastic with diverse shapes. A film is a thin plastic sheet, such 
as those in grocery bags and food wrappers [48]. Particle size measurements were 
performed using ImageJ software.

2.4 Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy analysis
Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy identifies functional groups of 
particles based on their unique infrared absorption patterns [35, 49]. Dried glass 
membrane filter (water samples) and Whatman CAT No. 42 filter paper (sediment 
samples) containing particles were processed for FT-IR spectra (Agilent Technology 
Cary 360 h) in a spectrum range of 600–3600  cm−1 [2].
2.5 Control of contamination
Steps to reduce the potential plastic contamination included avoiding plastic appa -
ratus and cleansing each apparatus with water and ethanol prior to use. Additionally, 
samples were meticulously wrapped in aluminium foil to prevent contamination dur -
ing storage and transportation. To prevent airborne contamination, processing was 
performed under the laminar flow, procedural blanks were maintained to exclude the 
contamination, reagents were prefiltered and saved in covered glass containers, and 
clean-lab practices such as wearing of cotton lab-coat were followed.
2.6 Statistical analysis
For the quantitative assessment of MPs distribution along the three sites, collected 
samples were analysed statistically. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk 
test indicated that the data was normally distributed. Thus, parametric test- one way 
ANOVA with post hoc Tukey’s test at P < 0.05 was applied and analysis was done with 
SPSS 18.0 statistical software.

3 Results and discussion
3.1 MPs concentration in the sample
The study sites comprised of sites with varying degree of human interferences. The 
analysis of MPs concentrations along the River Uhl has revealed significant spatial 
heterogeneity, with Sites II and III exhibiting notably higher levels than Site I. How -
ever, the discovery of MPs even in Site I is noteworthy because the site is situated in 
an area with minimal human intervention and located 2–4 km away from the nearest 
village, thus providing valuable insight into MPs pervading even remote environments 
with 60 ± 5 particles L−1 in water and 96 ± 7 particles Kg−1 in sediment (Table  1). 
The noteworthy abundance of MPs in Antarctica snow samples with the least human 
intervention reported an average of 29 particles L−1, with fibres predominating and 
polyethylene terephthalate (PET) as the primary polymer [5 ]. At Site II, where anthro -
pogenic activities such as agricultural practices occurred without stringent regula -
tions on plastic usage, MPs contamination was found to be intensified with a count of 
100 ± 16 MPs L−1 in water and 161 ± 18 MPs Kg−1 in sediment (Table  1). Agricultural 
and household unrecycled plastic can be considered a source of plastic pollution [10, 
39]. Meanwhile, Site III, characterized by the presence of dams, demonstrated a dif -
ferent dynamic (Table 1). Here, the water collected and distributed for hydroelectric 
and drinking purposes contains visible plastic debris, indicating the transport of MPs 
through hydrological processes. Researchers have found that dams influence the river 
transport of MP pollution by trapping and redistributing plastic debris downstream, 
altering flow dynamics, and potentially concentrating MP in reservoirs [18].
Site-wise MPs distribution on the River Uhl, reported Site I, with a comparatively 
low concentration in both water and sediment samples, with fewer than 100 particles 
(Table 1). In contrast, Sites II and III fell within the category of moderate to high con -
centration, with MPs levels ranging from 100 to 500 particles per litre or Kg [5, 27]. The 
findings of the present study from the River Uhl are largely consistent with previously 
reported MP investigations in freshwater systems (Table  2). Like the studies conducted 
by Izlal et al. (2024) in the Surma River and [23, 24] across river water, sediments, and 
biota, the present study recorded low to moderate MP contamination with clear spatial 
variability influenced by anthropogenic activities. In all these studies, sediments or sedi -
ment matrices exhibited equal or higher MP abundance than surface water, highlighting 
their role as important sinks for MPs.

These findings indicate the complex interplay of anthropogenic activities and hydro -
logical processes in shaping MPs contamination patterns. Factors like urbanization, agri-
cultural practices, tourism, and industrial discharge contribute to the MPs pollution in 
the streams [12].
MPs pollution presents diverse risks to both aquatic ecosystems and human health 
[40]. The smaller size and large surface area of MPs make them easily ingestible by a 
wide range of aquatic organisms in aquatic systems. When aquatic organisms ingest 
MPs, they can experience physical harm, reduced feeding efficiency, and behavioural 
changes. Further, it can disrupt food webs and ecosystem functions [19, 55]. Notably, 
MPs act as carriers for persistent organic pollutants (POPs) and heavy metals, which 
can adhere to their surfaces [7, 13]. These can synergistically increase toxicity, posing 
enhanced negative impacts on aquatic organisms and human consumers [28].
3.2 MPs characteristics
Under the compound microscope, a preliminary qualitative assessment of MPs identifi -
cation based on shape and colour was made to evaluate the origin, environmental fate, 
ecological risk (Fig. 1). MPs that exist in distinct shapes are categorized into fibres, frag -
ments, and films. MPs observed from the study sites were in the size range of 4–11 µm, 
15–36 µm, and 5–10 µm for fragments, fibres, and films, respectively (Fig. 2).

3.2.1 Shape
A quantitative assessment of water and sediment samples suggested the pervasive pres -
ence of MPs. The water samples collected from the three sites reported a predominance 
of fragmented MPs, constituting 83.16% followed by fibres (8.42%), and films (8.42%) 
(Fig. 3A). Similar trend was observed in sediment sample with fragments (87.61%), fibres 
(6.41%), and films (5.97%) (Fig.  3B). The dominance of fragmented MPs in both water 
and sediment samples observed in the present study aligns well with findings reported 
by Izlal et al. [23, 24] and Roy et al. [47], where fragments and fibres were identified as 
the most prevalent morphologies, indicating secondary fragmentation of larger plastic 
debris as a major source. In concordance with results, Karthik et al. [26] highlighted the 
prevalence of MPs fragments (47–50%) compared to fibres (24–27%) and foam (10–
19%). These smaller MPs particles may originate from the fragmentation of large pieces, 
influenced by various physical, biological, and chemical factors [31]. Similar trends of 
higher fragment abundance have been noted in other geographical locations, such as 
South China, which has Xijin Wetland Park in Nanning [61] and China Hong Lake and 
Dongting Lake [62], representative of a broader pattern of MPs pollution. Fibres can be 
traced to the presence of textile at sites due to waste dumping and sewage flow [39].

3.2.2 Colour
Colour-wise assessment of MPs in water samples indicated colourless MPs as the most 
abundant, accounting for 41.08% of the total, with blue (39.73%), purple (13.47%), and 
yellow (5.05%) variants (Fig.  3C). The sediment samples collected from the three sites 
exhibited concordant prevalence of MPs. Colourless MPs dominated in the sediment 
environment at 42.26%, with blue (38.72%), purple (15.27%), and yellow (3.76%) MPs 
(Fig.  3D). Interestingly, green coloured particles were observed only in water sample. 
Accordingly, site III (Barot) followed by site II (Zero point) and lastly site I (Kukar gun -
dha) followed the level of MPs contamination. Furthermore, the predominance of col -
ourless and blue MPs in the River Uhl corresponds closely with earlier studies [43, 47], 
which attributed these colours to common consumer plastics such as packaging materi -
als and bottles. Further, the abundance of colourless MPs suggested possible bleaching of 
MPs by UV light and other environmental factors [61].

ations in peak positions and intensities at Sites II and III. Sediment samples also exhib -

4 Conclusion
This study provides baseline evidence of MPs contamination in the glacier-fed River Uhl, 
Himachal Pradesh. Analysis of water and sediment samples revealed the ubiquitous pres-
ence of MPs in both environmental matrices, with fragments as the dominant shape, fol-
lowed by fibres and films. Predominance of colourless, followed by blue, purple, and yellow 
MPs particles, reflected commonly used consumer plastics as the primary source. MPs 
concentrations exhibited marked spatial heterogeneity, with higher abundances at Sites II 
and III compared to the relatively remote Site I, underscoring the influence of anthropo-
genic activities and hydrological modifications. Notably, the detection of MPs even at Site 
I highlights the ability of MPs to disperse and persist in less-disturbed Himalayan environ-
ments. FT-IR analysis confirmed the presence of prevalent polymers—polystyrene, poly-
ethylene, and polypropylene, while site-specific spectral variations suggested differences in 
polymer sources and degradation states between water and sediment. Despite limitations 
related to spatial coverage, absence of seasonal sampling and small sampling size, this study 
establishes an important reference point for MP pollution in glacier-fed Himalayan rivers.
Conclusively, addressing MP pollution requires a multifaceted approach encompassing 
prevention, remediation, and policy interventions. Further, MP source reduction strategies 
include promotion of sustainable consumption patterns, development of eco-friendly alter-
natives to plastic, wastewater management, and long-term monitoring strategies.
