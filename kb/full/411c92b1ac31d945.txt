Sustainable Removal of Triclosan from Aqueous Solutions 
using a Green‑Synthesized Biochar‑Supported Fe/Ni 
Material
Dinh Thi Lan Phuong  · Nam Huy Nguyen · Toan Vu Duc · 
Nguyen Trong Quang · Le Thi Thanh Tra · Thu Huong Le · Mai Phung Thi

Abstract Triclosan (TCS) is a persistent antimi-
crobial compound frequently detected in aquatic 
environments and inadequately removed by conven-
tional wastewater treatment processes. In this study, 
a biochar-supported Fe/Ni material (Bio-Fe/Ni) was 
synthesized via a green approach using Syzygium ner‑
vosum leaf extract as a natural reducing and stabiliz-
ing agent and rice husk derived biochar. Structural 
and spectroscopic analyses (SEM, EDS, XRD, FTIR, 
and BET) indicated the presence of Fe/Ni-contain-
ing phases distributed on a porous biochar enriched 
with oxygen-containing functional groups. Batch 
adsorption experiments demonstrated that Bio-Fe/
Ni achieved a maximum TCS removal efficiency of 
approximately 79–80% under the optimized conditions 
of pH 5, adsorbent dosage of 1.2  g L⁻ 1, contact time 
of 120 min, and temperature of 30 °C, corresponding 
to a maximum experimental adsorption capacity of 
6.6 mg g⁻1. Adsorption kinetics were best described by 
the pseudo-second-order model (R2 > 0.99), whereas 
equilibrium data were better represented by the Freun-
dlich isotherm, suggesting adsorption on a heterogene-
ous surface. Combined characterization and adsorption 
results suggest that TCS adsorption may involve pore 
filling, π–π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and possi-
ble contributions from Fe/Ni-associated surface func-
tionalities. Reusability tests revealed a gradual decline 
in adsorption performance over successive cycles, pos-
sibly because of incomplete desorption and gradual 
changes in the adsorbent surface during regeneration. 
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study 
reporting the use of Syzygium nervosum leaf extract 
for the green synthesis of a biochar-supported Fe/Ni 
material for triclosan adsorption. The results demon-
strate the feasibility of producing Fe/Ni-functionalized 
biochar through a plant-mediated green synthesis route 
and provide fundamental insights into its adsorption 
behavior toward triclosan under controlled laboratory 
conditions, establishing a basis for future optimization 
of adsorption performance and material stability.
Highlights 
• A biochar-supported Fe/Ni material was synthe-
sized via a green route using Syzygium nervosum 
leaf extract.
• Rice-husk biochar served as a porous support for 
Fe/Ni-containing species with mixed metallic and 
oxide phases.

1 Introduction
Triclosan (TCS, C₁₂H ₇Cl₃O₂) is a synthetic broad-
spectrum antimicrobial compound widely incorporated 
into personal care products, medical disinfectants, 
textiles, and household materials (Zhou et  al. (2023); 
Tong et al. (2024). Due to its extensive use and phys-
icochemical stability, TCS has become a ubiquitous 
contaminant in aquatic environments and is frequently 
detected in surface waters, wastewater effluents, sedi-
ments, and biosolids at concentrations ranging from ng 
 L−1 to μg L−1 (Jiang et al., 2024). Conventional waste-
water treatment plants are not specifically designed to 
remove TCS, resulting in its continuous release into 
receiving water bodies and contributing to its persis-
tence in the environment (Matesun et al., 2024; Wang 
and Liang 2021; Wang et al., 2024). Growing concerns 
regarding TCS contamination have led to increasingly 
stringent water quality guidelines established by regu-
latory agencies. For example, the Canadian Federal 
Water Quality Guideline for the protection of aquatic 
life establishes a threshold value of 0.47 µg L−1 for tri-
closan in freshwater. Similarly, environmental quality 
standards within the European regulatory framework 
are in the low ng L⁻1 range, highlighting the ecological 
risks posed by TCS even at trace concentrations (Wang 
& Liang, 2021; Gupta et al., 2024)
Numerous studies have demonstrated that TCS can 
bioaccumulate in aquatic organisms and agricultural 
crops, leading to potential human exposure through 
contaminated drinking water and food consumption 
(Adegbola et  al., 2024). Adverse effects associated 
with TCS include endocrine disruption, the develop-
ment of antibiotic resistance, oxidative stress, and tox-
icity to aquatic organisms, even at low concentrations 
(Karina Atengueño-Reyes et  al., 2023; Xiaolei Teng 
et al., 2024). Consequently, TCS has been recognized 
as an emerging contaminant of concern, prompting 
increasing efforts to develop effective and sustainable 
treatment technologies for its removal from aquatic 
environments (Chen Sun et al., 2023).
Various treatment technologies have been inves-
tigated for TCS removal, including biological deg-
radation, membrane filtration, advanced oxidation 
processes, photocatalysis, electrochemical treatment, 
and adsorption (Qingqing Pang et  al., 2024). Bio-
logical treatment is generally cost-effective but may 
be limited by the persistence of TCS and its antimi-
crobial activity. Advanced oxidation and photocata-
lytic processes can achieve high removal efficiencies; 
however, they often require substantial energy input, 
chemical reagents, or relatively complex operating 
conditions. Membrane-based technologies provide 
effective physical separation but are frequently con-
strained by membrane fouling, concentrating manage-
ment, and relatively high operational costs. Among 
these treatment approaches, adsorption has attracted 
considerable attention because of its simple opera-
tion, broad applicability, and compatibility with exist-
ing water treatment systems (Islam et al., 2025; Song 
et  al., 2024). In addition, adsorption generally does 
not generate transformation by-products and can be 
readily incorporated as a polishing or tertiary treat-
ment step, making it a promising approach for the 
removal of emerging contaminants from water.
Biochar derived from agricultural residues has 
emerged as a promising adsorbent because of its 
porous structure, abundant surface functionalities, and 
the utilization of renewable biomass resources (Hu 
et  al., 2020; Laishram et  al., 2025). Previous studies 
have suggested that triclosan adsorption onto biochar 
may involve multiple interactions, including hydropho-
bic partitioning, pore filling, hydrogen bonding, and 
π–π interactions between the aromatic domains of bio-
char and triclosan molecules (Cho et  al., 2021; Tong 
et al., 2016; Babu Vamsi and Devatha, 2024). Never -
theless, pristine biochar often exhibits limited adsorp-
tion capacity toward triclosan, prompting efforts to 
enhance its performance through surface modification.

Among various modification strategies, the incor -
poration of iron-based materials has attracted consid-
erable attention because it can alter surface properties 
and introduce additional adsorption domains (Gao 
et al., 2019). However, zero valent iron particles are 
susceptible to oxidation and agglomeration, which 
may reduce their effectiveness during water treat-
ment applications. To overcome these limitations, 
bimetallic Fe/Ni systems have been developed, as the 
presence of nickel may facilitate electron transfer, 
improve particle stability, and mitigate surface pas-
sivation (Yuanqiong Lin et al., 2019; Bingnan Zhou 
et  al., 2024). When immobilized on porous biochar 
matrices, Fe/Ni-containing particles may exhibit 
improved dispersion while modifying the surface 
characteristics and adsorption properties of the result-
ing composite material (Li et  al., 2017; Song et  al., 
2024).
In parallel with advances in material design, green 
synthesis strategies have attracted increasing atten-
tion as alternatives to conventional chemical reduc-
tion methods because they utilize naturally occurring 
biomolecules instead of synthetic reducing agents. 
Plant extracts rich in polyphenols, flavonoids, and 
other bioactive compounds can function as reducing, 
capping, and stabilizing agents during the synthesis 
of metal-containing materials, thereby reducing the 
use of chemical reducing agents such as sodium boro-
hydride (Góral-Kowalczyk et al., 2024). In addition, 
these phytochemicals may introduce oxygen-contain-
ing surface functionalities that can modify the physic-
ochemical properties of the synthesized materials and 
potentially influence their adsorption behavior (Gao 
et al., 2019; Gupta et al., 2024).
Syzygium nervosum is a polyphenol-rich plant 
widely distributed in Southeast Asia, including Viet-
nam, and its leaves have been reported to possess 
strong reducing and antioxidant properties (Lan et al., 
2025). Rice husk is an abundant agricultural by-prod-
uct in Vietnam and other rice-producing countries. 
Its conversion into biochar produces a porous carbo-
naceous material that can serve as support for Fe/Ni-
containing species while promoting the valorization 
of agricultural residues within a circular economy 
framework (Almeida-Naranjo et al., 2024).
Recent studies have demonstrated the applicability 
of green-synthesized Fe-based materials, Fe/Ni bime-
tallic systems, and biochar-derived adsorbents for the 
removal of triclosan and other emerging contaminants 
(Gao et al., 2019; Yuanqiong Lin  et al., 2019; Rik-
abi et  al., 2024; Shirani et  al., 2025). Nevertheless, 
studies integrating plant-mediated Fe/Ni synthesis 
with biochar-supported Fe/Ni materials for triclosan 
adsorption remain scarce. Moreover, the influence of 
plant-derived surface functionalities introduced dur -
ing green synthesis on triclosan adsorption behavior 
and the underlying adsorption mechanisms remains 
insufficiently understood.
Therefore, the objective of this study was to syn-
thesize a Bio-Fe/Ni material through a green syn-
thesis route using rice husk biochar as a porous sup-
port and Syzygium nervosum leaf extract as a natural 
reducing and stabilizing agent. The synthesized mate-
rial was characterized using SEM, EDS, XRD, FTIR, 
and BET analyses and subsequently evaluated for tri-
closan removal under different operating conditions. 
Adsorption kinetics, equilibrium behavior, thermody -
namic characteristics, regeneration performance, and 
the possible adsorption mechanisms were systemati-
cally investigated to provide fundamental insights into 
the adsorption behavior of the synthesized Bio-Fe/Ni 
material under controlled laboratory conditions. To 
the best of our knowledge, the use of Syzygium nervo‑
sum leaf extract as a natural reducing and stabilizing 
agent for fabricating biochar-supported Fe/Ni materi-
als for triclosan adsorption has not yet been reported. 
The present study demonstrates the feasibility of pro-
ducing Fe/Ni-functionalized biochar through a plant-
mediated green synthesis route using renewable bio-
mass resources. The findings provide fundamental 
insights into the adsorption behavior of triclosan and 
establish a basis for future optimization of adsorption 
performance and long-term material stability.
2 Materials and Methods
2.1 Materials and Chemicals
Triclosan (≥ 97%), iron(II) sulfate heptahydrate 
(FeSO₄.7H₂O, ≥ 99%) and nickel(II) nitrate hexa-
hydrate (NiNO3.6H₂O, ≥ 98%), sodium hydroxide 
(NaOH, ≥ 98%), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄, 95–98%), 
ethanol (≥ 99.8%) from Merck, ultrapure water (18.2 
MΩ.cm) which was used in the experiments.
Dried rice husks were collected from Thuan An 
commune, Hanoi, Vietnam. Fresh leaves of Syzygium

nervosum were collected from Thanh Tri, Ha Noi, 
Vietnam. The Syzygium nervosum leaves selected are

Fresh leaves of Syzygium nervosum were rinsed with 
deionized water, and shade dried before extraction.
2.2 Green Synthesis of Bio-Fe/Ni
Rice husk biochar was prepared by slow pyrolysis 
under oxygen-limited conditions at 500–550  °C for 
2  h. After cooling to room temperature, the biochar 
was ground and sieved to obtain a uniform particle 
size. The prepared biochar was stored in an airtight 
container until further use.
Dried Syzygium nervosum leaves (50  g) were cut 
into small pieces and mixed with 500  mL of deion-
ized water. The mixture was heated at 80  °C for 
60 min, allowed to cool to room temperature, and fil-
tered through a 0.45 μm cellulose acetate membrane 
(Whatman). The filtrate was stored at 4 °C and used 
as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent.
Based on preliminary optimization experiments, 
Bio-Fe/Ni was synthesized by mixing 100  mL of 
0.1  M FeSO₄ and 100  mL of 0.1  M Ni(NO 3)₂ with 
400 mL of Syzygium nervosum extract at a volumet-
ric ratio of 1:1:4 (Fe:Ni:extract). The mixture was 
continuously stirred at room temperature for 30 min. 
Biochar was dispersed in the Syzygium nervosum leaf 
extract under continuous stirring to ensure uniform 
wetting and dispersion. The reaction was maintained 
under controlled temperature and stirring conditions 
to promote the gradual reduction of metal ions and 
the in situ formation of Fe/Ni bimetallic particles on 
the biochar surface. After completion of the reaction, 
the solid product was collected by vacuum filtration, 
washed three times with deionized water and absolute 
ethanol to remove residual impurities, dried at 60 °C 
for 48 h, and stored in a desiccator until further use. 
The obtained material was denoted as Bio-Fe/Ni.
The pH of the reaction mixture during the green syn-
thesis process ranged from 6.4 to 6.8, and no additional 
pH adjustment was applied. This mildly acidic to near 
neutral condition was provided by the combined effect 
of the metal precursor solution and the Syzygium ner ‑
vosum leaf extract. This pH range is considered suitable 
and deposition of Fe/Ni species on the biochar surface 
while minimizing rapid metal hydroxide precipitation 
that may occur under strongly alkaline conditions.
The pyrolysis temperature of 500–550  °C was 
selected because this range provides a suitable bal-
ance between carbonization and preservation of sur -
face functional groups. Although higher pyrolysis 
temperatures may increase carbon content and aroma-
ticity, they can also reduce oxygen-containing func-
tional groups such as hydroxyl and carboxyl groups 
that may contribute to hydrogen bonding and metal 
anchoring. In addition, moderate pyrolysis tempera-
tures generally provide acceptable biochar yield while 
maintaining sufficient surface functionality for subse-
quent Fe/Ni immobilization. Therefore, the selected 
temperature range was considered appropriate for 
preparing a biochar support supporting Fe/Ni deposi-
tion and triclosan adsorption.
2.3 Material Characterization
The morphology, elemental composition, crystalline 
phases, and surface functional groups of the synthe-
sized Bio-Fe/Ni material were characterized using scan-
ning electron microscopy (SEM), energy-dispersive 
X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), 
and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). 
These analyses were performed to examine the struc-
tural and physicochemical characteristics of the synthe-
sized material before and after triclosan adsorption.
The morphology of the synthesized Bio-Fe/Ni 
material was examined using field-emission scan-
ning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Hitachi S-4800, 
Japan) operated at 5 kV. The elemental composition 
before and after TCS adsorption was determined by 
energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, INCA 
system, Oxford Instruments, UK). Additional ele-
mental analyses were performed using an Octane 
Elite 25 detector (EDAX Ametek, USA), where 
appropriate.
Surface functional groups were analyzed by Fou-
rier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Jasco 
FTIR-4600) using the KBr pellet method. Crystalline 
phases were identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD, 
Bruker AXS diffractometer) using Cu Kα radiation 
(λ = 1.5406 Å) operated at 45 kV and 40 mA over a 
2θ range of 10–80°. All characterization analyses 
were conducted following established procedures 
reported for biosynthesized Fe-based materials and 
biochar-supported adsorbents.

2.4 Triclosan Quantification
Triclosan (TCS) concentrations were determined by 
high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; 
Agilent 1100 Infinity II) equipped with a UV detector 
and a Zorbax Hypersil C18 column (200 × 4.0  mm, 
5  μm). The mobile phase consisted of metha-
nol and acetonitrile (30:70, v/v) at a flow rate of 
0.5  mL  min−1, with the column temperature main-
tained at 30 °C. Detection was performed at 280 nm. 
The injection volume was 5 µL, and the run time was 
10  min. All samples were filtered through 0.22  μm 
syringe filters prior to analysis. The calibration curve 
showed excellent linearity (R2 > 0.999). The limits of 
detection and quantification were 0.05 and 0.15  mg 
 L−1, respectively. Method accuracy was verified by 
spike and recovery experiments in deionized water, 
with recoveries ranging from 92 to 105%.
2.5 Batch Adsorption Experiments
Batch adsorption experiments were performed in 
50 mL centrifuge tubes containing 25 mL of triclosan 
(TCS) solution. Unless otherwise specified, the exper-
iments were conducted using an initial TCS concen-
tration of 5 mg L⁻1, an adsorbent dosage of 1.2 g L⁻ 1, 
pH 5.0 ± 0.1, a shaking speed of 200 rpm, and a tem-
perature of 30 °C. The pH was adjusted using 0.1 M 
NaOH or H₂SO₄. After the designated contact time, 
the suspensions were centrifuged at 2500  rpm for 
5  min and filtered through 0.2  μm membrane filters 
(Whatman, 47 mm diameter) prior to TCS analysis.
To evaluate the effects of operating parameters, 
individual variables were varied while all other exper-
imental conditions were kept constant. Specifically, 
the effect of solution pH (2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 9, and 11) was 
investigated using initial TCS concentrations of 10, 
15, and 20  mg L⁻ 1. The effects of adsorbent dosage 
(0.4–2.8 g L⁻ 1) and contact time (30–240 min) were 
evaluated using an initial TCS concentration of 5 mg 
L⁻1 under the optimum pH condition (pH 5) identified 
in the pH experiments. Adsorption isotherm experi-
ments were carried out using initial TCS concentra-
tions ranging from 5 to 25 mg L⁻1 under the optimum 
experimental conditions (pH 5, adsorbent dosage of 
1.2 g L⁻ 1, contact time of 120 min, and temperature 
of 30 °C). All experiments were performed in tripli-
cate with appropriate controls. Adsorption capacity 
and removal efficiency were calculated based on the 
mass balance between the initial and equilibrium TCS 
concentrations.

2.7 Reusability Capacity
Reusability experiments were performed to assess the 
reusability of the Bio-Fe/Ni material. After adsorp-
tion, the spent adsorbent was separated and subjected 
to desorption using an appropriate solvent system. 
The regenerated material was then reused in subse-
quent adsorption cycles under identical conditions. 
The removal efficiency over multiple cycles was 
recorded to evaluate the stability and practical appli-
cability of the material.
The reusability of the Bio-Fe/Ni adsorbent was 
assessed over five consecutive adsorption desorption 
cycles. After each adsorption run, the spent adsorbent 
was collected by centrifugation (4000  rpm, 10  min) 
and washed three times with deionized water to remove 
residual triclosan. The material was then regenerated by 
soaking the adsorbent in ethanol at a ratio of 1 g:20 mL 
(w/v) for 2 h under gentle stirring at room temperature. 
After desorption, the adsorbent was centrifuged and 
filtered, followed by three additional washings with 
20  mL of deionized water each, until the wash water 
reached approximately neutral pH (about 7). The regen-
erated adsorbent was dried at 60 °C for 12 h and reused 
in the subsequent cycle. Adsorption efficiency was cal-
culated after each cycle based on the initial and residual 
triclosan concentrations. All regeneration experiments 
were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.
2.8 Statistical Analysis
All statistical analyses were conducted using Micro-
soft Excel for preliminary calculations and Origin 
software (OriginLab Corporation, USA) for data 
visualization. Results are expressed as the mean of 
three replicates. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was 
conducted to determine statistical significance, and 
Duncan’s multiple range test was applied to compare 
means at a significance level of p < 0.05. All experi-
ments were conducted in triplicate, and the results are 
presented as mean ± standard deviation (Fig. 1).
3 Results and Discussion
3.1 Characterization of Bio-Fe/Ni Material
3.1.1 SEM, EDS, XRD Analysis
Figure 2 presents photographs of the synthesized Bio-
Fe/Ni material, together with SEM images acquired 
at magnifications corresponding to scale bars of 1 and 
5 µm, the corresponding EDS spectrum, and the XRD 
pattern. The synthesized material was obtained as a 
fine black powder. SEM observations showed that 
Fe/Ni particles were distributed on the surface of the 
rice husk derived biochar, which exhibited a porous 
structure with abundant cavities. The Fe–Ni particles 
were relatively well dispersed across the biochar sur -
face, although slight aggregation was observed. This 
behavior is commonly reported in bimetallic Fe/Ni 
systems and may result from particle–particle inter -
actions, high surface energy, and partial coalescence 
during the reduction and drying processes. Similar 
observations have been reported for Fe/Ni nanoparti-
cles supported on biochar, where the biochar matrix 
facilitates particle dispersion but does not completely 
prevent localized aggregation of metallic particles (Li 
et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2019).

The EDS spectrum confirmed the presence of Fe, 
Ni, Si, C, and O elements in the Bio-Fe/Ni material. 
Silicon was mainly attributed to the inherent silica 
content of rice husk, whereas the strong signals of 
carbon and oxygen originated primarily from the bio-
char matrix and oxygen-containing functional groups. 
Minor signals corresponding to sulfur and phospho-
rus were also detected. These elements may be asso-
ciated with residual inorganic species derived from 
the plant extract and synthesis precursors (Gao et al., 
2019). Sulfur species may partly originate from the 
FeSO₄ precursor used during synthesis. The detec-
tion of Fe and Ni signals, together with SEM observa-
tions, suggests that Fe/Ni particles were successfully 
distributed on the biochar surface during the green 
synthesis process.
The XRD pattern of the Bio-Fe/Ni material exhib-
ited a broad diffraction peak centered at approxi-
mately 2θ = 22°, which is characteristic of the amor -
phous carbon structure of biochar and the presence 
of SiO₂ derived from rice husk. Additional diffrac-
tion peaks located at around 2θ = 35.6°, 44.5°, and 
51.8° were attributed to Fe₂O₃, Fe–Ni alloy, and NiO 
phases, respectively. The coexistence of these reflec-
tions suggests the presence of Fe–Ni species asso-
ciated with the biochar matrix. The simultaneous 
occurrence of metallic (Fe–Ni) and oxide (Fe₂O₃ and 
NiO) phases indicates partial oxidation of the met-
als during synthesis or drying, which is commonly 
observed in green synthesis processes.
The relatively broad and low-intensity diffraction 
peaks suggest that the Fe/Ni-related phases possessed 
low crystallinity and small crystallite domains. Simi-
lar XRD characteristics have been reported for Fe–Ni 
or Fe-modified biochar materials synthesized using 
plant-derived reducing agents (Yuanqiong Lin et al., 
2019; Lan et al., 2025). The coexistence of metallic 
and oxide phases may provide diverse surface envi-
ronments that are potentially favorable for adsorption 
processes. The XRD analysis was primarily employed 
to identify the major crystalline phases present in the 
synthesized material. Because the diffraction profile 
was dominated by a broad amorphous background 
associated with the biochar matrix and the Fe/Ni-
related reflections were relatively weak, detailed crys-
tallographic analysis and crystallite size determina-
tion were beyond the scope of the present study.
3.1.2 FTIR Analysis
Figure  3 shows the FTIR spectrum of the Bio-Fe/
Ni material. The broad absorption band centered at 
approximately 3335 cm⁻1 is attributed to O–H stretch-
ing vibrations, which may originate from hydroxyl 
groups and adsorbed moisture within the biochar 
matrix. The band near 1622  cm−1 corresponds to 
C = C stretching vibrations associated with aromatic 
structures in the carbon framework, whereas the peak 
at approximately 1385  cm−1 is assigned to C-H bend-
ing vibrations.
The absorption band observed at 1118  cm−1 is 
attributed to C-O stretching vibrations of alcohol, 
phenolic, or carboxylic groups, indicating the pres-
ence of oxygen-containing functionalities on the 
material surface. The band at around 1035  cm−1 may 
be associated with C-N stretching vibrations or over -
lapping vibrations from oxygen-containing groups, 
suggesting the presence of residual organic species. 
Furthermore, bands located at approximately 615 and 
563 cm⁻1 are assigned to Fe–O and Ni–O vibrations, 
respectively, supporting the presence of iron and 
nickel containing oxide species in the material.
The coexistence of oxygen- and nitrogen-contain-
ing functional groups together with Fe–O and Ni–O 
bonds suggests interactions between the metal spe-
cies and the biochar matrix. Similar Fe/Ni–biochar 
materials have been reported to contain metal spe-

from the bulk solution to internal adsorption domains.

The effect of initial TCS concentration reflects the 
relationship between the number of contaminants pre-

proportion of TCS molecules can access available 
adsorption sites, resulting in higher removal efficien-

concentration dependent trends have been reported

changes in both triclosan speciation and the surface 
chemistry of the adsorbent. Triclosan is predomi-
nantly present in its neutral form under acidic and 
mildly acidic conditions, whereas deprotonation 
becomes increasingly important at alkaline pH. 
Accordingly, the decrease in removal efficiency 
observed above pH 6 may be associated with less 
favorable interactions between deprotonated TCS 
species and the adsorbent surface. In addition, 
deprotonation of oxygen-containing surface groups 
may modify the surface properties of the material 
and further reduce adsorption efficiency. Despite 
this decrease, adsorption may still occur through 
non-electrostatic interactions, including hydropho-
bic partitioning, hydrogen bonding, and possible 
π-π interactions between the aromatic structures of 
triclosan and the carbon matrix. Similar pH depend-

and biochar-based adsorbents used for TCS removal

et al., 2020; Almeida-Naranjo et al., 2024). Overall, 
the observed pH dependence suggests that changes 
in TCS speciation and adsorbent surface chemistry 
are considered important in governing the adsorp-
tion process.

readily accessible adsorption sites had become occu-

on the rate at which triclosan is transferred from the

rate and to compare the suitability of different kinetic 
equations for describing the experimental data. The 
results (Table  1) indicate that both pseudo-first order 
(PFO) and pseudo-second order (PSO) kinetic models 
can describe the adsorption process, but with different 
levels of accuracy. The correlation coefficients (R2)

PFO deviated significantly, further confirming the 
superiority of PSO.

increase in the PSO rate constant with temperature

higher temperatures. However, kinetic parameters 
alone cannot be used to infer the adsorption mecha-
nism or the thermodynamic nature of the process.

model provided the best mathematical description of 
the adsorption kinetics. Nevertheless, kinetic model 
fitting alone cannot be considered definitive evidence 
of the adsorption mechanism, which should be inter -
preted together with the available characterization 
results. In summary, within the scope of this study,

long-term material stability (Fig. 13).
3.6 Adsorption Mechanism
The adsorption mechanism of triclosan (TCS) onto 
the Bio-Fe/Ni material was inferred from the com-
bined evidence obtained from SEM, EDS, FTIR, 
BET, XRD, and batch adsorption experiments. 
Accordingly, the proposed mechanism should be 
regarded as an interpretation based on the available 
evidence rather than direct experimental proof. The 
results suggest that TCS adsorption involves multiple 
complementary interactions rather than a single dom-
inant mechanism (Fig. 14).
First, pore diffusion and pore-filling processes 
likely contributed to TCS uptake. BET analysis 
revealed a specific surface area of 25.09 m2  g−1, a 
pore volume of 0.115 cm3  g−1, and an average pore 
diameter of approximately 22–24  nm, indicating a 
predominantly mesoporous structure. Such pores pro-
vide accessible diffusion pathways and facilitate the 
transport of TCS molecules from the bulk solution 
to internal adsorption domains. Consistent with this 
interpretation, SEM images after adsorption exhibited 
a more compact surface morphology and partial pore 
coverage, indicating surface deposition of adsorbed 
species. Together with the BET results, these obser -
vations indirectly support the occurrence of pore dif-
fusion and pore-filling processes.

the plausible contributors to adsorption because tri-
closan is a hydrophobic aromatic compound with a

molecules exhibit a natural tendency to partition

3.7 Adsorption Thermodynamics
The thermodynamic behavior of triclosan adsorption 
onto Bio-Fe/Ni was evaluated using Langmuir equi-
librium constants obtained at different temperatures 
(298–308  K) (Table  4). The thermodynamic param-
eters, including Gibbs free energy change (ΔG°), 
enthalpy change (ΔH°), and entropy change (ΔS°), 
were calculated using the Van’t Hoff equation to pro-
vide insight into the energetic characteristics of the 
adsorption process.
The negative ΔG° values (−0.69 to −1.04 kJ mol⁻ 1) 
indicate that TCS adsorption onto Bio-Fe/Ni was ther -
modynamically favorable and spontaneous within the 
investigated temperature range. The relatively small 
magnitude of ΔG° suggests that physical adsorption 
may contribute substantially to the overall adsorp-
tion process. Similar thermodynamic behavior has 
been reported for triclosan adsorption onto biochar 
and other carbonaceous materials, where hydropho-
bic interactions, pore filling, and π-π interactions have 
been proposed as important adsorption pathways (Cho 
et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2020; Stoycheva et al., 2023).
The positive enthalpy change (ΔH° = 13.4 kJ mol⁻1) 
indicates that the adsorption process was endother -
mic. This result suggests that increasing temperature 
may enhance TCS adsorption, possibly by facilitat-
ing molecular diffusion and improving access to ener -
getically heterogeneous adsorption sites. However, the 
influence of temperature on adsorption capacity within 
the investigated range was relatively modest, indicat-
ing that temperature was not the sole factor governing 
adsorption performance. Similar endothermic behavior 
has been reported for biochar-based adsorbents used 
for the removal of organic contaminants (Almeida-
Naranjo et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024; Stoycheva et al., 
2023).
The positive entropy change (ΔS° = 48.5 J mol⁻1 K⁻1) 
indicates an increase in randomness at the solid–liq-
uid interface during adsorption. This behavior may be 
associated with the displacement of water molecules 
from the adsorbent surface and changes in the inter -
facial arrangement accompanying TCS adsorption. 
Similar entropy-driven adsorption behavior has been 
reported for biochar-based materials used for organic 
micropollutant removal (Hu et al., 2020; Shirani et al., 
2025).
Although the Freundlich model provided a better 
fit to the equilibrium data, the Langmuir equilibrium 
constants were used to estimate the thermodynamic 
parameters because they provide apparent equilib-
rium constants suitable for Van’t Hoff analysis. This 
approach has been widely adopted in adsorption ther -
modynamic studies, and the resulting thermodynamic 
parameters are generally interpreted as apparent ther -
modynamic descriptors rather than strict indicators of 
ideal monolayer adsorption (Almeida-Naranjo et  al., 
2024; Cho et al., 2021; Stoycheva et al., 2023). Thus, 
the thermodynamic results suggest that TCS adsorp-
tion onto Bio-Fe/Ni is a spontaneous, endothermic, 
and entropy favored process within the investigated 
temperature range. The relatively low ΔG° values and 
moderate ΔH° value are consistent with the interpreta-
tion that physical adsorption contributes substantially 
to TCS uptake. This interpretation is also consist-
ent with the adsorption behavior and characterization 
results obtained in the present study, although the 
adsorption mechanism should be interpreted together 
with the available structural and spectroscopic evi-
dence rather than from thermodynamic parameters 
alone (Juturu et al., 2025a; 2025b).
4 Conclusion
This study demonstrated a green and sustainable 
approach for the synthesis of a biochar-supported Fe/
Ni material using rice husk biochar and Syzygium 
nervosum leaf extract as a natural reducing and sta-
bilizing agent. The synthesized Bio-Fe/Ni material 
exhibited a porous structure containing Fe/Ni spe-
cies and abundant surface functional groups, which 
contributed to triclosan adsorption from aqueous 
solutions. The adsorption performance was strongly 
influenced by solution pH, with maximum removal 
observed at pH 5. The adsorption kinetics were 
best described by the pseudo-second-order model, 
whereas the equilibrium data were better represented

Although the adsorption capacity of Bio-Fe/Ni

feasibility of producing Fe/Ni functionalized biochar 
through a simple and environmentally benign synthe-
sis route using renewable biomass resources. The use 
of rice husk and plant-derived reducing agents is con-
sistent with circular economy principles and provides 
a foundation for developing sustainable adsorbents 
for emerging contaminant remediation.
Several limitations should be acknowledged. The 
adsorption experiments were performed using syn-
thetic triclosan solutions, and the effects of competing 
contaminants, natural organic matter, and long-term 
operational conditions were not investigated. In addi-
tion, advanced surface characterization techniques such 
as XPS were not employed to directly verify the contri-
bution of Fe/Ni-containing surface interactions. There-
fore, future studies should focus on improving regenera-
tion performance and material stability, increasing the 
accessibility of adsorption sites, elucidating adsorption 
mechanisms through advanced surface analyses such as 
XPS, and evaluating the performance of Bio-Fe/Ni in 
real wastewater matrices to further evaluate its perfor -
mance under environmentally relevant conditions.
