Abstract 18 
The use of food-processing wastewaters to produce microbial biomass-derived single cell 19 
protein (SCP) is a sustainable way to meet the global food demand. Yet, despite the potential 20 
benefits of lower costs and greater resource recovery compared to pure cultures, 21 
bioconversion processes relying on microbial community-based approaches to SCP 22 
production have received scarce attention. Here, we evaluated SCP production from soybean-23 
processing wastewaters under controlled reactor conditions using the existent microbial 24 
communities in these wastewaters. Six sequencing batch reactors of 4.5-L working volume 25 
were operated at 30 /i1 for 34 d in cycles consisting of 3-h anaerobic and 9-h aerobic phases. 26 
Four reactors received no microbial inoculum and the remaining two were amended with a 27 
1.5 L of mixed culture from a prior microbial community-based SCP production. Microbial 28 
characterization was done via 16S rRNA gene metabarcoding. Influent wastewater batches 29 
had variable chemical characteristics but a similar microbial composition. Reactors produced 30 
more SCP when fed with wastewaters of higher soluble total Kjeldahl nitrogen (sTKN) 31 
content and a lower carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (sCOD:sTKN). The biomass protein yield 32 
ranged from 0.24 to 3.13 g protein/g sTKN, with a maximum protein content of 50%. An 33 
average of 92% of sCOD and 73% of sTN removal was achieved. Distinct microbial 34 
communities were enriched in all six bioreactors after 34 d, where the prevailing genera 35 
included Azospirillum, Rhodobacter , Lactococcus , Novosphingobium , and 36 
Acidipropionibacterium. In contrast, the microbial community of influent wastewaters was 37 
dominated by Lactococcus and Weissella. We showed that constituents in soybean 38 
wastewater can be converted to SCP through microbial community-based growth processes 39 
and demonstrated the effect of variable influent wastewater composition on SCP production. 40 
Keywords: carbon-to-nitrogen ratio; essential amino acid; probiotic; single cell protein; 41 
animal feed; circular economy 42

1. Introduction 43 
Microbial protein or single cell protein (SCP) consists of dried microorganisms with a 44 
high protein content along with fats, carbohydrates, vitamins, and minerals (Suman et al., 45 
2015). It represents a promising alternative to fishmeal as a protein source in aquaculture, 46 
especially when raising sea bass, Atlantic salmon, rainbow trout, and whiteleg shrimp (Jones 47 
et al., 2020). Producing SCP from wastewaters would help alleviate the environmental impact 48 
caused by both traditional agricultural food production (Owsianiak et al., 2022) and 49 
wastewater treatment and disposal (Durkin et al., 2022; Spiller et al., 2020). SCP also has the 50 
potential to outperform staple crops in terms of protein yield per land area (Leger et al., 51 
2021). According to estimates, by 2050, the adoption of microbial protein could reduce the 52 
required cropland area, global nitrogen losses from croplands, and agricultural greenhouse 53 
gas emissions worldwide by 6%, 8%, and 7%, respectively (Pikaar et al., 2018). 54 
Microbial biomass may be produced by growing pure cultures (axenic SCP) or a 55 
mixture of strains or taxa (microbial community-based SCP), provided they can use 56 
wastewater as a source of carbon, energy and reducing power. A microbial community-based 57 
process has potential advantages over axenic culturing, such as a higher protein content due 58 
to synergistic interactions between different SCP-producing groups and utilization of various 59 
carbon and nitrogen sources in the substrate (Alloul et al., 2021a), process stability in terms 60 
of resistance and resilience to disturbances (Santillan et al., 2020b; Santillan et al., 2019a; 61 
Santillan & Wuertz, 2022), and the accumulation of intracellular components (Janarthanan et 62 
al., 2016). Further, this approach takes advantage of the microbial taxa already present in the 63 
wastewater by providing suitable growth conditions (Vethathirri et al., 2021). Indeed, the 64 
most suitable microbial organisms could be enriched within a mixed community to perform 65 
the targeted biotechnological process (Verstraete et al., 2022). Mixed microbial communities 66 
grown on wastewaters can meet the minimum dietary requirement of aquaculture animals 67

influent raw wastewaters and the enriched communities in such SCP production systems is 69 
scarce. Three types of bacterial consortia are used in microbial community-based systems, 70 
namely, aerobic heterotrophic bacteria, microalgae and aerobic heterotrophic bacteria, and 71 
purple phototrophic bacteria (Spiller et al., 2020; Vethathirri et al., 2021). Only the latter, 72 
which use light as energy source for the assimilation of organics and nutrients, have been 73 
directly enriched from wastewaters for SCP production (Hülsen et al., 2022). To our 74 
knowledge, the present study is the first to grow SCP based on aerobic heterotrophic bacteria 75 
present in the wastewater. 76 
Compared to other wastewaters, food-processing wastewaters are of interest for 77 
microbial protein production due to their much lower content in pathogens, heavy metals, and 78 
other toxic contaminants. Chemically characterizing such wastewaters helps to assess their 79 
suitability for microbial biomass formation, with bioavailability of carbon- and nitrogen-80 
containing organic compounds a key parameter (Vethathirri et al., 2021). In general, the 81 
chemical characteristics of food-processing wastewaters, including their carbon-to-nitrogen 82 
ratio (C:N), vary widely with production processes (Nayyar et al., 2021), making microbial 83 
community-based SCP production a challenging task. For it to be cost-effective and 84 
sustainable, the addition of external nutrients to maintain a suitable C:N should be avoided. 85 
Consequently, it is necessary to investigate how varying C:N affects both microbial growth 86 
and protein yield. 87

based SCP over time using different batches of the same source of soybean-processing 89 
wastewater. The objectives were to (1) microbially characterize the wastewater and 90 
community-based biomass; (2) determine suitable chemical characteristics in the feed to 91 
achieve a higher biomass yield and protein content; (3) characterize the amino acid content in 92

2. Materials and methods 97 
2.1 Experimental design 98 
Six 4.5-L bioreactors were operated as sequencing batch reactors on continuous 12-h 99 
cycles with intermittent aeration for 34 d, receiving wastewater from a soybean processing 100 
company in Singapore. Over the course of three months, nine 20-L carboys of wastewater 101 
from soybean soaking were collected at seven different occasions. Initially, two reactors were 102 
operated to test whether biomass could be enriched directly from soybean processing 103 
wastewater. Afterwards, a set of four reactors was operated to see if biomass could be again 104 
enriched directly from different batches of soybean processing wastewaters, and if having a 105 
starting inoculum would yield better SCP production than starting without it, receiving the 106 
same wastewater feed. Hence, four SBRs were operated without a starting inoculum: F1-2, F3, 107 
F4-5, and F6-7, whereas reactors IF4-5 and IF6-7 were started using 1.5-L inoculum from F3 after 108 
43 days. Subscript numbers indicate the wastewater batch used as feed. Reactors IF 4-5 and 109 
IF6-7 were comparable to reactors F 4-5 and F 6-7, respectively, which were fed with identical 110 
batches of wastewater but did not have a starting sludge inoculum. 111 
2.2 Operational parameters and bioreactor arrangement 112 
The reactor temperature was maintained at 30°C and sludge was continuously mixed 113 
at 375 rpm. Feeding phase occurred during the initial 5-10 min of a cycle, followed by 114 
alternating 180 min anoxic/anaerobic and 540 min aerobic phases. Cycles finished once 115 
soluble chemical oxygen demand (sCOD) of the mixed liquor was measured to be less than 116 
400 mg/L, after which time the biomass was left to settle for 60 min and 1.85 L of 117

2.4 Microbial analysis 150 
 Each soybean wastewater feed batch was subsampled once for microbial analysis and 151 
sludge samples were collected thrice a week from each reactor. A 50-mL feed sample was 152 
centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 3 min and 45 mL of supernatant discarded resulting in a tenfold 153 
increase in biomass concentration. Aliquots of 2 mL of concentrated wastewater and 2 mL of 154 
sludge samples were stored in cryogenic vials at -80°C for DNA extraction as previously 155 
described (Santillan et al., 2019b). Bacterial 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing was done in two 156 
steps as described in Santillan et al. (2020b). Primer set 341f/785r targeted the V3-V4 157 
variable regions of the 16S rRNA gene (Thijs et al., 2017). The libraries were sequenced on 158

quality filtering. Sequences were truncated after 280 and 255 nucleotides for forward and 164 
reverse reads, respectively, length at which average quality dropped below a Phred score of 165 
20. After truncation, reads with expected error rates higher than 3 and 5 for forward and 166 
reverse reads were removed. After filtering, error rate learning, ASV inference and denoising, 167

2.5 Fluorescence in situ hybridization 174 
Microbial characterization was further supported by fluorescence in situ hybridization 175 
(FISH) using probes for the domain bacteria and selected core SCP taxa . Sludge samples 176 
were amended with 4% paraformaldehyde and placed on ice for 2-3 h. The fixed samples 177 
were washed with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution and stored in a mixture of 178 
1×PBS and ethanol (1:1) at -20 °C until use. The cells were allowed to dry on microscopic 179 
slides and dehydrated in an ethanol series of 50, 80 and 96% for 3 min each. Hybridization 180 
buffer (0.9 M NaCl, 20 Mm Tris-HCl, 35% formamide, 0.01% sodium dodecyl sulfate) and 181 
probes were added to detect microorganisms of interest. Eubmix (Eub338, Eub 338ll, Eub 182 
338lll) targets most bacteria (Daims et al., 1999) and AZOI 655 probe targets species 183 
belonging to Azospirillum (Stoffels et al., 2001). After hybridization, the slides were washed 184 
with warm buffer for 10 min (0.9 M NaCl, 20 Mm Tris-HCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.01% sodium 185 
dodecyl sulfate) and rinsed thoroughly with cold water. FISH images were acquired using a 186 
LSM780 confocal laser scanning microscope. The Zen software was used for image 187 
processing and graphical analysis (Carl Zeiss, German). 188

3.2 Wastewaters with a lower sCOD:sTKN (C:N) and higher sTKN produced more SCP 220 
While the microbial communities of different batches of soybean-processing 221 
wastewaters were similar at the phylum level, the chemical composition was variable, 222 
particularly the nitrogen content. The sTKN and sCOD:sTKN values were in the range of 13 223 
-110 mg/L and 74 - 187, respectively, for the seven batches of wastewaters collected at 224 
different time points from the same soybean processing source (Table 1). According to the 225 
wastewater provider, this variability could be due to changes in the quantity of soybeans 226 
soaked in water for washing and the source of the soybeans being processed. In general, the 227 
darker the color of soybean-processing wastewater, the higher the sTKN level and the lower 228 
the sCOD:sTKN value, increasing its potential for SCP production (Vethathirri et al., 2021). 229 
To account for the variable N content in different batches of wastewater used in this study, 230 
we characterized them as either low (L) for sTKN = 13 - 61 mg/L or high (H) for sTKN = 62 231 
- 110 mg/L) (see Tables 1 and 2). 232

solids (TSS) concentration for 34 days (Figure 2a). The initial average biomass concentration 234 
of the four reactors that started without inoculum was the same as the average TSS of the 235

inoculum had an initial average TSS of 575 mg/L. Despite having started without inoculum, 237 
reactor F 3 showed a higher TSS concentration of 1967 mg/L on d34 than its offspring 238 
reactors IF4-5 and IF 6-7, which started with an inoculum produced from F 3. We attribute this 239 
difference to the favorable chemical characteristics of the wastewater batch used for F3, which 240 
had high sTKN levels and a low sCOD:sTKN compared to other feeds (Table 2). On the 241 
other hand, the lowest TSS value on d34 was 227 mg/L, observed in reactor F 1-2, which was 242

The protein content in the microbial biomass is important for fish feed applications 268 
(Azim et al., 2008; Webster & Lim, 2002). Higher protein contents of 49.9, 49.5 and 48.7% 269 
as dry biomass were observed in reactors IF 4-5, F 1-2, and F 4-5, respectively (Figure S2), 270 
regardless of the presence or absence of an initial inoculum. Similar protein percentages were 271 
observed in aerobic heterotrophic biomass produced from different food processing 272 
wastewaters (Muys et al., 2020). However, they were lower than reported values for other 273 
types of SCP biomass. For instance, purple non-sulfur bacteria (PNSB) growing on volatile 274 
fatty acids had a maximum protein content of 61% as dry cell weight (Peng et al., 2022), 275 
although this was measured with the modified Lowry method. Colorimetric protein assays, 276

higher sTKN content afterwards (Table 2). Additionally, none of the reactors had a constant 283 
protein fraction during the bioconversion period, including reactor F3 which was fed with the 284 
same wastewater batch throughout the study. Our data showed that the highest protein 285 
fraction (~ 50%) was achieved during 15 to 25 days. 286 
3.3 Microbial community-based protein meets amino acid requirement for aquaculture feed 287 
The SCP produced using several batches of soybean wastewaters either completely or 288 
partially met the demand of essential amino acids (EAA) in the aquaculture diet of trout 289 
(Ogino, 1980) and shrimp (Tacon et al., 2002) (Figure 3). Ten amino acids are regarded 290 
essential, because they are not produced by these aquaculture animals and thus need to be 291 
supplemented as part of their diet (Millamena, 2002). Microbial biomass produced in the six 292

3.4 Impact of inoculum on microbial community-based SCP production 310 
Microbial community-based synthesis of single cell protein from wastewaters has 311 
generally involved inoculation with a seed culture derived from previous experiments 312 
(Hülsen et al., 2018a; Hülsen et al., 2018b; Hülsen et al., 2020). In the present study, two out 313

3.5 Distinct microbial communities enriched from wastewater batches of variable quality 331 
The most enriched SCP genera in reactors included Azospirillum, Rhodobacter, 332 
Lactococcus, and Novosphingobium (Figure 4). Except for Lactococcus, the other three main 333 
genera belonged to the phylum Proteobacteria, which is the predominant phylum reported in 334 
microbial community-based SCP production systems (Vethathirri et al., 2021). Although 335 
Firmicutes was the most prevalent phylum in the soybean processing wastewaters, 336 
Proteobacteria became dominant in the enriched microbial biomass in the reactors. 337 
Therefore, the massive immigration of bacteria present in the influent did not determine the 338 
structure of the microbial community in the reactors, contrary to what has been reported for 339 
full-scale activated sludge plants (Dottorini et al., 2021). A similar observation was made in a 340 
full-scale membrane bioreactor plant where the influent bacterial populations contributed 341 
little to relative abundances of the core activated sludge community (Zhang & Meng, 2021). 342

The observed shift in the microbial community composition in our study was likely due to 343 
selection conditions imposed by the available nutrients in the soybean-processing 344

al., 2019b; Seshan et al., 2021). Further, the reactors that operated without a starting 346 
inoculum (F 1-2, F 3, F 4-5, F 6-7), and received different batches of soybean processing 347 
wastewaters as feed, differed in the three most abundant bacterial genera after 34 days 348 
(Figure 4), despite having a similar initial community dominated by Lactococcus and 349 
Weisella. This highlights the effect of variable chemical characteristics of the influent 350 
wastewaters on the selection of core SCP communities in the bioreactors. Similarly, the two 351 
reactors that received the same sludge inoculum (IF4-5, IF6-7) with high relative abundances of 352 
the genera Azospirillum and Lactococcus , had enriched different bacterial genera after 34 353 
days. These two reactors were fed different batches of soybean-processing wastewaters, 354 
which had a different chemical composition (Table 1). The two main genera in the reactors 355 
on d34 were Rhodobacter and Ignavibacterium and Novosphingobium and Zoogloea, 356 
respectively, also differing from the main genera in the influent wastewaters. Likewise, 357 
Lactococcus, which was the predominant genus in influent wastewaters, was not among the 358 
core SCP microbial communities except for two reactors (F 1-2 and F 6-7) that were fed with 359 
different wastewater batches. Similarly, Weissella , which was the second most abundant 360 
bacterial genus in the soybean wastewaters, was not enriched in any of the reactors. Taken 361 
together, these data suggest that organisms present in the influent wastewaters at low relative 362 
abundances (which may be below the limit of detection) can be enriched if provided 363 
appropriate selective conditions. 364

Acidipropionibacterium was among the three most enriched genera in reactors F 3 and 392 
IF4-5. Previously named Propionibacterium, taxa within this genus have a wide range of 393 
applications in the food and pharmaceutical industries due to their ability to produce vitamin 394

al., 2018). Some species within this genus, including Acidipropionibacterium acidipropionici 396 
(present in the reactors), play a major role in the food industry as either bio-preservatives or 397 
potential probiotics because of their generally recognized as safe (GRAS) status (Deptula et 398 
al., 2019). The genus Novosphingobium that was prevalent in reactor IF6-7 includes emerging 399 
diazotrophic strains capable of promoting rice cultivation in the absence of a nitrogen source 400 
(Rangjaroen et al., 2017). Novosphingobium nitrogenifigens and Novosphingobium 401 
sediminicola, which were both identified at the species level in the reactor, have been shown 402 
to accumulate polyhydroxyalkanoates when growing on nitrogen deficient pulp and paper-403 
mill effluent (Addison et al., 2007), and to achieve high N levels facilitating the growth of the 404 
sugarcane plant in N-free sands (Muangthong et al., 2015), respectively. Another abundant 405 
organism from the same reactor, Zooglea resiniphila, has been found to enhance the removal 406 
of major resin acid toxicants in pulp and paper-mill effluents after bioaugmentation (Yu & 407 
Mohn, 2002). The most enriched species in reactor IF 4-5, Rhodobacter gluconicum , was 408 
reported to be a dominant soil microbe involved in electricity generation in plant microbial 409 
fuel cells (Maddalwar et al., 2021). Ignavibacterium, another abundant genus specific to this 410 
reactor, was reported to be involved in the degradation of aromatic compounds in 411 
petrochemical wastewater effluents (Wang et al., 2021). 412

The overall protein production rates attained in this study were lower than those 414

3.8 Future directions to enhance SCP production from food-processing wastewaters 431 
Further optimization of operational parameters to enhance SCP production and, 432 
specifically, accumulation of essential amino acids, is desirable. We observed an average of 433 
92% of sCOD and 73% of sTN removal (Table 2). The current feeding scheme was chosen to 434 
provide adequate time for building microbial biomass from soybean processing wastewater in 435 
SBRs. Future experiments starting with a previously enriched sludge inoculum may employ a 436 
more frequent feeding regime to increase the supply of carbon and nitrogen for cell protein 437 
synthesis. A similar strategy increased the production rate of intracellular 438 
polyhydroxyalkanoates tenfold, using volatile fatty acids as feed under aerobic conditions 439 
(Valentino et al., 2014). Additionally, microbial communities can produce other valuable 440 
compounds like vitamins and co-enzyme Q10 (Peng et al., 2022) through bioconversion of 441

4. Conclusions 444 
• Soybean-processing wastewater can be converted into SCP meeting aquaculture feed 445 
requirements through a mixed-community bioreactor bioconversion approach. 446 
• SCP can be produced using the microbial communities already present in the food-447 
processing wastewaters, although SCP production is higher when reactors are inoculated 448 
with a suitable mixed-community culture. 449 
• Variable sTKN levels and sCOD:sTKN in the food-processing wastewaters result in 450 
distinct SCP microbial communities, regardless of the similar microbial composition of 451 
the influent. 452 
• Taxa at low abundance in the wastewaters can grow into core SCP producing organisms 453 
under suitable process conditions. 454 
• Microbial community-based bioconversion of food-processing wastewaters into SCP can 455 
enrich for taxa with the potential to promote plant growth, have probiotic effects, and 456 
produce valuable compounds like vitamins. 457
