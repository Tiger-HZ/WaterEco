Evaluation of the impact of body mass index and 
mid-upper arm circumference on the quality of 
cardiopulmonary resuscitation
Sinan Önder · İlter Ağaçkıran · Elif Öztürk İnce · Gülseren Ace · Bektaş Can Balkız ·

Abstract
High survival rates in cases of cardiac arrest can only be achieved through high-quality and effective chest 
compressions. The muscle strength and physical characteristics of the practitioners are among the factors that 
influence effective chest compressions. This study aims to investigate the relationship between the measure -
ment of mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), a surrogate anthropometric marker of muscle mass in the 
body, and the effectiveness of CPR. A total of 142 final-year medical students were selected as participants. 
The study examined the impact of the participants’ physical characteristics, body mass index (BMI), and 
MUAC measurements on CPR quality. Of the participants, 78 were male, and the rate of achieving appropri -
ate chest compression depth was found to be 99%. MUAC measurements of participants achieving appropriate 
compression depth ranged between 24 and 28 cm for women and 30–34 cm for men. While most participants 
with normal BMI achieved the ideal compression rate, no significant correlation was observed between BMI 
and CPR quality (n = 51, p = 0.812). Among women with a MUAC of 24 cm or more, 92.3% (n = 12) achieved 
appropriate chest compression depth, whereas this rate was 76.7% (n = 33) for men. Participants classified as 
underweight based on BMI had a compression success rate of 8% (n = 7), compared to 58% (n = 51) for those 
with normal BMI. In this study, CPR quality did not differ significantly by MUAC for both genders. Although 
participants within optimal MUAC ranges achieved adequate compression depth more frequently, these dif -
ferences were not statistically significant. Participants with a normal BMI were more successful in achieving 
the ideal compression rate. On the other hand, underweight participants could not maintain adequate chest 
compression depth.

Introduction
International CPR guidelines emphasize the importance of high-quality chest compressions to improve survival 
rates1–3. Effective CPR can maximize coronary perfusion and the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) in 
patients1. For effective, uninterrupted, and prolonged chest compressions, it is recommended that practitioners 
position themselves perpendicular to the patient’s body and use their own body weight, thus preventing early 
muscle fatigue in the practitioner. Besides training and the application of quality CPR, the personal and physical 
characteristics of the rescuer also influence the effectiveness of CPR. Previous studies have noted that factors 
such as the rescuer’s weight, height, physical fitness, and muscle strength affect the quality of CPR. However, 
there are limited practitioner-based studies on CPR quality, and existing research is insufficient in this regard4–6.
With technological advancements, various devices have been developed to monitor and improve CPR quality. 
These include metronomes and feedback devices. CPR feedback devices analyze the quality of resuscitation by 
providing information on parameters such as compression rate, compression fraction, the ratio of adequate chest 
compressions, and appropriate chest recoil1.
While previous studies have demonstrated that higher body weight and BMI are positively associated with 
achieving adequate chest compression depth, BMI cannot differentiate between muscle and fat mass7. Mid-Upper 
Arm Circumference (MUAC) is a validated, well-established surrogate marker for appendicular skeletal muscle 
mass in both nutritional and clinical assessments8,9. Therefore, evaluating MUAC as a rapidly obtainable surro -
gate for muscle mass could offer new insights beyond BMI. Although a recent study has explored arm circumfer-
ence in relation to CPR10, comprehensive data combining BMI and MUAC to evaluate their distinct impacts on 
CPR quality remains scarce.
This study aims to investigate the impact of physiological parameters, including the gender, height, weight, 
mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), and Body Mass Index (BMI) of chest compression practitioners, as well 
as their exercise habits, on the quality of chest compressions.
Methods
Study design
This prospective descriptive observational study was approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Eth-
ics Committee of Hacettepe University, with approval number GO 21/1270, dated December 7, 2021, and deci -
sion number 2021/20-28. Consent was obtained from each participant in the study. In the study, training was 
conducted according to and evaluated according to the American Heart Association's Adult Advance Cardiac Life 
Support guidelines3.
Between May 1 and August 31, 2022, 6th-year medical students at Hacettepe University Faculty of Medicine 
who agreed to participate were included in the study conducted at the Department of Emergency Medicine. A 
post-hoc power analysis indicated that the sample size provided sufficient statistical power to detect moderate 
effect sizes for the primary outcomes regarding BMI and MUAC. Participants performed uninterrupted chest 
compressions for 2 min on an adult PRESTAN CPR manikin, used for educational purposes, with a True CPR™ 
device (Physio-Control®, Redmond, WA, USA), also employed for training purposes.
Data on CPR quality obtained from the True CPR™ device, including chest compression depth, rate, count, 
compression fraction, and the percentage of compressions achieving proper chest recoil, were recorded in a study 
form for each volunteer. Chest compression fraction (CCF) was automatically calculated by the True CPR™ 
device's proprietary software, which continuously logs the exact duration of active chest compressions relative 
to the total 2-min scenario time.
Each participant’s height and weight were measured to calculate their BMI, and their MUAC was also measured.

BMI was calculated using the formula “BMI = kg/m2” and classified according to the criteria set by the World 
Health Organization11. MUAC measurements were taken by a single designated researcher using a standard -
ized measuring tape to minimize inter-rater variability. MUAC measurements were taken while participants 
were seated, with the forearm flexed at a 90-degree angle and resting on a table, focusing on the midpoint of the 
humerus bone. Additionally, participants’ age, gender, and exercise habits were recorded in the data form.
Inclusion criteria
To be eligible for the study, participants needed to:
 ● Be 6th-year students at Hacettepe University Faculty of Medicine.
 ● Consent to participate in the study.
 ● Have attended the “Basic Life Support and Advanced Cardiac Life Support” course in the 5th-year Emergency 
Medicine curriculum.
 ● Have participated in the “Basic Life Support and Advanced Life Support Algorithms” course and its practical 
application in the 6th-year Emergency Medicine Internship program.
 ● Have observed or participated in cardiopulmonary resuscitation in the emergency department at least once.
Exclusion criteria
Exclusion criteria included:
 ● Failure to provide consent for participation.
 ● Having physical or chronic conditions that restrict physical activity.
Statistical analysis
All data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 23 and Jamovi version 2.3.24.0, with a significance level set at 
p < 0.05. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (n) and percentage (%) and compared using χ 2 tests 
or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), 
while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (IQR). Comparisons between two groups were 
made using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate.
Results
Between May 1 and August 31, 2022, a total of 142 medical students participating in their emergency medicine 
internship at Hacettepe University Faculty of Medicine were included in the study. Of the 142 participants, 54.9% 
(n = 78) were male. The median age of the participants was 24 years (IQR: 2). The average height was 173 cm 
(SD ± 10.2; minimum 150 cm, maximum 200 cm). The median weight was 70.5 kg (IQR: 26), the median BMI 
was 23.2 kg/m2 (IQR: 4.95), and the median MUAC was 30 cm (IQR: 7). The mean compression rate was 104 
compressions per minute (range: 79–135), and the compression fraction was calculated as 100%. Since compres-
sion fraction was 100% across all groups, it was not included in statistical analyses. The median percentage of 
adequate chest compressions was 99% (IQR: 8), and the median rate of adequate chest recoil was 88.5% (IQR: 
43.5) (Table 1).
Additionally, when the participants’ physical characteristics were examined, those in the group achieving the 
ideal compression rate had an average height of 174 cm, median weight of 72.5 kg, median BMI of 23.4 kg/m2, 
and median MUAC of 30 cm. None of these physical characteristics yielded a statistically significant difference

For women, the MUAC of those who achieved adequate compression depth (5–6 cm) ranged from 24 to 
28 cm. Based on MUAC measurements, female participants were divided into three groups. Among women 
with MUAC < 24 cm, the proportion achieving adequate compression depth was 7.7%, compared to 84.6% in the 
24–28 cm group and 7.7% in the > 28 cm group. Although the group with MUAC measurements between 24 and 
28 cm was the most successful in achieving adequate compression depth, no statistically significant difference 
was observed among the groups (p = 0.410) (Table 5).
For men, the MUAC of those who achieved adequate compression depth (5–6 cm) ranged from 30 to 34 cm. 
Among men with MUAC < 30 cm, the proportion achieving adequate compression depth was 23.3%, compared 
to 53.5% in the 30–34 cm group and 23.3% in the > 34 cm group. Although the group with MUAC measurements 
between 30 and 34 cm was the most successful in achieving adequate compression depth, no statistically signifi-
cant difference was observed among the groups (p = 0.499) (Table 6).
Discussion
The performance of effective chest compressions has been recognized as a key determinant of successful out -
comes in cardiac arrest for over 40 years12. Both the American Heart Association (AHA) and the European 
Resuscitation Council (ERC) emphasize the importance of high-quality chest compressions as the cornerstone of 
resuscitation care1,2. In this study, the effects of BMI and MUAC on chest compression quality were investigated 
within the framework of the AHA’s core criteria.
It has been shown in earlier research that the mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) is a reliable indicator 
of muscle mass in the body 8,13. Recent studies have demonstrated a positive correlation between arm muscle 
thickness and overall muscle mass14,15. Since MUAC is practical and does not require specialized equipment, it 
is highly accessible. Aygun et al.10 reported significant positive correlations between maximum biceps circumfer-
ence and both compression quality and overall CPR quality, whereas no significant association between MUAC 
and compression depth was observed in our cohort; this discrepancy may reflect differences in anthropometric 
measurement methods and participant characteristics. However, few studies have examined the specific effect of 
MUAC on CPR performance, making this study one of the rarely to evaluate MUAC in conjunction with BMI as 
a determinant of CPR quality.

In our study, the average MUAC was found to be 26 cm in women and 32.5 cm in men. The highest proportion 
of adequate compression depth was observed in participants with MUAC values of 24–28 cm among women and 
30–34 cm among men; however, these differences were not statistically significant. Although participants within 
these specific MUAC ranges achieved higher rates of adequate depth, the study found no significant correlation 
between MUAC and the ability to achieve the ideal compression rate or adequate compression depth for either 
gender (p = 0.410 for women, p = 0.499 for men). Conversely, participants who achieved adequate chest recoil 
had specific MUAC ranges, with women being more successful in achieving complete chest recoil during CPR 
compared to men.
When examining CPR quality indicators by gender, this study found that both genders had similar compression 
rates. However, the percentage of compressions performed at an adequate depth (5–6 cm) was 96% for women 
and 100% for men, with men achieving higher rates of adequate compression depth. Previous studies by Ok et 
al., Kim et al., Zhang et al., Mayrand et al., and Russo et al. have similarly reported better compression depth 
performance in men6,16–19. According to these findings, female participants tend to perform faster but shallower 
chest compressions compared to their male counterparts. Consistent with the literature, our study also found that 
male participants achieved higher rates of adequate compression depth, likely due to their greater muscle mass. 
Muscle size and overall CPR performance can be influenced by various potential confounding factors, including 
training history, sex, age, and nutritional status. Although we focused on MUAC, it is essential to recognize that 
repeated CPR training may build muscle memory and technique that can compensate for differences in baseline 
muscle mass.
When examining the relationship between BMI and adequate compression depth, a weak positive correlation 
was found. Mayrand et al. reported a positive correlation between chest compression depth and increases in 
weight and height19. On the other hand, Ock et al. found no significant relationship between adequate compression 
depth and variables such as gender, height, or weight 20. Krikščionaitienė et al. found that participants classified 
as underweight were less likely to perform adequate chest compressions 21. Sánchez et al. noted that individuals 
with a BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2 maintained an adequate compression depth for 2 min, while those with a BMI < 23 kg/m2 
performed effectively only 80% of the time in the first minute, dropping to 30% by the second minute 22. Simi-
larly, Wang et al. found that individuals with above-average BMI (mean BMI 21.6 ± 2.3 kg/m2) performed better 
in CPR quality parameters23. Oh et al. showed that rescuers with higher BMI were more successful in achieving 
adequate compression depth24. Similarly, Eyupoglu et al. 7 found that participants who performed optimal CPR 
had significantly higher BMI values than those with suboptimal CPR, while BMI was not significantly associated 
with CPR performance in our study, possibly due to differences in study populations, training backgrounds, and 
CPR assessment protocols.
The general conclusion from previous studies is that anthropometric variables like height and weight can influ-
ence chest compression quality to some extent24–26. Higher BMI increases the likelihood of success in achieving 
adequate compression depth, but it may inversely affect chest recoil rates. Additionally, the greater muscle mass 
in men contributes to higher rates of adequate compression. Conversely, individuals with lower BMI face greater 
challenges in achieving adequate chest compression depth on CPR manikins27,28. Although previous studies have 
suggested that gender and BMI may influence CPR performance, our findings did not demonstrate statistically 
significant associations between BMI or MUAC and the CPR quality parameters evaluated.
Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, the single-center design and the specific cohort of 6th-year medical stu-
dents limit generalizability. By the time students reach the sixth year, they have already received BLS training 
in their fifth year; this prior exposure and repeated training likely homogenized their skill levels, which could 
have masked the isolated impact of physical characteristics like MUAC on CPR quality. Although quantitative 
CPR quality data from their 5th-year training were not recorded, all participants had successfully passed their

practical and theoretical internship examinations, ensuring a uniform baseline level of competence. Second, the 
2-min CPR sessions on a manikin cannot fully evaluate the "real" quality of CPR at the scene, as a brief simula-
tion fails to replicate the physical fatigue and dynamic challenges of a prolonged clinical cardiac arrest scenario. 
Furthermore, BMI cannot differentiate between muscle mass and fat mass, and the relatively narrow range of 
MUAC within our specific, young cohort of medical students may have limited our ability to detect statistically 
significant differences.
Conclusion
In conclusion, this study found no statistically significant difference in CPR quality based on MUAC for both 
genders. While an increase in MUAC showed a tendency towards better compression depth, it did not reach 
statistical significance. In contrast, higher MUAC was associated with lower rates of adequate chest recoil. Fur -
thermore, CPR practitioners with a normal BMI were more successful in achieving the ideal compression rate, 
while underweight practitioners struggled to perform adequate chest compressions.
