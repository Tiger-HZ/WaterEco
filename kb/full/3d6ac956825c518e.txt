Few tropical tree species develop reliable annual rings that can be successfully dated. Furthermore, the naturally scattered distribution of species often limits sample size, a key factor in dendrochronology. Crossdating is the only way to accurately date tree rings and develop chronologies spanning centuries. This manuscript describes a 10-year effort to build a network of tropical tree-ring chronologies in eastern Amazonia, demonstrates that the method developed by Douglass works in the tropics, and discusses the key challenges that prevented crossdating at some sites. Samples from 344 Cedrela odorata trees distributed across seven sites were collected from legally harvested and living trees in eastern Amazonia. Three tree-ring chronologies have been successfully dated: two in Rio Paru and one in the Altamira National Forest (1885-2016). Provisional Cedrela tree-ring chronologies are presented here: a 190-year record from Inupuku and a 329-year record from Mukuru. Both are located in the Jari River valley, home to the tallest trees ever discovered in the Amazon basin. A third 113-year record from the Monte Alegre site, situated in the Paru River State Forest, is also presented. Our results demonstrate the influence of local soil physical properties, topographic attributes, competition, and sample size on the success of crossdating.
