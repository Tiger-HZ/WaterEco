A substantial proportion of river basins worldwide lack reliable hydrological observations. Regions where expanding infrastructure is socially, environmentally, or politically constrained face persistent data limitations. These challenges are more pronounced in transboundary river systems, where hydrological data are often sparse or entirely inaccessible due to limited monitoring networks or a lack of information sharing between nations. As a result, integrated water management is hindered, and water conflicts are exacerbated. Remote sensing offers a promising pathway to overcome these limitations by providing consistent and useful hydrological information even in regions where direct measurements cannot be obtained. This thesis develops a framework that relies exclusively on publicly accessible satellite datasets to reconstruct reservoir dynamics and infer operational behavior in basins that are data-scarce or subject to political restrictions. The method combines radar altimetry (CryoSat-2, ICESat-2, Sentinel-3A/B), Harmonized Landsat and Sentinel-2 (HLS) optical imagery, and Sentinel-1 Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) data to progressively reduce information gaps from multi-week intervals to sub-weekly and ultimately to near-daily estimates of water level. Reservoir inflow is estimated using satellite-based rainfall, and outflow is inferred from temporal changes in satellite-derived water extent. These reconstructed time series of storage, inflow, and outflow are then combined in an optimization scheme to illustrate characteristic operational tendencies, while acknowledging that actual release practices may change over time. The framework is demonstrated through application to a reservoir on the transboundary Imjin River as a representative case, but the analysis is intentionally generalized to emphasize its broader applicability. Results show that publicly available satellite datasets are sufficient to derive meaningful reservoir operation characteristics in the absence of in situ measurements or international data exchange. The approach provides a transparent and independent means of supporting flood risk mitigation and enhancing downstream preparedness. Ultimately, the study demonstrates the feasibility of satellite-driven hydrology in data-poor regions and its value as a foundation for integrated water resources management (IWRM) in transboundary river basins.
