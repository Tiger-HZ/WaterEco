Effect of slow release fertilizer on dry matter 
accumulation, nutrient content and uptake by cotton 
 
Leitanthem Anupama Devi , SD Jadhao, SM Bhoyar, Amruta S Ekapure, 
NM Konde and NK Patke 
 
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/2618060X.2026.v9.i9e.6524 
 
Abstract 
A pot culture experiment was conducted during the Kharif season of 2025 -26 at the Department of Soil 
Science, Dr. Panjabrao Deshmukh Krishi Vidyapeeth, Akola, to study the effect of slow release fertilizer on 
dry matter accumulation, nutrient content and uptake by cotton. The experiment was conducted using cotton 
cv. Ajit 155 in a completely randomized design with ten treatments and three replications. The treatments 
comprised different combinations of NPK and nitrogen su pplied through slow release fertilizer, along with 
100 per cent NPK, 100 per cent NPK + FYM @ 5 t ha⁻¹ and slow release fertilizer equivalent to 100 per cent 
recommended nitrogen dose. Dry matter accumulation in leaves, stems and roots was recorded, while N, P 
and K content and uptake were determined. Among the treatments, T₉ (100% NPK + FYM @ 5 t ha⁻¹) 
recorded the highest dry matter accumulation in leaves (2.55 g plant⁻¹), stems (3.66 g plant⁻¹) and roots (2.08 
g plant⁻¹). The highest N, P and K contents were also recorded under T₉, with values of 0.87, 0.38 and 1.26 
per cent, respectively; however, the differences among treatments were non-significant. Nutrient uptake was 
highest under T₉, with N, P and K uptake of 53.98, 23.47 and 78.3 mg plant⁻¹, respec tively. The study 
indicated that application of 100 per cent NPK along with FYM @ 5 t ha⁻¹ was effective in enhancing dry 
matter accumulation and nutrient uptake of cotton under pot culture conditions. 
 
Keywords: Cotton, slow release fertilizer, dry matter accumulation, nutrient content, nutrient uptake, FYM, 
NPK 
 
Introduction 
The productivity of cotton depends not only on the quantity of nutrients supplied, but also on their 
timely availability and efficient utilization by the crop. Cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) is an 
important commercial fibre crop and plays a significant role in the agricultural economy of India. 
It is one of the major natural fibre crops cultivated widely across the world and contributes 
substantially to the textile sector (Ali et al., 2015; Abbas, 2020) [1, 2]. India ranks first in the world 
in terms of cotton acreage, with about 1 10.86 lakh hectares under cotton cultivation, accounting 
for nearly 36 per cent of the global cotton -growing area. Approximately 62 per cent of cotton 
production in India is obtained from rainfed areas, while 38 per cent is produced under irrigated 
conditions. During 2024-25, the productivity of cotton in India was about 437 kg ha⁻¹, making 
India one of the major producers, consumers and exporters of cotton globally ( Ministry of 
Textiles, GOI, 2025-26). Maharashtra accounts for the largest area under cotton cultivation in the 
country, covering about 41.23 lakh hectares, with an average productivity of 304 kg ha⁻¹ (Cotton 
Corporation of India, 2024-25) [5]. 
Cotton requires an adequate and balanced supply of nutrients throughout its growth period to 
support biomass production and nutrient accumulation. Dry matter accumulation is an important 
indicator of crop growth, while nutrient content and uptake reflect the capacity of the crop to 
acquire and accumulate essential nutrients. Efficient nutrient management is therefor e essential 
for optimum growth and nutrient utilization in cotton. However, conventional fertilizers may 
release nutrients more rapidly than crop demand, which can increase nutrient losses and reduce 
fertilizer-use efficiency. Slow-release fertilizers (SRF s) provide nutrients gradually over an 
extended period and can improve the synchronization between nutrient availability and crop 
demand. This regulated nutrient release may reduce nutrient losses and improve nutrient-use

efficiency. In cotton, controlled -release nitrogen fertilizers have 
been reported to synchronize nitrogen release with crop 
requirements and to increase nitrogen uptake and nitrogen -use 
efficiency compared with conventional urea (Geng et al., 2015) 
[8]. Similarly, controlled-release nitrogen and potassium fertilizers 
have been shown to provide nutrient release patterns closely 
matched to cotton nutrient requirements and to improve cotton

research has also demonstrated that appropr iate slow -release

nitrogen accumulation and nitrogen utilization in cotton (Lu et al.,

Therefore, the use of slow-release fertilizer may offer an effective 
approach for improving dry matter produ ction and nutrient 
acquisition by cotton. However, information on the response of 
cotton in terms of dry matter accumulation, nutrient content and 
nutrient uptake under different slow -release fertilizer treatments 
is comparatively limited. Hence, the prese nt investigation was 
undertaken to study the effect of slow -release fertilizer on dry 
matter accumulation, nutrient content and uptake by cotton. 
 
Material and Methods 
The pot culture experiment was conducted during kharif 2025-26 
under a shade -net house at the Department of Soil Science and 
Agricultural Chemistry, Dr. Panjabrao Deshmukh Krishi 
Vidyapeeth, Akola, Maharashtra, India. The experimental soil 
was a deep, clayey black soil (Vertisol). The experiment 
comprised ten nutrient management treatments, viz., Absolute 
control (T1), 50% NPK + 25% N through slow release fertilizer 
(SRF) (T2), 50% NPK + 15% N through SRF (T 3), 50% NPK + 
5% N through SRF (T 4), 75% NPK + 25% N through SRF (T 5), 
75% NPK + 15% N through SRF (T6), 75% NPK + 5% N through 
SRF (T7), 100% NPK (T 8), 100% NPK + FYM @ 5 t ha⁻¹ (T 9), 
and SRF equivalent to 100% recommended dose of N (T 10). The 
treatments were replicated three times in a Completely 
Randomized Design (CRD), resulting in a total of 30 
experimental pots. Cotton variety Ajit 155 was grown using 10 kg 
of soil per pot. The recommended dose of fertilizer was 1 20: 60: 
60 kg N: P₂O₅: K₂O ha⁻¹. FYM @ 5 t ha⁻¹ was incorporated before 
filling the pots as per treatment. Phosphorus and potassium were 
applied as basal doses, while nitrogen was applied in three equal 
splits at sowing, 30 and 60 days after sowing (DAS). In SRF 
treatments, the designated proportion of nitrogen was supplied 
through SRF as per the treatment schedule. 
At 90 DAS, the plants were carefully uprooted and separated into 
leaves, stems and roots. The roots were gently washed with water 
to remove adhering soil particles. The plant parts were air -dried, 
weighed separately to determine dry matter accumulation and 
expressed as g plant⁻¹. The dried plant samples were ground 
separately and used for determination of nitrogen, phosphorus and 
potassium content following standard analytical procedures. 
Nitrogen content was determined by the micro-Kjeldahl method, 
phosphorus content b y the vanado -molybdo-phosphoric yellow 
colour method using a spectrophotometer after tri -acid 
(HNO3:H2SO4:HClO4) digestion (Piper, 1966) [14], and potassium 
content by flame photometry. Nutrient uptake was calculated by 
multiplying dry matter accumulation with the corresponding 
nutrient content and expressed as mg plant⁻¹. 
 
 
 
The recorded data on dry matter accumulation, nutrient content 
and nutrient uptake were statistically analyzed using analysis of 
variance (ANOVA) appropriate for a C ompletely Randomized 
Design (CRD). Treatment means were compared using the critical 
difference (CD) at the 1 per cent level of significance. 
 
Results and Discussion 
a. Dry matter accumulation 
Dry matter accumulation in leaves, stems and roots of cotton was 
significantly influenced by the slow -release fertilizer treatments. 
The maximum dry matter accumulation in leaves (2.55 g plant⁻¹), 
stems (3.66 g plant⁻¹) and roots (2.08 g plant⁻¹) was recorded with 
100% NPK + FYM @ 5 t ha⁻¹ (T9), while the minimum values of 
1.54, 2.03 and 0.72 g plant⁻¹, respectively, were recorded under 
the absolute control (T1). In leaves, T9 was statistically at par with 
100% NPK (T8; 2.32 g plant⁻¹) and 75% NPK + 25% N through 
SRF (T5; 2.30 g plant⁻¹). Similarly, stem dry matter under T9 was 
at par with T 8 (3.30 g plant⁻¹) and T 5 (3.26 g plant⁻¹). However, 
T9 recorded significantly higher root dry matter than all other 
treatments. The higher biomass accumulation under T 9 may be 
attributed to the combined supply of readily available nut rients 
from NPK and the beneficial effects of FYM on nutrient 
availability and plant growth. The superior performance of T 9 
could be due to the integrated application of inorganic fertilizers 
and FYM, which provides nutrients through both readily available 
and gradually mineralizable sources and thereby promotes better 
nutrient uptake, vegetative growth and root development. 
Manzoor et al. (2022) [12] reported that a sustained supply of 
nitrogen during crop growth enhances nutrient utilization and 
biomass production, while Dongarwar et al. (2023) [7] observed 
improved nutrient availability, uptake and crop growth with 
integrated application of inorganic fertilizer and FYM. Similar 
improvement in cotton growth and biomass under slow-release N 
fertilization was reported by Lu et al. (2024) [10]. The lowest dry 
matter accumulation under the absolute control may be attributed 
to inadequate nutrient availability, which restricted plant growth 
and biomass production (Senthilvelu et al., 2009) [19].

Nitrogen 
The nitrogen content in the above -ground biomass of cotton 
ranged from 0.71 to 0.87 per cent among the treatments. The 
highest nitrogen content was recorded with 100% NPK + FYM 
@ 5 t ha⁻¹ (T9) (0.87%), followed by 75% NPK + 25% N through 
SRF (T 5) (0.86%) and 100% NPK (T 8) (0.85%), whereas the 
lowest nitrogen content was recorded under the absolute control 
(T1) (0.71%). However, the effect of treatments on nitrogen 
content was non -significant, indicating that the numerical 
differences among treatments were not statistically significant. 
Total nitrogen uptake by cotton ranged from 25.37 to 53.98 mg 
plant⁻¹ and was signific antly influenced by the different 
treatments. The highest nitrogen uptake (53.98 mg plant⁻¹) was 
recorded under 100% NPK + FYM @ 5 t ha⁻¹ (T 9), followed by 
100% NPK (T 8) (47.79 mg plant⁻¹) and 75% NPK + 25% N 
through SRF (T5) (47.71 mg plant⁻¹). The nitrog en uptake under 
T8 and T 5 was statistically at par with each other, as their 
difference was less than the CD at 5%, while T 9 recorded 
significantly higher uptake than these treatments. The lowest 
nitrogen uptake (25.37 mg plant⁻¹) was recorded under the 
absolute control (T1). 
The comparatively higher nitrogen content and uptake under T 9 
may be attributed to the combined application of inorganic 
fertilizers and FYM, which improved nitrogen availability and its 
subsequent absorption by cotton. FYM supplies nutrients 
gradually and enhances microbial activity and nitrogen 
mineralization, thereby maintaining nitrogen availability over a 
longer period. The organic matter supplied through FYM also acts 
as a nutrient reservoir, while the gradual release of nitrogen from 
SRF can improve synchronization between nitrogen supply and 
crop demand. Such sustained nitrogen availability promotes plant 
growth and biomass production, ultimately resulting in greater 
nitrogen accumulation in the above -ground biomass. Similar 
improvement in nitrogen availability and plant nitrogen 
concentration under integrated nutrient management was reported 
by Kumar et al. (2020) [9]. The beneficial effect of controlled -
release nitrogen in synchronizing nitrogen release with crop 
demand and improving nitrogen uptake and nitrogen -use 
efficiency has also been reported by Geng et al. (2015) [8] and 
Yang et al. (2016) [20]. Likewise, increased nitrogen uptake under 
integrated nutrient management and improved nitrogen 
fertilization practices were reported by Reddy et al. (2017) and 
Solanki et al. (2018) [16, 18].

Phosphorus 
The phosphorus content in the above -ground biomass of cotton 
ranged from 0.29 to 0.38% among the treatments. The highest 
phosphorus content (0.38%) was recorded with 100% NPK + 
FYM @ 5 t ha⁻¹ (T 9), followed by 75% NPK + 25% N through 
SRF (T 5) (0.37%), whereas the lowest (0.29%) was recorded 
under the absolute control (T 1). The effect of treatments on 
phosphorus content was non-significant. Total phosphorus uptake 
varied from 10.37 to 23.47 mg plant⁻¹. 
The highest phosphorus uptake (23.47 mg plant⁻¹) was recorded 
under T 9, followed by T 5 (20.55 mg plant⁻¹), which was 
statistically at par with T 9. The lowest phosphorus uptake (10.37

mg plant⁻¹) was recorded under the absolute control (T 1). The 
comparatively higher phosphorus content and uptake under T9 
may be attributed to the combined application of NPK and FYM, 
which improved phosphorus availability and favoured greater 
nutrient acquisition by the crop. The addition of FYM can 
stimulate microbial activity and phosphorus mineralization and 
mobilization during decomposition, while organic m atter may 
enhance the availability of native soil phosphorus. Improved 
nutrient availability together with better root development and 
plant growth could have contributed to greater phosphorus 
absorption and accumulation in the above -ground biomass. 
Similar findings were reported by Anwar-ul-Haq et al. (2014) [3] 
and Rushi et al. (2021) [17]. Furthermore, similar improvement in 
phosphorus uptake under combined application of inorganic 
fertilizers and FYM was reported by Rao et al. (2017) [15] and 
Charjan and Gaikwad (2005) [4]. 
 
Potassium 
The potassium content in the above -ground biomass of cotton 
ranged from 1.05 to 1.2 6%. The highest potassium content 
(1.24%) was recorded with 100% NPK + FYM @ 5 t ha⁻¹ (T 9) 
and 100% NPK (T 8) (1.24%), followed by 75% NPK + 25% N 
through SRF (T5) (1.21%), whereas the lowest potassium content 
(1.05%) was recorded under the absolute control (T1). The effect 
of treatments on potassium content was non-significant. 
Total potassium uptake ranged from 37.5 to 78.3 mg plant⁻¹. The 
highest potassium up take (78.3 mg plant⁻¹) was recorded under 
T9, followed by 100% NPK (T8) (69.8 mg plant⁻¹) and 75% NPK 
+ 25% N through SRF (T5) (67.1 mg plant⁻¹). T5 was statistically 
at par with T 8, while the lowest potassium uptake (37.5 mg 
plant⁻¹) was recorded under the absolute control (T1). 
The higher potassium uptake under T 9 may be attributed to the 
combined effect of balanced NPK fertilization and FYM 
application. The application of NPK supplied potassium in an 
available form, while FYM contributed to nutrient cycl ing, 
microbial activity and maintenance of a favourable soil 
environment, thereby supporting continuous potassium 
availability during crop growth. Organic matter addition can also 
influence the distribution and release of different forms of soil 
potassium, facilitating its supply to plant roots. The higher 
nutrient availability under integrated nutrient management, 
together with improved plant growth, may consequently have 
resulted in greater potassium accumulation by cotton. Similar 
findings were reported by Rao et al. (2017) [15], who observed 
higher K content and uptake with the combined application of 
NPK and FYM, while Deshmukh et al. (2012) [6] also reported 
increased potassium uptake in Bt. cotton with increasing levels of 
NPK fertilization and FYM application.

Conclusion 
The study revealed that the application of slow-release fertilizer 
influenced dry matter accumulation, nutrient content and nutrient 
uptake in cotton. Among the treatments, 100% NPK + FYM @ 5 
t ha⁻¹ (T9) recorded the highest dry matter accumulation in leaves, 
stems and roots and also recorded the highest N, P and K content 
in the above-ground biomass. The treatment also resulted in the 
highest N, P and K uptake, indicating improved nutrient 
absorption and accumulation by cotton. Although the differences 
in nutrient content among treat ments were non -significant, T 9 
showed numerically higher values for N, P and K content. The 
superior performance of T9 may be attributed to the combined and 
complementary nutrient supply from inorganic fertilizers and 
FYM, which favoured sustained nutrient availability, biomass 
production and nutrient uptake. Overall, 100% NPK + FYM @ 5 
t ha⁻¹ (T9) and 75% NPK + 25% N through SRF (T5) proved to be 
the most effective treatments for improving dry matter 
accumulation and nutrient a bsorption by cotton under t he 
experimental conditions.
