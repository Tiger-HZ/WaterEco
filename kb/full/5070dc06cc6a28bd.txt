Abstract 
Sustainable land management practices are promoted to restore degraded 
ecosystems in the Great Green Wall areas, yet their large -scale adoption 
depends on effective authentic and convincing engagement of individuals and rural 
communities. This study analys es the factors shaping adoption of sustainable land 
management practices, with particular attention to gender differences and the role of 
women. The research was conducted in six regions of Djibouti, including four 
intersected by the Great Green Wall initi ative. Mixed quantitative and qualitative 
surveys were carried out in 19 villages with 329 farmers and 21 community leaders. 
An adoption index based on ten sustainable land management practices was 
constructed and examined across national, regional, and vi llage scales using

Impact Statement 
This study provides new insights into the conditions under which sustainable land 
management (SLM) practices can be effectively adopted in Sahelian contexts. It 
demonstrates that the sustainability of agricultural systems cannot be adequately 
understood at a single scale, nor without explicitly integrating a gender perspective. 
By examining adoption patterns across multiple levels (household, community, and 
national), the study shows that interactions between gender and sustainable land 
management vary significantly depending on local contexts. The findings reveal that 
gender inequalities are not uniform, but are spatially differentiated and shaped by 
institutional, social, and economic conditions specific to each setting. These results call 
for moving beyon d standardized approaches that treat gender as a homogeneous 
variable. Instead, they highlight the need for context -specific policies and 
interventions that account for the diversity of women’s experiences within agricultural 
systems. By offering a multi -scale and context -sensitive analysis, this research 
contributes to improving the design of land restoration and climate adaptation 
strategies under the Great Green Wall initiative. More broadly, it supports the 
development of more inclusive and effective ap proaches to promoting sustainable 
agricultural systems in dryland regions.

Introduction 
In the dry areas of the Sahel, agriculture provides livelihoods for more than 60% 
of the population (Harsch, 2016; FAOa, 2024). This sector is significantly weakened by 
land degradation (Diarisso et al., 2016; Krenz et al., 2019; Camara et al., 2024), which is 
linked to continued population growth and the effects of climate change (IPCC, 2014 ; 
Crossland et al., 2018; Allen et al., 2018; Seleshie, 2022; Shayegh et Dasgupta, 2022) . Since 
1990, various sustainable land management practices have emerged as effe ctive 
solutions for restoring degraded land, improving agricultural productivity and 
ecosystem sustainability, and reducing the vulnerability of rural communities 
(Blanchard, 2010; Liniger et al., 2011; Epule et al., 2018; UNCCD, 2020). Scaling up these 
sustainable land management practices is a key priority for national, regional, and 
global initiatives, including AFR 100, the Bonn Challenge, Regreening Africa and the 
Great Green Wall (UNCCD, 2020). Beyond the financial and technical r esources 
mobilized through these initiatives, the active participation and commitment of rural 
communities are crucial for attaining long -term goals and results ( Brown et al., 2010; 
Liniger et al., 2011; FAOb, 2014; UNCCD, 2020; Bihan, 2021). Under the Great Green 
Wall program, the large-scale promotion of these practices is limited by the insufficient 
involvement of rural stakeholders, particularly women, who represent 70% of the 
agricultural workforce and play a crucial role in maintaining agricultural production 
systems ( UN Women, 2021 ). In this context, it is necessary to understand the main 
factors influencing women's adoption of these practices to increase their impact and 
ensure equitable access to the advantage s and benefits of initiatives to restore 
degraded ecosystems in rural areas. 
Several studies highlight women’s active role in sustainable land management 
(Kiptot et Franzel, 2011; Degrande et Arinloye, 2015; Theriault et al., 2017; Mbile et al., 2019; 
Wekesah et al. , 2019) . Women are involved in practices that are consistent with 
traditional knowledge and effective in preserving biodiversity (Kiptot and Franzel, 2011; 
Wekesah et al., 2019). Their contribution to the rehabilitation of marginal lands for the 
benefit of their families is recognized (Monimart et Doka, 2021). Women are involved in 
agroforestry by producing seedlings and planting nitrogen -fixing fruit trees in 
semiarid areas of southern Africa (Kiptot et Franzel, 2011). They are also involved in the 
assisted natural regeneration of trees that are useful for consumption, health 
protection, and artisanal use ( Monimart et Doka, 2021 ). The same is true of their 
expertise in preserving soil fertility through crop combinations or the use of compost 
in Chad ( ATVPE, 2014). The expertise of women in soil water conservation through 
the assembly and transport of rubble for the construction of stone bunds is recognized 
in Burkina Faso (Koudougou et al., 2017) and Niger ( Monimart et Doka, 2021 ). Women 
organize themselves into natural resource management groups and play a leadership 
role in land management ( Iddi et al., 2000; Mbile et al., 2019; Abane, 2007) in the 
implementation of sustainable land management projects (Grundy et al., 2004; Stiem and 
Krause, 2016; Samndong and Kjosavik, 2017). 
Women’s capacity to engage in sustainable land management is shaped by 
institutional, socioeconomic, and biophysical factors. The literature highlights the

effect on decisions to adopt these practices. Conversely, in Nepal, Karki ( 2023) assert

practices and encourages households to diversify their livelihoods. 
Despite extensive research on the adoption of sustainable land management 
practices, the contextual variability and spatial disparities in the determinants of 
adoption remain only partially documented (Van Aelst and Holvoet, 2016; Lawson et al., 
2020; Jha et Gupta, 2021; Nadeem et al., 2022; Mponela et al., 2023). Existing studies most 
often focus on only one of the following scales: national, regional, village or 
community (Koudougou et al., 2017; Fayama et al., 2023). Furthermore, existing studies 
most often focus on a single practice or a limited number of practices, whereas actions 
to restore degraded land combine a variety of practices ( Mbile et al., 2019; Musafili et 
al., 2024; Coleman et Mwangi, 2013) . This study aims to identify and enhance the 
understanding of the factors that shape women’s engagement in sustainable land 
management across Great Green Wall countries. To our knowledge, there are no data 
available to analyse the variability at different scales of factors influencing the 
adoption of sustainable land management practices according to gender, which is 
nevertheless necessary for sizing projects and targeting land restoration actions. 
The present research seeks to elucidate the factors that shape women’s 
engagement in sustainable land management practices at the household, community, 
and national levels in Djibouti. It is grounded in two main hypotheses. First the 
determinants of adoption of sustainable land management practices vary depending 
on the scale of analysis and the socio -environmental context in which they are 
embedded. Second, the influence of gender inequalities on the adoption of these 
practices is shaped by local instituti onal, socio -economic, and cultural conditions. 
These hypotheses are based on the assumption that adoption of sustainable land 
management practices is a context -dependent process shaped by the interaction of

To test these hypotheses, we integrate socioeconomic and biophysical data 
collected through a comprehensive survey representative at the national, community, 
and household levels in Djibouti. Particular attention is paid to the influence of 
farming systems, crop diversification, vulnerability to climate-related hazards, a land

factors influencing women's choices and involvement in sustainable land management

nuanced understanding of the differentiated mechanisms underpinning women’s 
participation in sustainable land management and to contribute to ongoing debates on 
gender-responsive environmental governance in dryland regions. 
 
Materials and Methods 
Study sites 
The study sites are located in Djibouti, whose capital city of the same name is 
situated at 11.8251° north latitude and 42.5903° east longitude (Supplementary Figure 
S1). Beyond Djibouti city, the study encompasses five regions and 19 rural villages, 11 
of which fall within the original intervention area of the Great Green Wall 
(Supplementary Figure S1). The climate is hot and arid, with an average annual rainfall 
of 130 mm ( Ozer and Mahamoud, 2013; EVA Djibouti, 2015) . Temperatures range from 
23 °C in the winter to 42 °C in the summer (IGADa, 2020). The topography of the sites 
is characterized by altitudes ranging from 300 m to 2,000 m in mountainous areas and 
less than 300 m in valleys or wadis. 
The soils are mostly low in organic matter, poorly developed, stony, and highly 
degraded (Cheik, 2023). Annual losses of arable soil are estimated to be between 4% 
and 5% ( EVA Djibouti, 2015) . Agriculture and livestock constitute the primary 
economic activities and sources of livelihood for this predominantly rural population. 
Given the irregularity of rainfall, oasis -type agriculture is therefore dominant. Such 
agriculture is practised around wadis, near shallow water tables connected to valleys, 
owing to the development of an irrigation system consisting mainly of motor pumps, 
wells, and channels (IGADa, 2020). Traditional and family farms are small (averaging 
approximately 0.5 ha) and are dominated by vegetable crops combined with 
arboriculture, cereal crops, and fodder crops.

with the Kobotoolbox platform. The first questionnaire, administered to female farm 
managers, is designed to collect data on their involvement and specific contribution to 
agricultural practices. The second questionnaire, administered to male farmers and 
heads of household, a ims to collect data on household functioning and men's 
perceptions of women's agricultural work. The two questionnaires were constructed 
using the same structure, comprising five thematic modules, which were divided into

Statistical analyses 
To assess the level of farmers’ engagement in sustainable land management, a 
synthetic adoption index was developed based on the ten most widely implemented 
practices identified in the study area: organic fertilization, agroforestry, intercropping, 
crop rotations, hedgerows and fences, water management, pasture management, zai 
pits and half -moons, fallowing, and stone bund the construction (Supplementary 
Table S2). Each practice was coded as a binary variable, taken the value of 1 when the 
practice was adopted by the farmer and 0 otherwise. The synthetic adoption index was 
obtained by summing the scores attributed to the ten practices and normalizing the 
resulting value by total number of practices considered (Supplementary Formula S2).

Results 
General results 
The surveyed population consists of one -third men and two -thirds women. 
Young people (16–35 years old) represent only 1/5 of the surveyed population, which 
is composed mainly of heads of household (Table 2). Despite the youthfulness of this 
population, 78% of women and 83% of men are married; the proportion of widowed 
or divorced women is five times greater than that of widowed or divorced men, which 
can also be explained by the decision to focus on female heads of household (Table 2). 
All of the single people surveyed are men (13%). Women also stand out for their lower 
levels of education (14%) compared to men (42%). Their level of involvement in 
agricultural activities is comparable to that of men, but their investment in commercial 
activities is much grea ter (Table 2) . Women are less represented in salaried 
employment, which is occupied predominantly by men. 
The households surveyed were divided into three categories according to the number 
of members (Table 3). The majority of these households are small (< 4 members), with 
households of more than 8 people remaining marginal. Agriculture and livestock 
farming are the main sources of income (70%), followed by wage labour (24%) and 
trade and/or transport (Table 3). Rural migration as a source of income diversification 
concerns 8% of the households surveyed. The average number of men in households 
that recorded at least one migration is 1.67 compared with 0.33 among women. 
Agricultural activities are carried out mainly in small gardens, agropastoral farms, or 
livestock farms. The percentage of women with access to land varies between 17% and 
54% (for purchase and inheritance, respectively). Inheritance and gifts are the main 
modes of access, followed by purchases (Table 3).

Effect of the regional scale 
The “region” variable is greatly significant (p = 3.30e -05), as it increases the

land management practices (Supplementary Table S3). Land tenure (the method of 
accessing land) obtains a score close to significance (p = 0.065). Analysis of the 
coefficients of the variable categories at the regional level indicates a trend similar to 
that at the national level. The determinants of the adoption of sustainable land 
management practices are gender, farm type, crop type, and agricultural vulnerability.

Gender inequality 
The inclusion of these interactions improves the model’s performance, with a 
coefficient of determination reaching R² = 0.61 (Supplementary Table S4). Notably, the 
direct coefficient associated with gender becomes nearly zero (β = 0.01), indicating that 
the effect of gender cannot be explained solely by the farmer’s sex, but rather by its 
interaction with other structural factors (Supplementary Table S4). The results show, 
in particular, a significant interaction between gender and the type of agricultural 
holding. In vegetable gardens, the interaction is strongly positive (β = 1.5, p < 0.001), 
meaning that men adopt sustainable land management practices more than women in 
this type of holding (Supplementary Table S4). 
 
Discussion 
This study highlight the multidimensional aspects of sustainable land 
management practice adoption within the Great Green Wall region of Djibouti. We 
identified, on the one hand, key biophysical factors influencing the adoption of 
practices at all scales o f investigation and, on the other hand, socioeconomic factors 
influencing the adoption of practices at the local level. These results confirm the 
hypothesis of variability in the factors influencing the adoption of sustainable land 
management practices acr oss different scales of analysis and demonstrate strong 
interactions between factors related to gender, access to productive resources,

and Holvoet, 2016; Theriault et al., 2017; Mbile et al., 2019; Wekesah et al., 2019; Jha and 
Gupta, 2021; Lawson et al., 2020; Mponela et al., 2023). 
The interactions among gender, access to land, and the adoption of sustainable 
land management practices result in a decrease in the effect of gender and the 
emergence of land tenure as a determining factor when moving from the national to 
the village leve l. The strong impact of the “gender” variable at the national level is 
explained by the fact that it is considered consistently in this study and that its 
influence is analysed through the average of observations. At the regional and village 
levels, the in fluence of the gender variable diminishes, whereas that of the land 
variable becomes significant. This finding suggests that in villages where women have 
access to land, they are more likely to adopt sustainable land management practices, 
which consistent with studies that have demonstrated the link between women’s 
access to agricultural land and their investment in adopting sustainable land 
management practices (Deininger and Ali, 2008; Benjamin et al., 2021; Nadeem et al., 2022; 
Stiem-Bhatia and Koudougou , 2022) . Land ownership has increased the likelihood of 
women planting, reforesting, and investing in agroforestry in Kenya and Malawi 
(Collantes et al., 2018). Gender -sensitive land certification and registration programs 
have increased the likelihood of women engaging in water conservation and soil 
management practices in Ethiopian villages by 20 to 30 percent (Dugasseh et al., 2021). 
However, land access alone does not guarantee the substantial engagement of women 
in sustainable land management practices. Variables such as plot size and natural soil 
fertility can influence women's participation in sustainable land management. In 
Ghana and Tanzania, recent studies have reported how significant gender differences 
in plot size affect women's investment in su stainable land management (Monimart, 
2012; Awoke et al., 2025). Our qualitative field investigations provide important 
insights into these dynamics. These distinct tenure pathways appear to shape adoption 
behavior differently. Women with inherited land tenure often display greater long -
term investment capacity and are more likely to recruit agricultural laborers —
frequently migrants from Ethiopia and Somalia—who contribute directly to the day -
to-day implementation of sustainable land management practices. By contrast, women 
accessing land through collective donation sche mes typically operate on smaller 
shared plots and tend to prioritize short-term, immediately productive practices such 
as organic manure application and water management, which require limited surface 
area and generate faster agronomic returns. Analysing the influence of these variables 
would therefore be useful to complement the multiscale analysis proposed in this 
study. 
Our study reveals that there are potential interactions among gender, rural 
migration, and the adoption of practices, resulting in significant adoption rates of 
sustainable land management practices in villages with high -level migration

indirect empowerment of female farmers, linked to changes in gender relations in farm 
management brought about by male migration within villages. The adoption of 
sustainable land management practices in villages with high migration rates could also 
be the result of a knock -on effect linked to households reinvesting part of their 
migration income in agricultural technologies designed to improve productivity in the 
long term. Several studies have shown that households with more members migrating 
reinvest some of the remittances in agricultural assets, thereby promoting innovation 
and sustainability (Martey and Etwire, 2025). Djibouti is also a transit country for many 
migrants from Ethiopia and Somalia attempting to reach the Middle East ( IGADb, 
2020). Several of the study villages are situated along the Ethiopia –Djibouti–Saudi 
Arabia migration corridor. These agricultural villages are host areas for temporary 
migrants who provide a dynamic agricultural workforce, with farming skills firmly 
acquired i n their country of origin, and who may have contributed to the local 
dissemination of sustainable land management p ractices. These observations are 
consistent with several studies on the diffusion of sustainable land management 
practices in migrant -hosting areas and on the role of intervillage or translocal 
community social networks (Rockenbauch et al., 2019; Chamberlin et al., 2020). This high 
frequency of temporary migrants constitutes another parameter that explains the 
emergence of the “rural migration” variable as a significant determinant of the 
adoption of sustainable land management practices at the village level. However, our 
study did not examine the profiles of the migrants and did not consider the length of 
their stays in the host villages or the diversity of agricultural practices experienced by 
these migrants. Research incorporating migrant profiles could f urther refine our 
understanding of how this agricultural workforce contributes to the diffusion of 
sustainable land management practices in villages.

Conclusions 
This study is based on the analysis of ten sustainable land management 
practices in the Great Green Wall area of Djibouti. This work highlights relatively 
stable biophysical variables influencing the adoption of sustainable land management 
practices as well as socioeconomic variables which fluctuate significantly depending 
on the scale considered. This study highlights the importance of a multiscale approach 
to analyse the gender -related determinants of the adoption of sustainable land 
management practices. The results indicate that at the national level, gender, the 
nature of the agricultural production system, crop diversity, and agricultural 
vulnerability significantly explain the adoption of these practices. By successively 
integrating the regional and village dimensions, variables such as access to land and 
the frequency of rural migration become significant deter minants of the adoption of 
these practices. The initial gender disparities diminish with the addition of contextual 
variables, highlighting that inequalities in access to resources and opportunities, 
shaped by territorial conditions, truly determine the di fferences in the adoption of 
practices between men and women and not the gender of the farmer alone. Our study 
thus found that (i) determinants of adoption of SLM does vary significantly by scale; 
and (ii) gender inequalities in uptake of sustainable land management are shaped by 
multi-factorial influences. We therefore recommend that future sustainable land 
management approaches better address both these insights to achieve greater success.
