Grain boundary (GB)-vacancy interactions play a pivotal role in tailoring material properties. However,
the prevailing paradigm that “GBs function as vacancy sources/sinks ”lacks robust atomic-scale
evidence, thereby failing to discriminate the intrinsic mechanisms governing vacancy dynamics and
leaving critical unresolved issues— most notably the GB capacity for vacancy generation/annihilation.
To ﬁll this gap, we adopt molecular dynamics simulations to systematically observe the vacancy
thermal equilibration (VTE) process, wherein low-angle tilt GBs (LATGBs) are engineered as the
exclusive vacancy sources and sinks. Our results unveil a remarkable phenomenon: LATGBs maintain
invariant atomic structures despite continuous massive vacancy absorption and emission throughout
VTE. Accordingly, we propose that LATGBs function as catalysts for vacancy generation/annihilation,
with unlimited vacancy absorption/emission capacity. Furthermore, we corroborate four critical
atomic-scale ﬁndings: (1) Vacancy generation/annihilation rate at edge dislocations reaches
8.62 ns⁻¹·nm⁻¹; (2) Atomic migration rate within dislocation vacant channels is at least three orders of
magnitude faster than grain interior diffusion; (3) Seven of eight sites surrounding each vacant channel
are catalytically active due to ultralow vacancy formation energies; (4) Compressive-tensile stress
interfaces along dislocations mediate vacancy migration between active sites and grain interiors.
These atomic-scale insights establish a complete catalytic cycle governing LATGB-mediated vacancy
generation/annihilation.
Grain boundaries (GBs) are ubiquitous features of polycrystalline materials.
It is well established1–3 that their profound modulation effect on key material
properties stems from their interactions with other defects. Among these,
GB-vacancy interactions have garnered particular attention, as they dictate
critical behaviors—such as diffusion, phase transformation, and plastic
deformation—while being closely coupled to structural stability, mechanical
strength, and damage tolerance.
The current dominant paradigm—that GBs act as vacancy sources/
sinks—traces back to the 1950s, anchored by two seminal observations:
Barnes et al.4 observed preferential precipitation of helium bubbles at GBs in
irradiated copper following helium ion implantation, while Burke5 found
that pores adjacent to GBs exhibited more rapid shrinkage than those within
grain interiors during sintering. Accordingly, the authors hypothesized that
GBs function as vacancy sources to su pply vacancies for helium bubble
nucleation and growth, and as vacancy sinks to absorb excess vacancies and
facilitate the shrinkage of nearby pores. Since then, numerous experimental
studies6–11 have documented the generation and annihilation of vacancies at
GBs, validating the source/sink paradigm from a phenomenological per-
spective. To date, this framework has remained well-entrenched and largely
unchallenged in the academic community.
However, a comprehensive, deﬁnitive elucidation of the GB-vacancy
interaction mechanism depends intrinsically on detailed atomic-scale
insights—ag o a lt h a tr e m a i n saf o r m i d a b l echallenge for state-of-the-art
experimental techniques 12. Thus, virtually all existing atomic-scale
mechanistic investigations of GB-vacancy interactions resort to computer
simulations, with employed methodologies including but not limited to

Against this backdrop, a critical fundamental question arises:by what
mechanisms,a n dto what extent, do GBs mediate vacancy generation and
annihilation in their purported roleas the vacancy sources and sinks? On
this topic, existing studies have focused exclusively on tilt GBs and have
primarily addressed the “mechanism” component of this question. The
climb theory is rooted in the dislocation model of tilt GBs 27—which con-
ceptualizes tilt GBs as ordered walls of parallel edge dislocations—and posits
that tilt GBs absorb or emit vacancies via the climb of GB dislocations28–33.
The phase transition theory indicates that tilt GBs mediate these processes
through two-dimensional phase transitions 15,34,35,i nw h i c hv a r y i n gG B
vacancy concentrations trigger reversible structural transformations. Using
MD simulations of cascade collisions in the vicinity of GBs, Bai et al. 14
proposed an interstitial theory, whereby tilt GBs preferentially trap self-
interstitial atoms and annihilate nearby vacancies by emitting these inter-
stitials, rather than directly absorbing the vacancies. Thus far, it is unequi-
vocally established that GBs act as bothsources and sinks for vacancies. This
consensus aligns with experimental observations and theoretical frame-
works elucidating the underlying mechanisms, albeit with the envisaged
mechanisms remaining distinctly divergent. Furthermore, the “extent”
component of the question remains unaddressed. When functioning as a
source, a GB inherently possesses an upper limit on vacancy emission;
sustained vacancy emission will undoubtedly exhaust its vacancy supply.
Similarly, when serving as a sink, a GBalso has an intrinsic upper limit for
vacancy accommodation; prolonged vacancy absorption will eventually lead
to saturation. This deﬁnes the capacityof GBs to act as a source or sink for
vacancies. More importantly, in their role as sources or sinks, GBs them-
selves necessarily undergo structural changes upon absorbing or emitting
vacancies. Speciﬁcally, as the vacancyﬂux increases, these structural changes
become more pronounced, culminatingin a state of exhaustion or satura-
tion—a physical manifestation of the capacity. This capacity issue is also
intimately intertwined with another long-standing open question: whether
the vacancy concentration within individual grains of a polycrystalline
material can attain thermal equilibrium. If GBs exhibit aﬁnite vacancy sink/
source capacity, the achievement of a uniform equilibrium vacancy con-
centration within grains would be hindered. Conversely, the sustained
establishment of such uniform equilibrium vacancy concentration across
grains would imply that the sink/so urce capacity of GBs is effectively
unbounded—a scenario that logically runs counter to the fundamental
deﬁnition of a “source and sink”. Yet, to the best of our knowledge, this
capacity has never been systematica lly characterized. In fact, a compre-
hensive investigation into the dynamic mechanisms underpinning intensive
vacancy generation and annihilation mediated by GBs remains notably
lacking.
In this study, we present theﬁrst direct and comprehensive observation
of the GB-mediated vacancy thermal equilibration (VTE) process in a face-
centered cubic (fcc) Al system, in which a pair of low-angle tilt GBs is
rationally engineered as the sole vacancy source and sink. By leveraging the
time-averaged atomic volume spectroscopy (TAVS) method36—a recently
developed, state-of-the-arttechnique for vacancy identiﬁcation—we mon-
itored the real-time, concurrent evolution of GBs and vacancies across the
entire VTE process. The principle underpinning TAVS centers on identi-
fying the atomic “cages” that enwrap vacancies, rather than the vacancies
themselves36. Speci ﬁcally, Voronoi tessellation analysis discriminates
between vacancy-enwrapping atoms (VAs) and normal atoms (NAs) based
on their time-averaged atomic volu mes. Subsequent nearest-neighbor
analysis clusters these VAs into discrete cages, thereby enabling compre-
hensive characterization of the intrinsic properties of individual vacancies—
including their spatial location, size,volume, morphology, and energy state.
It is precisely this key capability of TAVS —accurate real-time vacancy
identiﬁcation—that facilitated the ﬁrst direct observation of the full GB-
mediated VTE process.

We now shift our focus to the NVT ensemble. As emphasized pre-
viously, the NVT ensemble mimics the constant-volume constraint inher-
ent to polycrystalline systems. During intensive vacancy generation and
annihilation (i.e., the VTE proce ss), the NPT ensemble sustains a zero-
pressure state by adjusting the system volume —thereby decoupling the
effects of vacancy activity from those of the induced hydrostatic stress. In
contrast, the NVT ensemble develops a non-zero hydrostatic stress as a
direct consequence of volumeﬁxation, which in turn modulates the energy
and volume of both GBs and grains. As illustrated by the evolution of
hydrostatic stress σSys in Fig. 3e, j, stress remains strictly zero in the NPT
ensemble; in the NVT ensemble, however, stress rises with vacancy accu-
mulation in UE * and declines with vacancy depletion in SE and SE *.
Ultimately, the equilibrium hydrostatic stresses for the three NVT samples
are determined to be 15.83 ± 2.20 MPa, −57.05 ± 2.34 MPa, and
−98.51 ± 2.31 MPa, respectively. This buildup of hydrostatic stress mod-
ulates the overall VTE process. First, as depicted in Fig.3f, the ﬁnal equili-
brium concentrations exhibit slight d isparities across the three samples.
Second, GBs and grains undergo densiﬁcation or intra-structural dilation to
accommodate the net change in vacancy volume, as illustrated in Fig. 3i.
Accordingly, the GBs in UE * are subjected to a compressive stress state,
whereas those in SE and SE* experience tensile stress. Third, the variation in
NVT system energy therefore stems not only from the vacancy generation
and annihilation, but also from such densiﬁcation and dilation processes.
Thus, as shown in Fig. 3h, the changes in system energy and vacancy
formation energy no longer align; instead, the magnitude of this discrepancy
scales with the amplitude of hydrostatic stress. Finally, this effect directly
induces subtle variations in GB energy, which, as illustrated in Fig. 3g,
deviate from the zero baseline.
Nonetheless, we highlight that even the minor changes in GB structure
and energy within the NVT ensemble bear no direct relation to vacancy
generation and annihilation. Collec tively, our results demonstrate that
vacancy generation and annihilationdo not directly induce structural var-
iations in GBs, regardless of whether the system is governed by the NPT or
NVT ensemble. Incidentally, the average values of all parameters during the
equilibrium stage (100–200 ns), as presented in Fig. 3, are summarized in

Based on the preceding visualization and quantitative monitoring of
the VTE process, we draw two critical conclusions.
(1) Our results unambiguously demonstrate that tilt GBs absorb and emit
vacancies, thereby functioning as both vacancy sources and sinks to
mediate the VTE process. This conclusion is consistent with the cur-
rent understanding established from extensive experimental observa-
tions and theoretical descriptions.
(2) Notably, our ﬁndings reveal unequivocally that the vacancy absorption
or emission exerts no discernible in ﬂu e n c eo nt h eg r a i nb o u n d a r i e s
themselves. Speciﬁcally, throughout the VTE process, despite the
extensive vacancy generation and annihilation, GBs remain structu-
rally invariant. Even the subtle variations observed in the NVT
ensemble are attributable to the induced hydrostatic stress, rather than
to vacancy absorption or emission events themselves. This ﬁnding
challenges the prevailing paradigm and provides a fundamentally
distinct perspective on the role of GBs in mediating vacancy generation
and annihilation.
Collectively, these two conclusions establish a novel conceptual fra-
mework for the GB-vacancy interaction mechanism: tilt GBs act as dual
catalysts for vacancy generation and annihilation, thereby facilitating the
VTE process. On the one hand, this mechanism can be rationalized in terms
of thermodynamic driving forces and kinetic energy barriers. As vacancies
are thermodynamic equilibrium defects, any deviation of the intragranular
vacancy concentration from its equilibrium value —whether under-
saturation or oversaturation—creates a thermodynamic driving force that
drives the system toward equilibrium. However, the energy barrier asso-
ciated with direct vacancy generation or annihilation within grain interiors
is prohibitively high, rendering this pathway kinetically inaccessible. In
contrast, this barrier is drastically reduced at tilt GBs44–46. In other words, the
catalytic role of tilt GBs stems from their propensity to drastically lower the
energy barrier for the vacancy generation and annihilation. On the other
hand, a hallmark feature of a catalyst is that it retains its chemical identity
and structural integrity unchanged following the catalytic process. Notably,
the structural and energetic invariance of tilt GBs throughout the VTE
process aligns perfectly with this canonical deﬁnition of catalysis. On this
basis, we put forward a catalytic me chanism to clarify the fundamental
nature of GB-vacancy interactions.
The foregoing comprehensive quan titative monitoring of the VTE
process provides robust corroboration for the proposed catalytic mechan-
ism. Nevertheless, two key microscopic details remain to be fully elucidated.

Catalytically active sites, GBs’ invariance and catalytic cycle
To elucidate the catalytically active sites and the structural invariance of GBs,
we conducted a high-resolution temporal sampling analysis of the initial
2 ns of the VTE process under the NVT ensemble, as illustrated in the inset
of Fig. 3f. The objective herein is to pinpoint all vacancy generation sites
(VGS) and vacancy annihilation sites (VAS). The methodology for identi-
fying VGSs and VASs is described in detail in the Methods section. In a prior
study36,w eq u a n t iﬁed the jumping frequency of monovacancies in fcc-Al at
900 K as 128.75 ns⁻¹. This translates to a mean residence time of 7.77 ps
between consecutive vacancy jumps. Accordingly, the average minimum
duration for a round-trip vacancy jump event is 15.54 ps, which corre-
sponds precisely to the average minimum lifetime of the vacancy genera-
tion/annihilation round-trip. Based on this data, the time step employed for
VGS/VAS analysis was set to 1 ps. This time step is considerably shorter
than the average minimum lifetime of 15.54 ps, thereby providing suf ﬁ-
ciently high temporal resolution t o ensure that essentially no vacancy
generation or annihilation events are missed. Leveraging the 1 ps time step
—which yields a total of 2 × 10³ con ﬁgurations—every vacancy event
occurring over the 2 ns duration was fully documented, with their respective
VGSs and VASs identiﬁed. In total, 1303 VGSs and 1256 VASs were pin-

(3) Although the vacancy generatio n/annihilation pathway has been
delineated, one ﬁnal key issue remains to be addressed. When a
vacancy embryo is generated or annihilated within the vacant channel,
this process is inevitably accompanied by the concomitant generation
or consumption of a surplus atom within the channel: the formation of
a vacancy embryo coincides with the ejection of a surplus atom,
whereas the annihilation of a vacancy embryo requires the incor-
poration of such a surplus atom. Historically, the prevailing view
regarding this phenomenon has maintained that GBs accommodate
such surplus atoms via internal str uctural relaxation, a process that
would inevitably alter both the structure and energetics of GBs.
However, our results, quantitatively validated in Fig.3, clearly establish
that GBs do not accommodate these surplus atoms internally at all.
Instead, GBs must dissipate (or acquire) such surplus atoms to (or
from) adjacent grains owing to the robust structural invariance of GBs.
Thus, the key unresolved issue pertaining to this ﬁnal aspect is as
follows: How does the GB dissipate (or acquire) such surplus atoms to
(or from) the grains? The conventional view holds that vacancy gen-
eration/annihilation and the surp lus atom dissipation/acquisition
must occur as paired, spatially and temporally coupled events. Typi-
cally, within the grain interior, these two events coincide spatially and
temporally: when an atom transforms into a surplus atom (self-
interstitial), its original lattice site is concomitantly converted into a
vacancy, and vice versa. In contrast, within the vacancy channels at
GBs, the spatial and temporal decoupling of these paired events is
feasible. It is precisely this decoupling that enables the absorption or
emission of vacancies while maintaining the structural invariance of
GBs. This spatiotemporal decoup ling is substantiated by two key
kinetic characteristics of the vacant channels. First, there is an ultrahigh
vacancy generation/annihilation rate of 8.62 ns⁻¹·nm⁻¹, a value that is in
principle equivalent to the rate of surplus atom dissipation/acquisition.
Such an ultrahigh rate underscores that vacant channels are extremely
facile for the generation and annihilation of both vacancies and surplus
atoms. Second, the vacant channels feature a critical prerequisite: the
nearly unrestricted migration of surplus atoms within the channels. To
validate this unrestricted atomic mobility, we partitioned the system
into two distinct domains—grains and GBs—and computed the one-

Discussion
In this study, using the TAVS method for precise, real-time identiﬁcation of
vacancies, we have for the ﬁrst time achieved molecular dynamics simula-
tions of the VTE process, wherein low-angle tilt GBs serve as the exclusive
vacancy source or sink. Both visualization and quantitative monitoring of
the VTE processes conclusively reveal the following robust ﬁndings. (1)
During the VTE process, GBs indeed function as dual vacancy sources and
sinks—that is, all vacancy generation and annihilation events during the
equilibration process originate at GBs, consistent with the conventional
“source/sink” paradigm. (2) In contrast to this established paradigm,
however, GBs preserve their structural integrity throughout the entire
intensive vacancy generation/annihilation process. These observations lead
us to propose a catalytic mechanism governing low-angle tilt GB-mediated
vacancy generation and annihilation. To elucidate this catalytic mechanism
at the atomic scale, we quanti ﬁed the distribution of VGSs and VASs,
calculated the vacancy formation energy at lattice sites surrounding vacant
channels, and determined the one-dimensional mean squared displacement
in both GBs and grain interiors. Based on these comprehensive results, we
present an atomic-scale descriptionof the catalytic mechanism governing
low-angle tilt GB-mediated vacancy generation and annihilation. (1) Lattice
sites surrounding the vacant channels exhibit extremely low vacancy for-
mation energy, which markedly reduces the energy barrier for vacancy
formation, thereby enabling high-density, high-rate vacancy embryo gen-
eration and annihilation. These sites are thus deﬁned as catalytically active
sites, satisfying the ﬁrst prerequisite for a catalytic mechanism. (2) The
ultrahigh atomic mobility within vacant channels allows vacancies to be
generated or annihilated at speciﬁc locations, accompanied by the dissipa-
tion or acquisition of surplus atoms at distal sites within the channels. This
unique decoupling behavior enables l ow-angle tilt GBs to preserve their
structural invariance, ful ﬁlling the second prerequisite for a catalytic
mechanism. (3) Vacancy embryos continuously generated or annihilated at
seven catalytically active sites within the eight-site ring surrounding each
vacant channel migrate between GBs and adjacent grains via the interface
between compressive and tensile regions, which acts as the critical migration
pathway. Ultimately, the proposedcatalytic mechanism provides a deﬁni-
tive answer not only to the “by what mechanism” component of the fun-
damental question posed at the outset, but also to the “to what extent ”
component: the capacity of GBs to act as vacancy sources or sinks is

Methods
MD simulations of the VTE process
A bicrystal model containing 313,200 atoms was constructed to simulate the
VTE process. The model comprises two fcc grains with a misorientation
angle of 9.5°, each with dimensions of 12.138 × 14.616 × 14.616 nm³. The
system was then isothermally relaxed at 900 K for 200 ns to investigate the
VTE behavior, with data output every 0.1 ns and an optimal averaging
duration of 4 ps, as validated in our previous work36. For the NPT ensemble,
the system pressure was maintained at 0 bar; for the NVT ensemble, the
system volume was ﬁxed at the equilibrium value corresponding to 900 K
and 0 bar. The equations of motion were integrated numerically using the
Verlet-Leapfrog algorithm, with a time step of 2 fs. Temperature and
pressure were controlled using the Nosé-Hoover thermostat and barostat47,
respectively. All simulations were performed using the LAMMPS
package48,49, and interatomic interactions of aluminum were modeled using
the embedded-atom method (EAM) potential developed by Mendelev
et al.37.

GBs were delineated based on time-a veraged atomic volume, consistent
with the vacancy identiﬁcation protocol employed in this study. Candidate
GB atoms were screened using a ± 1% volume deviation threshold relative to
the atomic volume of defect-free sites.

Vacancy formation energy
The vacancy formation energy (ΔEf) at speciﬁc lattice sites surrounding the
vacant channels of GBs was calculated using classical molecular mechanics
within the LAMMPS package48,49. Consistent with the MD simulations, the
EAM potential developed by Mendelev et al.37 was employed. Prior to ΔEf
calculations, the entire system containing the ideal tilt GBs was fully relaxed
via the conjugate gradient algorithm, with convergence criteria of 10 ⁻⁵
eV·nm⁻¹ per atom (force tolerance) and 10⁻⁶ eV (energy tolerance). Twenty-
four representative lattice sites were then selected for calculation.
The vacancy formation energyΔEf at each selected site is deﬁned as the
energy difference between the supercell containing a single targeted
monovacancy and the defect-free reference supercell, expressed
mathematically as:

where E1v denotes the total energy of the supercell with one atom removed
from the target site, E0v is the total energy of the defect-free supercell, and
e0 = −3.41 eV is the energy of an isolated atom.
Identiﬁcation of VGS and VAS
The criteria for identifying VGS and VAS are deﬁned as follows. Based on
the TAVS framework, a vacancy is identiﬁed by the atomic cage enwrapping
it. For any given vacancy, the relationship between its atomic cages at two
consecutive time steps falls into one of the following categories:
(1) Complete overlap: The atomic cages are identical, indicating that the
vacancy remains stationary.
(2) Partial overlap: For a monovacancy, this is characterized by exactly 5
overlapping atoms, corresponding to a single vacancy jump.
(3) No overlap: This signi ﬁes a vacancy generation or annihilation event.
• If the vacancy cage exists only at the earlier time step but vanishes at the
later step (i.e., all atoms constituting the cage revert from VA to NA),
the event is classiﬁed as an annihilation.
• Conversely, if the vacancy cage appears only at the later time step, the
event is classiﬁed as a generation.
The lattice site associated with such a generation or annihilation event
is subsequently de ﬁned as a VGS or VAS, respectively, and recorded
accordingly.
