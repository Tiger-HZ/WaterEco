Abstract
This study investigates the interplay between social and technological factors affecting traditional craftspeople ’s
ongoing willingness to utilize generative arti ﬁcial intelligence (AIGC) tools, speci ﬁcally text-based models and image
generators, aiming to elucidate the cognitive and affective mechanisms underlying their behavior, grounded in social
inﬂuence theory and the stimulus –organism–response (SOR) model. This study uses a mixed-methods approach
combining partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) with fuzzy-set qualitative comparative
analysis (fsQCA) to analyze data from 209 traditional craftspeople in China. The study examined the linear and group
effects of social in ﬂuences and technical attributes on perceived value, trust, and willingness to sustain use. PLS-SEM
results indicate that industry support, peer in ﬂuence, content quality, and performance signi ﬁcantly enhance
craftspeople’s perceived value and trust, which, in turn, positively affect their continued willingness to use. The
effects of social identity and platform technology on trust are not signi ﬁcant. Mediation effect analysis indicated that
perceived value partially mediated multiple paths, whereas trust more often appeared as a full mediator. fsQCA
further revealed six suf ﬁciently grouped paths with high consistency, which were categorized into three dominant
mechanism types: content –performance compensation-driven, identity –peer reinforcement-driven, and social
endorsement–technology synergy. This study integrates and applies social in ﬂuence theory and the SOR model to
the study of AIGC adoption behavior among traditional craftspeople. The results demonstrate that for culturally
embedded and digitally vulnerable traditional craftspeople, social and technical cues operate through distinct
cognitive and affective routes, with trust playing a particularly central role in driving continuance intention. This
provides a nuanced view of the behavioral drivers within this traditional group of craftspeople.

language modeling, text-to-image diffusion modeling, and multimodal generation technologies (CCID Consulting,
2024). In this technological change, China has become a fertile ground for the diffusion of AIGC, with platforms such
as Wenxinyiyan, Pangu, and DeepSeek pervading education, information brokerage, advertising, and the broader

Traditional craftspeople, such as woodcarvers, potters, embroiderers, and goldsmiths, represent a socially and cul-

reach, and amplify the cultural inﬂuence of their creations (Liu et al.,2024; Pan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024; Yin et al.,
2023).
Evidence indicates that some craftspeople proactively adopt speci ﬁc AIGC tools —such as generative AI image
platforms (e.g., Midjourney, JiMeng AI) for pattern design and visual drafting, and text-based models (e.g., ChatGPT,
DeepSeek) for cultural storytelling and marketing copywriting —to re ﬁne creative problem-solving, boost productive
efﬁciency, and cultivate a global cultural identity (Mazur-W łodarczyk, 2024;Y u , 2024; Zhai, 2025). Conversely, a
segment expresses caution, voicing concerns regarding technological pro ﬁciency, ﬁnancial investments, cultural
appropriation, data management, and the potential for machine-driven job displacement (Bamoallem,2024; Cardini and
Noguera, 2024; Irfan and Salam, 2020;P f a h l ,2024;Z h a n ga n dH e ,2023). This divergence highlights the necessity of
examining the psychosocial processes through which non-technical cultural practitioners transform technological
capabilities into sustained involvement.
Crucially, while the initial adoption of AIGC tools might be driven by mere curiosity, low entry barriers, or
industry hype, the true measure of technological success lies in the intention to use them continuously. Traditional
artisans are likely to abandon new technology after a brief trial period if it infringes on their intellectual property
rights, lacks practical utility, or fails to respect the authenticity of their culture. Therefore, understanding the moti-
vations that drive their continued use of the tool (rather than just their initial acceptance) is a crucial bottleneck for
achieving meaningful digital transformation in cultural heritage.
Existing information systems research provides valuable insights into this issue; however, signi ﬁcant gaps remain.
Continuance intention has been widely studied across various ﬁelds to understand users ’ post-adoption behaviors.
Scholarship on this topic largely scrutinizes knowledge-centric professionals and everyday users, foregrounding
rational-cognitive benchmarks such as perceived usefulness and usability (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Gefen et al., 2003;
Hung et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026). In contrast, research on AIGC primarily focuses on explaining
technology acceptance, performance expectations, and willingness to use, while overlooking the interaction among
complex social factors, such as social norms, industry support, and trust (Bai and Yang, 2025; Gao et al., 2023; Jiang
et al., 2024). Within cultural heritage scholarship, research has primarily focused on policy frameworks and platform
functionalities, whereas the nuanced, ground-level technological perceptions of craftspeople remain markedly under-
examined (Liu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Consequently, we still lack holistic theoretical explanations that
clarify the reasons and mechanisms by which craftspeople continue to use AIGC after exposure to it.
To ﬁll this gap, the present study adopts social and technical perspectives by integrating social in ﬂuence theory
(Kelman, 1958) and the S-O-R model (Mehrabian and Russell, 1974) to construct an analytical framework. Emphasis
is placed on how social and technical attributes in ﬂuence behavioral decisions through psychological responses.
Social in ﬂuence theory emphasizes how industry support, peer endorsement, and descriptive norms shape attitude
change. The S-O-R model treats technical attributes as external stimuli that elicit internal cognitive-emotional states
(organisms), which then trigger behavioral reactions. By combining the two, it is possible to track how social-
environmental stimuli (industry support, peer in ﬂuence, social identity) and technical-environmental stimuli (per-
formance expectations, content quality, platform usability) interact to shape craftspeople ’s perceived value and trust,
which, in turn, drives their continued use intention. Based on this theoretical framework, this study focuses on the
following research questions:
RQ1: What social and technical factors signiﬁcantly inﬂuence the perceived value and trust of traditional craftspeople in
AIGC systems?
RQ2: How do perceived value and trust mediate the relationship between these in ﬂuences and continued will-
ingness to use?

RQ3: Are there multiple combinations of pathways that could lead to the sustained use of AIGC by craftspeople?
To answer the above questions, this study utilizes a mixed-method design. First, partial least squares structural
equation modeling (PLS-SEM) is applied to test the effects of the proposed paths; second, fuzzy-set qualitative
comparative analysis (fsQCA) is used to uncover equivalent con ﬁguration paths. The data were collected from an on-
site and online questionnaire survey of 209 traditional craftspeople in ﬁve provinces of China. This dual analytic
method is strictly necessary for this speci ﬁc population. While PLS-SEM effectively captures the linear net effects of
technical and social stimuli, traditional craftspeople, being culturally embedded and often digitally vulnerable,
typically rely on complex, interdependent conditions to adopt new technologies. Therefore, fsQCA is essential for
uncovering the nonlinear, asymmetric, and con ﬁgurational pathways that drive continuance intention in techno-
cultural contexts. The contributions of this study are threefold: ﬁrst, it extends the study of continuance to the
culturally embedded and under-attended group of non-technical cultural work users; second, by moving beyond a
contribution-by-assembly approach, it demonstrates how social and technical cues operate through distinct cognitive
and affective routes, revealing that trust plays a particularly central role in the continuance intention of these digitally
vulnerable users; and third, at the methodological level, it demonstrates the complementary advantages of structural
equation modeling (SEM) and fsQCA in explaining complex sociotechnological phenomena in the cultural
heritage ﬁeld.
The remainder of this article is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews the relevant literature and formulates the
research hypotheses; Section 3 describes the research design and data analysis process; Section 4 presents the
structural equation model and fsQCA results; Section 5 discusses the theoretical and practical implications; and
Section 6 concludes the study, acknowledges its limitations, and outlines directions for future research.
Literature review and research hypothesis
Social in ﬂuence theory
Social in ﬂuence theory ﬁrst distinguished between informational and normative in ﬂuences: the former refers to
individuals making judgments based on information provided by others, whereas the latter refers to individuals
following the expectations of others or groups because they want to be recognized by them (Deutsch and Gerard,
1955). Kelman ( 1958) further classi ﬁed the mechanisms by which social in ﬂuences work on individuals into three
types: conformity, identi ﬁcation, and internalization. This theoretical system reveals how others ’ behavior and group
attitudes in the social environment affect individual cognition and decision-making through the social evaluation
system. As the theoretical framework advanced, Venkatesh and Davis ( 2000) recast social factors as decisive external
fulcrums for reorienting attitudes, contending that these signals ignite behavioral intention outright and, via reframed
dispositions, cascade to mold subsequent conduct. Many studies have charted the deep embedding of social-in ﬂuence
theory within digital-pedagogy venues (Hwang, 2016), online-commerce realms (Kwahk and Ge, 2012; Liu, 2023),
and social-network habitats (Liu et al., 2019; Perez-Vega et al., 2016), jointly highlighting the indelible imprint that
social factors have on users ’ attitudinal and behavioral patterns. Taken together, these studies demonstrate the broad
applicability of social in ﬂuence theory, illustrating and validating the key role of factors such as social norms,
collective identity, and organizational support in in ﬂuencing users ’ attitudes towards new technologies and their
continuance intention.
However, most current empirical tests of social in ﬂuence theories primarily focus on groups such as consumers and
university students. There is a relative lack of research on the appropriateness of the unskilled cultural occupational
group–traditional craftspeople. Typically, traditional craftspeople conduct their creative activities in small units, and
their behavioral decisions are highly dependent on social networks, industry value orientations, and group identity
systems. They are in ﬂuenced by surrounding public opinion, industry support policies, and peer adoption behaviors
(Połeć and Murawska, 2021). These characteristics are highly compatible with the core logic of social in ﬂuence
theory and provide an important theoretical foundation for this study.

Therefore, to avoid theoretical redundancy and map directly onto Kelman ’s( 1958) foundational social in ﬂuence
mechanisms, this study explicitly operationalizes these antecedents. First, industry support aligns with the compliance
mechanism; this is particularly salient for traditional craftspeople, whose trade relies heavily on external institutional
resources, government grants, and cultural survival policies. Second, social identity corresponds to the identi ﬁcation
mechanism because artisans are deeply rooted in heritage communities, and aligning with group values is essential for
maintaining a self-deﬁning relationship with the community. Third, peer in ﬂuence operates through an internalization
mechanism: craft techniques are traditionally disseminated via intimate peer networks and master-apprentice models,
making peer adoption behavior highly internalized and dif ﬁcult to change. By constructing a structural model, we
reveal how these social factors in ﬂuence traditional craftspeople ’s perceptions, trust, and willingness to continue
using AIGC technology. We also extended the social in ﬂuence theory to user groups within the cultural industry.
S-O-R model
The stimulus –organism–response (SOR) model, derived from environmental psychology, reveals how the external
environment inﬂuences behavioral responses by altering an individual ’s internal state (Mehrabian and Russell, 1974).
The strength of the model lies in its ability to link external stimuli to internal mental states, forming a complete
explanatory path for behavior analysis.
In prior studies, the SOR model has been widely used to explore the mechanisms of user behavior. For instance,
Bitner (1992) used the SOR model to study the role and in ﬂuence of the physical environment on consumer behavior.
Wang and Chen ( 2023) found that leader characteristics in the webcasting environment can awaken consumers ’ user
experience and ultimately affect their consumption intentions through the SOR model. Recently, the application of
the SOR model has gradually extended to information technology. Nieves-Pavón et al. ( 2023) explored the rela-
tionship between mobile applications and user loyalty and usage behavior in the tourism industry using the SOR
model architecture, con ﬁrming the important role of loyalty in the relationship between information technology and
user behavior. Han et al. ( 2023) constructed a virtual reality (VR) shopping environment based on the SOR model to
explore telepresence and interactivity in VR technology and their in ﬂuence on consumer experience and usage
behavior, and discovered the important role of VR in the digitization of supply chain management for sustainable
management. These studies show that the SOR model can be applied to predict user psychology and behavior in
complex information technology environments; in particular, it can reveal the deep impact of external information
stimuli, such as technological attributes and platform design, on user attitudes and behavior.
In this study, AIGC is presented as an innovative technological tool, with its platform technology, content
generation quality, and performance serving as typical information and technological stimulus variables that may
drive sustained usage behavior among traditional craftspeople by in ﬂuencing their psychological states. Integrating
the S-O-R framework with social in ﬂuence theory provides new explanatory leverage beyond well-trodden IS
models, such as TAM2 or UTAUT, which often default to utilitarian or organizational contexts. While social
inﬂuence theory explains external normative pressures, the S-O-R model illuminates internal psychological pro-
cessing. Furthermore, it is critical to specify unique organism-level states in this techno-cultural context. While
variables such as satisfaction represent mere post-hoc use status evaluations and perceived threat to authenticity
captures purely defensive reactions, perceived value and trust provide a comprehensive dual-processing mechanism.
Perceived value serves as a cognitive organism that captures the rational assessment of AIGC ’s utility, efﬁciency, and
cultural ﬁt. Trust serves as an effective mechanism, primarily capturing users ’ conﬁdence in the system ’s functional
reliability and helpfulness. For craftspeople, while focusing on content dependability, this trust manifests as a vital
affective belief that the AIGC tool can reliably assist their creative process without disrupting it. Together, these dual
cognitive-affective states constitute the precise internal conditions required to sustain the AIGC.
Therefore, this study introduces the SOR model as a systematic supplement to the path of “technological envir-
onmental stimuli” within the social in ﬂuence theory to explain the role of “social environmental factors. ” Integrating
the SOR model with social in ﬂuence, technological in ﬂuence, psychological status, and behavioral will can help to
comprehensively construct the cognitive assessment and behavioral response mechanism for traditional craftspeople

Research hypothesis
Impact of industry support on perceived value and trust
In the adoption process of emerging technologies, industry support, as a social force with the attribute of institutional
guarantee, is usually provided by government departments, industry associations, or head enterprises through policy
guidance, standardization, and public opinion guidance, which has a key in ﬂuence on the cognitive construction and
behavioral expectations of users (Mohammadi et al., 2024; Sharma and Gupta, 2024). Such normative social
inﬂuences can enhance individuals ’ positive perceptions and psychological security regarding new technologies by
increasing their external authority and professionalism.
Speciﬁcally, when new technologies, such as AIGC, are endorsed by the government and promoted by the
industry, users are more likely to form higher perceived value judgments based on information from reliable sources;
that is, the technology is considered to have higher practical utility and innovative advantages, thus increasing its
perceived value (Gefen et al., 2003). Simultaneously, a standardized, uni ﬁed system with clear institutional support
can continuously reduce the uncertainty associated with its use, thereby enhancing users ’ trust in the technology

H1: Industry support has a signi ﬁcant positive effect on traditional craftspeople ’s perceived value of AIGC.

Impact of social identity on perceived value and trust
Social identity refers to the psychological process by which individuals attribute themselves to a speci ﬁc social group
and derive a sense of belonging, self-identity, and value from it. In this process, the group ’s normative in ﬂuence
prompts individuals to be more inclined to accept ideas and behavioral patterns that are generally accepted in the
group (Hogg, 2000). This group identity leads individuals to be more accepting of the group ’s generally accepted
behavioral patterns and technological concepts, which enhances their perceived value judgments of the new tech-
nology, viewing it as a useful tool that ﬁts their cultural and professional orientation (Hamari et al., 2015).
In addition, social identity can mitigate individuals ’ cognitive uncertainty regarding new technologies through
information sharing and behavioral imitation (Cocker et al., 2021). Informational in ﬂuences within a group can help
individuals assess the availability and reliability of a technology by familiarizing them with others ’ choices, thereby
increasing their trust. This process is particularly important for traditional craftspeople. They often develop similar
attitudes in informal interactions and communities of practice, drawing on peer agreement and cultural identity to
accept new technologies. When studying these interactions, it is crucial to distinguish between social identity and
peer in ﬂuence. Peer in ﬂuence involves observable behaviors and direct information from others (Riva et al., 2022),
whereas social identity is rooted in a strong sense of belonging and cultural connections. For culturally sensitive
groups, such as traditional craftspeople, building trust in disruptive technologies such as AIGC might require more
than just a general sense of belonging or internalized identity (Po łeć and Murawska, 2021). As guardians of cultural
heritage, they arguably have greater trust to gain. They may ultimately rely more heavily on the quality of tangible,
authentic content and actual performance outcomes than on general community sentiments (Cardini and Noguera,
2024). Nevertheless, following the foundational logic of social in ﬂuence theory, in which identity fosters psycho-
logical safety, we hypothesize the following:
H3: Social identity positively affects the perceived value of AIGC among traditional craftspeople.
H4: Social identity positively affects traditional craftspeople ’s trust in AIGC.
Impact of peer in ﬂuence on perceived value and trust
Peer in ﬂuence captures the process by which an individual ’s cognitions, affects, and behaviors are guided by the
attitudes and comportment of counterparts whose occupational, identity, or interest backgrounds closely align with
their own (Riva et al., 2022). Social-in ﬂuence discourse typically positions this mechanism in the informational
register: agents recalibrate their readings of unfamiliar phenomena by metabolizing data, experiential accounts, and
counsel from others (Tian et al., 2022). In practice, novices often rely on experienced peers when evaluating new
technologies to reduce uncertainty. Positive shared experiences regarding AIGC encourage traditional craftspeople to
view these tools as reliable and helpful for their creative process, thereby increasing their perceived value. Moreover,
the “acquaintance-validation” mechanism renders peer-sourced information more trustworthy than edicts from distant
authorities, thereby fortifying traditional craftspeople ’s faith in AIGC ’s stability and reliability (Yadav et al., 2022).
In traditional craft communities, word of mouth, skill exchange, and informal networks are often important agents
of knowledge transfer and tool adoption. Therefore, peer in ﬂuence is considered an important source of external
stimuli affecting skill perception and trust judgments. Based on this, the following hypotheses are proposed:
H5: Peer in ﬂuence positively impacts the perceived value of AIGC among traditional craftspeople.
H6: Peer in ﬂuence positively impacts traditional craftspeople ’s trust in AIGC.

Impact of platform technology on perceived value and trust
Platform technology is de ﬁned here as the foundational system quality of an AIGC tool, distinct from the quality of
the generated content. As the basic infrastructural support for a technology application system, the operational
stability, interface navigability (ease of use), and interactive reliability of platform technology are important factors
inﬂuencing users’ technology acceptance behavior (DeLone and McLean, 2003). Users’ willingness to continue using
a technology often stems from their overall perception that it can achieve ef ﬁciency, ease of use, and safety
(Venkatesh et al., 2012).
Speciﬁcally, AIGC technology, which is fully functional, stable, and user-friendly, can lower the threshold for
traditional craftspeople to use it, improve the ef ﬁciency and success rate of their access to content, and thus enhance
their perceived value of the technology. Simultaneously, the security of system operation and the immediacy of
interactive feedback can effectively alleviate users ’ uncertainty and perceptions of risk, further enhancing their trust in
technology (Ba and Pavlou, 2002). In traditional craft application scenarios, the adaptability of platform technology
determines whether traditional craftspeople can successfully integrate AIGC tools into their creative processes,
which, in turn, affects their psychological acceptance and intention to continue using the tool. However, in the
speciﬁc context of traditional crafts, the mechanism of trust formation can be highly strict. While platform tech-
nology, such as ease of use and a stable interface, provides a basic level of usability, it may not be suf ﬁcient to build
deep trust. Craftspeople are protective of their cultural authenticity and intellectual property rights. Therefore, their
trust is likely to depend more on the actual performance and stylistic accuracy of the generated content than on the
platform’s ease of use. Despite this potential contextual nuance, based on the classical information systems literature,
where system quality mitigates risk (Ba and Pavlou, 2002), we propose the following hypotheses:
H7: Platform technology positively impacts the perceived value of AIGC among traditional craftspeople.
H8: Platform technology positively impacts the trust of traditional craftspeople in AIGC.
Impact of content quality on perceived value and trust
Content quality designates the composite performance of machine-generated text, imagery, and audiovisual artifacts
with respect to accuracy, inventiveness, internal coherence, and practical utility (Peng et al., 2024). Anchored in the
SOR paradigm, content quality functions as a prototypical technological stimulus that markedly shapes users ’
perceptual states, thereby cascading into differentiated behavioral reactions (Wang et al., 2024). Elevated content
caliber augments users ’ perceived value (Pan et al., 2025). In particular, among the traditional artisans group, the
quality of the content directly affects their willingness to introduce AIGC as a creative tool into their daily practice.
Content quality also largely determines the level of trust users have in the technology system. Accurate, consistent,
and secure content output not only reduces users ’ concerns about system errors or deviations but also increases their
conﬁdence in the system ’s stability and reliability (Li and Yang, 2024).
In summary, content quality is re ﬂected not only in the AIGC acceptance process, serving as an output indicator of
technical performance, but also as a key variable in shaping positive psychological perceptions and trust attitudes
among users. Therefore, the following hypotheses are proposed:
H9: Content quality positively affects the perceived value of AIGC by traditional craftspeople.
H10: Higher content quality increases trust in AIGC among traditional craftspeople.
Impact of performance on perceived value and trust
Acting as an external technological stimulus (S), performance refers to the objective, tangible downstream outcomes,
such as the innovative value and economic effectiveness that users realize through the empowerment of technology in
all aspects of creation, operation, and dissemination (Goodhue and Thompson, 1995). This term covers the actual
value it brings in all aspects of design, production, and marketing (Lv and Wang, 2024).
The performance of AIGC as an external stimulus with positive feedback can elicit positive psychological
responses and in ﬂuence users ’ subsequent behavioral choices. When craftspeople receive more expressive work,

growth, will boost users ’ conﬁdence in the platform ’s technological capabilities and sustainability, thereby enhancing

H11: Performance positively affects the perceived value of AIGC by traditional craftspeople.
H12: Performance positively affects traditional craftspeople ’s trust in AIGC.
Inﬂuence of perceived value, trust, and continuance intention relationships
In the AIGC application, while performance represents the actual outcomes, perceived value serves as the internal
organism-level (O) evaluation. It refers to the subjective, cognitive cost-bene ﬁt calculation that users make after
comprehensively weighing the tangible returns of system functions against the time, effort, and learning costs
invested. As users continue to gain creative inspiration, time savings, or business opportunities from the AIGC
platform, their overall value perception will continue to increase (Gupta and Kim, 2007). The accumulation of
perceived value can effectively reduce technological uncertainty and enhance users ’ trust in the system ’s stability and
adaptability (Gefen et al., 2003).
In the context of traditional crafts, trust serves as a profound psychological safety mechanism and a reliable
dependence on the tool. Speci ﬁcally, in line with our measurement items, trust is de ﬁned as craftspeople ’s ﬁrm belief
that the AIGC tool can effectively address their creative concerns and consistently provide reliable and helpful
content. Although our items do not directly measure explicit constructs such as intellectual property assurance or
ethical transparency, we posit that, in this culturally sensitive context, the measured trust likely re ﬂects a broader
underlying sense of reliability and cultural safety. In the context of cultural industries, traditional craftspeople ’s trust
in creative tools determines whether they formally incorporate these tools into their daily creative processes (Chiu
et al., 2012). The accumulation of perceived value and trust builds users ’ continuance intention regarding the tool,
and this willingness re ﬂects not only users ’ positive attitudes but also the recognition of the long-term dependency
relationship. Based on this, we propose the following hypotheses:
H13: Perceived value has a positive effect on traditional craftspeople ’s trust in AIGC.
H14: Perceived value positively affects traditional craftspeople ’s willingness to continue using AIGC models.
H15: Trust has a positive effect on traditional craftspeople ’s willingness to continue using AIGC.
Research methodology
Questionnaire development
The questionnaire used in this study comprised two parts and aimed to systematically assess the cognitive and
behavioral responses of traditional craftspeople to AIGC technology. The ﬁrst part collected basic demographic
information from survey respondents, including statistical variables such as gender, age, education level, and years of
experience in the industry, which were used to portray the basic characteristics and structural distribution of the
sample and frequency of use. The second part measures core variables, including social in ﬂuences (industry support,
social identity, and peer in ﬂuence) and technical in ﬂuences (platform technology, content quality, and performance),
mediator variables (perceived value and trust), and outcome variables (continuance intention). All measurements
were constructed with reference to existing mature scales. To ensure contextual ﬁt, the item wording was speci ﬁcally
adapted; for example, generic terms such as technology or system were replaced with AIGC tools, and performance
outcomes were tailored to craft creation and marketing. Two Chinese –English bilingual experts with doctoral degrees

Sample selection and data collection
This study was approved by the ethics committee of the institution where the researcher was employed
(No.2025040101). The study focuses on a group of non-technical cultural users –traditional craftspeople. To ensure a
representative sample and target ﬁt, the study used purposive sampling, a method that accurately identi ﬁes target
respondents within a speci ﬁc group and is widely used in questionnaire research in cultural and educational contexts
(Magnone and Yezierski, 2024). Speci ﬁcally, the selected regions were chosen because they are highly active hubs
for speci ﬁc cultural heritage crafts and commercial artisanship, and the author also has long-term connections with
craftspeople and designers in these areas. To ensure that the sample was appropriate for studying continuance
intention, we targeted craftspeople who were known to have at least basic exposure to or prior experience with AIGC
tools. These participants were identi ﬁed and approached through local industry associations and craft communities.
The research team recruited four research assistants with relevant experience and used a combination of online
questionnaires and of ﬂine ﬁeld research to collect the data. Before completing the questionnaires, the research
assistants explained the purpose of the study and the use of the data to the interviewees and obtained their informed
consent to participate. To proactively mitigate common method bias (CMB) through procedural remedies (Podsakoff
et al., 2003), respondents were guaranteed strict anonymity, informed that there were no right or wrong answers, and
the order of the measurement items was randomized to reduce pattern-driven responses.
The online survey was distributed through the “Wenjuanxing” platform, and 130 questionnaires were obtained.
The ﬁeld survey was conducted in ﬁve regions in mainland China: Taiyuan and Jinzhong in Shanxi Province;
Guangzhou City in Guangdong Province; Hefei City in Anhui Province; and Chengdu City in Sichuan Province. A
total of 115 paper questionnaires were collected through ﬁeld visits to traditional handicraft workshops and exhi-
bitions. Each participant received a small random reward or an equivalent souvenir valued at 5 –10 RMB after

assuming a medium effect size ( f² = 0.15), an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, the minimum sample size
necessary to assess the maximum of seven predictors is 103. To assess potential non-response bias, we followed

source effects were not signi ﬁcant in this study.
Data analysis
This study used a dual data analysis approach: PLS-SEM and fsQCA.
PLS-SEM is a variance-based structural modeling technique. Speci ﬁcally, there are three main reasons for
choosing PLS-SEM: (1) our research goal is to be highly predictive and identify key drivers of sustained intention
(Sarstedt et al., 2021); (2) the structural model is very complex and contains multiple exogenous and endogenous
variables; and (3) PLS-SEM does not strictly require multivariate normal data distribution (Hair et al., 2019). At the
operational level, we used a path weighting scheme with up to 300 iterations and set 10 −7 as the stopping condition.
To determine the signi ﬁcance of the path coef ﬁcients, we performed a nonparametric self-help method using
10,000 subsamples.
This procedure gauges measurement reliability and validity while concurrently estimating causal linkages among
latent constructs, thereby mapping the linear in ﬂuence chain that runs from social and technological stimuli to
perceived value, trust, and, ultimately, continuance intention. Yet PLS-SEM centers on mean effects and linear
mechanisms, rendering it less adept at detecting non-linearities, contingent interactions, or con ﬁgurational (multi-
path) patterns across variables (Diwanji, 2022). To overcome this shortcoming, this study further introduced the
fsQCA method for complementary analysis. fsQCA is a set theory-based group analysis tool that emphasizes causal
complexity and is capable of identifying how many different combinations of conditions can work together to drive
the occurrence of an outcome through a multi-path mechanism (Ragin, 2009). Unlike traditional variable-centered
models, fsQCA emphasizes the logic of equivalence and asymmetry of outcomes and is particularly well suited for
analyzing the mechanisms by which sociotechnological dual factors drive complex behavioral intentions.
Considering that this study focuses on the fact that traditional craftspeople ’s willingness to use AIGC is affected by
various social and technological variables and that there are potential alternative pathways and interconditional
interactions, the combination of PLS-SEM and fsQCA can consider both the explanation of the mean mechanism and

Common method bias (CMB) and variance in ﬂation factor (VIF)
This study utilized a self-reported questionnaire collection method; therefore, it was necessary to assess the possible
CMB and variance in ﬂation factor (VIF). The results of Harman ’s one-way test indicated that the maximum factor
explained 30.42% of the total variance, which is lower than the 50% judgmental criterion (Podsakoff et al., 2003).
Relying solely on Harman ’s single-factor test is often considered insuf ﬁcient by modern standards. Therefore, we
conducted a full collinearity assessment to rigorously address the CMB, as recommended by Kock ( 2015). The full
collinearity VIFs for the latent variables in this study ranged from 1.153 to 2.039. All VIF values strictly satis ﬁed the
conservative threshold of 3.3 (Kock, 2015).
In addition, to evaluate potential multicollinearity at the measurement level, we assessed the outer VIF of the
speciﬁc indicators. These item-level VIFs ranged from 1.674 to 3.617, which is entirely below the recommended limit
of 5 (Hair et al., 2019), con ﬁrming that there is no critical multicollinearity issue within the measurement model.

Fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA)
Data calibration and necessity analysis
To complement the linear estimation paths provided by PLS-SEM, this study introduces a fsQCA methodology to
explore the grouping conditions that in ﬂuence the persistent use of AIGC among traditional craftspeople. This
method is particularly suitable for identifying causal asymmetries and equivalent multipaths behind behaviors and
compensating for the lack of explanatory power in variance-based methods. The study performed a pooled af ﬁliation
transformation on all Likert-scale data using fsQCA 3.0 software. Following the recommendations of Ragin and Fiss
(2008), we anchored the calibration points substantively to our 7-point Likert scale. Speci ﬁcally, we set 6 (Agree) for
“full afﬁliation” (0.95), 4 (Neutral) for the “crossover point” (0.50), and 2 (Disagree) for “full non-afﬁliation” (0.05),
respectively. To address mathematical uncertainty when a case falls exactly on the 0.50 crossover point, a constant of
+0.001 was added to all conditions before calibration (Fiss, 2011). Furthermore, a sensitivity evaluation was
performed by adjusting the calibration crossover point up and down by 0.50; the resulting con ﬁgurations remained
highly consistent, con ﬁrming the robustness of the calibration.
After calibration was completed, the data were analyzed for necessity, which was assessed according to the fsQCA
methodology criteria, where a condition is considered necessary if its consistency score exceeds 0.90. As shown in
Table 8, the consistency scores for all individual conditions are below the recommended threshold of 0.90 (Ragin,
2009). This indicates that no single variable serves as a necessary condition for the continuance intention of
traditional craftspeople. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct a con ﬁgurational analysis to uncover how combinations
of these factors work together to drive the outcome. However, single variables are often insuf ﬁcient to explain the
generative mechanisms of complex behaviors fully. Therefore, this study will next conduct a group path analysis to
reveal how multiple factors work together in different combinations to drive traditional craftspeople ’s willingness to
sustain their use of AIGC. This analytical logic is consistent with the causal complexity perspective in current
research on digital technology adoption.

Conﬁguration analysis
To identify multiple combinations of sufﬁcient conditions, this study conducted a truth-table analysis. Given the sample
size (N = 209), an acceptable frequency threshold of 1 was applied (Ragin,2009). We set a raw consistency threshold of
0.80 and a proportional reduction in inconsistency threshold of 0.75 (Ragin and Fiss, 2008). Based on our theoretical
framework, positive directional expectations were assumed for all conditions. Consequently, the conﬁgurations reported
in Table 9 represent an intermediate solution that provides the optimal balance between complexity and parsimony by
incorporating theoretical assumptions. The results of the analysis are shown in Table 9, which indicates that six
groupings of persistent-use intentions were identi ﬁed. Each con ﬁguration showed high consistency, which was higher
than 0.830. The overall model consistency reached 0.827, with a coverage rate of 0.561, which was higher than 0.50,
indicating that the model has a strong explanatory ability.
To further verify the robustness of the results, the researchers adjusted the consistency threshold upward to 0.90
and reran the analysis. The analysis results showed that the core and edge structure of each grouping remained
unchanged, with only minor differences in consistency and coverage; thus, we further con ﬁrmed the robustness and
reliability of the explanatory pathways.
By generalizing and analyzing the six grouping paths, they can be categorized into three main types, each
corresponding to a distinct mechanism and key variables.
(1) Content-Performance Compensation Path (Con ﬁgurations A1a and A1b). Both con ﬁgurations exhibit high-
quality content, performance, perceived value, and trust, but lack either platform technology (A1a) or industry
support (A1b). Despite weak external in ﬂuence resources, high content quality and performance can enhance
perceived value and trust, thereby promoting ongoing willingness to engage. The consistency levels for A1a and
A1b are 0.885 and 0.875, respectively, explaining raw coverages of 0.285 and 0.275, respectively.
(2) Identity –Peer Reinforcement Path (Con ﬁgurations A2a and A2b). Both con ﬁgurations rely on industry support
and peer in ﬂuence; however, A2b requires performance support to further enhance perceived value and trust,
thereby promoting continued willingness to use. The two con ﬁgurations explained coverage rates of 0.408 and
0.396, respectively, with consistency rates of 0.889 and 0.836, respectively.
(3) Social Endorsement –Technology Synergy Path (Con ﬁgurations A3a and A3b). Both paths integrate social
identity and performance, wherein A3b combines peer in ﬂuence and platform technology. Both paths
demonstrate strong consistency (0.833 and 0.855), with A3a achieving the highest raw coverage among all
paths (0.432), indicating its widespread prevalence among the sampled craftspeople.
Discussion
Discussion of ﬁndings
By integrating PLS-SEM and fsQCA, this study systematically portrays the formation mechanism of traditional
craftspeople’s persistent willingness to use the AIGC system from different perspectives. Under the dual framework
of the linear relationship between variables and the combined effects of variables, the research ﬁndings show a high
degree of consistency and mutual complementarity and expose the limitations of traditional technology adoption
paths in complex user groups, further verifying the applicability and explanatory power of theories of causal
complexity and group equivalence in the study of technology in cultural groups.
Answering RQ1: social and technical drivers of perceived value and trust
The results of the PLS-SEM analysis indicated that most social in ﬂuence variables and technology attribute variables
had signi ﬁcant positive effects on perceived value and trust. This suggests that, in the psychological perception of
https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-026-09204-6 Article in Press
Humanities and Social Sciences Communications
traditional craftspeople, social mechanisms are involved in the cognitive construction of technology acceptance,
alongside technology availability and value. This result is generally consistent with the dual-path structure in the
TAM2 model, which emphasizes social norms and performance expectations (Venkatesh and Davis, 2000). However,
a particularly revealing ﬁnding is that neither social identity nor platform technology signi ﬁcantly predicts trust.
Rather than treating this result as a statistical anomaly, this ﬁnding illuminates a profound context-speci ﬁc
mechanism. As our ﬁndings suggest, for traditional craftspeople, a sense of community belonging and system
usability may be suf ﬁcient to make AIGC seem worth trying —thereby signi ﬁcantly driving perceived value —but
they are not enough to make it feel safe enough to rely on. Establishing trust appears to require stronger evidence of
actual performance, high-quality output, and strict controllability. Trust for this demographic is a high-stakes emo-
tional barrier. This ﬁnding reveals a critical divergence from mainstream information systems research. While
traditional models (DeLone and McLean, 2003) posit that system quality inherently and directly fosters user trust, our
results demonstrate that in culturally sensitive contexts, operational stability is insuf ﬁcient. This aligns with recent
observations in heritage preservation literature (Cardini and Noguera, 2024), which argues that artisans prioritize
cultural authenticity over mere technical advancement. Their trust cannot be won merely by advanced interfaces but
requires consistent evidence that the system provides highly reliable and helpful content.
The ease of use or popularity of a platform among peers does not automatically translate into deep con ﬁdence if
craftspeople feel that the tool ’s outputs are unpredictable or unhelpful to their speci ﬁc needs. To develop genuine
trust, craftspeople require substantial evidence of functional reliability and strict controllability over the ﬁnal output.
The results of fsQCA explain the limitations of this trust-forming mechanism to some extent. Although platform
technology and social identity were not signi ﬁcant in a single path of action, they operate synergistically with trust,
peer in ﬂuence, or performance and constitute suf ﬁcient conditions in speci ﬁc groupings. This pattern of key roles
under speci ﬁc conditions suggests that the effects of these variables are non-independent and that their impacts
depend on the activation of speci ﬁc environmental variables, re ﬂecting the asymmetric causality emphasized by
fsQCA (Ragin and Fiss, 2008).
Answering RQ2: the mediating roles of perceived value and trust
The mediation effect analysis in this study reveals the distinct roles of perceived value and trust in the technology
adoption process of traditional craftspeople. The results show that perceived value exerts a complete mediating effect
on most paths, re ﬂecting its central position in the behavior-generation chain. Traditional craftspeople tend to form
initial acceptance perceptions of the AIGC system based on functional evaluations (work ef ﬁciency, utility, and
content quality); that is, subsequent behavioral willingness is possible only when the technology is perceived as
useful and worth investing in. In contrast, trust is mostly expressed as a partially mediating variable, whose role is
mainly to mitigate risks and provide emotional security, especially in contexts where performance is uncertain and
cultural compatibility is ambiguous. Even if users recognize AIGC ’s content creativity or production strengths,
behavioral intentions may still be hindered if they lack con ﬁdence in its controllability and cultural appropriateness.
This explains the exceptionally strong association observed between trust and continuance intention. Consequently,
the central, relatively strong role of trust in driving continuance is one of the main theoretical takeaways of this paper.
Because traditional craftspeople are highly cautious about adopting disruptive tools, “trust” acts as the ultimate
gatekeeper. Once this formidable affective barrier is overcome — that is, the craftspeople con ﬁdently feel that the
AIGC tool is genuinely helpful and its content is completely reliable —trust becomes the absolute dominant catalyst
for long-term integration. Therefore, this study supports a progressive three-stage psychological path model: external
conditions → perceived functional value (perceived value) → emotional safety and security (trust) → continuance
intention formation. The path emphasizes that perceived value is the rational starting point and trust is the emotional
fulcrum, both of which work together to drive behavioral transformation. In particular, for traditional artisans with
strong cultural attributes and limited digital literacy, establishing a moderate level of trust in the system ’s reliability
and usefulness may re ﬂect their broader af ﬁrmation of cultural security and psychological assurance. Technology
adoption is thus not only a process of tool selection but also a process of value negotiation and emotional
conﬁrmation.

By identifying trust as the ultimate gatekeeper, our study challenges purely utilitarian adoption models (such as
TAM or UTAUT), which often assume that perceived usefulness or performance expectancy alone is suf ﬁcient to
ensure continued use (Venkatesh et al., 2012). Instead, our ﬁndings align with research emphasizing affective and
risk-mitigating factors (Gefen et al., 2003; Chiu et al., 2012), con ﬁrming that for digitally vulnerable groups,
overcoming the emotional barrier posed by technological threats is a prerequisite for sustained behavioral
engagement.
Answering RQ3: con ﬁgurational pathways to sustained use
The fsQCA analysis identi ﬁes six highly consistent combinations of suf ﬁcient condition paths, clearly presenting
three typical types of mechanisms: content –performance compensation, identity –peer reinforcement, and social
endorsement–technology synergy. These paths re ﬂect the different psychological bases and behavioral logics of
traditional craftspeople in terms of cultural identity, community embeddedness, and technological trust, respectively.
First, the content –performance compensation trajectory shows that superior AIGC outputs and robust functionality
can offset gaps in hard infrastructure (A1a: platform technology) or soft scaffolding (A1b: industry backing). This
observation suggests that, even in the absence of robust formal institutions, tool-centered technological incentives
may still drive continuance —a pattern consistent with the utilitarian logic reported elsewhere in the IS canon (Davis,
1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Second, the identity –peer reinforcement pathway spotlights normative backing from
the sector and informational pressure from peers as decisive levers for elevating perceived value and trust. Even when
artisans lack the technical wherewithal to operate novel tools, industry signals and peer cues can still kindle a strong
willingness to adopt, further substantiating social in ﬂuence theory (Deutsch and Gerard, 1955; Kelman, 1958).
Finally, the social-endorsement –technology synergy pathway posits that the con ﬂuence of communal legitimization
and technical affordances markedly ampli ﬁes artisans ’ valuation of and con ﬁdence in AIGC, thereby safeguarding
continued use. The evidence reinforces the contention that dual social and technological forces jointly expedite the
diffusion and adoption of novel systems. A striking observation across all six con ﬁgurations is the ubiquitous
presence of perceived value and trust as core conditions. Because perceived value and trust are theoretically central
mediators in our SEM model, their frequent appearance in the fsQCA solutions is unsurprising. Rather than pre-
senting this as a dramatic discovery or a unique psychological bottleneck, we note that the con ﬁgurational results are
highly consistent with the SEM logic. They further suggest that continuance among craftspeople typically requires
both functional appraisal and psychological assurance. For traditional craftspeople, no combination of external social
endorsements or technical superiority is suf ﬁcient to trigger a continuance intention unless those stimuli successfully
activate a strong sense of internal utility and dependable trust, which, in this context, likely re ﬂects their need for a
broader sense of cultural safety. They are not merely stepping stones; they are the fundamental psychological bedrock
for technology adoption in this digitally vulnerable demographic.
Theoretical implications
This study provides a targeted and meaningful theoretical extension to the literature on technology continuance. First,
moving away from a contribution-by-assembly approach, we demonstrate how social and technical cues operate
through distinct cognitive and affective routes for culturally embedded and digitally vulnerable users. Our constructed
chain model ( “social and technological stimulus → perception/trust → continuance intention ”) clari ﬁes that in this
highly sensitive cultural scenario, trust is not merely a byproduct of usability but plays a particularly central role as an
affective gatekeeper. This theoretical integration produces new theoretical perspectives and nuanced insights into the
behavioral decisions of non-technical users.
Second, the study provides a novel application of the information system behavioral model to the “cultural
guardian” group—traditional craftspeople. This effectively addresses the long-term neglect of the behavioral
mechanisms of “digitally disadvantaged groups ” in information systems research and improves the inclusiveness and
adaptability of theories in this ﬁeld. In addition, the study further reveals the asymmetric intermediary roles of trust
https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-026-09204-6 Article in Press
Humanities and Social Sciences Communications
and perceived value in the adoption path, enriches the assumption of reciprocity between the two in the existing
literature, and emphasizes the function of trust as an emotional safeguard for cultural users. This ﬁnding is theore-
tically instructive for understanding the deep relationship between emotions, cultural identity, and system behavior
(Gefen et al., 2003).
Finally, the study jointly employs the dual methods of PLS-SEM and fsQCA to empirically verify the causal
complexity and path equivalence of the behavioral adoption process (EI Sawy et al., 2010), thereby addressing the
limitations of the traditional unilinear model. It provides a useful framework for analyzing the con ﬁgurational
paradigm in digital culture.
Practical implications
First, regarding platform design, driven by our empirical ﬁndings that general platform technology does not sig-
niﬁcantly induce trust and that trust is the strongest predictor of continuance intention, developers must go beyond
generic usability improvements and focus on building a specialized, trusted architecture. Since trust is the ultimate
driver of continued use, platforms must clearly address the concerns of cultural practitioners. We recommend
implementing robust IP protection measures and provenance-tracing mechanisms, such as blockchain-based water-
marks and patents, to protect artisans ’ original designs. Additionally, platforms should improve the interpretability of
algorithms to demystify the generation process and incorporate strict controls for human intervention. These controls
will allow artisans to guide, intervene in, and edit the AI ’s output at every stage, ensuring that they retain ultimate
creative sovereignty and maintain the authenticity of the culture. It is important to note that our empirical data do not
directly evaluate speci ﬁc technological interventions, such as blockchain watermarking or explainability systems.
Rather, these are sensible design implications inferred from the strong trust-building patterns observed in our results.
In addition, to lower the technical threshold, the platform can provide prompt engineering templates speci ﬁcally for
training on traditional cultural models.
Second, at the policy-support level, the system should be optimized to ensure stability and enable professional
content generation. Furthermore, the introduction of public resources and authoritative endorsement can effectively
promote digital adoption among cultural groups. Therefore, it is recommended that the government establish an
AIGC Integration Demonstration Program for Non-Heritage to build an enabling mechanism for integrating culture
and technology through funding, training, and certi ﬁcation. In addition, drawing directly from the identity, peer
reinforcement, and social endorsement –technology synergy pathways identi ﬁed in our fsQCA results, peer in ﬂuence
and social identity are suf ﬁcient drivers of continuance when combined with appropriate performance. Therefore, the
study also suggests that community programs, such as digital craft ambassadors and cultural master-apprentice
programs, should be used to activate networked trust transfer among traditional craft practitioners and enhance group
motivation.
Finally, trust construction is based on performance feedback and system understanding, suggesting that digital
skills training plays a fundamental role in the adoption path. Therefore, the education and training system should
focus on the teaching mode of project practice and cultural scenarios, guiding traditional culture practitioners to break
through technical barriers and enhance their psychological adaptability and creative transformation of AIGC tools. To
summarize, a multi-dimensional synergy from platform design, policy promotion, community building, and educa-
tional empowerment is the key to activating the sustainable development of AIGC in the cultural ﬁeld.
Research limitations and perspectives
Although this study is innovative in its theoretical construction and methodological application, it has some lim-
itations. First, because we relied on purposive sampling con ﬁned to traditional craftspeople in select Chinese locales,
the study’s external validity and cross-cultural portability are necessarily circumscribed; future research should probe
the model ’s generalizability through cross-national comparisons or broader multi-regional cohorts. Second, the
exclusive use of cross-sectional and self-reported observations hampers our ability to trace how artisans ’ engagement
Article in Press https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-026-09204-6
Humanities and Social Sciences Communications
with AIGC evolves over time. Moreover, it means that, despite passing rigorous statistical threshold tests, potential
CMB cannot be de ﬁnitively ruled out. Incorporating longitudinal designs and multi-source data in the future would
allow scholars to illuminate these behavioral dynamics with greater causal certainty. In addition, the study focuses on
main effects and mediating mechanisms. It does not incorporate the moderating role of individual-difference vari-
ables, such as digital literacy and technology anxiety, in the path relationship. The model ’s contextual sensitivity can
be extended in the future. Finally, although the existing theories explain the AIGC acceptance logic for cultural users,
they remain insuf ﬁcient in more complex integration scenarios, and emerging frameworks such as the context-
awareness model and the theory of technological identity construction can be introduced in the future to enhance the
theories’ outreach. These expansion directions will help deepen the understanding of cultural practitioners ’ digital
adoption behavior and the iterative development of theoretical models.
Conclusion
Focusing on traditional craftspeople, this study constructs and veri ﬁes a theoretical path through which social
inﬂuence factors and technical attributes affect their continuance intention and AIGC through perceived value and
trust. Based on the PLS-SEM results, a key takeaway of this study is the distinct mechanisms that drive perceived
value and trust. We found that while a sense of belonging and usability are suf ﬁcient to make AIGC seem worth
trying, they are insuf ﬁcient to make it feel safe. The relatively strong role of trust in continuance intention requires
robust evidence of performance, output quality, and controllability. The mediation analysis further reveals that
perceived value underpins users ’ functional cognition, whereas trust serves as a psychological security mechanism,
and the two together drive continuance intention. The fsQCA results indicate that a single factor does not dominate
the willingness to continue; it is the product of a combination of factors and can be formed through the three
mechanism paths (content –performance compensation-driven, identity –peer reinforcement-driven, and social
endorsement–technology synergy). This causal complexity con ﬁrms the limitations of the traditional linear-path
explanatory model and provides a personalized con ﬁguration reference for technology-promotion strategies in the
context of cultural digitization. In summary, this study offers meaningful extensions in terms of theoretical modeling,
methodological application, and research object focus, which not only enrich the research on technology adoption by
non-technical users in the ﬁeld of information systems but also provide theoretical support and practical inspiration
for the deep integration of AIGC in the ﬁeld of culture and creativity.
