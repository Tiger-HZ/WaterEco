Abstract
Western Uttar Pradesh is among the most severely polluted regions of India, a
condition driven by rapid urbanisation, dense vehicular traffic, an extensive small‑scale
manufacturing base and seasonal agricultural biomass burning. This review synthesises
ambient air quality data for seven urban centres (Ghaziabad, Noida, Meerut, Agra,
Firozabad, Saharanpur and Muzaffarnagar) from 2020 to 2026, covering the Air Quality
Index (AQI) and six criteria pollutants. Particulate concentrations remain persistently above
Central Pollution Control Board and World Health Organization reference values, with
winter maxima and monsoon minima at every site. Two patterns run counter to the
assumption of uniform deterioration: particulate loadings have fallen at most sites since
the 2021–2022 peak, whereas ozone and nitrogen dioxide have risen sharply and now
define the region’s principal remaining risk. The particulate improvement is confined to
the coarse fraction, so total mass has fallen while the respirable combustion fraction has
not. The review also examines three processes that remain poorly characterised for the
region: the possible role of airborne microplastic fibres in road and construction dust
as carriers of adsorbed heavy metals; fugitive, non‑stack emissions from informal micro,
small and medium enterprise clusters, which chimney‑based compliance monitoring cannot
capture; and the mixing of agricultural residue smoke from the north‑west with urban
industrial plumes. Because no localised measurements of these processes exist for Western
Uttar Pradesh, they are presented as evidence gaps and as a measurement agenda, not as
quantified findings. Monitoring must be widened beyond the criteria‑pollutant, single‑city
model towards regional airshed management.

health, ecosystem protection and climate resilience. Sustainable air‑quality management should prioritise regional airshed‑based

1. Introduction
Western Uttar Pradesh occupies the upper Ganga–Yamuna doab and functions as one of northern India’s most important economic
corridors. The region combines a dense industrial belt (the National Capital Region satellite cities of Ghaziabad and Noida, the
engineering and sports‑goods cluster of Meerut, the leather and footwear industry of Agra, the glass‑bangle furnaces of Firozabad,
the paper and pulp mills of Saharanpur and the sugar and jaggery units of Muzaffarnagar) with some of the most intensively
cultivated agricultural land in the country. The combined population of these districts exceeds 75 million. This concentration of
industry, agriculture and traffic within a single, topographically enclosed plain shapes air quality in three ways. Emission sources
are unusually diverse, spanning combustion, process, mobile and area sources within short distances of one another. Much of the

industrial base consists of informal or semi‑formal micro, small and medium enterprises whose emissions are neither metered nor
reported. Over both, the Indo‑Gangetic Plain experiences a persistent winter temperature inversion that suppresses the mixing
height and traps pollutants close to the surface for weeks at a time (Dumka, 2025). These conditions make the region an airshed
rather than a set of independent urban sources, yet regulatory monitoring and enforcement continue to operate on a city‑by‑city
basis.
Ambient monitoring across the region between 2020 and 2026 records repeated and prolonged exceedance of national ambient air
quality standards for particulate matter, a pattern documented across other Indian states over comparable periods (Ghosh et al.,
2024). Annual mean PM 10 concentrations at the more heavily loaded sites remain several times the CPCB annual limit of 60 µg/m3,
and annual mean PM 2.5 concentrations remain roughly two to three times the CPCB annual limit of 40 µg/m3 throughout the
record. Measured against the substantially stricter WHO 2021 global air quality guideline values of 15 µg/m3 for PM10 and 5 µg/m3
for PM2.5 (World Health Organization, 2021), the exceedance factors are an order of magnitude larger. Because the health‑relevant
metric is cumulative exposure rather than peak concentration, sustained exceedance of this magnitude across a population of this
size represents a chronic public health burden rather than an episodic pollution problem (Cohen et al., 2004; Künzli et al., 2000;
Landrigan, 2017).
The principal contributing sources identified for the region are vehicular exhaust and road dust resuspension along the Delhi–
Dehradun and Delhi–Agra corridors; combustion in brick kilns, glass furnaces and small foundries; construction and demolition
activity in the rapidly expanding urban peripheries; open burning of municipal and agricultural waste; and the seasonal advection
of crop residue smoke from Punjab and Haryana during the post‑monsoon harvest window (Awasthi et al., 2024). The relative
weight of these sources shifts through the year, which is why seasonal disaggregation is essential to any interpretation of the
annual means.

Beyond the criteria pollutants, three processes are repeatedly identified in the wider literature as material to airshed behaviour
but remain unquantified for Western Uttar Pradesh specifically. Weathered synthetic polymer fibres are now recognised as a com‑
ponent of urban and peri‑urban atmospheric dust. Oxidised functional groups on weathered fibre surfaces provide adsorption
sites for metal ions, so the fibre may act as a physical carrier that delivers adsorbed lead, chromium or cadmium deeper into the
respiratory tract than the free ionic species would reach, and retains it there longer. No published measurement of atmospheric
microplastic abundance, polymer composition or associated metal loading exists for these districts.
Compliance monitoring in India is built around stack sampling at fixed chimney locations. Emissions that escape through low
furnace openings, doorways, roof vents, material transfer points and open storage yards are structurally invisible to this design.
Given that a large share of the region’s glass, chemical, foundry and jaggery capacity sits in informal clusters with low‑height or

absent stacks, the fraction of total industrial emission that is unaccounted for may be substantial, but it has not been estimated for
the region. Post‑monsoon residue burning smoke advected from the north‑west arrives over the western districts already aged and
chemically processed, and mixes with locally generated industrial and vehicular emissions. The resulting mixture is not simply
additive: secondary organic and inorganic aerosol formation and ozone photochemistry depend on the ratio in which the two
plumes combine. Source apportionment that separates the rural biomass and urban industrial contributions has been carried out
for Delhi–NCR but not extended eastward into the Western Uttar Pradesh districts.
These three gaps share a common structural cause: the region’s monitoring architecture measures the pollutants it was designed
to regulate, at the locations where regulation is enforced. None of the three has been quantified for the region. Table 1 maps the
principal strands of prior work, grouped by the aspect of the regional airshed each concerns.
2. Data and Methods
2.1. Study Area
Seven urban centres of Western Uttar Pradesh were selected for analysis: Ghaziabad, Noida, Meerut, Agra, Firozabad, Saharanpur
and Muzaffarnagar. Selection was based on three criteria: the presence of a continuous ambient monitoring record for the full
study period, representation of the region’s distinct emission profiles, and administrative importance within the state’s air quality
management programme. The set spans the National Capital Region periphery (Ghaziabad, Noida), the central industrial belt
(Meerut, Muzaffarnagar), the southern tourism and manufacturing corridor (Agra, Firozabad) and the northern agro‑industrial
zone (Saharanpur).
2.2. Data sources and Study Period
The analysis draws on secondary ambient air quality data for the period 2020 to 2026. Annual mean concentrations for PM 10, PM2.5,
SO2, NO 2, O 3 and CO, together with annual mean AQI values, were compiled for each of the seven cities from publicly reported
continuous ambient air quality monitoring records. Historical air‑quality data for selected locations in Western Uttar Pradesh were
obtained from AQI.in, an independent air‑quality data platform. The platform provides air‑quality observations and AQI infor‑
mation from monitoring networks and other sources. Where possible, monitoring‑station information and pollutant observations
were cross‑referenced with data reported through the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) National Air Quality Index system.
No primary sampling was undertaken. Two features of the record require explicit statement. The 2026 values represent a par‑
tial year and are therefore not strictly comparable with the six complete years that precede them; comparisons involving 2026 are
treated as indicative throughout. Monitoring coverage is also uneven across the seven cities, since Ghaziabad and Noida are served
by a denser network than Firozabad or Muzaffarnagar, so inter‑city differences of small magnitude should not be over‑interpreted.
2.3. Pollutants, Units and Reference Standards
Concentrations of PM 10, PM 2.5, SO 2, NO 2 and O 3 are reported in micrograms per cubic metre ( µg/m3). Carbon monoxide is con‑
ventionally reported in milligrams per cubic metre (mg/m 3) under the Indian National Ambient Air Quality Standards; the CO
values compiled here are reported in the units of the source record and are used only for relative comparison across years and
cities, not for direct comparison against the mg/m 3 standard. Observed concentrations are assessed against CPCB ( 2009) National
Ambient Air Quality Standards and, where a stricter international benchmark is informative, against the WHO 2021 global air
quality guidelines. The corresponding limit values are set out in Table 4, alongside the physiological mechanism associated with
each pollutant. Air Quality Index values follow the CPCB National AQI scheme, in which the index is computed as the maximum
of the sub‑indices of the individual pollutants measured, and is classified as Good (0–50), Satisfactory (51–100), Moderate (101–200),
Poor (201–300), Very Poor (301–400) and Severe (401–500).
2.4. Analytical Approach
Three analyses were performed. Annual mean concentrations were tabulated by city and pollutant to establish the regional pol‑
lutant profile and to identify the direction of change over the study period (Table 2). Annual AQI series were then examined city
by city to distinguish sites on an improving trajectory from those on a deteriorating one, and the highest and lowest monthly AQI
values were extracted to characterise the seasonal amplitude at each site (Table 3). Finally, pollutant‑specific behaviour was exam‑
ined separately for the particulate and gaseous fractions, because the two groups display opposite trends over the study period
and therefore imply different control priorities. Because the underlying data are annual and monthly aggregates rather than raw
time series, the analysis is descriptive. No statistical testing, trend fitting or meteorological normalisation has been applied, and
no causal attribution to individual emission sources is claimed. Mechanistic and epidemiological explanations of the observed
patterns are drawn from the published literature rather than inferred from these aggregates.

3.1. Regional Pollutant Profile, 2020–2026
Table 2 presents annual mean AQI and criteria pollutant concentrations for the seven cities across the full study period. Read
across the region as a whole, the record does not describe uniform deterioration. It describes a divergence, in which the particulate
fraction and the gaseous fraction have moved in opposite directions. Particulate loadings peaked early in the period and have since
declined at every site. Regional PM10 maxima occur in 2021 or 2022 and fall thereafter, in several cases substantially: Noida declines
from 217 µg/m3 in 2022 to 111 µg/m3 in 2026, Ghaziabad from 233 µg/m3 to 125 µg/m3 over the same interval, and Firozabad from
225 µg/m3 in 2020 to 85 µg/m3 in 2026. PM2.5 shows the same early peak but a shallower decline, and at every site the 2026 value is
higher than the period minimum, which falls in 2023 at five sites and in 2024 at Meerut. The implication is that the coarse fraction,
dominated by road dust, construction activity and mechanically generated material, has responded to control measures, whereas
the fine combustion‑derived fraction has not. Gaseous pollutants show the reverse pattern. Ozone rises at all seven sites, in several
cases steeply: Muzaffarnagar increases from 8 µg/m3 in 2020 to 43 µg/m3 in 2026, Meerut from 11 to 44 µg/m3, and Noida from 9
to 30 µg/m3. Nitrogen dioxide rises at five of the seven sites, with Ghaziabad increasing from 19 to 32 µg/m3 and Muzaffarnagar
from 7 to 24 µg/m3; it is unchanged at Noida (13 µg/m3 in both 2020 and 2026) and falls at Firozabad (23 to 12 µg/m3). Because
tropospheric ozone is a secondary pollutant formed photochemically from nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds, the
parallel increase in both species is internally consistent and points to a growing precursor inventory, most plausibly the rising
vehicle fleet, that is not being offset by the dust control measures responsible for the particulate improvement. Sulphur dioxide
remains comparatively low in absolute terms across the region but rises sharply at two sites in 2026, reaching 21 µg/m3 at Agra and
18 µg/m3 at Firozabad against values of 4 to 9µg/m3 elsewhere. Both are locations with a concentrated furnace‑based industrial base,
and both warrant verification against the primary monitoring record before the increase is treated as established. The compiled
carbon monoxide series shows a step change between 2021 and 2022 at every city simultaneously, which is characteristic of a
change in instrumentation, reporting unit or averaging convention rather than of a regional emissions event; the CO column is
therefore excluded from further interpretation.

Ghaziabad and Noida follow closely parallel paths. Both reach their highest annual AQI in 2022 and decline steadily thereafter,
and in both cases the decline is driven predominantly by the coarse particulate fraction rather than by any broad improvement
across pollutants. Both cities nonetheless retain the region’s highest recorded monthly extremes, and in both the gaseous fraction
is rising even as the index falls. The improvement in these two cities is therefore real but narrow, and the AQI understates the
change in exposure character that lies beneath it.
Meerut, Agra, Firozabad and Muzaffarnagar share a common shape: a mid‑period minimum followed by renewed increase in
the final years of the record. Firozabad shows the most pronounced version of this pattern, falling from the highest annual AQI
recorded anywhere in the dataset in 2020 to its minimum in 2024 before rising again; Agra reaches its minimum in 2023 and has
risen in each subsequent year. In all four cities the recent increase coincides with rising nitrogen dioxide and ozone, and in Agra
and Firozabad with a marked increase in sulphur dioxide. The mid‑period minima at these sites should be interpreted cautiously,
since the 2020 to 2021 interval includes the period of reduced industrial and transport activity associated with pandemic restrictions.
Saharanpur is the only city in the set whose 2026 annual AQI exceeds its 2020 value, and its particulate and ozone concentrations
both rise across the record. As the least industrialised of the seven cities at the start of the period, it is also the one where the
trajectory is most plausibly attributable to growth in local emissions rather than to changes in regional background.
3.3. Seasonal Variation
The seasonal signal is the strongest single feature of the dataset and is consistent across all seven cities. At six of the seven sites the
highest monthly AQI of the study period falls in November, December or January, and at every site for which a monthly minimum
is reported the lowest value falls in July or August. The amplitude is large: at Ghaziabad the highest and lowest monthly values

differ by a factor of approximately seven, and comparable ratios apply at Noida, Meerut and Saharanpur. Two mechanisms account
for this pattern. In winter, radiative cooling of the surface produces a shallow, stable boundary layer over the plain, reducing the
volume into which emissions can disperse; wind speeds fall, and the post‑monsoon agricultural residue burning season adds a
large regional smoke burden on top of unchanged local emissions. In the monsoon, the reverse applies: convective mixing raises the
boundary layer, and wet deposition removes both particles and soluble gases from the column. The seasonal cycle therefore reflects
the interaction of meteorology with an emission inventory that is broadly constant through the year, rather than a seasonal change
in emissions alone. It follows that annual mean values, while convenient for inter‑year comparison, substantially understate the
exposures that actually occur during the winter months. A same‑window comparison of daily AQI values during July illustrates
the point in the opposite direction. Within this monsoon window the highest daily values recorded across the study period range
from 110 at Agra to 225 at Ghaziabad, and the lowest from 17 at Meerut to 30 at Ghaziabad. The entire monsoon range at every
site sits below the winter monthly means at the same site. The July 2026 values fall in the Moderate band at most sites, which is
the best air quality the region experiences at any point in the year.
3.4. Particulate Matter
The particulate record separates into two components that behave differently. PM 10 declines at all seven sites from an early‑period
maximum, and the magnitude of the decline is largest where the initial loading was highest. This is the signature of source control
acting on mechanically generated material: road dust suppression, construction site management, paving and mechanised sweep‑
ing all act preferentially on the coarse fraction, and their effect is proportionally greatest where uncontrolled dust dominated the
baseline. PM 2.5 does not follow. Its regional decline is shallower, its minimum occurs later, and at all seven sites the 2026 value
exceeds the mid‑period minimum. The fine fraction derives principally from combustion: vehicle exhaust, brick kilns, glass and
foundry furnaces, biomass and waste burning. None of these sources is affected by dust control. The consequence is a shift in the
composition of the particulate burden: the total mass falls, but the fraction of that mass composed of respirable combustion parti‑
cles, which carry the greater health risk per unit mass, rises. The PM 2.5 to PM10 ratio increases at every site over the study period.
Assessments based on PM 10 or on AQI alone will therefore record improvement in a region where the health‑relevant exposure
has changed character rather than diminished.
3.5. Gaseous Pollutants
Ozone increases at all seven sites and shows the largest proportional change of any pollutant in the dataset, with 2026 values at
Meerut and Muzaffarnagar approximately four to five times their 2020 values. Nitrogen dioxide rises at five of the seven sites. Since
ozone in the troposphere is not emitted directly but formed by the photochemical reaction of nitrogen oxides with volatile organic
compounds under strong insolation, the concurrent increase in both species indicates a growing precursor inventory in a region
that already receives high solar radiation for much of the year. This has a policy implication that runs against the intuition drawn
from the particulate record. Reducing coarse dust lowers atmospheric turbidity and can therefore increase the actinic flux available
to drive ozone photochemistry, so successful dust control may in part be accelerating ozone formation. Ozone also has a longer
atmospheric lifetime than primary particulate matter and forms downwind of its precursor sources, which means it is inherently
a regional rather than a municipal problem and cannot be managed within city boundaries. Sulphur dioxide remains low across
most of the region, consistent with the substitution of coal by piped natural gas in parts of the industrial base and with the low
sulphur content of current transport fuels. The exceptions are Agra and Firozabad, where the 2026 values are roughly two to five
times those recorded elsewhere. Both cities host concentrated furnace‑based industry, and the increase is large enough relative to
the preceding years to warrant verification against the primary monitoring record before it is accepted as a real emissions change.
4. Health Implications
The health effects associated with each criteria pollutant are established in the toxicological and epidemiological literature (Ander‑
son et al., 2012; Kelly & Fussell, 2015; 2012; Ghorani‑Azam et al., 2016). Table 4 sets out, for each criteria pollutant, the applicable
Indian standard, the physiological mechanism of injury and the principal clinical outcome associated with chronic exposure.
4.1. Respiratory Effects
The respiratory consequences of this exposure regime follow from particle size. Coarse particles are largely intercepted in the nose
and upper throat, where they produce irritation, chronic cough and acute exacerbation in patients with pre‑existing airway disease.
Fine particles bypass this filtration entirely, reach the alveoli and provoke localised inflammation, with reduced lung function and
chronic obstructive pulmonary disease as the long‑term outcome (Kurt et al., 2016; Pope, 1989; 2000). Gaseous irritants act on
the same tissue by a different route: sulphur dioxide dissolves in the airway lining fluid and provokes reflex bronchoconstriction
within minutes, while nitrogen dioxide impairs the alveolar macrophage population responsible for clearing inhaled pathogens,
raising susceptibility to bronchitis and pneumonia. Given that the gaseous fraction is the component rising across the region, the
respiratory burden should be expected to shift over time from the dust‑driven upper‑airway presentation towards infection‑related
and ozone‑related lower‑airway disease.

4.2. Cardiovascular and Systemic Effects
The ultrafine fraction of PM 2.5 does not remain in the lung. Particles below approximately 100 nm cross the alveolar–capillary
barrier and enter the systemic circulation, where they generate oxidative stress, promote endothelial dysfunction and accelerate
the progression of arterial plaque (Nemmar et al., 2004; Geiser et al., 2005). The epidemiological consequence, established across
a large international literature (Zmirou et al., 1998; Silva et al., 2016a; 2016b; Liu et al., 2018) and quantified for Indian states by
Pandey et al. ( 2021), is elevated incidence of ischaemic heart disease, myocardial infarction and stroke in populations chronically
exposed to elevated fine particulate concentrations. Because this pathway depends on the fine rather than the coarse fraction, the
compositional shift already noted, falling total particulate mass with a rising fine fraction, implies that cardiovascular risk in the
region may not be improving even where AQI values are falling.
Carbon monoxide operates by an unrelated mechanism, competing with oxygen for haemoglobin binding sites and reducing the
oxygen‑carrying capacity of the blood (Ghorani‑Azam et al., 2016). The acute presentation is dizziness, confusion and headache;
in patients with existing coronary disease, the reduction in oxygen delivery can precipitate angina.
4.3. Carcinogenic Risk
Particulate matter in the region carries adsorbed polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons generated by incomplete combustion of fos‑
sil fuels, biomass and waste plastic (Gangwar et al., 2019; Cho et al., 2026). Several of these compounds are established human
carcinogens, and long‑term inhalation exposure is associated with increased rates of cellular mutation and elevated lung cancer
risk (Turner et al., 2020). The relevant exposure metric is cumulative dose over years, which means that the sustained exceedance
documented for the region, rather than any individual pollution episode, constitutes the carcinogenic hazard.
4.4. Vulnerable Population Groups
The regional burden is not distributed evenly. Children are disproportionately affected because alveolarisation and airway growth
continue into adolescence: exposure during this window produces deficits in attained lung function that are not recovered later,
and a growing literature associates early‑life particulate exposure with adverse neurocognitive outcomes (Chu et al., 2026; Gordon
et al., 2018). Children also breathe a greater volume of air per unit body mass than adults and spend more time outdoors. At the
other end of the age distribution, older adults with established cardiorespiratory disease have reduced physiological reserve, so a
given increment in exposure produces a larger clinical effect (Pope, 2000; Mannucci & Franchini, 2017). Outdoor workers, among
them traffic police, street vendors, construction and brick kiln labourers, form a third group, exposed at concentrations well above
the ambient monitored average for the greater part of the working day, and generally without occupational health surveillance.
4.5. Airborne Microplastics as a Co‐exposure Pathway
This mechanism has a specific implication for the health assessment, and remains a hypothesis requiring testing rather than an
established regional finding. If weathered synthetic polymer fibres are present in construction and road dust at the concentrations
reported for other urban environments, they would constitute a distinct inhalation hazard for two reasons. Fibre geometry favours

DOI: https://doi.org/10.59983/s20260403019

deeper penetration and longer retention than equivalent‑mass spherical particles, and the oxidised functional groups generated
on weathered polymer surfaces provide binding sites for metal ions, so a fibre may deliver adsorbed lead, chromium or cadmium
to the distal airway and retain it there (Rao et al., 2025; Brahney et al., 2021). The combined exposure would be neither the pure
particulate exposure nor the pure metal exposure on which current risk assessments are based, and it would not be detected by
monitoring that measures particulate mass alone. Whether this pathway is material in Western Uttar Pradesh cannot be determined
from the available evidence, since no measurement of atmospheric microplastic abundance or associated metal loading has been
reported for the region. Establishing it is the first research priority for the region.
5. Environmental and Agricultural Impacts
Western Uttar Pradesh is among the most productive agricultural regions in India, and the same atmospheric conditions that
generate the regional health burden act directly on the crops grown beneath them. The agricultural cost of regional air pollution
is therefore not a secondary consideration but a direct economic loss superimposed on the health loss (Sillmann et al., 2021).
5.1. Crop Yield and Photosynthesis
Ground‑level ozone is the most phytotoxic of the regional pollutants, and the increase documented for the region therefore car‑
ries a direct agronomic cost. Ozone enters the leaf through the stomata during normal gas exchange and oxidises mesophyll cell
membranes, producing visible foliar necrosis, reduced photosynthetic rate and lower assimilate partitioning to grain. Wheat is
among the more ozone‑sensitive of the major cereals, and the region’s principal ozone season, when high insolation combines
with elevated precursor concentrations, overlaps the wheat grain‑filling period (Sillmann et al., 2021; Gupta et al., 2017). Sugar‑
cane, the other dominant regional crop, is exposed for a longer fraction of its growth cycle. Because ozone forms downwind of its
precursor sources, the agricultural areas between the urban centres may experience concentrations comparable to or higher than
the monitored urban values (Cisneros et al., 2010).
Particulate deposition acts by a separate and more visible mechanism. Coarse dust and black carbon from brick kilns, unpaved
roads and construction settle as a physical film on leaf surfaces, attenuating the photosynthetically active radiation reaching the
chloroplast and, where deposition is heavy, occluding stomata (Van Zelm et al., 2016). The effect is greatest within a few hundred
metres of the source, which in this region means the field margins along transport corridors and around kiln clusters. Unlike the
ozone effect, this component of the burden should be expected to have eased over the study period, in line with the PM 10 decline
recorded across the region.
5.2. Soil Acidification and Heavy‐metal Loading
Sulphur and nitrogen oxides emitted from industrial corridors are oxidised in the atmosphere and returned to the surface as
sulphuric and nitric acid, by wet deposition during the monsoon and by dry deposition through the remainder of the year. The
cumulative effect is a downward drift in soil pH, mobilisation of aluminium into the soil solution, depletion of exchangeable base
cations and suppression of the microbial communities responsible for nitrogen fixation and organic matter turnover (Cisneros et al.,
2010). The consequence is a slow reduction in intrinsic soil fertility that is partially masked by increased fertiliser application, and
which is therefore likely to be under‑recognised. A second and less reversible process is the atmospheric deposition of heavy metals.
Slag dust, foundry emissions and glass furnace particulate carry lead, chromium and cadmium. Sequential‑extraction work in the
Firozabad glass industrial area found cadmium, lead and copper to be strongly enriched by anthropogenic activity, with hazard
quotients for nickel, manganese and chromium and excess lifetime cancer risk for Cr(VI) and nickel exceeding safe thresholds
(Rajouriya et al., 2020). Once deposited these elements are not degraded; they partition between soil solution and exchange sites, are
taken up by roots, and accumulate through the food chain. Given that the affected soils are under continuous intensive cultivation,
the relevant time horizon for this contamination is decades, and the exposure route it creates, dietary rather than inhalational, falls
entirely outside the scope of air quality monitoring.
6. Monitoring Blind Spots in the Regional Airshed
The monitored criteria pollutant record is the best available description of regional air quality, but its design determines what it
can and cannot see, and two structural omissions are large enough to affect its interpretation.
6.1. Fugitive, Non‐stack Emissions
Indian industrial air quality compliance is built around stack monitoring: emissions are sampled at a defined chimney outlet under
defined conditions. This architecture measures the emissions that pass through the stack and, by construction, cannot measure
those that do not. Emissions escaping from low furnace openings, doorways and roof vents, from material charging and transfer
points, from open storage of raw material and slag, and from process leaks all fall outside it. The composition of the regional
industrial base makes this omission consequential rather than marginal. The glass and bangle furnaces of Firozabad, the chemical
and engineering units around Meerut and Ghaziabad, the jaggery and pulp units of Muzaffarnagar and Saharanpur, and the
https://www.sagens.org/journal/agens [431]
DOI: https://doi.org/10.59983/s20260403019
AgroEnvironmental Sustainability, 2026, 4(3), 424–435
foundry and leather clusters of Agra are dominated by micro, small and medium enterprises operating with low‑height or absent
stacks and limited process enclosure (Weyant et al., 2014; Rajouriya et al., 2020). Where such units cluster geographically, the
aggregate fugitive release can plausibly exceed the aggregate stacked release, which would mean that the measured contribution
of industry to regional loading is systematically understated. The magnitude of this unmeasured term remains a hypothesis; it has
not been estimated for these districts, and quantifying it is a second research priority.
6.2. Transboundary Mixing of Rural and Urban Plumes
The second omission is spatial rather than technical. Monitoring in the region is organised by city, and each urban network is
interpreted against its own local emission inventory. But the post‑monsoon smoke plume advected from the agricultural north‑
west arrives over these cities already chemically aged, and mixes with locally generated industrial and vehicular emissions in
proportions that vary with wind direction and boundary layer depth. The resulting mixture is not the arithmetic sum of its parts:
secondary aerosol formation and ozone photochemistry both depend non‑linearly on the ratio of the precursors supplied by each
plume. The practical consequence is that a city‑level monitoring station cannot distinguish the fraction of its measured loading that
is locally generated and therefore locally controllable from the fraction that is imported. Real‑time source apportionment capable of
making this separation has been implemented for Delhi–NCR but not extended eastward across the Western Uttar Pradesh districts
(Awasthi et al., 2024). Without it, municipal control measures are being designed against an emission inventory of unknown local
share.
6.3. Implications for the Regulatory Model
Both omissions arise from the same underlying problem. The monitoring system measures the pollutants it was designed to regu‑
late, at the locations where regulation is enforced, within the administrative boundaries in which enforcement is organised. Emis‑
sions that are diffuse in space, that occur outside a regulated outlet, or that cross a district boundary are not captured, not because
they are unimportant, but because no part of the architecture is responsible for them. This is the structural argument for an airshed
approach.
7. Limitations
Four limitations bound these conclusions. The analysis is entirely secondary: it compiles reported annual and monthly aggregates
and undertakes no primary sampling, so it inherits any error present in the source record and cannot verify it. The aggregates are
also descriptive rather than analytical, since no meteorological normalisation, trend fitting or statistical testing has been applied,
which means that observed year‑to‑year differences cannot be separated into an emissions component and a weather component.
Interannual variability in winter inversion strength alone is capable of producing changes of the magnitude reported here. A third
constraint is the choice of start year. The study period opens in 2020, when industrial and transport activity were substantially
reduced for part of the year. Any comparison that uses 2020 or 2021 as a baseline will therefore tend to overstate subsequent
deterioration, and the mid‑period minima observed at several sites should be read with this in mind. The 2026 values represent a
partial year and are indicative only. Two features of the compiled record require qualification. The carbon monoxide series shows
a simultaneous step change at all seven cities between 2021 and 2022 that is more consistent with a change in reporting unit or
instrumentation than with a regional emissions event, and the reported magnitudes are not compatible with the mg/m3 convention
of the Indian standard; the CO column is accordingly excluded from interpretation. The 2026 sulphur dioxide values for Agra and
Firozabad are two to five times those recorded at the other five cities and require confirmation. Monthly minima are not reported
for Muzaffarnagar or Agra, so the seasonal amplitude at those two sites cannot be quantified. Finally, the microplastic, fugitive
emission and transboundary mixing pathways are supported by mechanistic evidence from other regions but by no measurement
from Western Uttar Pradesh; they remain hypotheses, and no quantitative claim is made about their contribution to regional
loading.
8. Future Research Directions
These gaps translate into three measurable research programmes, each of which is feasible with existing instrumentation and each
of which would close a specific hole in the regional evidence base. High‑volume ambient samplers should be deployed at four
contrasting site types: a high‑traffic corridor, an active construction zone, an MSME cluster and a peri‑urban agricultural control,
with filter samples analysed by micro‑FTIR spectroscopy to establish fibre abundance, polymer composition and size distribution.
Paired elemental analysis of the same filters would establish whether adsorbed metal loading is detectable and how it varies with
fibre weathering state. Sampling should span at least one full winter and one monsoon season, since the hypothesised increase
in metal adsorption is expected to be greatest under the prolonged high‑humidity, high‑particulate conditions of winter smog
episodes. Perimeter‑based open‑path FTIR along the boundaries of representative MSME clusters in Firozabad, Meerut, Saharan‑
pur and Muzaffarnagar would allow the total flux leaving a cluster to be compared with the sum of its declared stack emissions,
yielding a direct estimate of the fugitive fraction. Sensor‑equipped unmanned aerial platforms would complement this by locating

individual release points within a cluster at a spatial resolution that fixed monitoring cannot achieve. The output required is a
revised industrial emission inventory that carries an explicit fugitive term. Machine learning applied to the existing monitoring
record, together with meteorological reanalysis and satellite fire‑count data, could be used to forecast the conditions under which
imported residue smoke and local urban emissions combine to produce the highest secondary pollutant concentrations (Zhu et al.,
2026; He et al., 2026). The practical objective is a short‑horizon operational forecast capable of supporting graded response actions,
rather than a research model. Model development should be paired with real‑time source apportionment so that forecast skill can
be attributed to the correct source term.
9. Implementation and Policy Actions
The regional record supports a shift from municipal air quality management towards regional airshed management, a direction
reinforced by the projected interaction of climate change with air pollution burdens (Kinney, 2018; Turnock et al., 2023). The case
for that shift is not primarily administrative. It follows from three properties of the data: ozone, the fastest‑rising pollutant in the
region, forms downwind of its precursors and cannot be controlled within the boundary in which those precursors are emitted; a
substantial share of winter particulate loading is imported across district boundaries; and the largest unmeasured emission term
is diffuse and clustered rather than located at regulated outlets. None of these three can be tackled by a city acting alone; airshed‑
scale modelling for Delhi–NCR indicates that controls confined to a single administrative area deliver only a fraction of the benefit
achievable across the full airshed. Table 5 shows staged programme, distinguishing measures that require no new institutional
arrangement from those that do. The sequencing is deliberate: the immediate actions generate the measurements on which the

10. Conclusion
The seven‑city record does not describe a region in uniform decline, nor one in recovery. It describes a transition. Between 2020 and
2026 the coarse particulate burden fell at every site, and the annual AQI fell with it, an outcome attributable to dust control acting
on mechanically generated material. Over the same period ozone rose at all seven sites and nitrogen dioxide at six, while the fine
particulate fraction declined only shallowly and has risen again at several cities. Regional exposure has shifted from coarse and
primary towards fine, secondary and gaseous, and the aggregate index used to report air quality is the metric least able to detect
https://www.sagens.org/journal/agens [433]
DOI: https://doi.org/10.59983/s20260403019

that shift. The health burden should therefore not be assumed to be falling in proportion to the AQI, and the agricultural cost is
rising, since ozone is both the fastest‑increasing and the most phytotoxic pollutant in the dataset. Three further processes lie beyond
what the present monitoring architecture can observe: weathered synthetic fibres as carriers of adsorbed metals, diffuse release
from informal industrial clusters, and non‑linear mixing of imported agricultural smoke with local plumes. None is quantified here.
Closing these gaps requires ambient FTIR characterisation, perimeter fugitive flux surveys and real‑time source apportionment.
