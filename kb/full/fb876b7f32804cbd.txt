Abstract
Background Medical students, owing to academic pressure and extensive digital device use, are particularly 
vulnerable to poor sleep quality. As smartphone addiction and bedtime procrastination are increasingly recognized 
as important behavioral factors influencing sleep, evaluating their interrelationship with sleep quality among Indian 
medical students may provide insights for developing targeted interventions to promote healthier digital and sleep 
behaviors.
Method A cross-sectional observational study was conducted among 180 medical students aged 18–25 years at 
a tertiary care Centre in Greater Noida, India, using a convenience sampling method after obtaining Institutional 
Ethics Committee approval. Smartphone addiction was assessed using the Smartphone Addiction Scale: Short 
Version (SAS-SV), sleep quality using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and bedtime procrastination using 
the Bedtime Procrastination Scale (BPS). Data was analyzed using SPSS version 28.0. Pearson correlation analysis was 
performed and p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
Results The mean global PSQI score was 6.78 ± 2.40 with 72.2% (130/180) indicating poor sleep quality (PSQI > 5) 
in the majority of participants. Based on the validated SAS-SV cut-off scores (≥ 31 for males and ≥ 33 for females), 
smartphone addiction was present in 56.5% of males and 40.3% of females. Bedtime procrastination showed 
no significant gender difference (p = 0.976). Smartphone addiction correlated strongly with poor sleep quality 
(r = 0.66, p < 0.01) and moderately with bedtime procrastination (r = 0.56, p < 0.01). Bedtime procrastination was also 
moderately associated with poor sleep quality (r = 0.52, p < 0.01). Multiple linear regression analysis demonstrated that 
higher smartphone addiction (β = 0.54, p < 0.001) and bedtime procrastination (β = 0.21, p = 0.002) were significant 
independent predictors of poor sleep quality, whereas age and sex were not significant predictors.
Conclusion Early identification and targeted interventions focusing on digital hygiene and sleep education are 
essential to improve sleep health and overall well-being in this population.

 Introduction
Smartphones have evolved from simple communica -
tion tools into indispensable companions that influ -
ence almost every aspect of daily life. Their widespread 
availability, affordability, and multifunctional capabili -
ties have led to unprecedented levels of use, particularly 
among young adults. While smartphones offer undeni -
able advantages such as instant access to educational 
resources, communication, and information, excessive 
and uncontrolled use has emerged as a growing public 
health concern. This pattern of maladaptive use, often 
described as smartphone addiction, has been associated 
with adverse physical, psychological, and behavioral out -
comes (El-Ali et al. 2021; Kumar et al. 2024)
For medical students, smartphones serve as valuable 
academic aids, facilitating access to digital textbooks, 
online lectures, clinical references, and peer commu -
nication. But prolonged screen exposure, compulsive 
checking behaviors, and difficulty disengaging from 
smartphones can negatively impact mental well-being 
and daily functioning, with sleep being one of the most 
affected domains (Kumar et al. 2024). Medical students 
are particularly vulnerable to excessive smartphone use 
and poor sleep because of demanding academic work -
loads, prolonged study hours, frequent examinations, 
clinical responsibilities, and psychological stress. These 
factors, combined with extensive reliance on smart -
phones for academic activities and communication, may 
increase their susceptibility to smartphone addiction, 
bedtime procrastination, and sleep disturbances (Chat -
terjee and Kar 2021). Adequate sleep quality is essential 
for optimal cognitive performance, emotional regulation, 
and physical health. Bedtime procrastination refers to the 
voluntary delay of going to bed despite intending to sleep 
earlier, in the absence of external constraints (Jain et al. 
2025).
Given the crucial role of sleep in learning, memory 
consolidation, and clinical decision making, under -
standing the interplay between smartphone addiction, 
bedtime procrastination, and sleep quality in medical 
students is of significant importance. It was hypothesized 
that smartphone addiction and bedtime procrastina -
tion would be significantly associated with poor sleep 
quality in this population. Therefore, the present cross 
sectional observational study aims to examine and corre -
late these variables, with the objective of providing valu -
able insights into their interrelationship. Such findings 
may contribute to early identification of at-risk students 
and inform the development of targeted interventions 
to promote healthier smartphone use and better sleep 
hygiene among future healthcare professionals.
Novelty/rationale
Although recent Indian studies have reported a high 
prevalence of smartphone addiction and poor sleep 
quality among medical students and demonstrated an 
association between problematic smartphone use and 
sleep disturbances (Chatterjee and Kar 2021; Jain et al. 
2025), few have explored the interrelationship between 
smartphone addiction, bedtime procrastination, and 
sleep quality within this population. Given the unique 
academic demands, clinical responsibilities, and exten -
sive reliance on smartphones for educational purposes, 
understanding these associations may facilitate early 
identification of students at risk for sleep disturbances 
and inform targeted preventive interventions.
Methodology
A cross-sectional observational study was conducted 
among medical students aged 18–25 years after obtain -
ing approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee 
(IEC No: ECR/1224/Inst./UP/2019) dated 21/10/2023). 
The study was carried out at a tertiary care medical insti -
tution in Greater Noida, India, and data were collected 
between January and April 2024.
Study population and sampling
Participants were recruited using a convenience sam -
pling method. All eligible medical students aged 18–25 
years, currently pursuing medical education and using 
smartphones, were approached during routine academic 
hours. Students who provided informed consent and 
fulfilled the eligibility criteria were enrolled until the 
required sample size was achieved.
Students with a self-reported history of psychiatric 
illness, current psychiatric treatment, or use of psycho -
tropic medications were excluded from participation. 
Psychiatric history was assessed during eligibility screen -
ing prior to enrollment.

The sample size was initially calculated using the single 
population proportion formula to estimate the preva -
lence of smartphone addiction. In the absence of reliable 
local prevalence estimates, a prevalence (p) of 50%, a 95% 
confidence level (Z = 1.96), and an absolute precision of 
7.5% were assumed, yielding a required sample size of 
approximately 180 participants. Although the principal 
analytical objective was to evaluate correlations among 
smartphone addiction, bedtime procrastination, and 
sleep quality, this sample size was also adequate for cor -
relation analysis.
A post hoc power analysis showed that, with 180 par -
ticipants and α = 0.05, the study had approximately 99% 
power to detect the observed correlation coefficients 
(r = 0.52–0.66), confirming sufficient power for the pri -
mary analyses.
A total of 190 medical students were approached for 
participation in the study. Of these, 187 students con -
sented and were recruited (response rate: 98.4%), and 
180 participants completed the questionnaire. Only fully 
completed questionnaires were included in the final anal-
ysis, and no imputation for missing data was performed. 
Consequently, a total of 180 medical students fulfilling 
the eligibility criteria were included in the final analysis.
Data collection tools
Smartphone addiction was assessed using the Smart -
phone Addiction Scale Short Version (SAS-SV). The 
SAS-SV is a validated 10 item self-report questionnaire 
scored on a six-point Likert scale. It demonstrates good 
reliability and validity for assessing smartphone addiction 
in both community and research settings (Buysse et al. 
1989). The scale evaluates six domains: daily-life distur -
bance, positive anticipation, withdrawal, cyberspace-ori -
ented relationships, overuse, and tolerance. Participants 
were classified as having smartphone addiction using the 
validated SAS-SV cut-off scores of ≥ 31 for males and ≥ 33 
for females (Kwon et al. 2013).
Sleep quality was evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep 
Quality Index (PSQI), a widely used self-reported instru -
ment that takes approximately 5–10 min to complete. 
The PSQI assesses sleep quality across seven compo -
nents: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep dura -
tion, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of 
sleeping medication, and daytime dysfunction (Buysse et 
al. 1989).
Bedtime procrastination was measured using the Bed -
time Procrastination Scale (BPS). The BPS is a nine-item 
self-report questionnaire designed to assess the tendency 
to voluntarily delay bedtime in the absence of external 
constraints. Responses are recorded on a five-point Lik -
ert scale, with higher scores indicating greater bedtime 
procrastination (Kroese et al. 2014).
Statistical analysis
Data were entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statis -
tics for Windows, Version 28.0(IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, 
USA). Appropriate statistical tests were applied based 
on data distribution. Descriptive statistics were used to 
summarize demographic variables and scale scores. Cor -
relation analysis was performed to assess the relationship 
between smartphone addiction, bedtime procrastination, 
and sleep quality. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statisti-
cally significant.
Additionally, multiple linear regression analysis was 
performed to identify independent predictors of sleep 
quality, with global PSQI score as the dependent variable 
and smartphone addiction and bedtime procrastination 
scores as independent variables.

Discussion
The present study examined the relationship between 
smartphone addiction, bedtime procrastination, and 
sleep quality among medical students, a population 
known to experience high academic demands and irregu-
lar sleep patterns. The findings reveal a high prevalence of 
poor sleep quality, significant levels of smartphone addic-
tion, and widespread bedtime procrastination, highlight -
ing an interlinked behavioral pattern that may adversely 
affect student’s well-being and academic performance.
In this study, the mean global PSQI score exceeded the 
established cut-off for poor sleep quality, indicating that 
the majority of medical students experienced suboptimal 
sleep. The prevalence of poor sleep quality observed in 
the present study (72.2%), this finding is consistent with 
earlier studies reporting disturbed sleep patterns among 
medical students due to academic stress, extended study 
hours, and lifestyle factors (Chatterjee and Kar 2021; 
Athakrishnan et al. 2021). The differences may be attrib -
uted to variations in study settings, academic workload, 
participant characteristics, and assessment methods. The 
high contribution of daytime dysfunction and reduced 
sleep duration suggests that inadequate sleep is not 
merely a nocturnal issue but has tangible consequences 
on daytime alertness, concentration, and functional per -
formance. Such impairments are particularly concerning 
in medical students, where sustained attention and cog -
nitive efficiency are critical (Sadeghi et al. 2025).
Smartphone addiction emerged as a prominent con -
cern, with more than half of male students and a substan-
tial proportion of female students meeting the criteria 
for addiction. The higher prevalence observed among 
males aligns with previous literature which have reported 
prevalence estimates ranging from approximately 40% to 
60%., suggesting gender differences in patterns of smart -
phone use, including greater engagement in gaming, 
online content consumption, and prolonged screen expo-
sure (Jain et al. 2025; Das et al. 2024; Cheng and Li 2019). 
However, the considerable proportion of addicted users 
among females indicates that problematic smartphone 
use is widespread across genders and not limited to a 
specific subgroup.
Bedtime procrastination was commonly observed in 
both male and female participants, with no significant 
gender-based differences. This finding suggests that bed -
time procrastination may be influenced more by shared 
environmental and behavioral factors such as academic 
workload, late-night smartphone use, and irregular 
schedules rather than by gender alone. The tendency to 
delay bedtime despite intending to sleep earlier reflects 
poor self-regulation and highlights the role of digital 
engagement during nighttime hours (Wang et al. 2025)
One of the most important findings of this study is the 
significant positive correlation among smartphone addic-
tion, bedtime procrastination, and poor sleep quality. 
Smartphone addiction demonstrated a strong associa -
tion with impaired sleep quality, supporting the hypoth -
esis that excessive smartphone use, particularly before 
bedtime, contributes to sleep disruption. Several behav -
ioural and physiological mechanisms may explain this 
relationship. First, prolonged smartphone use at night 
delays bedtime by displacing sleep time, as users con -
tinue engaging with social media, video streaming, gam -
ing, or messaging applications despite intending to sleep. 
Second, exposure to blue light emitted from smartphone 
screens suppresses melatonin secretion and delays the 
circadian rhythm, resulting in prolonged sleep onset 
latency and poorer sleep quality. Third, emotionally 
engaging or rewarding digital content increases cognitive 
and emotional arousal, making it more difficult to disen -
gage from the device and transition into sleep. Further -
more, excessive smartphone use has been associated with 
impaired self-regulation, reduced inhibitory control, and 
habitual reward-seeking behaviour, which may predis -
pose individuals to repeatedly postpone bedtime despite 
recognizing the adverse consequences (Babakhani et 
al. 2025). Among medical students, these mechanisms 
may be further intensified by academic stress, irregular 
schedules, examination pressure, and the frequent use of 
smartphones for educational as well as recreational pur -
poses, creating a behavioural environment that favours 
delayed sleep initiation.
Additionally, the moderate correlation between smart -
phone addiction and bedtime procrastination suggests

that problematic smartphone use may directly contrib -

reverse relationship is also plausible. Students experienc -
ing poor sleep quality may increasingly use smartphones 
during nighttime because of difficulty initiating or main -
taining sleep, boredom during wakefulness, or reliance 
on digital media for relaxation and social interaction. 
Consequently, a bidirectional relationship may exist in 
which smartphone addiction and poor sleep reinforce 
one another over time. Given the cross-sectional design 
of the present study, the temporal direction of these asso-
ciations cannot be established.
Furthermore, bedtime procrastination itself was mod -
erately correlated with poor sleep quality, reinforcing the 
concept that delayed bedtime represents an important 
behavioural pathway linking excessive smartphone use 
to sleep disturbances. Collectively, these findings suggest 
a vicious cycle in which excessive smartphone use pro -
motes bedtime procrastination, bedtime procrastination 
reduces sleep duration and quality, and poor sleep further 
increases reliance on smartphones, ultimately perpetuat -
ing daytime fatigue, impaired cognitive performance, and 
reduced overall well-being (Bozkurt et al. 2024; Huang et 
al. 2023).
The multiple regression analysis in the present study 
demonstrated that both smartphone addiction and bed -
time procrastination independently predicted poorer 
sleep quality after adjusting for age and sex, with smart -
phone addiction showing a stronger association. These 
findings are consistent with previous multivariable stud -
ies reporting that problematic smartphone use remains 
an independent predictor of poor sleep quality even after 
adjustment for potential confounders. Likewise, evidence 
has shown that mobile phone addiction independently 
predicts bedtime procrastination after controlling for 
relevant behavioral and psychological factors, suggest -
ing that excessive smartphone use contributes to delayed 
sleep initiation beyond the influence of other variables 
(Meng et al. 2024; Yin et al. 2025). Collectively, these 
findings support the notion that smartphone addiction 
and bedtime procrastination are distinct yet interrelated 
behavioral factors that independently contribute to poor 
sleep quality among university students.
The present study has certain limitations that should 
be acknowledged. Owing to its cross-sectional design, 
the present study can identify associations but cannot 
establish temporal or direct causal relationships between 
smartphone addiction, bedtime procrastination, and 
sleep quality. Furthermore, the possibility of reverse 
causation cannot be excluded. The use of convenience 
sampling from a single institution may introduce selec -
tion bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. 
Additionally, reliance on self-reported questionnaires is 
subject to recall and social desirability bias, despite the 
use of validated instruments. Although participants with 
diagnosed psychiatric illness were excluded, unmeasured 
confounders such as academic stress, caffeine intake, 
physical activity, and exposure to other digital devices 
may have influenced the observed associations. Future 
research should employ longitudinal and multi-center 
designs, incorporate objective measures of sleep and 
smartphone use, and explore mediation models to clarify 
causal pathways and inform targeted interventions aimed 
at improving sleep health among medical students. The 
findings underscore the need for targeted interventions 
aimed at improving digital hygiene and sleep behaviors 
among medical students. Educational programs focusing 
on responsible smartphone use, limiting screen exposure 
before bedtime, and promoting sleep hygiene practices 
may help mitigate these issues. Given the academic and 
clinical responsibilities of medical students, early identi -
fication and management of these behaviors are essential 
to safeguard both student health and future professional 
performance.
Clinical and educational implications
The findings of the present study have important impli -
cations for medical education and student well-being. 
Medical colleges may benefit from incorporating digital 
hygiene and sleep health awareness programs into exist -
ing wellness initiatives. Interventions such as educating 
students about limiting smartphone use before bedtime, 
encouraging structured sleep routines, and promoting 
strategies to reduce bedtime procrastination may help 
improve sleep quality. Regular screening and counselling 
services for students experiencing problematic smart -
phone use could also be integrated into institutional 
support systems to promote academic performance and 
psychological well-being.
Conclusion
The present study highlights the need for early identifica-
tion of maladaptive smartphone use and targeted inter -
ventions focusing on digital hygiene and sleep education.
Implementing strategies such as limiting smartphone 
use before bedtime, promoting healthy sleep routines, 
and increasing awareness of the consequences of bedtime 
procrastination may improve sleep quality and overall 
well-being in medical students. Addressing these issues is 
essential not only for students’ health but also for main -
taining optimal academic performance and future profes-
sional competence.
