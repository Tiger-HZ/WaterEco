Abstract
Introduction The optimal reduction strategy for displaced femoral neck fractures (DFNFs) in non-elderly adults remains 
controversial. Although open reduction may improve radiographic alignment, its impact on outcomes remains unclear. 
Materials and methods Adults aged < 60 years with DFNFs who were treated with internal fixation between 2017 and 2023 
were retrospectively reviewed. Patients were categorized into closed and open reduction groups. Reduction quality was 
assessed using the Garden alignment index and the Haidukewych reduction criteria. Multivariate analyses were performed 
for the overall cohort and patients with Pauwels type III fractures. Outcomes included non-union, osteonecrosis, femoral 
neck shortening, and major reoperations. 
Results In total, 98 patients were included, among whom 83 and 15 underwent closed and open reduction, respectively. 
Although open reduction achieved a higher rate of excellent reduction (53.3% vs. 16.9%, p = 0.012), it was not associated 
with lower rates of non-union or osteonecrosis. In the overall cohort, age was associated with non-union (odds ratio [OR], 
1.09; p = 0.024), whereas poorer reduction quality was associated with major reoperation (fair: OR 7.03, p = 0.012; poor: OR 
47.6, p = 0.007). In patients with Pauwels type III fractures, poorer reduction quality predicted conversion to arthroplasty 
(fair: OR 9.84, p = 0.014), and age was associated with non-union and osteonecrosis. Femoral neck shortening of > 10 mm 
at 3 months was associated with age and open reduction, whereas at 12 months, age and intraoperative blood loss remained 
significant predictors. 
Conclusions Open reduction achieved a higher rate of excellent reduction but did not reduce complication rates compared 
with closed reduction. Fracture severity, patient age, and reduction quality remained the principal determinants of outcome, 
supporting a fracture morphology-guided fixation strategy and realistic patient counseling.

Superior reduction quality without improved outcomes: open versus 
closed reduction for displaced femoral neck fractures in adults under 
60 years

Introduction
Displaced femoral neck fractures (DFNFs) in individu -
als younger than 60 years are uncommon, with an esti -
mated annual incidence of 4–72 per 100,000 people [ 1, 
2]. Although mortality is lower than that in older patients, 
these fractures typically result from higher-energy trauma 
and are often more severe [ 3, 4]. Moreover, disruption of 
the femoral head vascular supply is thought to contribute 
to an increased risk of non-union and osteonecrosis in this 
population [5]. Reported rates of treatment failure, includ -
ing fixation failure, non-union, symptomatic malunion, and 
osteonecrosis, vary substantially by fracture pattern, reduc-
tion quality, and fixation strategy, ranging from 5 to 90%

[6–9]. Approximately 15% of young adults with DFNFs 
eventually require conversion to total hip arthroplasty, usu-
ally within two years [9]. However, the age threshold defin-
ing “young” or “non-elderly” patients remains inconsistent 
across the literature. Although many studies have used 50 
years as the cutoff, contemporary treatment strategies fre -
quently extend head-preserving fixation to physiologically 
active patients approaching 60 years of age [10, 11].
Joint preservation is the goal of physiologically young 
patients; however, the optimal surgical approach remains 
debatable. Reduction quality is the most critical surgeon-
controlled determinant of outcomes and is strongly associ -
ated with uneventful union, whereas inadequate reduction 
increases the risk of treatment failure by more than five -
fold [6, 12, 13]. Although open reduction is often advocated 
to achieve anatomical reduction, pooled analyses have 
reported similar rates of acceptable reduction between open 
and closed techniques, with open reduction associated with 
higher rates of reoperation and complications [6, 12–17].
In contrast, closed reduction relies on fluoroscopic assess-
ment of alignment and displacement. Despite its presumed 
advantage in preserving femoral head vascularity, closed 
reduction has poor to moderate inter- and intra-observer 
reliability, limiting the reproducibility of reduction quality 
[18]. Postoperative femoral neck collapse, characterized by 
varus deformity or shortening, may result from inadequate 
reduction, unstable fracture patterns, injury mechanisms, or 
implant selection and is associated with poorer functional 
outcomes [19].
The purpose of this study was to compare the reduction 
quality and postoperative radiographic outcomes between 
closed and open reduction in adults younger than 60 years 
with DFNFs. The primary outcome was the reduction in 
quality, assessed using the established criteria. Secondary 
outcomes included radiographic stability, femoral neck col-
lapse and shortening, as well as fixation failure, including 
non-union, osteonecrosis, and major reoperations.
Methods and materials
Patient enrolment and data collection
Medical and radiographic records of patients younger 
than 60 years with DFNFs who underwent osteosynthesis 
between January 2017 and June 2023 were retrospectively 
reviewed using the Institutional Trauma Registry. Displace-
ment was defined as a Garden type III or IV fracture [ 20]. 
Patients with bilateral fractures, pathological fractures, or 
prior hip surgery were excluded.
Eligible patients were categorized into closed and open 
reduction groups based on the reduction technique applied. 
Demographic characteristics, injury-related variables, and 
operative details were systematically extracted from elec -
tronic medical records and operative reports. Attending 
orthopedic trauma surgeons or fellowship-trained surgeons 
performed all the surgeries. In cases where orthopedic 
residents participated as primary operators, the procedures 
were performed under the direct supervision of the attend -
ing orthopedic trauma surgeons. This study was approved 
by our Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 202600177B0).
Surgical strategy
Osteosynthesis was generally performed within 24 h of 
DFNF confirmation, unless contraindicated. The choice of 
closed or open reduction was determined by the treating 
surgeon’s clinical judgment based on fracture morphology, 
intraoperative reducibility, and overall patient condition.
For closed reduction, patients were positioned supine 
on a traction table, and reduction was achieved under flu -
oroscopic guidance (Ziehm Solo; Ziehm Imaging GmbH, 
Nuremberg, Germany), with provisional femoral head con -
trol using a percutaneously inserted Kirschner wire used 
as a joystick (Fig. 1). Reduction quality was assessed on 
anteroposterior and lateral femoral neck views, and defini -
tive fixation was performed after satisfactory alignment 
was confirmed. Fixation constructs included three or four 
percutaneous cannulated screws (CS, Syntec Technology 
Co., Hsinchu, Taiwan), a sliding hip screw (SHS) with an 
anti-rotation screw (Syntec Technology Co.), the Femoral 
Neck System (FNS, DePuy Synthes, Paoli, PA, USA), or the 
INTERTAN™ cephalomedullary nail (Smith & Nephew, 
London, UK).
For open reduction, the patients were positioned supine 
on a radiolucent table (Modular Table System, Mizuho OSI, 
Union City, CA, USA), and reduction was performed using 
the Smith–Petersen approach [ 21]. These procedures were 
primarily performed by senior orthopedic trauma surgeons 
with experience in femoral neck preservation surgery. After 
exposure of the anterior hip capsule, a Z-shaped capsulot -
omy was performed to directly visualize the fracture site 
and facilitate anatomical reduction. Care was taken to avoid 
excessive posterior-superior capsular stripping and to pre -
serve the retinacular soft tissues supplying the femoral head. 
Fracture fixation was then performed under combined direct 
visualization and fluoroscopic guidance. In most cases, an 
additional medial buttress plate (MBP) using a one-third 
semitubular plate (Syntec Technology Co.) was added to 
enhance stability (Fig. 2). Definitive fixation implants were 
otherwise identical to those used for closed reduction.

Applied classifications
Femoral neck fractures were classified using the Garden and 
Pauwels systems [20]. The Garden classification categorizes 
fractures into types I to IV based on fracture completeness 
and displacement [ 20]. Pauwels’ classification was deter -
mined according to the inclination of the fracture line rela -
tive to the horizontal plane: type I, < 30°; type II, 30°–50°; 
and type III, > 50° [20].
Reduction quality was assessed using two established 
indices. The Garden alignment index (GAI) was measured 
on postoperative anteroposterior and lateral radiographs to 
evaluate the alignment of femoral head trabeculae relative 
to the femoral shaft [ 22]. Based on the GAI, the reduction 
quality was categorized as anatomical, acceptable (155°–
180° in both views), borderline (< 155° or > 180° in either 
view), or unacceptable (< 150° or > 185° in the anteropos -
terior view).
Reduction quality was further evaluated using the Haid -
ukewych reduction criteria (HRC) [12]. The HRC provides 
a quantitative assessment of femoral neck fracture reduction 
based on the maximum residual displacement or angula -
tion measured on postoperative anteroposterior and lateral 
radiographs. Reduction was graded as excellent (< 2 mm 
displacement or < 5° angulation in any plane), good (2–5 
mm displacement or 5–10° angulation), fair (5–10 mm dis -
placement and/or 10–20° angulation), or poor (> 10 mm dis-
placement, > 20° angulation, or residual varus alignment).
Femoral neck shortening was assessed using radio -
graphic measurement methods described by Zlowodzki et 
al. and Polat et al. [ 19, 23]. Shortening was determined by 
comparing serial anteroposterior radiographs of the affected 
hip, and clinically relevant shortening was defined as a 
decrease of ≥ 10 mm. Posteromedial cortical comminu -
tion was defined as the absence of a continuous opposing 
posterior and medial femoral neck cortex on postoperative 
anteroposterior or lateral radiographs [16].
Interobserver reliability was assessed by two orthopedic 
trauma surgeons, with discrepancies resolved by a senior 
surgeon.
Definition of complications
Major complications included non-union, osteonecrosis, or 
major reoperations. Non-union was defined as the absence 
of radiographic healing at 6 months and/or loss of fixation 
integrity [ 24]. Osteonecrosis was diagnosed and staged 
according to the Association Research Circulation Osse -
ous classification [ 25], with stage III or higher considered 
a failure. Major reoperation included conversion to total 
hip arthroplasty or hemiarthroplasty, muscle pedicle bone 
grafting, or early fixation revision. Elective implant removal 
after fracture union was not considered a complication.
Statistical analysis
Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. All 
statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 
22 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY , USA). Continuous 
and categorical variables were compared using indepen -
dent t-tests and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, 
respectively. Femoral neck shortening was measured using 
two-way ANOV A, and neck-shaft angle (NSA) was ana -
lyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test and Friedman test 
for inter- and intragroup comparisons at each time point. 
Intraobserver reliability was assessed using intraclass corre-
lation coefficients (ICC) (95% confidence interval [CI]) and 
Fleiss-Kappa (κ). Stepwise multivariate logistic regression 
was used to identify predictors of adverse outcomes. Statis-
tical significance was set at p < 0.05.
Results
Patient characteristics
During the study period, 98 patients met the inclusion criteria 
(closed, n = 83; open, n = 15) (Table 1). Patients in the open 
reduction group were younger (39.6 ± 12.2 vs. 46.3 ± 10.8 
years, p = 0.03). The open reduction group had a longer 
operative time (195.9 ± 47.0 vs. 130.2 ± 78.9 min, p = 0.002) 
and greater intraoperative blood loss (296.7 ± 262.2 vs. 
92.6 ± 199.4 mL, p = 0.001) than the closed reduction group. 
In the open reduction group, the primary fixation devices 
included cannulated screws in 12 patients, SHS in 1 patient, 
and FNS in 2 patients. Fourteen of the 15 patients addition-
ally received medial buttress plate augmentation, whereas 
1 patient underwent open reduction with cannulated screw 
fixation alone.
Complications and hip survival
During follow-up, osteonecrosis and non-union occurred in 
17 (17.3%) and 23 (23.5%) patients, respectively (Table 1). 
Non-union was more frequent in the open reduction group 
(46.7% vs. 19.3%, p = 0.04), whereas the incidence of osteo-
necrosis did not differ between the groups.
Overall, 30 patients (30.1%) underwent major reopera -
tion. Of these, 27 (27.6%) ultimately required conversion to

136.1° to 127.4° and 129.0° at the corresponding time

cant progression thereafter.

Multivariate analysis of complications in the overall 
cohort
Multivariate analysis of the overall cohort identified 
age as an independent predictor of non-union (odds ratio 
[OR] = 1.089, p = 0.024) (Table 3). Reduction quality 
assessed by the HRC and posterior cortical comminution 
were associated with conversion to arthroplasty, with fair 
and poor reductions conferring substantially higher odds 
than excellent reductions. Osteonecrosis was associated 
with patient sex (OR = 4.21, p = 0.043) but showed no sig -
nificant association with reduction-related variables.
Multivariate analysis in Pauwels type III fractures
Given the imbalance in fracture severity between the groups, 
additional multivariate analyses were performed in patients 
with Pauwels type III fractures (Table 4). Within this high-
risk subgroup, non-union was associated with older age, 
greater intraoperative blood loss, and poorer reduction, after 
adjustment for confounders. Osteonecrosis was associated 
with older age (OR = 0.935, p = 0.037). Reduction quality 
assessed by the HRC was associated with major reoperation 
(p = 0.032), with fair reduction conferring higher odds than 
excellent reduction (OR = 23.49, p = 0.005). Delayed sur -
gery showed a trend towards predicting major reoperation 
(p = 0.05). Femoral neck shortening > 10 mm at 3 months 
was associated with older age (OR = 1.132, p = 0.038) and 
open reduction (OR = 51.02, p = 0.01). At 12 months, older 
age and intraoperative blood loss remained significant 
predictors.

Discussion
Preservation of the native femoral head remains the pri -
mary goal in non-elderly patients with DFNFs because of 
the limited longevity and long-term consequences of hip 
arthroplasty in this population [ 26]. However, fixation fail-
ure remains common, with reported rates of non-union, 
osteonecrosis, conversion to arthroplasty, and reopera -
tion ranging from 9 to 30%, 14–18%, 14–24%, and 50%, 
respectively [ 9–11, 16, 27]. Although closed reduction is 
often preferred as the initial strategy because of its per -
ceived advantage of preserving femoral head vascularity, 
open reduction is sometimes required to achieve anatomical 
alignment in irreducible or highly unstable fractures. In our 
cohort, the overall outcomes, including non-union, osteo -
necrosis, reoperation, and conversion to arthroplasty, were 
comparable to those reported in contemporary series.
Achieving anatomical reduction in DFNFs remains 
technically demanding, regardless of whether closed or 
open techniques are used. Soft-tissue interposition, corti -
cal impaction, and unfavorable fracture morphology can 
prevent successful closed reduction, whereas three-dimen -
sional deformities, particularly residual varus or posterior 
tilt, are frequently underestimated on fluoroscopic assess -
ment [ 28, 29]. Previous studies have consistently demon -
strated that poor reduction quality is a strong predictor of 
fixation failure, with reported failure rates approaching 80% 
when acceptable alignment is not achieved [ 12]. In our 
cohort, fractures with relatively stable configurations were 
generally reduced and stabilized satisfactorily, whereas the 
most unstable patterns, predominantly Pauwels type III, 
were overrepresented among patients requiring open reduc-
tion. These vertically oriented fractures pose the greatest 
challenge to achieving and maintaining anatomic reduction 
and, therefore, warrant focused discussion of fixation strate-
gies in this high-risk subgroup.
Pauwels type III femoral neck fractures are character -
ized by high vertical shear forces and inherent instability. 
Accordingly, fixation constructs must provide robust resis -
tance to shear and rotation. Contemporary biomechanical 
and clinical evidence favors fixed-angle or angular-stable 
constructs, such as an SHS with anti-rotation screw (s) or 
the FNS, over multiple CSs in vertical fracture patterns 
[10, 30, 31]. An MBP has been proposed as an adjunct to 
enhance stability by neutralizing inferomedial sliding and 
converting shear forces into compression across the fracture 
plane, thereby improving the mechanical environment for 
union [32, 33].
In a large multicenter study of 565 young adults with 
DFNFs, Roser et al. reported higher failure rates with CSs 
(56%) than with fixed-angle constructs (36%). Among 
fixed-angle strategies, SHS combined with an anti-rotation 
screw and MBP demonstrated the lowest failure rate (11%), 
supporting the importance of implant selection and con -
struct stability in vertically unstable fractures [ 10]. In the 
present cohort, among the open reduction cases with MBP 
augmentation, cannulated screws were used more frequently 
than fixed-angle fixation, which may have contributed to 
the relatively high complication rate observed in the open 
reduction group. In selected cases, InterTAN was used as 
an angular-stable cephalocervical construct with integrated 
rotational control, and previous biomechanical studies have 
suggested its potential advantages in unstable Pauwels type 
III fracture models [34].
Importantly, the MBP was used as an adjunctive infero -
medial support rather than the primary load-bearing fixa -
tion construct. Overall construct stability still primarily 
depended on the lateral fixation device and fracture heal -
ing progression. In our failed cases, the predominant failure

it may not fully compensate for insufficient lateral fixation 
stability in highly vertical and unstable fracture patterns.

reduction, particularly in Pauwels type III fractures where 
indirect reduction is often insufficient, these unstable frac -

In our cohort, the NSA decreased significantly from the 
immediate postoperative period to 3 months in both the 
closed and open reduction groups, with no further signifi -
cant progression. This temporal pattern aligns with prior 
reports showing that femoral neck collapse, manifesting as 
shortening and varus deformity, occurs predominantly in the 
early postoperative phase before plateauing [ 23, 35]. Chen 
et al. reported that most shortening occurs within the first 
1–3 months after fixation, whereas Polat et al. observed a 
median onset at 30 days, supporting the concept that clini -
cally relevant collapse represents an early mechanical phe -
nomenon rather than late loss of reduction [23, 35].
Therefore, the early decrease in the NSA observed in 
our study is more plausibly attributable to the mechanical 
settling of an intrinsically unstable intracapsular fracture 
under physiological load. Fracture characteristics such as 
high Pauwels angles, medial or posteromedial comminu -
tion, and residual varus alignment are well-established risk 
factors for early mechanical compromise and femoral neck 
shortening [ 13, 16, 23, 36, 37]. Accordingly, the greater 
early varus change observed in the open reduction group 
most likely reflects the preferential selection of more unsta-
ble fracture patterns rather than a detrimental effect of the 
open approach itself. Although concerns have been raised 
regarding greater soft-tissue disruption with open reduction, 
current evidence does not demonstrate a higher incidence 
of non-union or osteonecrosis attributable to open reduc -
tion and internal fixation [16, 38]. When performed through 
an anterior approach with a planned capsulotomy, such as 
the Smith–Petersen approach, preservation of the ascend -
ing cervical arterioles supplying the femoral head is achiev-
able, supporting the concept that osteonecrosis is primarily 
related to the initial fracture insult rather than to surgical 
exposure itself [39, 40]. Taken together, our findings suggest 
that the choice between closed and open reduction should be 
guided by fracture morphology and the ability to achieve 
stable anatomical reduction, rather than by the expectation 
of superior outcomes with either approach alone.
Despite efforts to minimize bias, this study had several 
limitations. First, the retrospective design carries the inher -
ent risks of selection and information bias, and the small 
single-center cohort may limit the generalizability of our 
findings. Furthermore, the reviewers were not fully blinded 
to the surgical approach because the implant configura -
tion was visible on the radiographs. Using 60 years as the 
upper age limit introduced greater heterogeneity in injury 
mechanisms and fracture characteristics, including a higher 
proportion of lower-energy injuries in older patients within 
the cohort. This may have influenced the treatment selec -
tion and complication profiles despite the subgroup analysis 
of Pauwels type III fractures. The modest sample size also 
limited the statistical power to identify independent risk fac-
tors after stratification by surgical approach. Because of the 
relatively small sample size and unequal group distribution, 
some analyses required non-parametric statistical methods, 
which may have reduced statistical sensitivity. In addi -
tion, the involvement of multiple orthopedic surgeons may 
have introduced variability related to differences in surgi -
cal technique and decision-making. The decision to perform 
open or closed reduction was not randomized. Therefore, 
selection bias is inherent. Although all procedures were 
performed or supervised by attending orthopedic trauma 
surgeons, surgeon-related variability in fixation technique 
and implant configuration could not be fully assessed in this 
retrospective study and may have influenced the postop -
erative outcomes. Radiographic assessment is the primary 
method for evaluating reduction quality and postoperative 
alignment; however, more precise modalities, such as com-
puted tomography, are not routinely available. Variations 
in patient positioning during radiographic acquisition may 
also have affected the angular measurements. Although a 
standardized postoperative weight-bearing rehabilitation 
protocol was recommended, actual compliance could not be 
objectively confirmed in every patient. Therefore, prema -
ture weight bearing may have contributed to early mechani-
cal failure in some cases. Future prospective multicenter 
studies with larger sample sizes and standardized imaging 
protocols, including advanced cross-sectional imaging, are 
warranted to better define the outcome determinants and 
validate our findings.
In conclusion in young patients with DFNFs, fracture 
morphology and patient age were more influential determi -
nants of outcomes than the choice between closed and open 
reduction. Open reduction achieved a higher proportion of 
excellent reductions but did not reduce complication rates 
compared with closed reduction, highlighting the impor -
tance of fracture morphology-guided fixation and realistic 
patient counseling.
