Abstract
Biological systems under chronic resource overload often exhibit asymmetric transitions into high-load states that are dif -
ficult to reverse. Within a bow-tie (hourglass) framework, such dynamics arise when diverse inputs are funnelled through 
a constrained regulatory core governing system-level responses. Here, lake eutrophication and human obesity are analysed 
as structurally distinct yet dynamically analogous manifestations of resource overload. In lakes, external nutrient inputs 
and internal biogeochemical feedbacks drive shifts from clear-water, macrophyte-dominated regimes to hypertrophic, 
phytoplankton-dominated states. In humans, sustained caloric surplus interacts with metabolic–hormonal regulation and 
behavioural–social drivers to promote the development and stabilisation of obesity. In both systems, reinforcing feedbacks 
organise around a central regulatory core, reducing flexibility, generating hysteresis, and constraining recovery trajectories. 
Despite these similarities, key asymmetries emerge. In lakes, dynamics under overload are dominated by a limited set of 
reinforcing feedbacks, whereas in obesity regulation remains distributed across interacting physiological, behavioural, and 
environmental domains. In both cases, responses involve cascade-like propagation of effects, taking the form of trophic 
cascades in lakes and cross-domain feedback cascades in humans. These results show that similar system-level dynamics, 
including alternative stable states, tipping points, and hysteresis with constrained reversibility, can arise from differently 
structured regulatory architectures. The comparison demonstrates that reversibility is system-specific and shaped by the 
organisation of the regulatory core and associated feedbacks. Interpreting eutrophication and obesity through a bow-tie 
framework provides a comparative, architecture-based perspective on resource overload and helps explain why effective 
interventions require coordinated actions targeting multiple components of the feedback structure.

Introduction
In contemporary science, increasing specialization limits 
the integration of knowledge across disciplines and hinders 
the identification of general patterns in complex biological 
systems (Holling 2001). Conceptual comparisons address 
this limitation not by asserting equivalence between sys -
tems, but by revealing how different regulatory structures 
can generate similar dynamical behaviours (Olson et al. 
2019).

(Wilborn et al. 2005; Hall 2023; Sorensen 2023). In both 
cases, system functioning becomes progressively restricted 
as resource excess alters regulatory processes; however, the 
underlying mechanisms and the extent of hysteresis differ, 
reflecting differences in regulatory organization.
Bow-tie architecture provides a unifying framework 
for analysing these differences. In such systems, diverse 
inputs are funnelled through a constrained regulatory core 
that generates multiple outputs (Csete and Doyle 2004; 
Kitano 2004). This structure supports robust functioning 
under moderate conditions but creates vulnerability under 
sustained overload: processing capacity becomes saturated, 
control concentrates within a limited set of pathways, and 
reinforcing feedbacks stabilise high-load states and limit 
recovery trajectories. Existing environmental management 
frameworks such as DPSIR and DAPSI(W)R(M) success -
fully organise pressures, states, and responses (Elliott et al. 
2017), but are predominantly applied within single domains, 
and do not explicitly represent the internal regulatory cores, 
defined here as constrained sets of processes that integrate 
diverse inputs and generate system-level responses.
Here, the bow-tie architecture provides a conceptual 
framework for organising known drivers, feedbacks, and 
state transitions, without relying on explicit network recon-
struction. Eutrophication and obesity are analysed as sys -
tem-specific manifestations of resource overload acting on 
central regulatory cores. The aim is not to equate lakes with 
humans, but to show that similar system-level dynamics, 
including alternative stable states, tipping-point behaviour, 
and hysteresis with constrained reversibility, can arise from 
differently organised regulatory architectures. Asymmetries 
in reversibility reflect differences in regulatory organisation.
Conceptual background
The analysis draws on core concepts from complex systems 
research, focusing on how regulatory organization shapes 
system dynamics rather than on specific mechanisms. Feed-
backs are processes in which system outputs influence 
subsequent dynamics, either reinforcing or counteracting 
change (Holling 1973). Within this framework, resilience 
denotes the capacity to maintain functional state under 
perturbation, while hysteresis describes path-dependent 
responses in which recovery requires different conditions 
than those that triggered system change (Beisner et al. 2003; 
Scheffer et al. 2009).
Regulatory architecture refers to the organization of inter-
acting components and feedbacks that delimit the range of 
attainable system states. It is conceptualized here as a bow-
tie structure in which diverse inputs are funnelled through 
a constrained regulatory core and give rise to multiple out -
puts (Csete and Doyle 2004; Kitano 2004). In simple terms, 
bow-tie architectures can be understood as “many inputs – a 
constrained core – many outputs”.
This organization entails a trade-off between robustness 
and vulnerability. Concentrating regulation in a core can 
stabilize system functioning under moderate conditions, but 
sustained overload can saturate processing capacity, chan -
nel dynamics into a limited set of reinforcing feedbacks, and 
reduce alternative regulatory pathways. Under such condi -
tions, systems may shift into high-load states stabilized by 
positive feedbacks and characterized by hysteresis.
Here, the bow-tie concept is used at the level of regula -
tory architecture rather than specific mechanisms to char -
acterize the organization of mechanisms around a central 
regulatory layer. The focus is not on process equivalence, 
but on shared architectural properties: a narrow core that 
stabilizes normal functioning yet, under chronic overload, 
promotes feedback-dominated states with hysteresis and 
limited reversibility.
Lake and human body as ecosystems
Lakes are classical examples of ecosystems, consisting 
of interacting biological communities and their physical 
environment within a defined spatial unit, shaped by catch -
ment-driven inputs of water and nutrients (Harper 1992; 
Henderson 2015).
More recently, the ecosystem concept has been extended 
to the human body, which comprises multiple heterogeneous 
habitats differing in physical and nutritional conditions and 
hosting diverse microbial communities (Robinson et al. 
2010; Zhou et al. 2013). In this context, the gut microbiome 
has been implicated in processes associated with obesity 
(Van de Guchte et al. 2020; Wilson et al. 2020).
The human immediate environment (e.g. urban or rural 
setting) can be interpreted as a functional analogue of a lake 
catchment, shaping resource inputs and system dynamics. 
This analogy is structural and functional rather than spa -
tial and does not imply normative, medical, or evaluative 
equivalence. This perspective enables comparison of both 
systems as integrated ecological entities in which structure, 
function, and regulation emerge across multiple scales, 
consistent with broader uses of “health” as a descriptor of 
functional condition in both ecological and biological sys -
tems (Sfara and El-Hani 2023). On this basis, both lakes 
and human bodies can be treated as ecosystems subject to

Indices and classifications
Commonly used indices reduce complex systems to one-
dimensional representations of state. In lakes, trophic sta -
tus is assessed using the Carlson Trophic State Index (TSI; 
Carlson 1977), whereas in humans body mass is represented 
by the body mass index (BMI; Burkhauser and Cawley 
2008). In both cases, system condition is expressed along a 
deficiency–excess gradient, and Table 1 provides a heuris -
tic alignment of trophic categories with body weight classes 
despite fundamental differences in system organisation.
However, such indices compress multidimensional regu-
latory processes into single metrics, obscuring differences 
in regulatory architecture and providing limited insight into 
system dynamics, feedback structure, and responses to per-
turbation. In particular, they mask the stability landscape 
and the feedbacks underlying hysteresis, motivating a shift 
towards an explicit analysis of regulatory architecture.
Regulatory organization and feedback 
dynamics
In complex systems, reinforcing feedbacks can generate 
self-amplifying dynamics that drive systems towards high-
load states. Such processes are well documented in both 
hypertrophication (Harper 1992; Scheffer et al. 1993) and 
obesity (Wilborn et al. 2005; Hall 2023).
These dynamics involve both bottom-up and top-down 
regulation, defined differently across systems. In lake eco -
systems, bottom-up processes are driven by resource avail -
ability (e.g. nutrients), whereas top-down regulation reflects 
trophic control by higher levels (Moss et al. 1994; Stephen 
et al. 1998). In humans, bottom-up processes include meta-
bolic, hormonal, and genetic regulation, while top-down 
processes encompass behavioural, social, and environmen -
tal influences, including broader institutional and cultural 
drivers such as public-health policies and social norms 
(Pietiläinen et al. 2008; Luo and Lin 2021).
Despite these parallels, the organization of feedbacks dif-
fers between systems and can be described within a bow-tie 
architecture, in which diverse inputs are processed through 
a central regulatory core (Csete and Doyle 2004). In lakes, 
this core is primarily biogeochemical; in humans, it is phys-
iological. Under sustained overload, dynamics may become 
dominated by a limited set of reinforcing feedbacks, with 
alternative regulatory pathways progressively reduced.
Bow-tie architectures stabilise system functioning under 
moderate conditions but, under sustained overload, pro -
mote feedback-dominated dynamics and regime shifts. This 
behaviour aligns with resilience theory (Holling 1973; Beis-
ner et al. 2003), in which systems may cross critical thresh-
olds and shift into alternative stable states. Differences in 
the nature of the core influence persistence and recovery 
potential, and the following sections examine these system-
specific mechanisms in detail.
Eutrophication and the state of hypertrophy
The ecological consequences of eutrophication, including 
algal blooms and oxygen depletion, have been recognised 
since early limnological studies (Hasler 1947). Elevated 
trophic status in aquatic ecosystems is primarily driven 
by increased nutrient inputs from the watershed (Harper 
1992; Henderson 2015; Kornijów 2024). Although nutrient 
enrichment may occur under natural conditions, anthropo -
genic inputs, such as deforestation, wastewater discharge, 
and fertiliser use, are the dominant drivers of eutrophication 
(Table 2).

top-down mechanisms (Fig. 2). The decline of predatory 
fish weakens top-down control, allowing zooplanktivorous 
fish to increase, thereby reducing zooplankton grazing on 
phytoplankton and amplifying algal blooms (Stephen et al. 
1998). This transition reflects a structural reorganisation in 
which distributed interactions are replaced by a limited set 
of dominant feedbacks, including trophic cascade effects 
that amplify phytoplankton dominance (Carpenter et al. 
1985). Under hypertrophic conditions, system dynamics 
become dominated by a small set of reinforcing feedbacks, 
while alternative regulatory pathways are reduced.
This shift reflects a reduction in regulatory diversity, 
with control concentrated within a constrained regulatory 
core, consistent with bow-tie organization. These coupled 
feedbacks stabilise a hypertrophic state characterised by 
persistent cyanobacterial dominance. Consequently, cya -
nobacterial blooms, oxygen depletion, hydrogen sulphide 
formation, and toxin production (e.g. microcystins) degrade 
habitat quality and pose risks to aquatic organisms and 
humans (Barica 1980; Brooks et al. 2016; Le Moal et al.

Consequently, biodiversity, ecosystem functioning, and 
service provision are substantially reduced (Table 4). In 
hypertrophic systems, feedbacks become so strong that 
additional nutrient inputs produce limited further structural 
change but accelerate internal accumulation processes.

Weight gain and obesity
Processes underlying obesity follow a sequence analogous 
to that observed in eutrophication, linking resource input, 
system response, feedback amplification, and system-level 
consequences. Obesity develops under sustained positive 
energy balance, where energy intake chronically exceeds 
physiological requirements (Wilborn et al. 2005; Hall 2023; 
Sorensen 2023). The magnitude and trajectory of weight 
gain depend not only on energy intake but also on individ -
ual properties, including genetic predisposition, metabolic 
state, and physiological regulation.
Conceptually, the bottom-up domain captures individual-
level metabolic and behavioural feedbacks sustaining adi -
posity, whereas the top-down domain captures social and 
structural feedbacks that create and maintain obesogenic 
environments. At the physiological level, bottom-up pro -
cesses involve metabolic, hormonal, and neuroendocrine 
regulation: hormonal dysregulation, including leptin and 
insulin resistance, reinforces appetite and energy storage, 
generating feedback loops that promote further weight gain 
(Table 2; Fig. 3).
Top-down regulation involves behavioural, social, and 
environmental drivers, including food availability, lifestyle 
patterns, and socioeconomic conditions (Kaisari et al. 2019; 
Sarvari et al. 2022). These factors shape energy intake, 
physical activity, and stress responses, while social feed -
backs such as stigma and chronic stress further reinforce 
behavioural and metabolic patterns (Tables 2 and 3; Fig. 4). 
This framing emphasizes system-level feedbacks linking 
behaviour, physiology, and environment rather than simple, 
one-directional individual-level causation. These dynamics 
involve cross-scale feedback cascades, where metabolic, 
behavioural, and environmental processes reinforce one 
another, amplifying and stabilising the obese state.

by a limited set of reinforcing feedbacks, regulatory pro -

configuration reflects a distinct form of bow-tie architecture, 
in which multiple feedback pathways remain active despite 
the concentration of control within a central regulatory core.
Although obesity is associated with substantial physi -
ological and metabolic consequences, its functional effects 
are heterogeneous and not uniformly expressed as system-
level dysfunction. Unlike hypertrophic lakes, which typi -
cally exhibit impaired ecosystem functioning, systemic 
overload in humans does not consistently produce overt 
dysfunction. This asymmetry reflects differences in regula -
tory architecture and is central to the comparison developed 
here.
Theories of alternative stable states and the set 
point of body weight
Transitions between alternative states are typically asymmet-
ric: shifts towards hypertrophic phytoplankton dominance 
occur more readily than transitions back to oligotrophic or 
mesotrophic macrophyte-dominated conditions, as reinforc-
ing feedbacks stabilize the turbid state and narrow the range 
of attainable system states, thereby increasing the threshold 
for a reverse regime shift (Scheffer and Carpenter 2003). 
These transitions emerge from interactions between slow 
pressures and threshold events that reorganise feedback 
structure and system dynamics (Fig. 5). This process can 
be described by distinguishing key components of regime 
shifts, namely slow drivers, triggering events, shifts in the 
regulatory core, and reinforcing feedbacks stabilising the 
new state, as summarised in Table 5.
Applying the alternative stable states framework to 
body weight regulation, two contrasting states can be dis -
tinguished: lean and obese. These states are maintained by 
stabilising physiological and behavioural feedbacks. Sus -
tained positive energy balance can push the system beyond 
a threshold, triggering a transition to obesity that is stabi -
lised by reinforcing feedbacks. As in lake ecosystems, these 
transitions are asymmetric, because feedback mechanisms 
constrain shifts towards lower adiposity. For analytical pur-
poses, both lake ecosystems and human metabolic regula -
tion are represented as transitions between contrasting states 
(clear vs. turbid; lean vs. obese). This abstraction highlights 
threshold dynamics and hysteresis, while recognising that 
real systems may exhibit intermediate or heterogeneous 
configurations. The theory of alternative stable states has a 
clear conceptual parallel in set-point models of body-weight 
regulation. These models propose that body mass is regu -
lated around a relatively stable range maintained by physi -
ological feedbacks, which resist sustained deviations and 
contribute to relapse after weight loss (Keesey and Hirvonen 
1997; Speakman et al. 2011; Speakman and Hall 2023).
Contemporary formulations incorporate dynamic equi -
librium and dual intervention points, while retaining the 
core idea that regulatory feedbacks stabilise body weight 
within a limited range (Speakman et al. 2011; Hall 2023).

Remedial actions – restoration and 
treatment
Restoration of degraded hypertrophic lakes primarily tar -
gets the reduction of phosphorus and nitrogen availabil -
ity (Table 6). Measures such as oxygenation, sediment 
removal, phosphorus inactivation, and biomanipulation 
aim to weaken reinforcing feedbacks, promote macrophyte 
recovery, and re-establish trophic control (Shapiro et al. 
1975; Moss et al. 1996; Jeppesen et al. 2007; Tammeorg et 
al. 2024).
However, restoration is often slow, costly, and only par -
tially effective. Feedbacks maintaining the hypertrophic 
state increase the threshold for reverse regime shifts, making 
long-term recovery uncertain; interventions rarely restore 
the original state and may introduce additional disturbances 
(Søndergaard et al. 2007). This reflects a general constraint 
imposed by feedback architecture: recovery is limited when 
reinforcing feedbacks stabilise high-load states and control 
is distributed across multiple components. Accordingly, 
both lake restoration and obesity treatment require multi-
pronged interventions targeting different elements of the 
feedback network (Table 6).
In humans, dietary, behavioural, pharmacological, and 
surgical interventions aim to reduce energy intake or alter 
components of the regulatory system, but long-term weight 
loss is difficult to sustain. Physiological and behavioural 
feedbacks resist change and promote relapse, consistent 
with models of body weight regulation (Wilborn et al. 2005; 
Speakman et al. 2011; Sorensen 2023). 
These limitations reflect system-specific constraints on 
recovery. In lakes, recovery is primarily limited by exter -
nal nutrient legacies and internal loading, whereas in obe -
sity it is shaped by intrinsic regulatory features, including 
physiological set-points and behavioural feedbacks. In both 
systems, single interventions are often insufficient, as feed -
backs are distributed around a capacity-limited regulatory 
core, allowing high-load states to persist and limiting sus -
tained recovery.
Conceptual synthesis: a bow-tie framework 
for cross-system dynamics
Regulatory organization in complex biological systems can 
be described by a bow-tie architecture (also referred to as an 
hourglass architecture in systems biology), in which diverse 
inputs are funnelled through a central core that integrates 
mass, energy, and information and generates a wide range 
of outputs (Csete and Doyle 2004; Kitano 2004). While 
this structure supports efficient and robust functioning, it 
also creates structural vulnerability. Sustained overload of 
the core can saturate processing capacity, concentrate con -
trol within a limited set of pathways, and amplify positive 
feedbacks, reducing compensatory responses and allowing 
perturbations to propagate system-wide, thereby stabilising 
high-load states.
Within this framework, chronic overload can reorganise 
feedback structure and generate transitions into alternative 
stable states (Beisner et al. 2003; Scheffer and Carpenter 
2003). These dynamics involve cascade-like propagation 
of effects across system components, although the struc -
ture of such cascades differs between systems. In lakes, 
they include trophic cascades across discrete trophic lev -
els, whereas in obesity they take the form of cross-domain 
feedback cascades linking physiological, behavioural, and 
environmental processes.
The comparison between lake eutrophication and human 
obesity highlights a cross-domain alignment of key sys -
tem components, linking state variables (trophic status vs. 
adiposity), control parameters (nutrient loading vs. caloric 
surplus), and feedback mechanisms (biogeochemical vs. 
metabolic–hormonal). This alignment provides a basis for 
comparing system dynamics without assuming direct equiv-
alence between underlying mechanisms.

external nutrient legacies and internal loading, whereas in

asymmetry implies that resilience is not a uniform property 
but depends on which variables are regulated and against 
which perturbations.
Finally, similar feedback-dominated states can arise 
from differently structured regulatory hierarchies: in lakes, 
dynamics are largely governed by bottom-up processes, 
whereas in obesity they are more tightly coupled to higher-
level top-down drivers. This comparison illustrates how 
similar dynamical patterns can emerge from distinct regula-
tory architectures.
Implications for management and policy
Single-target interventions often fail because feedbacks are 
distributed around a central regulatory core. In both eutro -
phication and obesity, reinforcing feedbacks cluster around 
this core; interventions that address only one pathway may 
leave others intact, allowing the system to persist in a high-
load state or return to it following disturbance. Effective 
management therefore requires coordinated actions target -
ing multiple components of the feedback architecture.
This perspective complements existing environmental 
management frameworks such as DPSIR and DAPSI(W)
R(M) by shifting attention from pressures alone to the orga-
nization of regulatory processes. Interventions can thus be 
evaluated not only in terms of reducing external inputs, but 
also in terms of their capacity to modify feedback structure 
and restore regulatory flexibility.
From this viewpoint, effective strategies should aim to 
weaken reinforcing feedbacks, restore alternative pathways, 
and increase system responsiveness. This implies combin -
ing measures that act on external drivers with those that 
modify internal regulation and behavioural or structural 
processes, thereby addressing different components of the 
regulatory architecture.
The distinction between bottom-up and top-down pro -
cesses is used analogically across systems: in lakes, it 
refers to trophic and resource-driven regulation, whereas 
in humans it encompasses physiological, behavioural, and 
socio-environmental influences.

Conclusions
This study shows that sustained resource overload acting 
on central regulatory cores can generate similar dynamical 
patterns across distinct biological systems. Within a bow-tie 
framework, eutrophication in lakes and obesity in humans 
can be understood as manifestations of feedback-driven 
dynamics, including alternative stable states, hysteresis, and 
asymmetric transitions.
Similar system-level behaviour arises from structurally 
different regulatory architectures, and resilience depends 
on system-specific organisation rather than being a uniform 
property. This perspective distinguishes general principles 
of complex system dynamics from system-specific mecha -
nisms and highlights regulatory architecture as a key object 
of theoretical and empirical inquiry.
These results indicate that shared dynamics emerge not 
from common mechanisms, but from how regulatory archi-
tecture structures system responses to sustained resource 
overload. These differences imply that systems with more 
distributed regulatory architectures should exhibit slower 
but more persistent constraints on recovery, whereas sys -
tems with compressed feedback structures should exhibit 
sharper but more externally constrained reversibility.
