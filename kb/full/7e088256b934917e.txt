Abstract
Rectal suction biopsy is an established diagnostic procedure for suspected Hirschsprung disease, but reliable
performance depends on having multiple pieces of equipment, consumables, specimen containers, and
documentation immediately available. In our paediatric surgical clinical area, the required items were stored
in different locations, resulting in avoidable searching, workflow interruptions, and the possibility of
missing essential equipment during preparation. A quality improvement (QI) project was undertaken to
standardize preparation through a dedicated, clearly labelled rectal suction biopsy equipment box. A
procedure-specific checklist was developed, the box was placed in an agreed accessible location, and staff
were oriented to its use and the restocking process. The intervention was trialled for more than one month
and evaluated using an anonymous staff survey and observational feedback. Contemporaneous project
documentation recorded improved immediate equipment availability, reduced time spent searching for
items, improved workflow efficiency, and high staff satisfaction. Sustainability measures included a contents
checklist inside the box, assigned responsibility for restocking, and a permanent storage location. The
original response-level survey dataset and objective timing measurements were not available for re-analysis
when this report was prepared; therefore, no percentages, p-values, or numerical effect estimates are
presented. This low-cost intervention provides a reproducible framework for standardizing preparation for a
specialized paediatric surgical procedure. Future cycles should prospectively record preparation time,
missing-item events, restocking failures, cost, and sustained compliance.

Introduction
Hirschsprung disease is a congenital disorder of the enteric nervous system for which diagnosis depends on
representative rectal histology. Rectal suction biopsy is a minimally invasive technique in which suction is
used to obtain small samples of rectal mucosa and submucosa for histopathological assessment, allowing
evaluation for the absence of ganglion cells and associated diagnostic features. Because an adequate
specimen must contain sufficient submucosa, reliable preparation of the biopsy device, specimen
containers, consumables, and documentation is important before the procedure begins. Updated European
Reference Network guidance continues to place rectal biopsy at the centre of diagnostic confirmation, with
biopsy technique and specimen adequacy remaining important components of the diagnostic pathway 
[1]
. A
systematic review and meta-analysis of paediatric rectal biopsy techniques found that suction biopsy
provides a high proportion of conclusive samples with a very low pooled complication rate 
[2]
.
For a focused procedure such as rectal suction biopsy, technical success is influenced not only by the biopsy
device and operator but also by the reliable preparation of consumables, specimen containers, forms, and
supporting equipment. Equipment standardization has been used successfully in paediatric surgery to
reduce costs, reduce variation, and improve perceived efficiency. Standardized laparoscopic appendectomy
equipment reduced operative supply costs without worsening operative outcomes 
[3]
, while standardization
of paediatric inguinal hernia instrument sets substantially reduced tray size and processing time 
[4]
.
The local problem in our unit was operational rather than diagnostic: the equipment required for rectal
suction biopsy was stored in multiple locations. Staff therefore had to collect individual items before each
procedure, sometimes leaving the immediate clinical area to locate them. This introduced avoidable delays
and the possibility that an item might be omitted during preparation. Similar hospital Quality Improvement
(QI) work has shown that procedure-specific equipment organization can reduce the time spent gathering
equipment 
[5,6]
.
The aim of this project was to improve the immediate availability of essential rectal suction biopsy
equipment and reduce avoidable preparation delays by introducing a dedicated standardized equipment box.

Local problem and aim
Before the intervention, equipment required for rectal suction biopsy was distributed across several storage
locations. Staff reported time lost searching for basic items, an occasional need to leave the clinical area,
and concern that essential equipment could be missed. The project aim was to improve immediate
equipment availability and reduce preparation delays by creating a single standardized source for the
routinely required items. A fishbone/root-cause analysis was constructed from the documented pre-

Intervention design
A dedicated rectal suction biopsy box was assembled using the locally established workflow. The box was
clearly labelled, stored in a designated accessible location, and accompanied by a checklist. Staff were
informed of its location, use, and restocking process. The intervention did not change the indication for
biopsy, the biopsy technique, or the clinical diagnostic pathway; it reorganized the preparation process
around a single procedure-specific equipment source. The standardized box contents are listed in Table

Governance and sustainability
The project was registered on the hospital QI portal and evaluated a local service-process intervention using
anonymous staff feedback. No patient-identifiable data were collected for this evaluation. Sustainability
measures consisted of a contents checklist inside the box, assigned responsibility for restocking, and an
agreed permanent storage location. A re-audit at three to six months was planned to evaluate sustained
performance.
Discussion
This project addressed a recurrent systems problem by reorganizing the equipment required for a specialized
paediatric surgical procedure into a single standardized preparation source. The intervention was
deliberately simple: it did not change clinical decision-making or biopsy technique but reduced workflow
variation around preparation.
The rationale is supported by published paediatric surgical work. Avansino JR et al. demonstrated that
standardization of operative equipment for laparoscopic appendectomy reduced supply costs by
approximately 20% without adversely affecting operative time, length of stay, or intraoperative
complications 
[3]
. Koyle MA et al. used improvement-science methodology to reduce and standardize
instrumentation for paediatric inguinal hernia repair, shortening instrument-processing time and receiving
favourable staff feedback regarding efficiency and quality 
[4]
. These studies support standardization as a
legitimate target for service improvement in paediatric surgery.
The intervention also aligns closely with QI work outside the operating theatre. Karapinar Y et al. described
procedure-specific trays and treatment-room standardization to improve equipment-gathering efficiency
[5]
. More recently, Glover L et al. reported a QI project in which reorganizing venepuncture and cannulation
equipment markedly reduced average gathering times after successive PDSA cycles 
[6]
. The common
principle is that a predictable equipment layout reduces unnecessary searching and cognitive load.
The main strengths of the present project are its low cost, direct relevance to day-to-day paediatric surgical
practice, ease of replication, and explicit sustainability plan. A procedure-specific contents list allows the
intervention to be reproduced or adapted in other units. The project also involved both medical and nursing
users through anonymous feedback rather than relying only on the opinion of the project lead.
The principal limitation is the absence of retained raw outcome data. Although the contemporaneous project
documentation records improved equipment availability, reduced searching, better workflow, and high
satisfaction, the magnitude of change cannot now be calculated. The planned process measure of
preparation time was not available for analysis. This prevents statistical testing and limits causal inference.
In addition, the post-intervention questionnaire asked staff to compare their current experience with the
pre-intervention state, introducing recall and response bias. Future survey responses should therefore be
collected immediately after individual rectal suction biopsy procedures rather than relying on retrospective
comparison.
A second limitation is that patient-level outcomes were not measured. It would therefore be inappropriate to
claim that the intervention directly improved patient safety, diagnostic yield, or complication rates. The
defensible conclusion is that the intervention standardized preparation and reduced a recognized source of
workflow friction. The next cycle should prospectively record preparation time for each procedure, missing-
item events, occasions when staff leave the clinical area to retrieve equipment, restocking failures,
maintenance costs, and staff satisfaction collected immediately after the procedure (Appendix 1). A run
chart across consecutive biopsies would allow the effect and sustainability of the intervention to be
quantified more convincingly.
SQUIRE 2.0 emphasizes transparent reporting of context, intervention, study of the intervention, and
limitations 
[7]
. In keeping with that approach, missing quantitative source data are explicitly reported rather
than reconstructed. This strengthens research integrity even though it reduces the amount of numerical
analysis that can be presented.
Conclusions
A dedicated rectal suction biopsy equipment box provided a simple and reproducible method for
standardizing preparation for a specialized paediatric surgical procedure. The intervention brought routinely
required equipment into one accessible location and was associated, according to contemporaneous QI
documentation, with improved perceived equipment availability, less time spent searching, better workflow
efficiency, and high staff satisfaction. These outcome findings were derived primarily from subjective,
qualitative staff feedback rather than prospectively measured quantitative endpoints. A contents checklist,
assigned responsibility for restocking, and a permanent storage location were introduced to support
sustained use.
