Abstract
Drought is an increasingly important constraint to sustainable sugarcane production 
because prolonged water deficit can impair root development, nutrient acquisition, 
photosynthesis, biomass accumulation, sucrose accumulation, and ultimately 
cane and sugar yield. Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) have emerged 
as a potential soil-based strategy for improving sugarcane performance under 
water-limited conditions through multiple direct and indirect mechanisms. This 
review critically evaluates the current evidence regarding PGPR-mediated drought 
mitigation in sugarcane, with special emphasis on nutrient acquisition, root-system 
development, phytohormonal regulation, ACC deaminase activity, biological nitrogen 
fixation, siderophore and extracellular polymeric substance production, antioxidant 
responses, and pathogen suppression. Available evidence suggests that PGPR can 
improve intermediate traits such as root growth, nutrient status, photosynthetic 
performance, water relations, and oxidative-stress regulation; however, the extent and 
consistency of these responses are highly variable among microbial strains, sugarcane 
genotypes, drought severity and duration, soil properties, climatic conditions, and 
management practices. Importantly, positive responses observed under laboratory, 
pot, and greenhouse conditions cannot be assumed to translate directly into 
stable yield benefits under field conditions. Severe or prolonged drought may 
restrict microbial survival, metabolic activity, root exudation, nutrient diffusion, and 
rhizosphere colonization, thereby reducing the effectiveness of inoculants. Evidence 
for several proposed mechanisms, including microbial hormone production, zinc, 
silicon, and sulphur mobilization, pathogen suppression under combined drought, 
and post-drought recovery, remains limited or indirect in sugarcane. Similarly, 
microbial consortia should not be assumed to be synergistic without appropriate 
comparative and factorial validation. Major priorities, therefore, include multi-location 
and multi-season field trials, standardized quantitative measurements of microbial 
persistence and plant responses, genotype- and soil-specific inoculant selection, 
longitudinal assessment across drought and recovery phases, and evaluation of 
economic feasibility and formulation stability. Overall, PGPR represents a promising

 1 Introduction
Global climate change, together with the intensification of agricultural practices, has 
amplified both abiotic and biotic stressors, posing serious threats to global crop produc -
tivity and agroecosystem sustainability [ 72, 217, 249]. Among abiotic stresses, drought 
stress is widely recognized as one of the most severe constraints limiting global agricul -
tural productivity that leads to significant reductions in crop productivity, particularly 
under prolonged water-deficit conditions coupled with high temperatures [ 195]. Cli -
mate projections indicate that drought-affected croplands may substantially expand by 
2050, accompanied by significant declines in freshwater availability [ 79]. Drought stress 
severely affects plant physiology by impairing chlorophyll biosynthesis, reducing rela -
tive water content, limiting stomatal conductance, and restricting carbon assimilation, 
thereby leading to diminished biomass production and yield reductions in several crop -
ping systems, including sugarcane [11, 108, 148]. At the molecular level, drought triggers 
complex regulatory responses involving differential expression of genes associated with 
abscisic acid (ABA) signaling, osmotic regulation, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) 
detoxification mechanisms [223, 241].
Sugarcane ( Saccharum spp.) is a major industrial crop cultivated in more than 110 
countries, with global production exceeding 2.0 billion metric tons in 2023, and Brazil 
and India together contributing more than half of the global output [ 80, 81]. Owing to 
its long growth duration, high biomass accumulation, and substantial water and nutrient 
requirements, sugarcane is particularly vulnerable to drought stress [84, 205]. Water-def-
icit conditions impair sugarcane root development, nutrient acquisition, stalk elonga -
tion, photosynthetic activity, and sucrose accumulation, often causing yield reductions 
of 50–70% depending on genotype, growth stage, and stress severity [ 138]. In addition 
to directly affecting plant physiology, prolonged drought alters rhizosphere microbial 
dynamics and nutrient cycling, thereby influencing plant-microbe interactions that 
may contribute to sugarcane drought adaptation [ 58]. Therefore, improving sugarcane 
resilience under water-limited conditions requires strategies that support plant growth, 
nutrient acquisition, and beneficial rhizosphere functions.
The use of rhizospheric microorganisms to buffer plants against drought stress is an 
emerging strategy in agriculture [ 31, 155, 203]. Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria 
(PGPR) are safe alternatives to chemical inputs and improve plant nutritional status and 
abiotic stress tolerance by reducing drought stress in different crops through improve -
ments in root architecture, water and nutrient uptake, phytohormone production, 
modulation of stress-responsive signaling, osmotic adjustment, antioxidant defence, 
and restoration of soil microbial diversity. The potential of genera and species like Acti-
nomycetes, Azospirillum brasilense , Bacillus safensis , Burkholderia phytofirmans , Glu -
conacetobacter, and Ochrobactrum pseudogrignonense has been proved in minimizing 
drought stress in various crops [ 126, 141, 142]. In sugarcane, drought management has 
relied mainly on genetic improvement, irrigation scheduling, nutrient optimization, and 
biotechnological approaches, with rhizosphere-based strategies increasingly explored as 
but context-dependent component of integrated drought management in sugarcane 
rather than a universally reliable standalone solution.

This review therefore critically discusses the current evidence for PGPR-mediated 
drought mitigation in sugarcane, including nutrient acquisition, root development and 
physiological responses, phytohormonal control, microbial stress-alleviating mecha -

microbial strains and consortia, sugarcane genotypes, drought severity and duration, 
and experimental conditions. The review also critically addresses inconsistent responses 
across studies and the challenges of extrapolating results from controlled trials to field 
conditions. Finally, research priorities are identified to advance mechanistic understand-
ing, inoculant persistence, and multi-environment validation so as to define the condi -
tions under which PGPR can deliver repeatable benefits to drought-stressed sugarcane 
production systems.
2 Drought stress in sugarcane
Erratic rainfall patterns and prolonged dry spells, intensified by climate change, have 
increasingly exposed many agricultural regions to water-deficit conditions, severely 
impairing crop development and productivity worldwide [ 104, 117, 173]. Projections 
indicate that by 2050, more than half of the world’s arable land may experience severe 
water scarcity, posing a significant threat to global agricultural sustainability and crop 
production [ 112, 194]. The sensitivity of sugarcane to drought largely depends on the 
severity and duration of stress as well as the genotype involved. Drought adversely affects 
several physiological traits, including plant height, leaf area, photosynthetic activity, and 
nitrogen uptake, ultimately leading to significant reductions in sugar yield [ 20, 89]. To 
mitigate these challenges, sugarcane growers are increasingly adopting integrated man -
agement strategies, including drought-tolerant cultivars, efficient irrigation scheduling, 
biofertilizer application, and practices for conserving soil moisture [1, 209].
Under tropical and subtropical irrigated conditions, sugarcane has been reported 
to require approximately 20 million liters of water per hectare during a complete crop 
cycle; however, actual water requirements vary considerably depending on environmen -
tal conditions, crop duration, evapotranspiration demand, and management practices 
[199]. Exposure to drought during critical developmental stages, particularly the tillering 
and grand growth phases, severely limits sugarcane growth and productivity by induc -
ing pronounced morphological changes, including increased tiller mortality, reduced 
cane height, shortened internodal length, decreased cane weight, lower millable cane 
population, reduced juice yield, and ultimately significant yield losses [ 28]. In addition, 
drought stress disrupts key physiological processes, such as photosynthesis, leaf ion 
balance, transpiration rate, cellular turgor, and assimilate partitioning, thereby limiting 
biomass accumulation and overall productivity [ 90, 242]. Consequently, water scarcity 
during peak growth stages not only restricts plant biomass but also reduces sucrose

accumulation, with yield losses reported to reach up to 60% under severe drought condi-
tions [237]. In India, approximately 60–70% of sugarcane cultivation is carried out under 
rainfed or partially irrigated conditions, making the crop highly vulnerable to monsoon 
variability and drought stress [138, 209].
India and Brazil, two of the world’s leading sugarcane-producing countries, share sig -
nificant contributions towards the research and development of sugarcane [ 76]. Brazil is 
at the leading edge of research on the sugarcane microbiome, laying a strong foundation 
for climate-resilient approaches to sugarcane production systems [ 15, 212]. In the same 
vein, both countries have also made great strides in understanding microbial communi -
ties that are associated with sugarcane, biological nitrogen fixation, and microbial inocu-

and innovations from India and Brazil can guide development of a globally applicable 
framework for improving drought resilience and sustainability through sugarcane grow -

60% have been reported, depending on genotype and irrigation management practices 
[2, 134]. Irregular rainfall patterns and declining groundwater availability significantly 
reduce cane yield and sugar recovery [ 9, 47, 96]. Similar drought-induced yield variabil -
ity has also been documented in major sugarcane-producing countries due to frequent 
climatic aberrations [ 243, 254]. For instance, in Brazil, drought stress has been shown 
to significantly reduce cane yield and several yield-related parameters under prolonged 
water-deficit conditions [ 226]. Likewise, rainfed sugarcane cultivation in Thailand fre -
quently encounters water deficits that substantially limit plant growth, biomass accumu-
lation, and yield components under drought conditions [214].
3 Role of PGPRs in drought stress in sugarcane
The application of PGPR presents a sustainable and efficient alternative to mitigate the 
impacts of drought in sugarcane [ 68, 160]. These beneficial microbes support plant 
health through multiple mechanisms, including biological nitrogen fixation, mineral sol-
ubilization, and the production of bioactive compounds such as ammonia and hydrogen 
cyanide [ 25, 193, 225]. Inoculation with these rhizobacteria has been demonstrated to 
stimulate root system development, enhance antioxidant defense capacity, and upregu -
late photosynthesis-related gene expression, collectively improving plant physiologi -
cal performance and drought resilience under water-limited conditions. In sugarcane, 
diverse rhizosphere-associated microbial genera, including Arthrobacter, Microbacte -
rium, Streptomycetales, Bacillus, and some strains of Pseudomonas, have been reported 
to mitigate drought stress by producing phytohormones and stress-alleviating metabo -
lites that improve plant water status and physiological resilience [ 159, 162]. Other gen -
era like Azospirillum, Azotobacter, Burkholderia, Gluconacetobacter, Herbaspirillum, 
and Rhizobium have also shown considerable potential to promote sugarcane growth, 
particularly under water-deficit conditions [ 67, 69]. Under field conditions, PGPR such 
as Azospirillum brasilense, Bacillus subtilis, and Pseudomonas spp., applied individually 
or as microbial consortia, have been reported to enhance nutrient-use efficiency and 
improve sugarcane productivity through mechanisms including mineral solubilization, 
phytohormone production, stimulation of root proliferation, and enhanced rhizosphere 
activity that collectively promote plant growth and nutrient acquisition [ 41, 82]. How-
ever, studies comparing several microbial taxa in analogous environmental conditions

are few and far between, so no known universally better inoculants can be found. Future 
investigations should focus on comparing the candidate PGPR strains and also estab -
lishing multi-location field validations and genotype-specific assessments to confirm 
which of the microbial groups can reliably contribute to agronomic improvements under 
drought-prone sugarcane production systems.
The positive impact of PGPR inoculation on sugarcane performance has been stud -
ied earlier. For instance, Rosa, Galindo [ 177] reported that PGPR application reduced 
the requirement for phosphate fertilizers while simultaneously improving cane yield and 
technological quality parameters. Similarly, Tayade, Geetha [ 211] observed increased 
soil biological activity, enhanced rhizosphere functioning, and improved ratoon cane 
productivity following bio-intensive microbial modulation under tropical Indian con -
ditions. Although many reports have been published on PGPR improving the perfor -
mance of sugarcane under drought, it is difficult to directly quantify the PGPR effect 
in different experiments due to large differences in microbial strains, sugarcane geno -
types, drought intensity, experimental duration, and environmental conditions. Thus, 
the reported results are significantly less likely to be consistent from study to study. 
Quantitative results on specific instances demonstrate that PGPR inoculation resulted 
in better root biomass, chlorophyll content, nutrient uptake, relative water content, and 
cane yield; however, the extent of the changes is varied among experimental settings 
[62]. Therefore, further research should offer similar information on effect magnitude, 
yield response, physiological factors, levels of microbial colonization, and environmental 
parameters. Standardizing these characteristics would enable future meta-analyses and 
help identify microbial strains that provide consistent gains in drought resistance across 
various sugarcane production systems.
The severity, length, and timing of drought stress greatly influence the effectiveness 
of PGPR in mitigating drought. The duration of drought is also important for plant 
and microbial responses. Short-term water deficits can induce adaptive physiological 
responses that may be partially mitigated by hormonal control and osmotic adjustment 
mediated by PGPR. Instead, persistent drought can cause lasting declines in photosyn -
thesis, nitrogen uptake, and microbial activity. Previous studies have demonstrated that 
prolonged drought stress triggers the accumulation of phytohormones and osmolytes, 
leading to adaptive modifications in root architecture and enhanced stress tolerance in 
plants [245]. However, under severe and prolonged drought circumstances, reduced soil 
moisture and root exudates substantially impaired the microbial survival, root coloniza -
tion, and metabolic activity of microorganisms [ 124, 143, 229]. The plant developmental 
stage affects drought responses and the efficiency of PGPR inoculation. Limitations of 
water during germination and the early developmental stages of sugarcane can reduce 
bud sprouting, tillering, root establishment, and canopy expansion. During the grand 
growth stage, it mainly limits stalk elongation, biomass accumulation, and nutrient 
uptake. However, harvest stages might affect sucrose accumulation, cane quality, and 
eventual yield [23, 103].
4 Factors influencing PGPR effectiveness under drought stress in sugarcane
Studies have shown that PGPR can improve the performance of sugarcane under 
drought conditions, although the efficacy of PGPR is strongly impacted by environ -
mental, agronomic, and host factors. This suggests that drought remediation produced

Srivastava et al. Discover Soil (2026) 3:173 
by PGPR should not be considered equivalent to the same response in all production 
systems. However, the intensity and length of positive benefits are highly dependent 
on the severity of drought, crop growth stage, genotype, climatic circumstances, irri -
gation regime, and soil qualities. The efficiency of PGPR usually relies on the inten -
sity of drought. Under moderate water deficiency, microbial inoculation stimulates 
root growth, nutrient uptake, osmotic adjustment, and antioxidant activity and hence 
enhances the performance of plants [10, 19].
The establishment and effectiveness of PGPR may vary with growth stages, since plant 
physiological requirements and root exudation patterns change during crop develop -
ment. PGPR response variants have variability in genetics. Sugarcane cultivars differ 
significantly in root system architecture, rooting depth, water-use efficiency, osmotic 
adjustment capability, and drought tolerance potential. Root cultivars with deeper roots 
and drought-adapted cultivars with larger, deeper moisture reservoirs in the soil are 
more receptive to beneficial rhizosphere microorganisms, bettering root–microbe inter -
actions in many circumstances. Thus, the efficacy of an inoculant in one genotype may 
not be easily transferred to other cultivars with distinct physiological properties [27].
The efficacy of PGPR also depends largely on environmental conditions. Sugarcane 
areas in the tropics usually have higher temperatures and longer growth seasons than 
subtropical areas with somewhat low winter temperatures. Such climatic changes are 
crucial in controlling microbial activity, processes of plant root growth, nitrogen cycling, 
and plant physiological responses to drought [57, 216]. Thus, PGPR strains acclimated to 
an agro-ecological location may show distinct climatic performances in comparison to 
another. Irrigation management can also affect the persistence and efficacy of imported 
microbes, as well as in rainfed systems, variations in soil moisture and the frequency 
of drought episodes may greatly influence microbial population dynamics and rhizo -
sphere activity. However, supplementary watering may favor microbial survival and 
prolong positive plant-microbe interactions [ 10, 19, 144]. Therefore, irrigation regime 
has been emphasized as an important factor to be considered in field-induced inoculant 
performance.
Soil texture and physicochemical parameters also contribute to PGPR-induced 
drought mitigation. Sandy soils are inclined to moisture changes, which have been pro -
posed to impair microbial colonization and survival. Sandy soils have a poorer water-
holding capacity and limit nutrient retention in soil. Clay and loam soils, on the other 
hand, hold moisture and nutrients and tend to provide far more suitable conditions 
to maintain and exploit bacteria. Soil pH, organic matter content, and the indigenous 
microbial communities also affect the establishment and performance of introduced 
PGPR strains [ 55, 143]. These findings collectively indicate that the successful use of 
PGPR in sugarcane farming has to consider drought intensity, crop growth stage, gen -
otype, meteorological conditions, irrigation techniques, soil type, etc. Further investi -
gations are required in future to explore multi-location and multi-season field studies 
under diverse agro-ecosystem-based regimes to find out effective microbial strains and 
to optimize inoculant proposals for specific systems of sugarcane growth.

5 Rhizosphere as a determinant of PGPR efficacy under drought
The efficiency of PGPR under drought is majorly dependent on the rhizosphere. The 
dynamic interface between rhizosphere and microbes involves interaction between 
roots, imported and indigenous microorganisms, and soil physicochemical parameters. 
Drought simultaneously modifies root development and exudation, alters native micro -
bial populations, lowers water-mediated nutrient transfer, and modifies soil pore-water 
distribution [99, 143]. Thus, the success of a PGPR strain is not only related to its func -
tional features but also to its capacity to establish and maintain itself in this drought-
modified environment.
Water deficiency alters root architecture, membrane permeability, and carbon allo -
cation, which influences the quality and amount of root exudates (sugars, amino acids, 
organic acids, and phenolics) [88, 99]. These changes alter the carbon supplies accessible 
to the rhizosphere microorganisms, thus affecting the patterns of microbial recruitment. 
This phenomenon means that PGPR strains, depending on specific substrates released 
by the roots, may behave differently under drought, whereas strains already adapted to 
the changed resource environment may be favored [ 124, 220, 245]. However, there is as 
yet little direct sugarcane data tying specific drought-induced alterations in exudates to 
PGPR formation, unlike other plant systems. Changes in carbon supply due to drought 
affect not just the inoculated strain but normal bacterial and fungal populations. Inocu -
lation thus happens in a pre-existing population of microbes rather than in an empty 
niche, which may help explain why beneficial in vitro PGPR performance typically does 
not translate to soil settings [125, 163, 239].
6 Soil physicochemical properties as determinants of PGPR performance
Soil physicochemical properties provide the physical and chemical framework within 
which these plant–microbe interactions occur. Soil pH affects the solubility of nutrients, 
the growth of microorganisms, enzyme activity, and the chemical forms of nutrients 
available to microorganisms for mobilization. pH affects the fixation of phosphorus, and 
thus the efficiency of organic acid-mediated phosphate solubilization by PGPR can be 
modified [105, 200]. Thus, a strain with high activity in solubilizing phosphate in one soil 
might exert a minor effect in another soil with a different pH and phosphorus chemistry. 
Soil texture and the amount of clay present are also important because they control the 
distribution of pore sizes, the amount of water retained, aeration, diffusion of nutrients, 
and the spatial distribution of the microorganisms. Conversely, clay-rich soils may offer 
increased water retention but may also change nutrient adsorption and microbial acces -
sibility [44].
Soil organic matter supplies carbon substrates to promote microbial development and 
may influence the persistence of introduced inoculants under water-limited environ -
ments. However, availability of organic carbon does not necessarily guarantee effective 
establishment of PGPR, as introduced strains have to fight with indigenous bacteria for 
these resources. Similarly, the clay mineralogy can affect the potassium availability and 
nutrient-buffering capacity and hence modulate the magnitude of responses to potas -
sium-solubilizing PGPR [ 6, 163]. These interactions also reveal why a strain selected for 
high nutrient-solubilization activity under laboratory conditions can operate differently 
when introduced into soils with variable pH, organic matter, texture, mineralogy, and 
nutrient status.

7 Integrated effects of drought, soil properties, and PGPR
The combined effects of drought and soil properties, along with PGPR, can gener -
ate substantial variation in PGPR performance across sugarcane agroecosystems. For 
example, severe drought may simultaneously reduce root activity and alter exudate avail-
ability, restrict microbial movement, decrease nutrient diffusion, alter the native micro -
biome, and increase competition for limited carbon resources [ 13, 99, 109]. Under such 
conditions, a PGPR strain may retain a desirable functional trait in principle but fail to 
express that trait at a level sufficient to influence plant performance [ 150, 164]. Under 
moderate water constraint, by contrast, greater root activity and rhizosphere connectiv -
ity may permit more effective microbial establishment and nutrient mobilization. This 
explains why PGPR responses are often stronger and more reproducible under variable 
drought conditions and their nutrient-solubilizing efficiency can be reduced by decreas -
ing microbial growth, organic acid production, root colonization, and nutrient diffusion 
[190].
Therefore, PGPR efficacy should be evaluated as an emergent property of the plant–
microbe–soil system rather than as an intrinsic characteristic of the inoculant. Future 
sugarcane experiments should simultaneously characterize root exudation, indigenous 
microbial community structure, inoculant abundance and persistence, soil moisture, 
pH, organic matter, texture/clay content, nutrient availability, and plant responses. Com-
parisons across contrasting soils and sugarcane genotypes would be particularly valu -
able for identifying the conditions under which a particular PGPR strain can successfully 
establish and provide reproducible benefits [44, 199]. This systems-level approach would 
also help distinguish true microbial inefficacy from failure of establishment caused by an 
unsuitable rhizosphere environment.
8 Beneficial activities of PGPR
Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) alleviate drought-induced physiological, 
biochemical, and molecular constraints in sugarcane such as stomatal dysregulation, 
reduced photosynthetic efficiency, hormonal imbalance, nutrient uptake limitations, and 
oxidative stress by modulating plant hormonal signaling, enhancing root system archi -
tecture, improving nutrient availability, and stabilizing the rhizosphere environment. 
In the rhizosphere, plant-microbe interactions are effective in aiding crop development 
and mitigating the negative effects of drought on crops through nitrogen fixation, nutri -
ent acquisition by antimicrobial metabolites, phosphorus solubilization, binding of 
microbial biofilms to plant roots, and induction of phytohormone production (Fig.  1) 
[12, 218, 221]. Under water-limited conditions, sugarcane experiences reduced cell tur -
gor, impaired assimilate partitioning, and disruptions in hormone homeostasis. PGPR 
intervenes at these physiological bottlenecks by fine-tuning stress-responsive pathways, 
enabling sugarcane to maintain growth-defense balance. Rather than acting through iso-
lated traits, PGPR function as integrated biological modulators that link drought physi -
ology with microbial-mediated stress alleviation. These effects are broadly categorized 
into direct and indirect mechanisms, which collectively enhance drought resilience.
8.1 Direct mechanisms of PGPR in enhancing sugarcane drought tolerance
PGPR directly influence plant development by producing bioactive compounds and 
facilitating nutrient acquisition in the rhizosphere. Key mechanisms include the

synthesis of phytohormones such as abscisic acid (ABA), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), gib-
berellic acid (GA), and cytokinins, all of which regulate plant physiological responses 
under stress conditions [ 108, 223]. They also enhance nutrient availability by solubiliz -
ing phosphorus, potassium, sulfur, and zinc and by producing siderophores that improve 
iron uptake under deficient conditions [95, 158].
8.1.1 Phytohormone-mediated stress mitigation
PGPR plays a central role in modulating plant hormones, particularly abscisic acid 
(ABA), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), gibberellins (GA), and cytokinins (CK) as key regula -
tors of water stress responses, helping balance growth and stress responses and coor -
dinating root development, water conservation, and stress signaling Punpee [ 166]. 
However, how plants respond to hormones is based on concentration, and most plant 
hormones have optimum levels that often differ from those that induce growth inhibi -
tion or stress effects. For instance, moderate levels of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) typically 
encourage root growth and lateral root production, whereas high IAA levels inhibit root 
growth and change root growth patterns [152, 179]. In addition, small-to-moderate accu-
mulation of ABA controls stomatal regulation and drought adaptation, while prolonged 
or excessive ABA signaling impairs growth and carbon assimilation [ 10, 51]. Cytoki -
nins, gibberellins, and ethylene have shown similar concentration-dependent effects. 
Despite these many reports, relatively few studies have quantified hormone concentra -
tions directly in PGPR-inoculated sugarcane plants during drought. Thus, the effect of 
microbial hormone secretion, plant-level hormone accumulation, and drought tolerance 
is poorly understood. Furthermore, further studies that incorporate hormone quan -
tification, microbial colonization estimation, and physiological validation are required 
to generate biologically plausible thresholds and to establish the role of PGPR-derived 
hormones to help adjust sugarcane to drought conditions. Although PGPR-associated 
production of these hormones has been reported, the concentrations achieved in sugar -
cane rhizospheres under field conditions remain largely unknown, making it difficult to

 • Abscisic Acid
Abscisic acid (ABA) is a key drought-responsive hormone synthesized by plant roots, 
which controls the stomatal opening and closure and produces the first signal during 
plant stress [ 102]. ABA-mediated signaling reduces transpiration losses and enhances 
water-use efficiency while also promoting metabolic adjustment that prioritizes stress 
survival over vegetative growth [ 181, 252]. Exposure to moisture stress rapidly stimu -
lates endogenous abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis in both roots and leaves by activating 
9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase (NCED), the key rate-limiting enzyme governing the 
ABA biosynthetic pathway (Fig. 2) [58, 227].
Molecularly, drought-related abscisic acid (ABA) accumulation activates PYR/PYL/
RCAR receptors, inhibits PP2C phosphatases, and enhances SnRK2 kinase signaling 
pathways, leading to the expression of stress-responsive genes encoding LEA proteins, 
dehydrins, and ROS-scavenging enzymes [ 58, 247]. Transcriptomic analyses of sugar -
cane have shown upregulation of ABA-responsive genes under water deficit, providing 
indications that ABA-dependent signaling plays a conserved role in drought adaptation. 
It has been found that several plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), such as 
Bacillus spp., Azospirillum brasilense , and Pseudomonas spp., can secrete ABA in vitro 
[53, 194]. There is therefore scant direct evidence of the relationship between microbial 
ABA production and the activation of ABA-signaling pathways in drought-stressed sug -
arcane. While the majority of sugarcane studies concentrate on physiological responses, 
including water status, photosynthetic performance, antioxidant activity, and growth

 • Indole-3-Acetic Acid
Sugarcane, a highly water-demanding crop, relies strongly on auxin-mediated root sys -
tem plasticity to sustain water uptake and physiological functions under drought stress 
[210]. Under water-limited conditions, optimal levels of IAA promote primary root 
elongation, lateral root formation, and root hair development, enabling plants to access 
deeper and less-depleted soil moisture reserves. In contrast, excessive auxin accumu -
lation can suppress primary root growth while promoting lateral root proliferation, 
reflecting the dual and context-specific nature of auxin signaling [ 71, 251]. This adap -
tive response is further strengthened by IAA-producing rhizobacteria, which enhance 
nutrient acquisition and water-use efficiency and contribute to sustained stalk biomass 
and yield stability by modulating local auxin gradients in the rhizosphere [ 34, 187, 206]. 
Collectively, the integration of plant - and microbe-derived IAA signaling represents 
an important mechanism contributing to drought tolerance in sugarcane. Rhizobium, 
Azospirillum, Azotobacter , Microbacterium , Mycobacterium , Pseudomonas , Stenotro -
phomonas, Sphingomonas, and Arthrobacter strains are some of the most well-known 
IAA-producing rhizobacterial genera [7, 33]. At the molecular level, auxin signaling dur-
ing drought involves regulation of the AUX/IAA, GH3, and SAUR gene families, which 
interact with abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathways to regulate antioxidant defense and 
cell wall plasticity [181, 247]. The crosstalk between auxin and ABA signaling is increas -
ingly recognized as a key regulatory module coordinating adaptive root remodeling and 
growth regulation under water-deficit conditions (Fig. 3). In sugarcane, drought-induced 
hormonal modulation has been associated with altered root development and the acti -
vation of stress-responsive genes [ 123, 174], further highlighting the integrative role of 
auxin signaling in drought adaptation.
While microbial indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production has been suggested as a prom -
ising path to alleviate drought stress, quantitative data based on IAA in rhizosphere and 
plant tissues from PGPR-inoculated sugarcane are limited. Therefore, the ranges of con -
centrations at which IAA have a beneficial or a harmful effect in sugarcane are poorly 
characterized, and most current interpretations are based on evidence from model 
plants and other crop species instead of data from sugarcane-specific dose–response 
studies. This knowledge gap limits our understanding of the physiological relevance of 
microbial IAA production under drought conditions. IAA also has an extensive interac -
tion with ethylene synthesis. Elevated auxin concentrations can stimulate the expression 
of ACC synthase, the key enzyme involved in the synthesis of 1-aminocyclopropane-
1-carboxylate (ACC), the immediate precursor of ethylene [ 246]. This interaction

highlights the importance of hormonal balance rather than absolute hormone produc -

thereby helping maintain the beneficial effects of auxin-mediated root development dur-
ing drought stress [10, 19, 91].

 • Cytokinin
Cytokinins (CK) play a central role in regulating cell division, shoot growth, and leaf 
development [135, 166]. However, under drought stress, endogenous CK levels typically 
decline, shifting the growth balance toward enhanced root proliferation. Reduced cyto -
kinin levels favor the development of deeper and more extensive root systems, enabling 
plants to access water from deeper soil layers. This adaptive shift is often accompanied 
by increased abscisic acid (ABA) accumulation, which improves stomatal regulation and 
enhances drought resilience [ 61, 119]. Experimental evidence indicates that manipula -
tion of cytokinin levels can significantly influence plant drought responses. Several stud -
ies have shown that exogenous cytokinin application improves plant growth and stress 
tolerance under water-limited conditions in diverse plant species, including sugarcane 
[131], pineapple [ 16], Arabidopsis thaliana [ 207], Kentucky bluegrass [ 100], and rye -
grass [232]. Under moderate water-deficit conditions (approximately 50% field capacity), 
cytokinin-enhanced plants maintained about 30–40% higher chlorophyll content and 
40–50% greater green leaf area [145].
The quantitative estimation of PGPR-derived cytokinins against drought tolerance 
is scarce in sugarcane. One of the significant limitations of current research is that no 
studies currently provide simultaneous quantitative analysis of microbial cytokinin 
production, endogenous cytokinin concentrations, microbial colonization levels, and 
drought-response capacities in sugarcane. Thus, the role of PGPR-derived cytokinins 
in the adaptation to drought in plants is still unknown. While certain bacterial strains 
(such as Bacillus, Azospirillum, Pseudomonas , and Paenibacillus) have been identified 
as capable of producing cytokinin-like compounds in culture-based experiments. Stud -
ies by several researchers indicate that the favorable effects of PGPR for sugarcane in 
drought-stricken conditions may be due to more robust physiological processes in root 
growth, nutrient acquisition, osmotic adaptation, antioxidant and hormonal regulation 
pathways, than only the production of cytokinins (Fig. 5) [41, 86, 147].

 • Phosphorus (P)
In sugarcane, adequate phosphorus supply is crucial for tillering, nutrient absorption, 
and sucrose synthesis [ 42, 123, 156]. A wide range of bacterial genera, including Azo-
tobacter, Bacillus , Pseudomonas , Rhizobium , and Enterobacter, possess phosphate-
solubilizing capabilities. However, the effectiveness of these microbes under drought

conditions in sugarcane is often strain-specific and influenced by environmental factors. 
While many studies observe increases in plant phosphorus concentrations after inocu -
lation with PSB, the responses vary substantially and depend on the microbial strain, 
soil characteristics, phosphorus availability, drought severity, and sugarcane genotype 
[151, 175, 198]. In sugarcane, similar variability has been observed under different soil

improvements in plant P status have been associated with 8–18% increases in cane yield 
and enhanced sucrose content when microbial inoculation was integrated with 75–100% 
of the recommended P fertilizer dose [177, 186].
Despite these benefits, the effectiveness of PSB is strongly influenced by drought 
severity and soil phosphorus availability. In moderate water deficits, microbial growth, 
organic acid production, and root colonization can still be promoted after PSB and thus 
increase phosphorus availability [29, 230]. But during extreme or prolonged drought, the 
metabolic activity of microflora, root growth, and nutrient diffusion can be significantly 
reduced, which reduces the efficiency of phosphate solubilization [ 38, 256]. Thus, PSB-
mediated improvements are more stable under mild-to-moderate drought than under 
extreme water scarcity. Soil phosphorus status is also a significant determinant of the 
effectiveness of phosphate-solubilizing bacteria. Responses are primarily greatest in soils 
containing considerable amounts of insoluble phosphorus that can be mobilized, for 
example, by microbial activity.
 • Potassium (K)
Potassium (K) is an essential macronutrient that plays a critical role in enzyme activa -
tion, osmotic regulation, stomatal movement, photosynthesis, and stress signaling in 
plants [43]. Potassium-solubilizing bacteria (KSB), specifically in the genera Bacillus and 
Pseudomonas, have been described as potential potassium mobilizers playing a crucial 
role in enhancing K bioavailability under water-limited conditions by creating organic 
acids and weathering minerals. The evidence base for K-solubilization-infused drought 
responses is largely derived from wheat, maize, and other cereals, where the inocula -
tion has resulted in higher uptake of potassium, antioxidant potential, and stress toler -
ance [76, 133, 137]. In sugarcane, potassium is required for stomatal regulation, osmotic 
adjustment, carbohydrate translocation, and maintenance of photosynthetic activity, but 
little research is available to confirm directly the role of potassium-solubilizing PGPR 
in these processes under drought conditions. As a result, additional validation of such 
mechanisms identified in other crops, which apply here to sugarcane, must also be 
ensured. Although enhanced potassium nutrition may theoretically facilitate stomatal 
regulation and osmotic adjustment and improve plant water relations during drought 
conditions, their direct measurements remain scarce in sugarcane [ 128, 170, 199]. Col-
lectively, these findings highlight that microbial potassium solubilization represents an 
important biological strategy for alleviating drought-induced nutrient limitations and 
enhancing crop resilience, particularly in water-scarce and nutrient-depleted soils.
 • Zinc (Zn)
Zinc is essential for enzyme function, hormone biosynthesis, and plant metabolism and 
is especially crucial during stress responses. Under drought stress, reduced soil mois -
ture negatively affects zinc mobility by restricting diffusion and mass flow toward roots,

thereby exacerbating Zn deficiency [ 143]. Several plant growth-promoting rhizobacte -
ria (PGPR), including Gluconacetobacter diazotrophicus , Bacillus subtilis , Enterobacter 
cloacae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, have demonstrated zinc-solubilizing capabilities. 
In vitro studies have demonstrated that bacterial isolates have the potential to solubi -
lize insoluble zinc compounds such as ZnO, ZnCO₃, or Zn₃ (PO₄)₂ [ 172, 188]. However, 
effective solubility does not necessarily lead to an increased availability of Zn² ⁺ in the 
rhizosphere, zinc uptake by roots, or improved plant functioning under dry conditions. 
Factors that affect the amount of solubilized zinc available for plant uptake include soil 
chemistry, microbial survival, root activity, and drought severity. To date, few studies 
have quantitatively assessed the microbial zinc-solubilizing activity, plant zinc accumu -
lation, and physiological responses simultaneously under water-deficit circumstances in 
sugarcane [ 140, 219]. In comparison with other systems such as phosphorus solubili -
zation, biological nitrogen fixation, or phytohormone-induced root growth, microbial 
zinc mobilization’s contribution towards drought adaptation in sugarcane is relatively 
uninvestigated. Therefore, zinc solubilization should be treated up to now as a poten -
tially advantageous but not well described mechanism to be experimentally confirmed. 
Zinc solubilizing bacteria have shown good potential in laboratory- and greenhouse-
based experiments. However, the available evidence is not sufficient to conclude that the 
microbial zinc solubilization significantly enhances zinc uptake and drought tolerance 
in sugarcane for field applications. More current studies should connect microbial zinc 
mobilization with plant zinc nutrition, physiological responses, and yield performance 
under varied drought circumstances.
 • Silicon (Si)
Silicon (Si) is not classified as an essential nutrient for most plants; however, it is widely 
recognized as a beneficial element, particularly in monocot crops such as rice, wheat, 
and sugarcane [ 74, 129]. Under drought stress, silicon improves water-use efficiency, 
structural reinforcement of cell walls, reduced transpiration, enhanced antioxidant 
defense, and stabilization of photosynthetic machinery [ 94, 257]. In soil, silicon is pri -
marily present in insoluble forms such as insoluble silicate minerals, including feldspars, 
quartz, and clay-associated silicates, or as silica (SiO₂), whereas plants absorb silicon in 
the soluble form of monosilicic acid (H₄SiO₄) [ 129]. Several bacterial genera, including 
Bacillus, Pseudomonas , Burkholderia , and Enterobacter, have been reported to solubi -
lize silicate minerals under laboratory conditions [ 133, 224]. However, there is no direct 
evidence for microbial silicate weathering and increased H₄SiO₄ availability in drought-
stressed sugarcane rhizospheres [77, 110].
Under moderate drought conditions, microbial enhancement of Si availability has 
been associated with reduced transpiration rates, improved relative water content, and 
enhanced photosynthetic performance in several crops [ 94]. For example, inoculation 
with silicon-mobilizing Pseudomonas fluorescens has been shown to increase soluble 
Si availability and improve physiological drought tolerance in maize under controlled 
moisture stress [224]. As a high silicon-accumulating crop, sugarcane benefits from ade -
quate Si nutrition through improved stalk strength, enhanced leaf erectness, and greater 
tolerance to water deficit and lodging stress [ 129]. In sugarcane production systems, 
soil or foliar silicon supplementation, often interacting with microbial activity, has also 
been linked to improved cane yield and sugar recovery, particularly in Si-deficient soils

[111]. The efficiency of silicon-based interventions is thus strongly dependent on the 
initial soil silicon supply. Positive responses are often more evident in silicon-deficient 
soils where the amount of plant-available silicon is low and where extra silicon inputs 
can significantly increase absorption. The estimation of agronomic value of the silicon-
solubilizing microorganisms in sugarcane production systems should consider the soil 
mineralogy, weathering status, and existing silicon availability. However, there is a sig -
nificant drawback of existing research is the lack of field studies that correlate microbial 
silicate weathering with soil monosilicic acid elevations, plant silicon accumulation, and 
drought-tolerance responses of sugarcane.
 • Sulfur (S)
Drought stress reduces sulfate mobility and mass flow toward roots due to lower soil 
moisture, thereby limiting sulfur uptake and disrupting metabolic and antioxidant pro -
cesses critical for stress tolerance [ 121]. Sulfur-mediated enhancement of glutathione, 
cysteine, and sulfated secondary metabolites improves ROS scavenging and stabilizes 
cellular membranes under water deficit [ 116]. Sulfur-solubilizing bacteria (SSB) notably 
certain strains of Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Thiobacillus, and mycorrhizal fungi can miner-
alize organic sulfur compounds into free sulfate ions (SO₄² ⁻), increasing plant-available 
sulfur in the rhizosphere [59, 97].
Although SSB reported for increasing sulfur availability and drought tolerance in 
wheat and maize [ 245], there are limited information for such benefits in sugarcane. 
However, in sugarcane, direct evidence connecting microbial sulfur mobilization to 
improved sulfur nutrition, physiological performance, or drought tolerance is currently 
limited. Though direct evidence of sulfur-solubilizing bacteria (SSB) in sugarcane is lim -
ited, PGPR with known phosphate and potassium solubilizing capacities may also sup -
port sulfur mobilization. Its deficiency impairs growth, reduces cane quality, and lowers 
sucrose yield. SSBs enhance sulfur uptake, stimulate root development, and improve 
overall plant vigor, reducing the dependency on chemical sulfur fertilizers [118].
9.1 Indirect mechanisms of PGPR enhancing sugarcane drought resilience
Indirect mechanisms of PGPR do not directly promote plant growth; instead, they play a 
critical role in enhancing plant resilience under drought stress by modulating the rhizo -
sphere environment. These mechanisms alleviate drought-induced ethylene stress, sup -
press phytopathogens, and strengthen plant defense responses. Major indirect pathways 
include biological control of pathogens, ACC deaminase activity, and the production of 
siderophores, ammonia, and hydrogen cyanide (Table 2).

structural barriers, reducing the synthesis of defense-related metabolites, and redirect -
ing carbon resources toward osmoprotection rather than immune responses [ 157, 171].

stressed sugarcane [240]. Therefore, the hypothesis that ISR can promote PGPR-induced

scriptomic, proteomic, and gene expression studies.

antimicrobial compounds capable of suppressing economically important sugarcane 
pathogens, particularly C. falcatum and, to a lesser extent, C. paradoxa. However, most 
available studies focus on disease suppression under normal growing conditions, and 
relatively few have evaluated the stability of these biocontrol interactions under drought 
stress [168, 228].
Despite these promising findings, pathogen-specific validation of PGPR-mediated 
biocontrol under field-scale severe drought conditions in sugarcane remains limited. 
Therefore, future research should integrate moisture-gradient experiments, pathogen 
virulence assessments, and microbial metabolite profiling to better understand the sta -
bility of PGPR-mediated disease suppression under drought stress. Overall, PGPR-based 
biocontrol should be viewed as a stress-mitigating and risk-buffering mechanism that is 
most effective under moderate drought conditions, where both plant immune responses 
and microbial metabolic activity remain functional.
 • Siderophore Production
Siderophores are low-molecular-weight, high-affinity iron-chelating compounds that 
can increase the solubility and mobility of ferric iron (Fe³⁺) [10, 65, 215]. Several drought-
tolerant PGPR genera, including Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Azotobacter, and Azospirillum, 
have been reported to produce siderophores, in conjunction with other adaptive traits 
such as ACC deaminase activity and phytohormone synthesis, collectively enhancing 
plant growth, antioxidant capacity, and stress resilience in cereals, legumes, and hor -
ticultural crops under moisture deficit conditions [ 93, 165, 180]. Beyond improving 
iron nutrition, microbial siderophores also contribute to biocontrol under drought by

competitively restricting iron availability to phytopathogens, suppressing their growth 
and virulence in the rhizosphere [73, 75].
Drought induced enrichment of bacterial groups like Rhizobiales and Streptomyce-
tales may reflect adaptive restructuring of the sugarcane rhizosphere microbiome, but 
these insights are largely correlative and cannot be confirmed directly into improved 
siderophore production or plant iron nutrition [ 60]. At present, little is known about 
the association of shifts in the composition of microbial communities with increased 
iron acquisition by drought-stressed sugarcane. Likewise, although siderophore-bear -
ing PGPR have been found to improve iron uptake and plant performance in some 
crops, relatively few studies have directly quantified rhizosphere iron dynamics, plant 
iron accumulation, or leaf iron concentrations in sugarcane following PGPR inocula -
tion under drought conditions. As a result, the association of siderophore-mediated 
iron mobilization to the drought tolerance of sugarcane is insufficiently characterized. 
However, the extent to which microbial siderophore production enhances iron uptake 
by drought-stressed sugarcane remains insufficiently quantified.
 • Ammonia and Nitrogen Fixation
Biological nitrogen fixation in sugarcane has been traditionally acknowledged as one of 
the important sources of nitrogen, especially associated with diazotrophic microorgan -
isms, including Gluconacetobacter diazotrophicus , Azospirillum spp. , Herbaspirillum 
spp., and Burkholderia spp. However, estimates of the contribution of biologically fixed 
nitrogen to total plant nitrogen nutrition vary widely depending on sugarcane genotype, 
soil fertility, environmental conditions, microbial strain, and drought severity. While 
some investigations have suggested significant nitrogen contributions under favorable 
conditions, biological nitrogen fixation often supplies only a fraction of total crop nitro -
gen demand and may decline under severe water deficit [ 37, 50, 191]. In addition to 
nitrogen fixation, many PGPR release ammonia as a metabolic by-product, which con -
tributes directly to plant nitrogen nutrition and indirectly suppresses soil-borne patho -
gens, strengthening plant resilience under drought stress [ 73, 93]. Microbial nitrogen 
fixation performance under drought environment is additionally hampered by nitroge -
nase, the key enzyme responsible for atmospheric nitrogen reduction. Drought-induced 
alterations in soil aeration, oxygen diffusion and microbial metabolism can also poten -
tially affect nitrogenase activity. Accordingly, while some drought-adapted diazotrophs 
have protective mechanisms (e.g. osmolyte accumulation, antioxidant defense systems 
as well as biofilm formation) in place to adapt, biological nitrogen fixation generally 
declines as drought severity increases [147].
Recent studies further indicate that drought stress reshapes the sugarcane rhizosphere 
microbiome by enriching drought-tolerant, nitrogen-fixing taxa, highlighting the adap -
tive significance of microbial nitrogen fixation and ammonia production in sustaining 
sugarcane productivity under water-limited environments [ 253]. Collectively, ammonia 
production and biological nitrogen fixation by PGPR represent key microbial mecha -
nisms that integrate nutrient acquisition, stress tolerance, and plant health, offering a 
sustainable strategy for improving crop performance under drought-prone agricul -
tural systems. A major knowledge gap is the limited availability of field studies directly 
quantifying biological nitrogen fixation rates in drought-stressed sugarcane using iso -
topic techniques and linking these measurements with plant nitrogen status, yield, and

water-use efficiency. Future research should determine how drought intensity, genotype, 
microbial colonization strategy, and management practices influence the actual contri -
bution of diazotrophs to sugarcane nitrogen nutrition under field conditions.
 • Hydrogen Cyanide (HCN) Production
In sugarcane, a crop highly susceptible to both drought stress and soil-borne diseases, 
PGPR strains with combined drought tolerance and biocontrol traits including HCN 
production have been reported to enhance plant vigor and stress resilience, while 
drought-induced shifts in the sugarcane rhizosphere microbiome favor metabolically 
versatile antagonistic bacteria capable of pathogen suppression [ 46, 253]. PGPR have 
typically synthesized HCN in concentrations that are thought to inhibit pathogen activ -
ity but not damage plants, although the current evidence for safe concentrations for 
sugarcane rhizospheres is missing. Furthermore, the modification of root exudation pat-
terns, microbial community composition, soil aeration, and rhizosphere pH in a drought 
could also potentially affect local HCN levels and bioavailability. Therefore, the tradeoff 
between the potential beneficial pathogen inhibition, and the possible phytotoxic or eco-
logical effects, need to be determined especially in very severe drought [ 73, 176, 177].
Several HCN-producing PGPR have shown antagonistic activity against fungal patho -
gens includes red rot ( Colletotrichum falcatum), pineapple disease ( Ceratocystis para -
doxa) and smut ( Sporisorium scitamineum) in laboratory and greenhouse experiments 
but there is little direct evidence of the role of HCN-mediated biocontrol in drought 
resilience in sugarcane is inferred rather than experimentally demonstrated [ 9, 18, 36]. 
Another significant caveat is that most studies evaluating PGPR for HCN synthesis have 
used qualitative laboratory assays and have been conducted under optimal growth con -
ditions. In comparison, relatively few studies have measured direct HCN production in 
drought-stressed rhizospheres or examined how water deficit affects HCN synthesis, 
diffusion, persistence, and biological activity [176].
 • ACC Deaminase Production
In sugarcane, rhizobacteria such as Pseudomonas and Azospirillum spp . have been 
reported to enhance root development, biomass accumulation, and drought resilience 
through ACC deaminase activity, resulting in improved relative water content, chloro -
phyll retention, and antioxidant enzyme activity under water-limited conditions [ 168, 
202, 204]. ACC (1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate) deaminase plays a pivotal role 
in mitigating drought-induced ethylene stress in crops. This enzyme cleaves ACC, the 
immediate precursor of ethylene, into α-ketobutyrate and ammonia, thereby lower -
ing plant ethylene levels and alleviating stress-induced growth inhibition [ 91, 92, 182]. 
Through this mechanism, ACC deaminase-producing rhizobacteria enable sustained 
root growth, improved water uptake, stomatal regulation, and enhanced photosynthetic 
efficiency, ultimately contributing to improved tolerance to moisture-deficit conditions 
[63, 91, 229]. ACC deaminase-mediated mitigation of ethylene stress is expected to be 
most effective in mild- to moderate-drought than drastic water deficiency, although this 
hypothesis has not been rigorously tested in sugarcane field environments. A notable 
drawback of the ACC deaminase mechanism is its reliance on active microbial metabo -
lism and sustained rhizosphere colonization.

and the type of carbon source present in the rhizosphere [ 92]. As a result, not all ACC 
deaminase-producing isolates provide equivalent benefits in improving plant drought 
tolerance. Since, ACC deaminase activity also depends on active bacterial metabolism, 
prolonged or extreme desiccation may limit enzyme production and reduce rhizosphere 
colonization efficiency [143, 191]. Therefore, the effectiveness of ACC deaminase-medi -
ated regulation of plant ethylene levels may decrease under extreme water limitation. 
These limitations highlight the importance of selecting drought-resilient and metaboli -
cally stable bacterial strains for successful field applications.
However, ACC deaminase is regarded as one of the most promising PGPR traits for 
drought mitigation, several important knowledge gaps still exist in sugarcane. Limited 
information has been reported on ACC deaminase activity ranges among sugarcane-
associated bacterial isolates, soil moisture thresholds at which it can remain active, and 
the performance of various strains in different field settings. Further research to include 
the microbial functional assays into a direct assessment of the ethylene dynamics in dif -
ferent drought intensities will be needed to show if and how ACC deaminase impacts 
the structural resilience of sugarcane under drought conditions.
A key drawback of studies on the indirect PGPR-mediated drought tolerance mecha -
nisms is the absence of uniform quantitative techniques between studies. Attributes 
including ACC deaminase activity, siderophore production, ammonia production, and 
HCN synthesis are usually reported qualitatively or measured using different method -
ologies, thus rendering cross-strain comparisons difficult. Furthermore, many microor -
ganisms reported to have these traits have been evaluated in model crops or laboratory 
systems rather than in sugarcane under field drought conditions. Consequently, the rela-
tive contribution of such individual indirect mechanisms in sugarcane to drought adap -
tation remains inadequately quantified and should be interpreted with caution.
10 Single-strain vs consortium-based PGPR approaches
Distinguishing between single-strain inoculants and microbial consortia is an important 
issue for the evaluation of PGPR-mediated drought tolerance. Single-strain inoculants 
make sense for mechanistic studies because they permit determination of individual 
microbe characteristics, ACC deaminase activity, phytohormone production, exopoly -
saccharide synthesis, nutrient solubilization, and biological nitrogen fixation [ 187, 250]. 
However, their efficacy could be restricted in some field conditions, where a number 
of stress-responsive functions require simultaneous application. Microbial consortia is 
a combination of two or more compatible microorganisms with complementary func -
tional groups, including but not limited to nutrient mobilization, phytohormone pro -
duction, nitrogen fixation, and antioxidant defense [ 30, 146]. These actions can support 
a range of complementary tasks within microbial communities, promoting plant growth 
and drought tolerance. However, previous studies have demonstrated that consortium-
based inoculants perform better in the field than single strains of the same plant, result -
ing in higher quality. These positive interactions can differ because microbial relations 
may be weak, neutral, or antagonistic, and such interactions can inhibit the efficacy of 
microbial assays due to competition for nutrients, ecological niches, or root colonization

Srivastava et al. Discover Soil (2026) 3:173 
sites. It is probable that variation in biofertilizer performance could also depend on 
variations in microbial composition, formulation quality, storage conditions, and post-
application persistence [78]. In sugarcane, direct comparison between single-strain and 
consortium-based inoculants is very limited. For example, Bacillus megaterium strains 
JTB1 and MT22 and their consortium improved sugarcane growth, root architecture, 
antioxidant activity, and photosynthetic gene expression under drought, but the consor -
tium did not consistently outperform the individual strains across all measured traits 
[41]. These approaches should further be in the same environmental conditions to see if 
microbial consortia have a greater resilience against drought in different sugarcane gen -
otypes, soils, and production systems.
11 Integration, trade-offs, and context dependency of PGPR-mediated 
drought responses
PGPR-mediated drought tolerance in sugarcane arises from the interaction of multiple 
microbial traits, plant physiological processes, and environmental conditions, not a sin -
gle microbial function. Functions such as ACC deaminase activity, indole-3-acetic acid 
(IAA) production, exopolysaccharide (EPS) synthesis, phosphate solubilization, biologi -
cal nitrogen fixation, and siderophore production together influence root development, 
water and nutrient uptake, hormonal regulation, osmotic adjustment, and antioxidant 
metabolism [ 40, 197]. However, the co-occurrence of several beneficial traits does not 
necessarily imply a synergistic effect of these traits. An additive response occurs when 
the combined effect of two or more traits is approximately equivalent to the sum of their 
individual contributions, whereas a synergistic response occurs when their interaction 
produces an effect greater than that expected from the individual effects.
An interaction that is potentially complementary is IAA-driven root development and 
ACC deaminase-mediated regulation of stress-induced ethylene. IAA stimulates pri -
mary and lateral root growth, while ACC deaminase reduces stress- associated ethylene 
that would otherwise limit root elongation [ 147, 197]. However, this relationship is not 
necessarily synergistic. Too much auxin can increase biomass and water consumption, 
which could be counterproductive in extreme drought, and the optimal balance is prob -
ably determined by the trade-off between stimulation of growth and water saving. How -
ever, there is no factorial study that isolates this specific interaction in sugarcane, and 
hence, this is a testable hypothesis and not an established impact.
A similar caveat applies to nutrient-mobilizing traits. Mineral solubilization, nitro -
gen fixation, and siderophore-mediated iron acquisition can each independently sup -
port photosynthesis and antioxidant metabolism under drought, and their combined 
presence may simply be additive [ 40]. Confirming true synergy requires evidence that 
combined effects exceed the sum of individual ones, currently insufficient for sugarcane. 
There may also be physiological trade-offs: producing siderophores, EPS, hormones, or 
enzymes all draw on shared microbial resources, so investment in one function could 
limit another depending on carbon/nutrient status, growth phase, and environmen -
tal stress. A proposed trade-off between siderophore production and ACC deaminase 
activity is mechanistically plausible but not yet experimentally confirmed through direct 
measurement of microbial resource allocation.
Field data point to the need for restraint in assuming consortia to be synergistic 
by default. Bacillus megaterium strains JTB1 and MT22, used individually and as a 
Page 27 of 48
Srivastava et al. Discover Soil (2026) 3:173 
consortium, improved sugarcane root growth, antioxidant, and molecular responses in 
the face of drought, but the consortium did not always outperform the individual strains 
[41]. This study demonstrates that combining strains can result in trait-specific, neu -
tral or additive effects, rather than universal synergy, depending on strain compatibility, 
competition, colonization sites, persistence, and host genotype thus, consortia should be 
benchmarked against their individual components under the same conditions.
Trade-offs also occur at the plant level increasing root growth improves water/nutri -
ent uptake, but at a carbon cost, and gains may plateau with increasing drought sever -
ity. So one improved characteristic doesn’t necessarily translate to whole-plant drought 
resistance it’s really about whether the combined microbial effects maintain plant water 
status, photosynthesis, biomass, and eventually cane/sugar yield. The efficacy of PGPR 
is not an intrinsic characteristic of a strain but depends on the prevailing conditions, 
including plant genotype, soil properties, drought intensity, temperature, nutritional 
status, formulation, and timing of inoculation. Future studies should employ factorial, 
multi-strain designs to simultaneously quantify colonization, physiology, and yield, con -
firmed across various seasons and locales, rather than assuming synergy from the simple 
existence of numerous favourable features.
12 Biocontrol, drought stress, and disease interactions
Biocontrol may indirectly increase the drought resilience of sugarcane by relieving 
pathogen stress, but this should be decoupled from the direct physiological drought 
tolerance mechanisms of PGPR. Water shortages can aggravate red rot ( Colletotrichum 
falcatum), and different sugarcane varieties respond differently to it. The rhizosphere 
bacteria include species of Bacillus, Pseudomonas , Gluconacetobacter , Ochrobactrum, 
and Acinetobacter. In vitro, selected strains have shown antagonism to C. falcatum and, 
in greenhouse tests, have reduced disease severity and promoted development [106, 228, 
240]. However, these strains need to be validated in natural habitats to be deemed reli -
able biocontrol agents.
Importantly, biocontrol efficacy under adequate moisture cannot be assumed to hold 
under severe drought. Water deficit alters microbial growth, root exudation, rhizosphere 
connectivity, and pathogen–host dynamics, and direct evidence quantifying biocontrol 
performance loss under drought in sugarcane remains limited [35, 248]. Most biocontrol 
studies to date were conducted under lab, greenhouse, or well-watered conditions rather 
than combined drought × pathogen stress [26, 49, 235].
Biocontrol should therefore be treated as a potentially complementary, not demon -
strated, component of PGPR-mediated drought tolerance in sugarcane. Future work 
should use factorial designs (water regime × pathogen challenge × microbial inocula -
tion) measuring pathogen development, microbial survival/colonization, plant water 
status, disease severity, biomass, and final yield, to determine whether disease suppres -
sion persists under severe drought and whether effects are additive, synergistic, neutral, 
or antagonistic.

13 Temporal dynamics of PGPR responses during drought development and 
recovery
The response of plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) to drought is now under-
stood as a dynamic, multi-phase process rather than a static condition, occurring over 
discrete stages of stress onset, progression, peak severity, and post-rewatering recovery. 
In the early phase of water deficit, PGPR rapidly alter host ethylene signaling through 
ACC deaminase activity and initiate an upregulation of exopolysaccharide production, 
processes that buffer the rhizosphere microenvironment before the accrual of more 
severe physiological damage. The timing of inoculation relative to stress onset also 
appears to shape efficacy: in sugarcane cv. SP70-1143, Gluconacetobacter diazotrophi -
cus PAL5 applied seven days prior to the imposition of water deficit provided one of 
the clearest demonstrations of pre-emptive PGPR conditioning ahead of stress [ 223]. 
As stress intensifies, this response transitions toward osmolyte biosynthesis, phytohor -
mone-mediated root architectural remodeling, and priming of host antioxidant enzyme 
systems, alongside compositional shifts in the broader rhizosphere community that 
favor desiccation-tolerant, spore and biofilm-forming taxa.
This temporal sensitivity is further modulated by irrigation regime and host genotype. 
A study by May, Moreira [132] reported that B. aryabhattai produced more pronounced 
growth-promoting effects at 7- and 14-day irrigation intervals, with the magnitude of 
the response varying across genotypes. Similarly, a field trial by Almeida, Santos [ 15], in 
which pre-sprouted seedlings were inoculated with B. licheniformis and B. subtilis, found 
that inoculation most strongly enhanced crop growth rate under the most restricted 
water regimes, with these benefits becoming increasingly evident as plant establishment 
progressed. Comprehensive reviews of PGPR-mediated drought tolerance have consoli -
dated evidence for several key mechanisms, including nitrogen fixation, phosphate solu -
bilization, hormone production, and antioxidant regulation. These reviews also indicate 
that PGPR are generally more effective at enhancing photosynthesis and limiting oxida -
tive damage under drought conditions than under non-stress conditions, underscoring 
the stress-contingent nature of these benefits.
Critically, the recovery phase following rewatering is emerging as a mechanistically 
distinct rather than merely reversive stage. Research by Santos, Edward [ 184] on rice 
root microbiomes has shown that drought-stressed root communities are developmen -
tally delayed relative to well-watered communities and that the duration of prior drought 
exposure shapes the subsequent trajectory and rate of microbiome recovery. Parallel 
high-resolution metabolomic work on rewatered sugar beet plants has similarly dem -
onstrated that shoot and root tissues follow divergent recovery kinetics, with a transient 
normalization of most metabolites within roughly eight days followed by a secondary 
accumulation of amino acids in leaves that may represent a beneficial stress imprint for 
future drought events [ 233]. Together, these findings suggest that PGPR do not simply 
retreat to a pre-stress baseline upon rewatering but instead retain functional and com -
positional legacies potentially underlying priming or “stress memory” effects that merit 
dedicated time-course, multi-omics investigation rather than the endpoint sampling that 
has dominated the field to date.

14 Technological innovations in the use of PGPR for sustainable agriculture
Recent developments in microbial biotechnology offered novel avenues for enhancing 
the performance and reliability of PGPR (Plant Growth-Promoting Rhizobacteria) under 
drought. Instead of targeting genetic engineering in a host plant, increasing attention in 
this context is turned to finding, selecting, and modifying microbial characteristics that 
are directly related to stress relief. Genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metab -
olomics approaches are combining to identify genes implicated in ACC deaminase 
activity (acdS), biological nitrogen fixation (nif genes), siderophore biosynthesis, osmo -
protectant production, exopolysaccharide synthesis, and rhizosphere colonization [4, 17, 
238]. These technologies allow the identification of drought-resilient strains with better 
functional stability under various environmental conditions. Marker-free genome-edit -
ing methods and targeted genetic engineering have also been investigated for improving 
desirable microbial traits; however, most of these methods have not been verified in field 
sugarcane systems and are currently at the experimental phase [ 85, 202]. Innovations 
in the inoculant production process, besides strain enhancement, are also improving 
the survival and efficacy of PGPR under water-limited circumstances. Synthetic micro -
bial communities with co-evolved functional traits of complementary nature, including 
ACC deaminase production, nutrient mobilization, and osmotic stress tolerance, may 
hold a better stability than single-strain inoculants in any range of heterogeneous envi -
ronmental conditions [201]. Similarly, microbial encapsulation, polymer-based carriers, 
and nano-enabled delivery systems improve shelf life, rhizosphere establishment, and 
protection against environmental stresses [ 78, 196]. Despite these enhancements, field 
validation in sugarcane is scarce, and the performance of engineered or highly selective 
PGPR strains under extreme drought conditions, different soil types, or various sugar -
cane genotypes needs to be further explored prior to large-scale commercial applica -
tions of PGPR compounds [41, 52].
15 Commercialization and adoption of PGPR technologies in sugarcane

microbial inoculants using plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) including 
Bacillus, Azospirillum, Pseudomonas, Rhizobium, and Gluconacetobacter genera. Several 
of these microorganisms are sold worldwide as biofertilizers, biostimulants, or products 
that help plant growth, and have exhibited beneficial effects on nutrient acquisition, root 
formation, and crop production following a variety of environmental conditions [ 24, 64, 
66]. For sugarcane, the microbial products that contain nitrogen-fixing bacteria as well 
as growth-promoting bacteria such as Gluconacetobacter diazotrophicus , Herbaspiril-

crop growth and increasing efficiency in nutrient-use [5, 67, 167]. Even with encouraging 
experiment results, only a few commercial products have been developed and validated 
specifically for drought mitigation in sugarcane [ 150] (Table 3). One important factor is 
the gap between the efficiency of greenhouses and work performance in field work. Such 
environmental variability, soil moisture and temperature variations, soil physicochemi -
cal characteristics, and direct interaction with indigenous microbial communities can 
markedly affect inoculant survival and efficacy under field conditions [114, 149].
In turn, strains that are consistently effective under controlled conditions have the 
potential to show varying responses for various locations, seasons, and production

16 Recent sugarcane-specific evidence from field and water-deficit Studies
The current data for sugarcane-specific PGPR is heterogeneous in terms of setting, 
strain, genotype, and results, both in field and controlled-environment research, and 
should not be considered equally validated. A recent field trial in Brazil has shown that 
sugarcane inoculated with Bacillus licheniformis and B. subtilis showed better germi -
nation count, water-use efficiency, and improved root/shoot growth under different 
degrees of water stress (0%, 33%, 66%, and 100%) [ 15]. Also, Carnietto, Santos [ 44] have 
recently reported that the effects of inoculating Bacillus subtilis FMCH002 and Bacillus 
licheniformis FMCH001 under water scarcity were dependent on the soil type. Thus, the 
efficiency of PGPR is not only dependent on the bacterial strain but also on soil condi -
tions. However, the work does not give any direct confirmation in the field, since it was 
conducted under controlled laboratory circumstances.
A pot experiment with Bacillus megaterium strains JTB1 and MT22 (alone and as a 
consortium) under 100/50/25% field capacity revealed improved root development, bio -
mass, antioxidant responses, and photosynthetic properties. But the individual strains 
performed usually the same as the consortium, which indicates that strain combinations 
are not necessarily better than inoculating with a single strain [41]. Differential responses 
of sugarcane genotypes to microbial inoculation have been evidenced recently. In recent 
studies, growth, biomass, and nitrogen content were measured using a diazotrophic 
consortium of Gluconacetobacter diazotrophicus and other beneficial bacteria [ 68, 70, 
191]. But there is scant concrete evidence that these inoculants may alleviate drought 
stress in field conditions. Thus, positive effects of G. diazotrophicus on nitrogen fixation 
or plant development should not be taken as proof of drought protection. In general, the 
recent published work added to the sugarcane-specific data base, but consistent benefits 
for mature-crop cane and sugar yield during drought remain inadequately proven. The 
variation among genotypes of sugarcane, soil types, bacterial strains, and water regimes 
further suggests that the effectiveness of PGPR should be evaluated as a genotype × 
microbial inoculant × environmental interaction rather than as a universal attribute of a 
particular strain.
17 Current evidence and remaining uncertainties
PGPR have also been reported to exert positive effects on sugarcane growth and 
drought-induced physiological responses; however, the magnitude and consistency of 
these effects vary considerably among response variables and experimental systems.

Several greenhouse and pot studies have reported improvements in nutrient uptake, 
antioxidant activity, osmotic adjustment, root development, and water-use efficiency fol-
lowing PGPR inoculation [15, 41, 86, 193].
Broader meta-analyses and systematic reviews further support the potential of PGPR 
to increase plant tolerance to drought stress, but responses are generally not standard -
ized and depend on species, drought severity, bacterial qualities and experimental design 
[114, 255]. As a result, controlled experiments with good results should not be assumed 
to be replicable for all sugarcane-producing environments. A considerable area of uncer-
tainty in present research is the lack of multi-site, multi-season field trials that exam -
ine inoculation performance in naturally fluctuating drought regimes, soil, climate and 
management conditions. Field efficiency might be affected by the severity of drought, 
soil physicochemical properties, temperature, sugarcane genotype, inoculation method, 
formulation quality and, to some extent the interactions at the field level with indigenous 
microbial communities [52, 149]. As a result, the different effects of PGPR strains, which 
have consistent performance in greenhouse or pot conditions, may vary by regions, sea -
sons and production systems. Consequently, the current evidence indicates that PGPR 
has potential as a well-studied crop control technique for enhancing drought-resistant 
sugarcane yields, but issues regarding reproducibility, effect size, long-lasting effect, 
yield stability, and commercial scale application should be addressed as well. Future 
studies should report the success of the inoculants, and the failure or inconsistency of 
the inoculants per se in producing agronomic values under field conditions [78, 83].
18 Limitations and inconsistent responses of PGPR under drought conditions
The PGPR-mediated drought tolerance in sugarcane is not universal but conditional and 
is significantly affected by the genotype, microbial identity, inoculation approach, nutri -
tional status, and environment. Improvement of physiological/biochemical features does 
not always lead to yield improvement. PGPR inoculation increased nutrient acquisition, 
technological quality, and yield, but only under intermediate phosphorus-fertilization 
levels, with no positive effect at zero P or at the highest P₂O₅ rate, suggesting that the 
response is dependent on the surrounding nutrient environment rather than an inherent 
inoculant property [ 169]. Likewise, Bacillus megaterium strains JTB1 and MT22, alone 
or as a consortium, under moderate/severe drought enhanced root growth and antioxi -
dant and molecular traits, but the consortium did not perform better than single strains 
[39], indicating that mixing strains does not automatically improve agronomic response. 
Strong laboratory/greenhouse performance also should not be read as evidence of field 
reliability. In the field, PGPR face competition from native microbes, limited root niches, 
and variable soil moisture, temperature, and physicochemical conditions; moderate-to-
severe drought further limits microbial activity, colonization, and metabolite production 
[60, 123, 127]. Since inoculant persistence and colonization are rarely measured directly 
in drought-stressed sugarcane studies, it’s often unclear whether a null result reflects 
true inoculant failure or simply poor establishment.
Competition with the native rhizosphere microbiome adds further variability: intro -
duced strains compete for nutrients, carbon, and spatial niches with adapted native 
microbes, and drought-altered root exudation can favor natives over introduced strains. 
Genotype, root architecture, soil texture, organic matter, pH, nutrient availability, 
drought intensity, temperature, and management practices together drive substantial 
Page 33 of 48
Srivastava et al. Discover Soil (2026) 3:173 
spatial and temporal variation in PGPR performance [ 48, 213], explaining why con -
trolled-condition successes often shrink, flatten, or disappear under field conditions. 
Publication bias is a further limitation: positive, statistically significant results are pub -
lished and cited more often than null or mixed outcomes, likely inflating the apparent 
success rate of PGPR, a particular concern given how few field (versus controlled-envi -
ronment) drought studies exist in sugarcane. Neutral or unsuccessful outcomes should 
be treated as integral evidence.
Overall, current evidence supports a conditional response model in which absence of 
yield improvement should not be read as complete biological failure when intermediate 
traits (root development, nutrient acquisition, antioxidant activity, water-use efficiency) 
improve nor should improvement in those traits be assumed to guarantee yield success. 
Future studies should jointly measure microbial survival/colonization, physiological 
responses, and final yield across multiple environments and seasons to separate genuine 
efficacy from context-dependent artifacts.
19 Challenges and future perspectives
There are several studies describing the feasibility and extent of PGPR applications in 
agricultural applications, which raises translation problems from controlled studies into 
real-world applications. Research showing an impact on drought tolerance is strong in 
the literature, including studies on PGPR, yet most of the available research is conducted 
using greenhouses, growth chambers, and pot experiments in controlled environmen -
tal conditions. These studies have advanced our knowledge of microbial responses to 
drought, including phytohormone production, nutrient mobilization, ACC deaminase 
activity, osmotic adjustment, and antioxidant regulation. Controlled conditions are 
not able to fully recapitulate the complex and variable conditions of a field environ -
ment. Under field conditions, different interacting factors such as temperature swings, 
rain variability, soil heterogeneity, irrigation, nutrient availability, and competition with 
indigenous species may affect PGPR-introduced strains with respect to their survival, 
colonization, and functional response. As such, microbial inoculants that exert strong 
growth-facilitating effects under controlled environments might perform heteroge -
neously in agricultural habitats [ 19, 55]. Controlled experiments often report 20–60% 
improvements in plant growth parameters following PGPR inoculation, whereas field 
trials typically show 5–25% gains or inconsistent results due to environmental variabil -
ity. Multi-location field evaluations frequently report variable responses across sites and 
growing seasons, highlighting the difficulty of translating laboratory efficacy into consis -
tent field performance [22, 154]. This gap between laboratory success and field reliability 
remains a key barrier to their widespread adoption.
Competition with indigenous soil microorganisms further limits the establishment 
and persistence of introduced PGPR strains. Native microbes, being well adapted to local 
conditions, often outcompete inoculated strains for nutrients and ecological niches. 
Additionally, drought-induced changes in root exudation patterns can disrupt micro -
bial recruitment and reduce PGPR efficiency [ 19, 213]. Crop genotype also influences 
outcomes, as variations in root architecture, exudate composition, and plant immunity 
affect microbial colonization, making it difficult to develop universally effective inocu -
lants for diverse sugarcane cultivars [154].

Srivastava et al. Discover Soil (2026) 3:173 
Soil physicochemical properties, including pH, nutrient status, and organic matter 
content, along with environmental factors such as extreme temperature and irregular 
rainfall, further contribute to variability in PGPR performance [ 32]. These challenges 
are particularly significant in drought-prone regions, where stress conditions are more 
severe. Moreover, limited understanding of complex plant-microbe-soil interactions 
under drought conditions restricts the ability to optimize PGPR functionality at the field 
level.
Commercialization of PGPR-based products also presents challenges, including the 
need for cost-effective large-scale production, improved formulation technologies, and 
enhanced shelf life. Despite their environmental benefits, these factors can increase pro -
duction costs and limit adoption by farmers. Future prospects lie in integrating PGPR 
with advanced agricultural practices and emerging technologies. The development of 
robust microbial consortia, improved bioformulations, and better delivery systems can 
enhance microbial survival and effectiveness. In addition, combining PGPR applications 
with precision agriculture tools such as soil health monitoring, climate-smart irrigation, 
and digital decision-support systems can optimize their field performance. In conclu -
sion, while PGPR hold significant potential for improving drought resilience and sus -
tainability in sugarcane cultivation, overcoming challenges related to field variability, 
microbial establishment, formulation, and large-scale application are essential. Contin -
ued research, technological advancements, and supportive policy measures will be criti -
cal to fully realize the potential of PGPR in climate-resilient agriculture.
In the context of sugarcane, field-based drought studies report less evidence than 
greenhouse studies. Moreover, the vast majority of field evaluations published to date 
have been performed at individual sites or a single growing season, limiting the ability 
to evaluate the long-term stability as well as reproducibility of inoculant performance 
across different environmental conditions. Limited studies have investigated cane yield, 
sucrose accumulation, water-use efficiency, and microbial persistence under natural 
drought stress across single sites and multiple seasons. The discrepancy between experi -
mental and field effectiveness is one of the main obstacles for scaling PGPR technology 
up. The successful field application does not stop at the development of valuable micro -
bial strains but also includes formulation stability, shelf life of inoculants, rhizosphere 
competitiveness, environmental adaptation, and general performance compatibility in 
different production systems. Thus, the vast empirical evidence indicates the potential 
for PGPR to be applied to sustainable drought management but large scale potential 
needs to be interpreted with wariness and skepticism until well-researched in a wide 
range of agroecological sites and growing periods [19, 33].
Economic feasibility is a major obstacle to the extensive use of PGPR technologies in 
sugarcane cultivation. Sugarcane is generally cultivated as a low-margin, high-volume 
crop, particularly in developing countries, where the cost of production is a significant 
determinant of management choices. These PGPR inoculants increase nutrient-use effi -
ciency and drought resistance potential, but their economic advantages must always 
surpass the costs of inoculant production, formulation, transportation, storage, and 
application in order for inoculation to be an economic benefit [ 149, 167]. Currently, a 
relatively limited number of studies have performed full cost-benefit analyses on PGPR 
applications under drought-driven sugarcane production systems, which may make the 
economic value of such methods unable to be accurately measured in a commercial

context. Another significant challenge is that of inoculant formulation and storage sta -
bility [14, 64].
Most PGPR strains have poor viability during long storage, especially in tropical and 
subtropical areas with high humidity and temperature conditions. Poor microbial viabil -
ity during storage can severely deteriorate field performance and cause variations in the 
effects. Despite carrier-based formulations, microbial encapsulation technologies and 
polymer-protected bioformulations have all increased shelf life, maintaining sufficient 
cell viability during transportation and on-farm storage remains a major challenge, espe-
cially in areas of the world with limited cold-chain infrastructure [78, 113]. Farmer adop-
tion is another bottleneck. The introduction of PGPR technologies may be successful 
not only based on biological efficiency but also depends upon farmer awareness, avail -
ability of high-quality products, knowledge and confidence in efficacy, and compatibility 
with existing systems. In the majority of regions where sugarcane is planted, it can be 
believed that microbial inoculants are potentially high-risk inputs in many cases due to 
their short-term effects being less predictable and less efficient than traditional fertil -
izers [149, 153]. Variability in product efficacy, lack of extension support, and immature 
distribution networks could also limit adoption. Thus, better farmer education, better 
quality control standards, and reliable supply chains can all be keys to successful PGPR 
implementation [64, 66].
Interactions between microbial inoculants and traditional agricultural inputs are also 
underdeveloped. Fertilizer, fungicides, insecticides, herbicides, and seed-treatment 
chemicals could influence PGPR survival, colonization, and function. Some agrochemi -
cals are antimicrobial and may inhibit establishment of microbes or change rhizosphere 
community composition, thus reducing inoculant efficacy [ 185]. In contrast, some 
integrated nutrient management systems might improve the performance of PGPR by 
generating complementary resources. It is thus important to further investigate PGPR 
compatibility with widely used sugarcane production inputs under field conditions.
Another fundamental challenge is scaling up the experiment from laboratory studies 
to commercial production. Microbial strains often show reduced efficacy under field 
conditions compared to greenhouse conditions because of the variability of environ -
mental conditions, competition with native microbial communities, and heterogeneity 
of soil characteristics. In addition, industrial-scale fermentation, downstream process -
ing, formulation development, quality control, and regulatory approval bring additional 
technical and economic [ 66, 78]. Genetic stability, functional consistency, and shelf life 
at large-scale production remain critical for commercial success. Continued research in 
this field should progress beyond proof-of-concept studies to multi-location and multi-
season field trials on heterogeneous soil types, climatic conditions, irrigation regimes, 
and sugarcane genotypes (Table  4). We must also take a more targeted approach to 
drought severity thresholds, microbial persistence, inoculation timing, compatibility 
with agricultural inputs, and economic performance. The integration of PGPR with pre -
cision agriculture, climate-smart irrigation, microbial consortia, and novel bioformula -
tion technologies has the potential to improve consistency and adoption. However, the 
effective use of PGPR in drought-prone sugarcane systems will be a collective responsi -
bility of researchers, industry stakeholders, policymakers, extension agencies, and grow -
ers, with particular emphasis on biological efficacy and economic viability. The research

20 Conclusion
Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria offer a biologically plausible and potentially 
sustainable approach for supporting sugarcane under water-limited conditions. The 
literature reviewed in this article indicates that PGPR can influence several processes 
relevant to drought adaptation, including root development, nutrient mobilization, bio -
logical nitrogen fixation, phytohormonal regulation, ACC deaminase activity, osmotic 
adjustment, antioxidant defense, rhizosphere stabilization, and pathogen suppression. 
However, the strength of evidence is not uniform across these mechanisms. The most 
consistent evidence concerns improvements in plant growth, nutrient acquisition, root 
traits, and selected physiological responses, whereas direct evidence linking individual 
microbial mechanisms to improved cane and sugar yield under naturally occurring 
drought remains comparatively limited.
A central conclusion of this review is that PGPR efficacy should not be regarded as an 
intrinsic and universally transferable property of a microbial strain. Instead, it emerges 
from interactions among the inoculant, sugarcane genotype, drought intensity and dura-
tion, developmental stage, soil physicochemical characteristics, indigenous microbial 
communities, irrigation regime, temperature, nutrient status, formulation, and inocu -
lation timing. The rhizosphere therefore represents a critical determinant of inoculant 
establishment and functional expression. Moderate water stress could show observable 
benefits from PGPR, but severe and long-term droughts could limit microbial survival, 
root activity, metabolite production, nutrient diffusion, and colonization, which could 
potentially lead to a reduction of these effects. In addition, the evidence is a warning 
against the anticipation that multiple beneficial microbial traits or consortium-based 
inoculants will necessarily result in synergistic effects. Gains in intermediate traits such 
as root biomass, chlorophyll, nutrient uptake, antioxidant activity, or water-use effi -
ciency do not necessarily translate into increased cane or sugar yield. Conversely, the 
absence of a yield response should not necessarily be considered complete biological 
failure if meaningful improvements are seen in physiological or nutritional traits. Con -
sequently, neutral, inconsistent, and negative results should be incorporated into future 
evaluations rather than being overlooked.
Three research priorities are particularly important. First, multi-location, multi-season 
field experiments should quantify microbial survival and colonization together with soil 
moisture, plant physiology, nutrient acquisition, cane yield, sucrose yield, and water-
use efficiency. Second, standardized experimental frameworks should evaluate PGPR 
responses across drought severity gradients, sugarcane genotypes, soil types, inocula -
tion timings, and drought-recovery phases. Third, development and commercialization 
should integrate robust strain selection with formulation stability, shelf life, compatibility 
with fertilizers and agrochemicals, cost–benefit analysis, and farmer-oriented delivery 
systems. Thus, PGPR should presently be considered a context-dependent component 
of integrated drought management, with substantial promise but insufficient evidence 
to support their characterization as a universally reliable solution for drought-resilient 
sugarcane production.
