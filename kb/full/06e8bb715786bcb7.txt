Effect of potassium levels on soil properties and nutrient
availability under Kodo millet (Paspalum Scrobiculatum
L.)
Rakshitha R1, GN Thippeshappa2, Jayaprakash SM3, Veeranna HK4, Rajashekharappa K5
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/2618060X.2026.v9.i10b.6758
A BSTRACT
A field experiment entitled “Effect of Potassium Levels on Soil Properties and Nutrient Status of Kodo
Millet (Paspalum scrobiculatum L.)” was conducted during Kharif 2025 at the College of Agriculture,
Shivamogga, Karnataka, to evaluate the influence of graded levels of potassium on soil physico-chemi-
cal properties and nutrient availability. The experiment was laid out in a Randomized Complete Block
Design (RCBD) with three replications and seven treatments comprising absolute control, recom-
mended dose of fertilizers (RDF) and different levels of potassium applied along with RDF. The results
revealed that soil pH, electrical conductivity, organic carbon, exchangeable calcium and magnesium,
and available micronutrients were not significantly influenced by potassium application. However,
available phosphorus, potassium and sulphur in soil were significantly affected by the treatments.
Application of potassium along with RDF improved the availability of these nutrients and enhanced the
overall soil fertility status. Higher potassium levels maintained greater available potassium throughout
the crop growth period, while moderate potassium supplementation along with RDF was found effec-
tive in improving the availability of phosphorus and sulphur. Thus, potassium application beyond RDF
improved soil nutrient availability without adversely affecting the physico-chemical properties of soil,
indicating its potential role in sustaining soil fertility under kodo millet cultivation.
Keywords: Kodo millet, Potassium levels, Soil properties, Nutrient availability, Soil fertility, Available
potassium, Phosphorus and sulphur
Introduction
In recent years, millets have gained significant attention as an important alternative to major cereal crops due
to their nutritional value and adaptability to adverse climatic conditions. Millets have been cultivated since
ancient civilizations and were among the earliest crops grown during the early plough-based agricultural era.
Traditionally, millets were classified into major and minor groups based on the extent of cultivation and seed
size. Sorghum and pearl millet are categorized as major millets, whereas finger millet, foxtail millet, proso
millet, barnyard millet, kodo millet, little millet, and browntop millet are considered minor or small millets.
However, with the growing recognition of their rich nutritional composition, millets are now widely referred
to as nutri-cereals. These crops serve as staple foods in many countries of the semi-arid tropics, particularly
in Asia and Africa, where low rainfall and poor soil fertility limit the cultivation of other major food crops.
In such regions, millets continue to play a crucial role in ensuring food and nutritional security, providing es-
sential carbohydrates, protein, vitamins, minerals, and dietary fibre.
Kodo millet is well known for its remarkable drought tolerance compared to other millets. It can grow suc-
cessfully on marginal soils and produce economically viable grain yields even under adverse environmental
conditions. The crop requires relatively less water
and exhibits excellent drought tolerance, making it suitable for rainfed agriculture. Kodo millet can thrive in
low-fertility soils because its nutrient requirement is minimal, and excessive nutrient application may even
lead to lodging. It has the ability to produce relatively high yields within a short duration, which adds to its
economic importance. Among the small millets, it is considered one of the hardiest crops and is generally
cultivated during the kharif season with the onset of the monsoon.
Potassium is an essential nutrient element required by all living organisms, including plants and animals, and
its importance in Indian agriculture has increased with the intensification of farming systems. It exists in
plants as a monovalent cation (K+) and is present in the highest concentration in the cell sap; therefore, it is
often referred to as the “master cation.” Potassium is highly mobile within the plant and plays a crucial role
in several physiological and biochemical processes. It is involved in water relations, enzyme activation, os-
motic regulation, energy transfer, translocation of assimilates, photosynthesis, and protein synthesis (Mengel
and Kirkby, 1978) [1]. Nearly sixty enzymes require potassium for their activation. In addition to its role in
metabolic processes such as grain and seed formation, potassium improves the quality of agricultural pro

In rainfed regions, the yield potential of small millets, including kodo
millet, remains relatively low due to limited fertilizer use and inade-
quate crop management practices. Although kodo millet is often con-
sidered a low-input crop, improved cultivars respond favourably to
balanced nutrient management. Balanced application of nitrogen (N),
phosphorus (P) and potassium (K) is essential for achieving optimum
crop growth and productivity. Nitrogen promotes vegetative growth
and chlorophyll synthesis, phosphorus supports root development and
energy transfer, while potassium regulates enzyme activation, water
relations, photosynthesis, assimilate translocation and stress tolerance.
The application of only nitrogen and phosphorus without adequate
potassium may result in nutrient imbalance, reduced nutrient use effi-
ciency, poor translocation of photosynthates, lower drought tolerance
and suboptimal yield realization.
At present, nutrient recommendations for kodo millet primarily focus
on nitrogen and phosphorus, while specific potassium recommenda-
tions are generally lacking. Continuous cultivation under such unbal-
anced fertilization practices may lead to depletion of soil potassium
reserves and reduced crop productivity over time. Therefore, evaluat-
ing appropriate potassium levels under the existing N and P fertiliza-
tion regime is essential for developing a balanced nutrient manage-
ment strategy for sustainable production of kodo millet. Keeping the
above facts in view, the investigation was carried out to study the ef-
fect of potassium levels on soil properties and nutrient availability un-
der kodo millet (Paspalum scrobiculatum).
Methodology
Before initiation of the experiment, a representative composite soil
sample was collected from the experimental field, air-dried under
shade, processed and passed through a 2 mm sieve for characteriza-
tion. The experimental soil was sandy loam in texture and acidic in
reaction, with a pH of 5.31, electrical conductivity of 0.131 dS m−1 at
25 °C and organic carbon content of 3.38 g kg−1. The soil contained
289.32 kg ha−1 available N, 86.32 kg ha−1 of available P2O5 and
280.44 kg ha−1 of available K2O. Exchangeable Ca and Mg were 1.98
and 0.94 cmol(p+) kg−1, respectively, while available S 12.08 mg
kg−1. The DTPA-extractable Zn, Cu, Mn and Fe contents were 1.20,
2.78, 12.24 and 28.15 mg kg−1, respectively.
The experiment was laid out in a Randomized Complete Block
Design with seven treatments and three replications. Kodo millet
(Paspalum scrobiculatum L.) cv. TNAU 86 was used as the test crop.
The gross plot size was 4.5 m × 3.0 m, with a row spacing of 22.5 cm
and a seed rate of 12.5 kg ha−1. The recommended dose of fertilizer
was 20:20:00 kg N: P2O5: K2O ha−1, with FYM applied at 2.5 t ha−1.
The treatments comprised an absolute control (T1), RDF (T2), RDF +
K2O at 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 kg ha−1 (T3, T4, T5, T6 and T7
respectively)

Soil samples were collected from the experimental plots at 0–15 cm
depth before sowing and after harvest to assess the changes in soil
physico-chemical and nutrient properties due to potassium applica-
tion. The collected samples were shade-dried, gently powdered,
passed through a 2 mm sieve and stored for further analysis. Soil pH
was determined in a 1:2.5 soil-to-water suspension, while electrical
conductivity was measured in the same extract using a conductivity
meter. Organic carbon was estimated by the Walkley and Black
method (1934) [2]. Available nitrogen was determined by the alkaline
potassium permanganate method (Subbaiah and Asija, 1956) [3],
available phosphorus by Bray’s method as described by Jackson,
(1973) [4], and available potassium by extraction with neutral normal
ammonium acetate followed by flame photometric determination
(Jackson, 1973) [4]. Exchangeable calcium and magnesium were de-
termined using neutral normal ammonium acetate extraction followed
by EDTA titration (Jackson, 1973) [4], while available sulphur was es-
timated by the calcium chloride extraction and turbidimetric method
(Black, 1965) [5]. DTPA-extractable iron, manganese, zinc and copper
were determined using DTPA extraction followed by estimation using
an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (Lindsay et al., 1978) [6].
The data obtained from the experiment were subjected to analysis of
variance appropriate for the Randomized Complete Block Design fol-
lowing the procedure described by Gomez and Gomez (1984) [7]. The
significance of treatment effects was tested using the F-test at 5 per
cent probability level. The standard error of mean (Sem ±) was calcu-
lated for each parameter, and the critical difference (CD) at 5 per cent
probability was worked out wherever the treatment effect was signifi-
cant. Non-significant treatment effects were denoted as NS.

Available nitrogen
Available nitrogen content of soil after harvest differed significantly
among the treatments. The significantly higher available nitrogen
(281.32 kg ha−1) was recorded in T2 (RDF = 20:20:00 N: P2O5: K2O
kg ha−1), which was on par with T3 (RDF + 10 kg K2O ha−1)
(279.31 kg ha−1) and significantly superior to the remaining treat-
ments. The comparatively lower available nitrogen (265.24 kg ha−1)
observed in T5 (RDF + 20 kg K2O ha−1) may be attributed to en-
hanced crop growth and greater nitrogen uptake under optimum
potassium supply. Potassium plays a vital role in root development,
nutrient transport and efficient utilization of nutrients, resulting in in-
creased removal of nitrogen by the crop and consequently lower
residual nitrogen in the soil after harvest. The improved nutrient use
efficiency and higher biomass production under optimum potassium
nutrition might have contributed to greater depletion of available soil
nitrogen (Rout et al., 2017 [11], Divyashree et al., 2018 [12]) [11].
Similar findings indicating reduced residual soil nitrogen were re-
ported by Dwivedi et al. (2016) [13].

Available phosphorus
The post-harvest soil available phosphorus was significantly influ-
enced by potassium application. Among the treatments, T5 (RDF +
20 kg K2O ha−1) recorded significantly higher available P2O5 content
(86.79 kg ha−1) followed by T6 (RDF + 25 kg K2O ha−1) (84.57 kg
ha−1) and lowest was recorded in absolute control (79.00 kg ha−1).
The increased availability of phosphorus under optimum potassium
application may be due to improved nutrient interaction and enhanced
nutrient recycling within the soil–plant system. Better root prolifera-
tion and increased biological activity in the rhizosphere under bal-
anced nutrient supply may have contributed to greater phosphorus
availability in the soil. In addition, efficient utilization of applied nu-
trients and reduced nutrient losses might have helped maintain higher
residual phosphorus levels after harvest. Similar results were reported
by Jat et al. (2014) [14] and Asodariya et al. (2023) [15].
Available potassium
Available potassium content of soil differed significantly among the
treatments at 30, 60, 90 DAS and at harvest. The available potassium
content increased progressively with increasing levels of potassium
application. Among the treatments, T7 (RDF + 30 kg K2O ha−1)
recorded the highest available potassium content at all growth stages
(299.56, 278.29, 275.08, 268.44 kg ha−1 at 30, 60, 90DAS and at har-
vest respectively), whereas the lowest values were recorded in the ab-
solute control. The higher availability of potassium under T7 was due
to the direct addition of potassium through fertilizer application, re-
sulting in increased concentration of exchangeable and soil solution
potassium. The higher residual potassium content at harvest under T6
and T7 indicates that potassium application exceeded crop demand,
resulting in greater potassium retention in the soil. Wherever there
was no application of potassium, the depletion of potassium was ob-
served. Available K was depleted with either no K (T1 & T2) or re-
duced dose of K fertilizer. Similar findings were reported by Srinivasa
et al. (2019) [16] and Sundaresh and Basavaraja (2017) [17] .

Exchangeable calcium
The post-harvest soil exchangeable calcium content under kodo millet
plots varied non significantly and ranged from 1.80 to 1.88 cmol (p+)
kg−1 among different levels of K and RDF. The higher value of 1.88
cmol (p+) kg−1 was observed in T5 (RDF + 20 kg K2O ha−1), while
the lower exchangeable Ca (1.81 cmol (p+) kg−1) was recorded in T7
(RDF + 30 kg K2O ha−1). This may be attributed to the moderate lev-
els of potassium applied, which were insufficient to induce a marked
antagonistic effect on calcium availability. Ahmad and Maathuis
(2014) [18] also reported that adequate potassium fertilization gener-
ally does not adversely affect calcium availability under balanced
fertilization.
Exchangeable magnesium
The post-harvest soil exchangeable magnesium content under kodo
millet varied narrowly from 0.82 to 0.89 cmol (p+) kg−1. The higher
exchangeable Mg (0.89 cmol (p+) kg−1) was recorded in T5 (RDF +
20 kg K2O ha−1), while lower exchangeable Mg (0.83 cmol (p+)
kg−1) was recorded in T7 (RDF + 30 kg K2O ha−1). A slight numeri-
cal decline in Mg was observed at higher potassium levels, though the
differences were statistically non-significant. Similar observations
were made by Mengel and Kirkby (2001) [1] and Ahmad and
Maathuis (2014) [18].
Available sulphur (mg kg−1)
The post-harvest soil sulphur status varied significantly and ranged
from 10.38 mg kg−1 to 12.57 mg kg−1 among the treatments.
Significantly higher sulphur content (12.57 mg kg−1) was observed in
T5 (RDF + 20 kg K2O ha−1) and the lowest sulphur content
(10.38 mg kg−1) was observed in T1 (Absolute control) compared to
other treatments. This may be attributed to the synergistic interaction
between potassium and sulphur in the soil–plant system. Adequate
potassium nutrition enhances root growth, nutrient uptake and overall
crop vigour, which improves sulphur utilization and contributes to
better sulphur cycling in the soil. Similar findings were reported by
Baque et al. (2006) [19].
DTPA extractable micronutrients
The post-harvest DTPA extractable micronutrient contents under kodo
millet showed only minor variations among treatments and the differ-
ences were statistically non-significant. Iron content ranged from
20.97 to 26.42 mg kg−1, zinc from 0.68 to 0.73 mg kg−1, manganese
from 10.08 to 12.10 mg kg−1, and copper from 1.85 to 2.60 mg kg−1.
The highest values of Fe (26.42 mg kg−1), Zn (0.73 mg kg−1), Mn
(12.10 mg kg−1) and Cu (2.60 mg kg−1) were recorded in T5, whereas
the lowest values of Fe (20.97 mg kg−1), Zn (0.68 mg kg−1), Mn
(10.08 mg kg−1) and Cu (1.85 mg kg−1) were observed in the abso-
lute control (T1). However, the variations among treatments were sta-
tistically non-significant. Adequate potassium nutrition promotes root
growth and root exudation of organic acids, which can enhance the
solubility and mobilization of micronutrients such as iron, zinc and
manganese in the rhizosphere. Similar findings were reported by Jat
et al. (2014) [14] and Ramachandrappa et al. (2014) [20]
Conclusion
The present investigation revealed that potassium application had a
positive influence on post-harvest soil properties under kodo millet.
Potassium application improved the availability of phosphorus, potas-
sium and sulphur, while soil pH, EC, organic carbon, exchangeable
Ca, Mg and DTPA-extractable micronutrients remained statistically
unaffected. Among the different potassium levels, application of
20 kg K2O ha−1 in addition to RDF maintained a higher residual nu-
trient status and contributed to better soil fertility after harvest. Soil
pH, electrical conductivity and organic carbon were not significantly
affected by potassium application, indicating that potassium fertiliza-
tion did not adversely influence the chemical properties of the soil.
Higher potassium levels beyond 20 kg K2O ha−1 did not result in sub-
stantial additional improvement in soil nutrient status. Therefore, ap-
plication of 20 kg K2O ha−1 along with the recommended dose of ni-
trogen and phosphorus can be considered an optimum nutrient man
