Mapping irrigable land potential for surface 
irrigation through GIS‑based multi‑criteria 
analysis in Ethiopia’s Gidabo watershed
Henok Mekonnen Aragaw1* and Azmeraw Alemu Tiku1 
Abstract 
Identifying land suitable for surface irrigation is essential for improving agricultural pro-
duction while ensuring efficient use of land and water resources. However, such assess-
ments are often challenging in data-scarce regions where terrain, soil conditions, land 
use, and access to water and infrastructure vary widely. This study evaluated land suit-
ability for surface irrigation in the 3180 km2 Gidabo Watershed in Ethiopia’s Rift Valley 
Lakes Basin. Seven biophysical and accessibility factors were analyzed: slope, soil depth, 
soil texture, soil drainage, land use/land cover, and distance to rivers and roads. These 
factors were standardized using FAO guidelines and local expert knowledge and inte-
grated through the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) and GIS-based weighted overlay 
analysis. The results indicate that slope was the most influential factor, with a weight 
of 46.34%, followed by distance to rivers (28.51%) and soil characteristics (14.46%). The 
AHP consistency ratio of 0.053 indicated acceptable consistency in the expert judg-
ments. Overall, 93.23% of the watershed was physically suitable for surface irrigation 
to varying degrees (S1–S3). However, only 35.79% (113,831 ha) fell within the highly 
and moderately suitable classes (S1–S2), representing areas with more favorable 
conditions for irrigation development. Highly suitable land (S1) accounted for 4.32% 
of the watershed and was mainly located in valley bottoms and lowland areas 
along the Gidabo River and its major tributaries. In contrast, steep slopes exceeding 
8%, which cover about 62% of the watershed, represent the main physical constraint. 
The distribution of S1–S2 land also varied considerably among sub-basins, ranging 
from 471 to 21,486 ha. These findings highlight that while most of the watershed 
has some level of physical suitability, the area with favorable conditions for practical 
irrigation development is much more limited. The resulting suitability map provides 
a useful tool for prioritizing areas for detailed feasibility studies and guiding irrigation 
investment planning in the Gidabo Watershed and similar data-scarce watersheds 
in sub-Saharan Africa.

1 Introduction
The increasing demand for food production, driven by global population growth, pre -
sents persistent challenges in balancing agricultural productivity with sustainable 
resource use [3, 28]. Irrigated agriculture occupies only 20% of cultivated land but con -
tributes nearly 40% of global food production, underscoring its critical role in stabilizing 
food supplies [18, 38]. Assessing land suitability for surface irrigation is therefore a stra -
tegic priority, particularly in regions vulnerable to climate variability and rapid popula -
tion growth [8, 18].
Land suitability assessment (LSA) provides a scientific basis for sustainable devel -
opment by identifying where irrigation can be implemented cost-effectively and with 
minimal environmental degradation [5, 6]. Integrating Geographic Information Systems 
(GIS) with Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA), particularly the Analytical Hier -
archy Process (AHP), has proven highly effective for mapping irrigation suitability [4, 
12]. AHP organizes complex problems into hierarchical structures, enabling systematic 
decision-making through pairwise comparisons [35].
Ethiopia exemplifies both the potential and constraints of irrigation development in 
sub-Saharan Africa. Agriculture contributes approximately 44% of GDP and employs 
nearly 80% of the population, yet despite an estimated 3.7 million hectares of irrigable 
land, less than 15% has been developed [10, 18, 22, 31, 33]. The sector remains predomi-
nantly rain-fed, making it highly vulnerable to rainfall variability and climate change 
[21]. Climate projections for East Africa indicate increasing uncertainty in precipitation 
patterns [27]. Recognizing this, the Government of Ethiopia has prioritized irrigation 
through successive national development strategies, including the Growth and Trans -
formation Plans and the current Ten-Year Development Plan (2020/21–2029/30) (EPDC 
[17]). These strategies recognize irrigation as central to agricultural transformation and 
food security, with targets to develop substantial new irrigated areas [33]. However, 
planning has often proceeded without detailed assessments of land suitability, leading to 
operational challenges, including water shortages and underutilization of infrastructure 
[1, 8].
Several studies have addressed this gap in Ethiopian basins. Girma et  al. [24] found 
8.5% of the Omo-Gibe Basin highly suitable for surface irrigation. Hagos et  al. [26] 
reported 63% of the Minch Yekest catchment as highly suitable. Muluneh et  al. [34] 
assessed the Rift Valley Lakes Basin (RVLB) and found that 6.2% of the land was highly 
suitable. More recently, Legesse et  al. [29] identified slope (40.74%) as the dominant 
limiting factor in the upper Awash Basin, with only 35.8% of the area highly to moder -
ately suitable. Similarly, Abagissa et al. [2] reported that 36% of the Gololcha Watershed 
was unsuitable due to slope, which received the highest weight (46.36%) in their AHP 
analysis.
Despite these efforts, a comprehensive LSA for surface irrigation in the Gidabo Water-
shed is still lacking. This gap is consequential because the watershed possesses three 
distinctive characteristics: (i) it straddles the steep eastern escarpment of the Main Ethi -
opian Rift (elevation 1171–3213 m), generating sharp transitions in slope and soil depth; 
(ii) its land-use mosaic smallholder enset-coffee-maize systems with agroforestry, differs 
from cereal-dominated or pastoral systems; (iii) its deeply incised river valley requires 
locally validated proximity-to-river thresholds. Beyond watershed-specific needs, this

study introduces three methodological innovations: (i) transparent documentation of 
expert panel composition and voting outcomes; (ii) a two-round Delphi-style threshold 
validation with explicit consensus criteria; and (iii) sub-basin dual ranking (absolute and 
percentage area) to distinguish sub-basins suitable for central infrastructure from those 
better served by distributed small-scale schemes.
This study addresses this knowledge gap by conducting a systematic GIS-based multi-
criteria LSA for surface irrigation in the Gidabo Watershed. The specific objectives are 
to: (i) map the spatial distribution of suitability parameters; (ii) determine their relative 
weights using AHP with rigorous consistency assessment; (iii) integrate weighted crite -
ria through GIS-based weighted overlay; and (iv) quantify suitable lands at watershed 
and sub-basin scales. In general, this study addresses the question: Where, and under 
what biophysical and infrastructure constraints, is surface irrigation economically viable 
in the Gidabo Watershed? By providing a spatially explicit, reproducible assessment, this 
study aims to inform the prioritization of irrigation investments and offer a transferable 
framework for data-scarce regions in sub-Saharan Africa.
2 Study area description
The Gidabo Watershed is situated in the Rift Valley Lakes Basin of Ethiopia, between 
latitudes 6°05′ to 6°45′ N and longitudes 38°05′ to 38°35′ E (Fig.  1). The watershed cov-
ers approximately 3,180.49 km2. The watershed’s topography is characterized by a steep

The climate is characterized as sub-humid, with a bimodal rainfall pattern: the main

watershed supports diverse land uses, including agriculture (cereals, coffee, enset, veg -

3 Methodology
This study employed an integrated approach combining Geographic Information System 
(GIS) techniques with the AHP and MCDM methods to evaluate and map land suit -
ability for surface irrigation development in the Gidabo Watershed. The methodology 
framework is presented in Fig. 2.
3.1 Data sources and preparation
3.1.1 Digital elevation model (DEM)
A 30 m resolution SRTM DEM was acquired from the USGS Earth Explorer platform 
(http:// earth explo rer. usgs. gov). The DEM was used for watershed delineation, slope 
analysis, and derivation of topographic parameters.
3.1.2 Land use/land cover (LULC)
Landsat 8 OLI/TIRS imagery (path 168, rows 55–56, February 2020, 30  m resolution) 
was obtained from USGS. Image preprocessing, including layer stacking, geometric cor -
rection, and sub-setting, was performed in ERDAS Imagine. Supervised classification 
using the maximum likelihood algorithm was employed, supported by 249 ground truth 
points collected during field surveys, supplemented by Google Earth imagery and topo -
graphic maps.
3.1.3 Soil data
A 1:250,000-scale soil map was obtained from the Ministry of Water and Energy 
(MoWE) of Ethiopia. Soil types were classified according to the FAO/UNESCO soil clas-
sification system. Soil physical properties (texture, depth, and drainage) were derived 
from the soil map to characterize irrigation suitability.
3.1.4 Infrastructure data
Road network data were obtained from the Ethiopian Roads Authority (ERA) geoda -
tabase (Ethio-Roads), while river network data were extracted from the Ethio-Rivers 
database maintained by the Ministry of Water and Energy. Road network data included 
all-weather primary asphalt roads, secondary gravel roads, and major regional feeder 
routes. Euclidean distance tools in ArcGIS were used to generate proximity maps (in 
meters) for both roads and perennial rivers, representing accessibility to markets and 
water sources, respectively (Fig. 3).

3.2 Selection of suitability factors
Based on an extensive literature review [1 , 2, 11, 25, 29], FAO guidelines [19], and 
consultations with local agricultural and water resources experts, seven key param -
eters were identified as critical determinants for surface irrigation suitability: slope, 
soil depth, soil texture, soil drainage, land use/land cover (LULC), proximity to rivers, 
and proximity to roads. Each parameter was selected based on its direct or indirect 
influence on the feasibility, cost-effectiveness, and sustainability of surface irrigation 
systems.
To ensure the credibility and relevance of the selected criteria, a panel of experts 
was convened to participate in two structured exercises: (i) validation of the seven 
selected parameters (confirmation that no critical factor was omitted), and (ii) adjust -
ment of classification thresholds based on local conditions. The panel comprised 12 
experts from three institutional categories, selected based on their disciplinary exper -
tise, professional experience in irrigation development, and familiarity with the Gid -
abo Watershed. Table 1 summarizes the composition of the expert panel.

3.3 Criteria standardization and reclassification
All spatial datasets were projected to WGS 1984 UTM Zone 37N and resampled to a 
30-m grid using the nearest-neighbor method to maintain data integrity. Euclidean dis -
tance tools in ArcGIS 10.8 were used to generate proximity maps. This ensured pixel-
to-pixel compatibility for subsequent overlay analyses. Each factor was reclassified into 
four suitability classes according to the FAO framework:  Highly Suitable (S1), Mod -
erately Suitable (S2), Marginally Suitable (S3), and Not Suitable (N). The classification 
thresholds were established based on an extensive review of peer-reviewed literature 
[2, 3, 11–13, 25, 26, 32] and FAO guidelines, and expert knowledge of local conditions. 
Table 2 presents the detailed suitability classification criteria for each parameter. Thresh-
olds were determined through a three-step process: (i initial values were extracted from 
FAO guidelines and peer-reviewed studies in Ethiopian and East African basins; (ii these 
values were presented to the expert panel for validation and adjustment based on local 
conditions; (iii final thresholds represent consensus among at least 10 of the 12 experts 
following two rounds of discussion.
For slope thresholds, the 0–2% range (S1) follows FAO [19] and Abagissa et al. [2] as 
ideal for surface irrigation, while 2–5% (S2) is permissible with moderate terracing based 
on Belay and Bewket [9] and Dawit et al. [13], the 5–8% range (S3) requires significant 
earthworks and was validated by the expert panel based on local scheme experience, and 
>8% (N) was unanimously agreed as infeasible without major terracing. For soil depth,

(S2) is from Gonfa et al. [25], 25–50 cm (S3) from Legesse et al. [29], and < 25 cm (N) 
from FAO [19] confirmed by Lithic Leptosols mapping. Soil texture thresholds follow

3.4 Analytic hierarchy process (AHP) for weight determination
3.4.1 Pairwise comparison matrix
The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) was employed to determine the relative impor -
tance (weights) of each parameter in influencing land suitability for surface irrigation. 
A hierarchical structure was established with the goal (land suitability for surface irriga -
tion) at the top, followed by the seven evaluation parameters at the criterion level [23].
A pairwise comparison matrix was constructed based on expert judgments from 
soil scientists, irrigation engineers, hydrologists, and agricultural planners from Dilla 
University, the Ministry of Water and Energy, and the Rift Valley Lakes Basin Author -
ity. Experts were asked to rate the relative importance of each pair of parameters using 
Saaty’s 1–9 fundamental scale, where 1 indicates equal importance, and 9 indicates 
extreme importance of one factor over the other. The geometric mean of individual 
expert judgments was calculated to aggregate the pairwise comparisons into a single 
consensus matrix.

3.4.2 Normalization and weight calculation
The pairwise comparison matrix was normalized by dividing each element by the sum of 
its column. Parameter weights (eigenvector) were then computed as the average of the 
normalized values across rows. The principal eigenvalue (λmax) was calculated by mul -
tiplying the pairwise comparison matrix by the weight vector and averaging the ratios of 
the resulting vector components to the corresponding weights.

3.6 Sub‑basin level analysis
The 12 delineated sub-basins (derived from the 30 m DEM, with a threshold area of 
3,000  ha for stream definition) were used as spatial units to calculate the irrigable-
suitable area. For each sub-basin, the total area of S1 and S2 classes was extracted 
using zonal statistics in ArcGIS, and the percentage of sub-basin area classified as 
irrigable (S1+ S2) was computed. This sub-basin-level disaggregation enables targeted 
prioritization of irrigation investments.
4 Results
4.1 Land suitability for surface irrigation
4.1.1 Individual factor suitability
4.1.1.1 Slope suitability Slope suitability is summarized in Table 4, with physically suit-
able areas (0–8%) covering 37.98% of the watershed, concentrated in valley bottoms along 
the Gidabo River (Fig. 4). Moderately suitable areas (2–5%) occupy 546.22  km2 (17.17%), 
while marginally suitable areas (5–8%) cover 493.33  km2 (15.51%). Steep slopes (> 8%), 
comprising 62.02% (1,972.24  km2) of the watershed, were classified as not suitable, pri -
marily in the highland escarpments of the Soka Sonicha mountain range.
The dominance of steep slopes (> 8%) across 62% of the watershed represents the 
most significant topographic constraint to surface irrigation development in the 
Gidabo Watershed. This finding is consistent with studies in other Ethiopian high -
land watersheds, where terrain complexity substantially limits irrigation suitability. 
For instance, Abagissa et al. [2 ] reported that 36% of the Gololcha River Watershed 
was unsuitable for surface irrigation due to slope constraints, while Legesse et al. [29] 
found that 54.38% of the Dendi District in the upper Awash Basin fell within unsuit -
able slope classes (> 8%). Similarly, Gonfa et al. [25] identified slope as the dominant 
limiting factor in the Becho Plain, accounting for 46.34% of the total weight in their

5 Discussion
5.1 Dominant controls on irrigation suitability
The AHP-derived weights identified slope (46.34%) as the most influential factor deter -
mining surface irrigation suitability in the Gidabo Watershed, followed by proximity 
to rivers (28.51%), soil properties (14.46%), proximity to roads (6.01%), and land use/
land cover (4.64%). The primacy of slope aligns with established land evaluation princi -
ples and numerous Ethiopian irrigation suitability studies [4, 25]. Surface irrigation sys -
tems require gentle gradients to ensure uniform water distribution, minimize the risk of 
erosion, and reduce land preparation costs. The finding that 62% of the watershed has 
slopes exceeding 8% represents the most significant topographic constraint, effectively 
excluding the highland escarpments of the Soka Sonicha mountain range from consid -
eration for irrigation. This proportion is comparable to findings from the Dendi Dis -
trict, where Legesse et al. [29] reported 54.38% of slopes as unsuitable, and the Gololcha 
River Watershed, where Abagissa et al. [2] found 36% of slopes unsuitable due to slope 
constraints. The slightly higher proportion in the Gidabo Watershed reflects its steeper 
overall topography, characteristic of the Rift Valley escarpment zone.
The high weight assigned to proximity to rivers (28.51%) underscores the critical 
importance of access to water sources for surface irrigation systems. This finding is 
consistent with the Dendi District, where Legesse et al. [29] assigned river proximity a 
weight of 40.74%, and the Becho Plain, where Gonfa et al. [25] identified water-source 
proximity as a primary determinant. The concentration of suitable areas within 2  km 
of perennial rivers (35% of the watershed) reflects the practical reality that conveyance 
costs increase substantially with distance from water sources. Areas more than 4  km 
from rivers (28% of the watershed) would require pumping or extended canal systems, 
increasing both capital costs and operational energy requirements and making them 
economically unviable for smallholder irrigation schemes [9].
Soil properties, though weighted lower than slope and river proximity (14.46%), 
remain critically important. The integrated soil suitability analysis classified 64.92% of 
soils as suitable (S1–S2) for irrigation, with Humic Nitosols and Eutric Vertisols being 
the most favorable. These soils are characterized by deep profiles (> 120 cm), high clay 
content, and high water-holding capacity (150–200  mm/m), making them particularly 
suitable for irrigated agriculture. The 50.27% of the watershed with soil depth >120 cm 
represents a major comparative advantage for irrigation expansion, as deep soils enhance 
root proliferation, moisture storage, and nutrient buffering [4]. However, the presence of 
Lithic Leptosols on steep slopes (25.80% of the watershed) creates a compounded con -
straint, effectively excluding large portions of the upper watershed from consideration 
for irrigation. This co-occurrence of shallow soils and steep terrain mirrors observations 
from the Gololcha watershed, where shallow soils were consistently associated with 
steep slopes [2], and from the Upper Awash Basin, where deeper soils were found in 
depositional areas with gentle slopes [1].
5.2 Spatial patterns and environmental gradients
The integrated land suitability analysis revealed a pronounced spatial gradient in irri -
gation suitability across the Gidabo Watershed. Highly suitable lands (S1, 4.32%) are

concentrated in valley bottoms and lowland plains along the Gidabo River and its major 
tributaries, where gentle slopes (< 2%), deep soils (> 120  cm), and favorable drainage 
conditions converge. Moderately suitable lands (S2, 31.47%) occupy adjacent areas with 
slopes of 2–5% and moderately well-drained soils, while marginally suitable lands (S3, 
57.44%) dominate the mid-slope positions where slope (5–8%) and imperfect drain -

Nitosols, and near-neutral pH, creating optimal edaphic conditions for surface irriga -
tion. In contrast, the southern and eastern zones exhibit steeper slopes, shallower Lepto-
sols, and more acidic conditions, which limit the feasibility of intensive surface irrigation 
without significant amendment. This gradient is consistent with observations from the 
Dhidhessa River Basin, where Dawit et al. [13] found that irrigation potential was high -
est in downstream reaches with gentle slopes and deeper soils, and from the Becho 
Plain, where Gonfa et al. [25] reported that Vertisols and Nitisols in valley bottoms were 
the most favorable soil types.
The proportion of highly suitable land (4.32%) in the Gidabo Watershed is compara -
ble to, but slightly lower than, that reported in other Ethiopian basin-scale studies. For 
example, Girma et al. [24] reported 8.5% S1 land in the Omo-Gibe Basin, while Muluneh 
et al. [34] identified approximately 6% highly suitable land across the Rift Valley Lakes 
Basin. The lower proportion in the Gidabo watershed reflects its steeper overall topog -
raphy and the more restrictive classification thresholds applied in this study, particu -
larly the reduction of the S2 river proximity upper bound from 4 to 3 km based on local 
expert consensus (10/12 agreement). This adjustment acknowledges the practical con -
straints of canal construction in the dissected topography of the Gidabo River valley, 
where conveyance losses increase substantially beyond 3 km.
5.3 Comparison with regional assessments
The finding that 93.23% of the watershed is physically suitable when marginally suitable 
lands are included (S1–S3) aligns with the narrative of substantial irrigation potential in 
Ethiopian basins. However, the critical insight is that only 35.79% falls within the highly 
to moderately suitable classes (S1–S2), which are economically viable for development. 
This distinction between physical suitability (technical feasibility) and economic suit -
ability (cost-effectiveness) is often blurred in irrigation planning, leading to overestima -
tion of the developable area [1, 14].
The S1–S2 proportion of 35.79% (113,831  ha) is consistent with the Dendi District, 
where Legesse et al. [29] reported 35.8% of the area as S1–S2, and the Gololcha water -
shed, where Abagissa et al. [2] found 40% S1–S2. This convergence suggests that, despite 
variations in local topography, approximately one-third of Ethiopian highland watershed 
areas may be economically suitable for surface irrigation when biophysical and infra -
structure factors are systematically integrated. The remaining two-thirds are constrained 
by either steep slopes, shallow soils, protected land uses, or excessive distance from 
water sources or roads.

5.4 Implications for irrigation planning and policy
The land suitability results have direct implications for irrigation development strategies 
in the Gidabo Watershed and analogous Ethiopian highland basins. First, the dominance 
of slope as a limiting factor suggests that surface irrigation should be restricted to areas 
with slopes < 5% (S1–S2). Areas with slopes of 5–8% (S3) may be considered for irriga -
tion only with substantial terracing investments, which increase capital costs by an esti -
mated 30–60%, according to regional studies [9]. Given the prevalence of smallholder 
farming systems with limited access to credit, S3 lands should be deferred for irrigation 
development until a cost–benefit analysis is completed or reserved for alternative uses 
such as rain-fed agriculture, agroforestry, or grazing.
Second, the high weight assigned to river proximity (28.51%) supports prioritizing 
areas within 2 km of perennial rivers for immediate irrigation investment. These areas 
minimize conveyance costs and reduce the need for pumping infrastructure, making 
them viable for smallholder schemes. Areas within 2–3 km of rivers (S2) remain viable 
but may require public subsidy or credit support. Areas more than 3  km from rivers 
should be excluded from surface irrigation planning unless conjunctive use of ground -
water or water storage infrastructure is feasible [37].
Third, the soil suitability analysis provides crop-specific guidance. 64.9% of soils are 
classified as suitable (S1–S2) and are dominated by Humic Nitosols and Eutric Vertisols, 
which have high water-holding capacity (150–200  mm/m), allowing longer irrigation 
intervals (7–10 days) and reducing labor requirements. These soils are highly suitable for 
water-intensive crops such as maize and tomato. However, Vertisols in low-lying areas 
(25.66% of the watershed) have high clay content and are classified as poorly drained 
under natural conditions. These soils require careful water management, including land 
leveling, controlled furrow inflow rates, and provision of drainage outlets to prevent 
waterlogging.  In surface irrigation systems, water ponding is primarily a function of 
inadequate land preparation and excessive discharge rather than soil texture. Neverthe -
less, the swelling characteristics and slow infiltration rates of Vertisols make them more 
sensitive to these operational factors, necessitating site-specific design considerations. 
For these areas, raised bed planting or subsurface drainage should be specified in irri -
gation system designs, following recommendations from the Becho Plain [25] and the 
Dhidhessa River Basin [13].
Fourth, the LULC analysis indicates that agricultural land (15.10%) and grassland 
(23.09%) represent the most suitable categories for irrigation conversion, as they require 
minimal land clearance and have established access. Converting these areas to irrigated

Aragaw and Tiku Discover Environment (2026) 4:524 
 
agriculture may displace existing rain-fed production, requiring careful transition plan -
ning to avoid disruptions to food security. Bushland and mixed forest (31.66%, mar -
ginally suitable) would require clearance investments, which should be factored into 
project economics. Evergreen forests, wetlands, and water bodies (classified as not suit -
able) should be protected from irrigation development to maintain ecosystem services, 
including dry-season baseflow maintenance, sediment retention, and biodiversity con -
servation [16, 20].
5.5 Methodological contributions and transferability
This study contributes methodologically by demonstrating a transparent, reproducible, 
and locally validated approach for land suitability assessment in data-scarce regions. The 
integration of three key methodological elements enhances the credibility and applica -
bility of the results.
First, the use of a 12-member expert panel from three institutional categories (aca -
demic researchers, government water resources specialists, and agricultural experts) for 
factor validation and threshold adjustment ensures that the suitability criteria reflect 
both international standards (FAO guidelines) and local conditions. The two-round 
Delphi-style process, with consensus defined as ≥ 10 of 12 experts agreeing, provides 
a rigorous basis for threshold determination that can be replicated in other basins. 
The detailed documentation of expert composition, credentials, and voting outcomes 
(Tables 1 and 2) enhances transparency and allows readers to assess the credibility of the 
weight assignments.
Second, the AHP weight determination process, conducted by a subset of 10 experts 
with demonstrated familiarity with comparative judgment methods, yielded a con -
sistency ratio of 0.053, well below the 0.10 threshold. This indicates that the pairwise 
comparisons were logically consistent and that the derived weights (slope 46.34%, river 
proximity 28.51%, soil 14.46%, road proximity 6.01%, LULC 4.64%) are reliable for the 
overlay analysis. The consistency ratio of 0.053 is comparable to or better than those 
reported in similar Ethiopian studies: Legesse et  al. [29] reported CR = 0.07, Abagissa 
et al. [2] reported CR = 0.09, and Gonfa et al. [25] reported CR = 0.08. This consistency 
across studies suggests that the AHP method produces stable weight estimates when 
applied to irrigation suitability assessment in Ethiopian highland contexts.
Third, the sub-basin-level disaggregation of suitability results (Table  12) provides 
actionable spatial intelligence for investment prioritization. Ranking sub-basins by both 
absolute suitable area and the percentage of sub-basin area suitable enables decision-
makers to distinguish between sub-basins with large absolute potential (e.g., sub-basin 3, 
21,486 ha) and those with a high concentration of suitable land (e.g., sub-basin 6, 57.4% 
of its area). This dual ranking is essential for efficient resource allocation: sub-basins 
with large absolute suitable area justify investment in central infrastructure (canals, 
intake structures), while sub-basins with high concentration but small absolute area may 
be better served by small-scale, distributed schemes.
The methodological framework is readily transferable to other data-sparse regions. 
The core requirements, SRTM DEM, Landsat imagery, national soil maps, and road/river 
network data, are widely available across sub-Saharan Africa. The expert panel approach 
can be adapted to local institutional contexts, and the AHP weight determination can 
Page 24 of 27Aragaw and Tiku Discover Environment (2026) 4:524 
be conducted using free software. The primary limitation for transferability is the avail -
ability of local experts with sufficient familiarity with both irrigation engineering and the 
study area. In regions where such expertise is limited, the threshold values and weights 
from this study could serve as initial estimates, subject to local validation through sensi -
tivity analyses.
5.6 Limitations and future research directions
Several limitations of the land suitability assessment should be acknowledged. First, 
the assessment focused on biophysical and infrastructure factors and did not explic -
itly incorporate socioeconomic variables such as land tenure, farmer capacity, market 
accessibility, or institutional constraints. The absence of these factors means that areas 
identified as physically suitable (e.g., sub-basins 10, 3, and 7) may face significant eco -
nomic or social barriers to actual development, including fragmented landholdings that 
complicate collective irrigation schemes, limited farmer access to credit for irrigation 
infrastructure, or competing water uses not captured in the hydrological model [30, 34]. 
Consequently, the suitable area of 113,831 ha reported here should be interpreted as a 
biophysically based upper bound rather than a prediction of actual development out -
comes. Future research should integrate socioeconomic indicators into multi-criteria 
decision-making frameworks, potentially through participatory GIS approaches that 
combine remote sensing with community-based mapping.
Second, the soil data were obtained from a 1:250,000 scale national map, which may 
not capture local-scale variability in soil properties relevant to farm-level irrigation 
planning. This is particularly problematic for soil drainage classification, where local 
variations in topography and subsurface flow paths can significantly affect the risk of 
waterlogging. Future research should incorporate higher-resolution soil surveys, includ -
ing field measurements of saturated hydraulic conductivity and infiltration rates, to 
refine the suitability classification at the sub-basin scale.
Third, the assumption that proximity to roads (weighted at 6.01%) adequately cap -
tures market accessibility may be simplistic, as road quality, seasonal accessibility, and 
distance to specific markets (rather than any road) influence transaction costs. Future 
research should incorporate more detailed infrastructure data, including road surface 
type, all-weather access, and travel time to major market centers, to refine the accessibil-
ity component of the suitability assessment.
Fourth, the land suitability assessment did not consider alternative irrigation technolo-
gies (e.g., drip or sprinkler) that may be feasible on steeper slopes or poorer soils. While 
surface irrigation remains the most common and affordable technology for smallholder 
farmers in Ethiopia, future research should evaluate the suitability of alternative systems 
for S3 and N lands, comparing their capital costs, water-use efficiency, and crop produc-
tivity with surface irrigation on S1–S2 lands.
Finally, we acknowledge that sensitivity analysis could provide additional insight 
into the robustness of the AHP-derived weights; such analysis was beyond the 
scope of the current study. The inherent consistency checking within the AHP pro -
cess (CR = 0.053), the strong expert consensus (≥ 10 of 12 experts on all thresh -
olds), and the alignment of our weights with comparable studies (e.g., 46.34% slope 
weight matching Abagissa et al. [2 ] provide confidence in our results without formal

sensitivity testing. Future research could conduct one-at-a-time (OAT) or global sen -
sitivity analyses to further validate the weight assignments.
6 Conclusion
This study assessed where surface irrigation is most suitable in the Gidabo Watershed 
by combining GIS, AHP , and information on slope, soils, land use, and access to riv -
ers and roads. The analysis showed that 93.23% of the watershed is physically suitable 
to some degree (S1–S3). However, only 35.79% (113,831  ha) falls within the highly 
and moderately suitable classes (S1–S2). This difference is important because it shows 
that the total area that appears physically suitable can substantially overstate the area 
with favorable conditions for practical irrigation development. Highly suitable land 
covers only 4.32% of the watershed and is mainly concentrated in valley bottoms and 
lowland plains along the Gidabo River and its major tributaries.
Slope was the most important factor in the assessment, with an AHP weight of 
46.34%. More than 62% of the watershed has slopes greater than 8%, which represents 
the main topographic constraint to conventional surface irrigation. Distance to rivers 
was the second most important factor (28.51%), highlighting the importance of access 
to reliable water sources and the cost of conveying water to fields. Soil properties also 
influenced suitability, particularly in areas with shallow soils or poor drainage.
The distribution of suitable land varied considerably among the 12 sub-basins. Sub-
basin 3 contained the largest S1–S2 area (21,486 ha), followed by sub-basins 12 and 
10, while sub-basin 6 had the smallest area (471 ha). Together, sub-basins 3, 12, and 
10 contained 51.7% of the total S1–S2 area. These areas, therefore, provide an impor -
tant starting point for more detailed irrigation feasibility studies and investment plan -
ning. However, the suitability map should be regarded as a screening and planning 
tool rather than a substitute for site-specific engineering, economic, environmental, 
and water-availability assessments.
Overall, the study reveals that combining spatial information on terrain, soils, land 
use, and infrastructure can help identify areas with more favorable conditions for sur -
face irrigation development in a data-scarce watershed. The results can support more 
targeted field investigations, help guide irrigation investment planning in the Gidabo 
watershed, and provide a useful basis for similar assessments in other heterogeneous 
watersheds in sub-Saharan Africa. Future studies should complement the present assess-
ment with detailed soil and field surveys, water-availability analysis, economic feasibility 
assessment, and sensitivity analysis to further refine irrigation development priorities.
