Operational evidence standards for machine
learning in wastewater treatment
Siyuan Jiang, Ying Yang, Keqiang Zhang, Yaoru Mao & Xiuwen Cheng
 Check for updates
Machine learning studies in water treatment often
report high retrospective accuracy but rarely show
whether models can support operational decisions.
We analyse 423 full-text studies and a bounded six-
plant wastewater-treatment scenario. Plant
deployment (2.8%), real-time testing (5.2%), future-
facing validation (18.2%) and uncertainty reporting
(8.5%) remain uncommon. We propose an
operation-aware evidence standard linking sensing
context, validation timing, uncertainty and control
role to deployment claims.
Why operational evidence matters
Machine learning (ML) is increasingly used to predict water-treatment
performance, optimise process settings and support monitoring
decisions1–5. The reported accuracy of these models is often high. In our 423-
study full-text corpus, 202 studies report R² as the primary metric and the
median reported R² is 0.97. This number is informative aboutﬁtt oh i s t o r i c a l
records, but it does not by itself show whether a model can guide an
operating decision whose outcome is not yet known.
The operating question is narrower and more demanding than
retrospective ﬁt. A plant operator deciding how much coagulant to dose,
whether to investigate a predicted permit exceedance, or whether to allow
an ML output into a control loop must act on measurements available
before the response is observed. A model can interpolate within a curated
dataset while remaining operationally uninformative when sensors are
sparse, process states are partly observed, compliance-relevant outputs
are delayed or future data leak into validation; benchmark-simulation
and digital-twin studies can help de ﬁne operating settings, but they still
require clear reporting of what information is available before a plant
decision is made 6–11. In regulated wastewater settings, these distinctions
matter because ML-assisted actions may affect compliance and safety
responsibilities12–15.
Here we examine whether the evidence reported in water-treatment
ML matches this operational decisioncontext. We combine two empirical
components. First, a 423-study evidence map quantiﬁes how often studies
report deployment-relevant evidence practices. Second, a six-plant waste-
water-treatment scenario tests a bou nded routine-monitoring question
under forward-looking validation. We use these results to formulate an
operation-aware evidence standard that links model claims to sensing
context, validation timi ng, uncertainty and control role. The six-plant
scenario is used as an illustration of evidence requirements, not as a general
benchmark for all water-treatment ML.
What the literature reports
T h el i t e r a t u r ee v i d e n c em a ps h o w sal arge gap between accuracy reporting
and operation-facing evidence. In the 423-study corpus, plant deployment is
reported in only 12 studies (2.8%, Wilson 95% CI 1.6-4.9%), real-time
testing with live plant data in 5.2%, and uncertainty quantiﬁcation in 8.5%.
Future-facing validation, deﬁned as temporal split, walk-forward or external
validation, appears in 18.2%. Public code (7.3%) and public data (13.2%) are
also uncommon, limiting independent retesting of most claims (Fig.1). The
ﬁeld has grown from 14 papers in 2018 to 104 in 2023, and artiﬁcial neural
networks appear in 68.6% of studies, but the evidence practices needed for
operation remain much less frequently reported.
The low deployment rate is not driven by a single document type or
journal subset. Restricting to journal articles gives a 3.2% deployment rate, a
core-water-journal subset gives 2.2%, and the high-impact subset contains
no deployed studies at all (Tables S11 and S12). All 12 deployment labels in
the analysis corpus were checked by full-text manual review. These ﬁlters
and checks do not establish prevalence beyond the screened corpus, but they
show that the operational-evidence gap is not a simple artefact of
conference-like records or lower-visibility venues.
We therefore treat the evidence-map indicators as separate descriptive
ﬁelds rather than as a composite score. Plant deployment is an outcome, not
a component of an index used to explain deployment. R²-tier deployment
counts are reported descriptively , and we do not infer an association
between retrospective accuracy and deployment from the small number of
deployed studies. Keeping deployment, validation timing, uncertainty, code
availability, and data availability separate is what makes the missing evi-
dence visible.

An operation-aware evidence standard
T h ee v i d e n c em a pa n dt h ep l a n ts c enario point to the same practical
requirement: accuracy metrics need to be accompanied by evidence that
matches the intended operating role. We de ﬁne an operation-aware evi-
dence standard as a minimum set of disclosures that states what a model is
for, what information it can see at the decision point, how it was validated in
time, how uncertainty is reported, and what happens if the output is
acted upon.
The standard has four core axes. Sensing context labels each predictor
as a feedforward variable available before the decision, a feedback variable
observed after the response, an actuator history, a laboratory measurement,
a simulated variable or an external covariate. Validation timing asks whether
testing is random, temporal, walk-forward, or external. Uncertainty asks
whether prediction intervals, calibration or abstention rules are reported.
Control role and risk ask whether the output is used for of ﬂine analysis,
shadow testing, advisory decisions, supervised autonomy or closed-loop
control. For compliance-relevant efﬂuent prediction, the minimum evi-
dence should include a future-facing validation design and uncertainty
intervals. For advisory dosing, authors should report reversibility, operator
review and comparison with the relevant conventional rule. For closed-loop
control, the evidence should include safety constraints, fail-safe logic and
comparison with a conventional or mechanistic baseline16–20.
T h es a m ea x e ss u p p o r ts t a g e dd e p l oyment, recognising practical
constraints on AI use in wastewater treatment21. In shadow mode a model
runs beside existing operations with out affecting decisions; in advisory
mode operators review predictions and retain authority; in supervised
autonomy the model can execute bounded actions under oversight and fail-
safe rules; and fully autonomous operation should be reserved for cases with
future-facing validation, uncertainty calibration, operational monitoring
and documented control constraints22,23. For weakly observed or high-risk
processes, advisory deployment may b e the appropriate endpoint rather
than a transition to full autonomy (Fig.3).
Implications and limitations
This study reframes the evidence problem in water-treatment ML. The issue
is not that retrospective accuracy is useless; it is that retrospective accuracy
answers a different question from operational use. The literature evidence
map shows that deployment-relevant reporting remains uncommon, while
the plant scenario shows how a modelclaim changes when sensing context
and validation timing are made explicit. Together, these results support a
reporting standard rather than a single universal performance threshold.
For authors, the standard provides away to align model claims with the
evidence actually collected. For utili ties, it separates low-risk shadow or
advisory use from higher-risk control applications. For reviewers and reg-
ulators, it identiﬁes the disclosures needed before an ML-assisted decision
affects compliance-relevant operations. Physics-informed and hybrid ML
can ﬁt naturally into this standard when physical constraints are tested and
reported as evidence, rather than used only as labels; in our corpus, only 47
of 423 studies (11.1%) mention such concepts.
Several limitations bound the conclusions. The 423-study corpus is a
screened full-text corpus from 2018 to 2025, not a census of every water-
treatment ML paper. The extraction workﬂow used LLM-assisted coding
followed by validation and manual checks, so rare or ambiguous reporting
practices may still be missed. Deployment counts are small and are used

How the evidence was assembled
Literature evidence map. The literature analysis used a 500-record source
extraction pool and a 423-study full-text analysis corpus covering
2018–2025. Records were screened for water-treatment ML relevance and
coded for sub-ﬁeld, model family, data source, validation method, reported
performance metric, deployment evidence, real-time testing, uncertainty
quantiﬁcation, interpretability, code availability and data availability. The
primary manuscript numbers are taken from the project truth tables
generated from these coded records.
Extraction reliability was evaluated against a 50-paper gold-standard
subset. In that benchmark, boolean- ﬁeld mean accuracy was 0.973,
categorical-ﬁeld mean accuracy was 0.89 and macro F1 was 0.849.
Deployment labels were treated as load-bearingﬁelds: all positive deploy-
ment labels in the 423-study corpus were checked by full-text manual
review. Robustness analyses repeated the main deployment and reporting
summaries after restricting to journal a rticles, excluding conference-like
records, restricting to core water journals and considering a high-impact-
journal subset.
Six-plant operational scenario. The plant analysis used derived records
from six anonymised wastewater treatment plants. Plants were labelled
1–6, real dates were replaced by relative time and raw records were not
released. The modelling table contained 6006 complete timestamped
records. Each plant had 8 –13 routinely monitored inﬂuent variables and
up to ﬁve compliance-relevant efﬂuent targets, depending on availability.
The feedforward scenario used only routine in ﬂuent chemistry available
before efﬂuent feedback was observed.
For each plant-target task, ridge regression, random forest, and his-
togram gradient boosting were compared with mean, persistence, and
inﬂuent-plus-lagged-efﬂuent baselines. Random cross-validation and walk-
forward evaluation used the same date -based split logic so that records
sharing a timestamp could not straddle training and test partitions. Walk-
forward folds retrained on past date blocks withﬁxed hyperparameters and
no tuning on the future test block. Model skill was summarised as R², with
negative values retained because they indicate performance worse than the
relevant baseline.
