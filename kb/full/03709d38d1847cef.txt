Invasive South African populations of the freshwater snail 
Tarebia granifera belong to a single globally distributed lineage 
within a species complex

Nico J. Smit2

Abstract
The Asian thiarid snail Tarebia granifera is a highly successful invasive species and has had substantial impacts in South 
African freshwater ecosystems, often out competing and replacing native snail populations. However, there is limited 
understanding of the molecular diversity of the snail beyond its native Southeast Asian range, with few attempts to identify 
origins or potential number of invasion events in to new freshwater environments. Similarly, identifying T. granifera via 
morphological methods is complex and a method by which several  efforts to monitor the spread of the snail and the parasites 
they transmit have depended upon. By employing mitochondrial  cytochrome c oxidase subunit I DNA barcoding, phylo-
genetic and species delimitation analyses it has been possible to identify the occurrence of three putative species within T. 
granifera. However, crucially, the phylogenetic and population genetic analyses revealed only a single lineage to contain 
all invasive haplotypes of T. granifera including those from South Africa. Also, the population genetic analyses revealed 
a single haplotype in the South African populations, suggesting a rapid expansion event into several aquatic ecosystems 
across the country. The current findings are consistent with a single invasion event; however, the current study highlights 
the inability of single marker techniques to reveal the fine-scale evolutionary history of invasion for T. granifera. Develop-
ing genomic tools is crucial to monitor the spread of snail species  across southern Africa, to assess  their ecological impact 
and for trematode disease surveillance.

Introduction
Invasive species are second only to habitat destruction in 
their impact on native biodiversity (Dueñas et al. 2021; Weyl 
et al. 2020). Within freshwater ecosystems, invasive species 
can replace native species, as well as becoming ecosystem 
engineers, leading to habitat degradation resulting in altera-
tion of the biomass of consumers and even affecting nutrient 
fluxes within an environment (Gerber et al. 2025; Moslemi 
et al. 2012). However, biological invasions by cryptic spe-
cies further complicate the issue, as morphologically similar 
but genetically distinct taxa can differ considerably in their 
ecological impact, invasion dynamics and responses to man-
agement, which could lead to an underestimation of biodi-
versity loss and misattribution of invasion effects (Bickford 
et al. 2007; Pfenninger and Schwenk 2007). Because morpho-
logically similar lineages within aquatic mollusc species com-
plexes can be indistinguishable, invasive lineages and multi-
ple, independent invasions of such lineages can escape early 
detection and monitoring, enabling them to spread extensively 
before recognition and complicating risk assessments and 
biosecurity efforts (Carlton 2009; Geller et al. 2010). This 
can be further complicated when invasive species also have 
the capacity to alter disease transmission within a habitat,

either by acting as a novel compatible host for native patho-
gens or by introducing novel pathogens into an environment 
(Thieltges et al. 2025). This is particularly true for invasive 
freshwater molluscs, with high reproductive rates, tolerance 
to a wide range of environmental conductions and being a 
crucial part of trematode life cycles, making invasions by such 
organisms a global One Health concern.
The parthenogenic snail Tarebia granifera (Lamarck, 
1816) represents one of the most successful invasive mol-
luscs of tropical and subtropical freshwater systems and has 
been suggested to be a complex of multiple cryptic species 
(Pearson et al. 2024; Veeravechsukij et al. 2018). The snail 
has a natural range from India through to the mainland of 
Southeast Asia, with northern range in China and Taiwan, 
through to the Philippines and south to the Indonesian 
Archipelago and the islands of the Western Pacific (Veer -
avechsukij et al. 2018). Within its native range, T. granifera 
functions as an important first intermediate host for zoonotic 
trematodes within the Heterophyidae and Opisthorchiidae, 
including the cancer-causing liver fluke Clonorchis sinensis 
(Paller et al. 2021; Pearson et al. 2024). As a consequence of 
the international aquarium trade, T. granifera is now found 
in several states in the USA, many of the Caribbean islands, 
Central and South America, Israel and sub-Saharan Africa 
(Pearson et al. 2024; Veeravechsukij et al. 2018).
Thiarid species are notoriously challenging to identify 
accurately and T. granifera is no exception owing to exten-
sive phenotypic plasticity, particularly in shell morphology. 
Veeravechsukij et al. (2018) showed that across the natural 
range of T. granifera in Thailand and Malaysia there was sig-
nificant morphological variability within populations, with 
several characteristics overlapping with other thiarid snail 
species. However, snails morphologically identified as T. 
granifera based on shell structure, brood pouch morphology 
and internal anatomy were genotyped using the mitochon-
drial cytochrome c oxidasesubunit I (COX1) DNA barcoding 
region and the resultant phylogenetic reconstruction showed 
at least two distinct lineages. The analyses revealed that there 
was at least a 13.8% divergence between the lineages, which 
also appeared to separate geographically based on snail popu-
lations from the north and the south that represented not only 
different altitudes but also different habitats in Thailand. This 
led to Veeravechsukij et al. (2018) concluding that T. granifera 
should be considered a cryptic species made up of a complex 
of at least two genetically distinct species but also highlighted 
that deeper analyses were required to confirm this to be the 
case. This difficulty in defining T. granifera increases the chal-
lenge of attempting to not only identify the snail to species 
level but also identifying invasion routes and the potential 
origins of establishing populations. As freshwater snails are 
also known to invade localities through multiple independent 
invasion events, the occurrence of cryptic invasions, where 
another genetically distinct lineage of a species invades a 
location where the same non-native species exists, frequently 
go undetected, as has been described in other molluscs of 
medical and veterinary importance, including Physidae snails 
invading Africa (Lawton et al. 2018 ; Albrecht et al. 2025), 
apple snail Pomacea sp. invading South America and Asia 
(Lv et al. 2013) and Melanoides tuberculata into the Africa 
and the USA (Tolley-Jordan et al. 2023).
Although Veeravechsukij et al. ( 2018) highlighted the 
existence of at least two distinct mitochondrial lineages of 
T. granifera, there have been no studies that have attempted 
to identify which of these occurs outside of its native range 
beyond Southeast Asia nor have any studies applied molecu-
lar techniques to identify the occurrence of multiple cryp-
tic invasion events. South Africa has seen a major invasion 
event of T. granifera, which have established in numerous 
water bodies across South Africa since it was originally 
identified in KwaZulu-Natal in 1999 (Appleton and Nadasan 
2002), with a particularly high prevalence in the northeast of 
the country (Malherbe 2018). Furthermore, recent surveys in 
Mozambique and Zimbabwe have confirmed the presence of 
T. granifera at multiple freshwater sites within these coun-
tries (Appleton et al. 2009; Gerber et al. 2024). The inva-
sion success of T. granifera within South African freshwater 
ecosystems provides the ideal scenario to test the hypoth -
eses that (i) invasive populations of T. granifera outside its 
native range, including South Africa, are derived from a 
single lineage of the species complex previously identified 
by Veeravechsukij et al. (2018) and that (ii) multiple geno-
types from a single lineage of the T. granifera complex have 
invaded South Africa, with establishment through  more 
than one founder event. By undertaking comparisons of 
publicly available T. granifera and COX1 sequences gener-
ated here, this current study employed DNA barcoding and 
phylogenetic approaches to accurately identify the number 
of cryptic lineages within the snail populations collected 
from freshwater ecosystems of South Africa.
Material and methods
Sampling and snail collections
Tarebia granifera were collected from sites in the northern 
and eastern parts of South Africa (Fig. 1). These include 
seven sites on the Limpopo River (LIMP-2) system, includ-
ing five tributaries: the Mogalakwena (MOGA), Luvuvhu 
(LUVU), Olifants (OLIF-1 and OLIF-2), Groot-Letaba 
(G-LETA) and Letaba (LETA) rivers (all in the Limpopo 
province), as well as from a single site (PHON-4) on the 
Phongolo River (KwaZulu-Natal province) during surveys 
in 2021 and a single site in the North West province during 
2024 (Fig. 1). Snails were hand-picked from vegetation and 
stones for 30 min, as described by Nguyen et al. (2021).

since they have a higher likelihood of trematode infection 
(Giannelli et al. 2016). Individual snails were then fixed in 
labelled tubes with 96% ethanol and preserved in a mobile 
freezer (−4 °C) on-site until genetic analyses at North-West

ZA/LP/107276) from the Limpopo Department of Economic 
Development, Environment and Tourism and Ezemvelo 
KZN Wildlife (permit no. OP 864/2021), as well as ethical 
clearance from the NWU AnimCare Animal Research Ethics 
Committee (ethics no. NWU-01539-20-A9).
DNA extraction, PCR amplification and sequencing
A total of 35 individual snails were sequenced, representing 
the seven sampling sites sampled from the Limpopo River 
system and from the Phongolo River and five individuals 
from North West province. Snail shells were carefully 
crushed with pliers and the entire snail removed. A small 
piece of each snail’s foot tissue and hepatopancreas were 
removed using sterile forceps and scalpel blades (Schwelm 
et al. 2020; Nguyen et al. 2021). Equipment was sterilised 
with bleach, then rinsed with sterile distilled water and 
finally washed with 96% molecular-grade ethanol fol-
lowing each dissection to prevent cross-contamination to 
ensure polymerase chain reaction (PCR) efficacy (Schols 
2019). The tissue samples were preserved in separate tubes 
with 96% molecular-grade ethanol and stored in a freezer 
(−20 °C) until further analysis. Hepatopancreas samples 
were divided in two, with one half crushed and placed in 
separate sterile  1.5ml microcentrifuge tubes for further 
analysis.
The ethanol was decanted and the remaining ethanol 
was evaporated in a heat block at 56 °C. Total genomic 
DNA was extracted from both snail foot tissue and

The mitochondrial COX1 is the only marker to date 
used to study the population genetics and phylogenetics of 
Tarebia, thus the molecular DNA barcoding method used 
by Lawton et al. (2015, 2018) was employed in this cur -
rent study. Primers LCO1490 (5′-GGT CAA CAA ATC ATA 
AAG ATA TTG G-3′) and HCO2198 (5′ -TAA ACT TCA 
GGG TGA CCA AAA AAT CA-3′) as described by Folmer 
et al. (1994) were used to perform PCR in 25 μl reactions. 
An aliquot of 5 μl of the resultant amplicon was visualised 
on 1% agarose gels stained with GelRed. The remaining 
20 μl of PCR positive samples were then sequenced using 
fluorescent dye terminator sequencing kits and run for 
analyses on an Applied Biosystems 3730XL automated 
sequencer. Primer sequences were removed from the 5′ 
ends of the raw sequences during editing and assembly 
in Bioedit (Hall 1999), with resultant sequences of up to 
655 bp prior to alignment.
Phylogenetic analyses and species delimitation
Initial identification of the T. granifera sequences was com-
pleted by undertaking a simple BLASTN search performed 
against GenBank through the National Center for Biotech -
nology Information site (http:// www. ncbi. nlm. nih. gov/) con-
firming all individuals to belong to this species complex. All 
available COX1 sequences for T. granifera were retrieved for 
comparison, representing snail populations from across their 
native range in Asia, including Thailand, Malaysia, Singa-
pore, Timor and China, as well as non-native ranges includ-
ing USA, Tahiti and Moorea (Supplementary Table S1). 
Using Thiara amarula as an outgroup, an alignment contain-
ing a total of 139 T. granifera COX1 sequences was created 
using MUSCLE (http:// www. ebi. ac. uk/ Tools/ msa/ muscle/). 
Phylogenetic analyses of these sequences were performed to 
identify the occurrence of distinct snail lineages that could 
be associated with invasions. Phylogenetic analyses were 
performed using maximum likelihood (ML) approaches 
implemented through MEGA 11 (Tamura et al. 2021). It is 
also important to note that owing to the fragment analysed 
being a short portion of COX1, the sequences were analysed 
using a single unpartitioned dataset under one best-fit sub-
stitution model, the standard approach for invertebrate DNA 
barcoding. The Kimura 2-parameter model was identified 
as the most appropriate nucleotide substitution model for 
phylogenetic reconstruction using the Bayesian information 
criterion (BIC), and 1000 bootstrap replicates were used to 
calculate nodal support, both of which were implemented 
in MEGA 11.
Lineage identification and species delimitation
Several complementary approaches were employed to 
assess species delimitation across the COX1 dataset, 
owing to the cryptic nature of Thiaridae snails. Although 
these approaches are not statistically independent, each of 
the delimitation methods make different assumptions of 
the alignment, and congruence of the results from each of 
the methods enables a more robust inference than a single 
method. Their combined outputs were used to infer the 
occurrence of distinct molecular operational taxonomic units 
(MOTU) and ultimately potential candidate species. Ini-
tially, simple pairwise p-distances were calculated between 
lineages and clades in MEGA 11 to assess percentage of 
divergence, using the 3% divergence concept within COX1 
DNA barcodes to define putative distinct species, as sug-
gested by Hebert et al. (2003). This heuristic rule of thumb 
was applied to provide an initial, exploratory indication of 
divergence between clades. However, more robust evidence 
was sought through the 4× ratio, as described by Birky et al. 
(2010). This criterion derives from population-genetic the-
ory and was developed specifically for species detection and 
identification in asexual organisms; it compares the ratio of 
the mean pairwise differences between mitochondrial DNA 
sequences from two distinct clades (K ) with the mean pair-
wise differences within a clade (ϴ ), with K/ϴ > 4 between 
clades representing two distinct species. The larger value 
produced in the final calculation is considered to represent 
the true difference between species, reducing the likelihood 
of a false positive. The 4× ratio has previously been applied 
to thiarid snails of the genus Aylacostoma, for which geneti-
cally distinct species were also recognised from mitochon-
drial sequence data (Vogler et al. 2014, 2016). Thirdly, the 
distance-based automatic barcode gap discovery (ABGD) 
method was employed on the whole of the T. granifera 
COX1 alignment and implemented through Spart explorer 
server (spartexplorer.mnhn.fr). The ABGD analyses identi-
fied the genetic barcode gap between intra- and inter-spe -
cific variation. As th analyses use pairwise genetic distances

between snail COX1 sequences, it does not require prior 
species assignments; this then allows the sequences to be 
recursively separated  into candidate putative species based 
on statistically supported thresholds of P  < 0.01. Finally, 
species delimitation based on tree topology was performed 
using the Poisson tree processes (PTP) maximum likeli-
hood model as applied to the ML phylogenetic reconstruc-
tion based on COX1 individual haplotypes and implemented 
through the species delimitation server (species.h-its.org). 
The PTP analyses infer species boundaries from differences 
in branching rates across the tree, assuming substitutions 
accumulate according to a Poisson process but at different 
rates within and between species, with short branches con-
sidered indicative of within species coalescent processes and 
longer branches indicative of between species diversifica-
tions. These multiple approaches for species delimitation 
were applied owing to the parthenogenic nature of T. grani-
fera and the recognised limitations of single locus analyses. 
All MOTUs recovered in this study were treated as candidate 
species and considered putative.
Inter‑population genetic variation
To identify the occurrence of multiple incursion events into 
South African environments, standard population genetic 
analyses were performed to assess the diversity within 
and between T. granifera lineages and countries. Unique 
COX1 haplotypes were identified from the full alignment in 
DnaSPv6 (Rozas et al. 2017), and simple population genetic 
comparisons were performed between COX1 sequences gen-
erated from different geographical isolates using standard 
measures of molecular diversity. It is important to note that 
when the analyses considered the entire global population 
of T. granifera, sequences from China, USA, Tahiti and 
Moorea were included; however, they were omitted from any 
country-level analyses, as each of these countries was rep-
resented by a single COX1 sequence. Owing to the invasive 
nature of T. granifera, analyses were performed to identify 
signatures of demographic changes in the snail’s population. 
Pairwise mismatch analyses were performed to identify the 
occurrence of population expansion events in DnaSPv6. The 
distribution of frequency of pairwise differences between 
sequences within each lineage was performed for under 
1000 replicate simulations. Subsequently, the raggedness (r) 
statistic was used to measure the smoothness of observed 
mismatches to test for any substantial departure from uni-
modality. Additionally, both the Fu’s F s and Tajima’s D 
were calculated for each geographical isolate and lineage, to 
identify any significant departure from neutrality as another 
indicator of population expansion events. Finally, to provide 
insights into the structure and association of populations, the 
evolutionary inter-relationships between unique T. granifera 
COX1 haplotypes most parsimonious haplotype networks 
were constructed with TCS as implemented through PopArt 
(Leigh and Bryant 2015).
Results
Phylogenetic analyses and evidence of cryptic 
species in Tarebia granifera
The 139 COX1 sequences used in this study represented 17 
distinct haplotypes, and the ML phylogenetic analyses split 
T. granifera into three distinct MOTUs within two well-sup-
ported lineages, with nodal bootstrap values of 100 and both 
lineages containing snails from across Asia (Fig. 2). Lineage 
1 contained snail sequences from Thailand and Malaysia, 
with lineage 2 being composed of snails from a wider geo-
graphical spread, including not only Thailand and Malaysia 
but also Timor, Singapore, China, Tahiti, Moorea, USA and 
South Africa. Lineage 2 was further split into two  sub-
clades, with all snails sampled from invasive populations, 
including those from South Africa, only being found within 
sub-clade A (Fig. 2). Both sub-clade A and B had sequences 
from Malaysian snail populations in basal positions as well 
as representatives scattered throughout.
To determine the occurrence of putative distinct species, 
initially genetic p -distance was calculated between each 
of the MOTUs. When lineage 1 was compared with each 
of the sub-clades within lineage 2, the p -distances ranged 
between 13.5% and 13.7% divergence (Fig.  3A). This was 
contrasted when both sub-clade A and B were compared 
with only 3.18% divergence, but both comparisons did 
exceed the Hebert et al. (2003) 3% minimum divergence 
to indicate distinct species (Fig.  3A). The ABGD analyses 
also revealed three initial and recursive partitions supported 
by P  = 0.001, with each of the partitions corresponding 
to the three MOTUs identified in the phylogenetic analyses 
(Fig. 3B). This was further supported by the PTP analyses 
which also highlighted the MOTUs as distinct. Lineage 1 
was strongly supported as a distinct species with ML sup-
port estimates of 0.9. Similarly, sub-clade A within lineage 
2 was also well supported as a potential distinct species with 
moderate support at 0.8 (Fig. 3B). However, sub-clade B had 
comparatively lower support with values of 0.5. Finally, the 
4× ratio analyses were applied to determine occurrence of 
speciation across the lineages. The analyses did identify the 
lineages as representing separate species (Table  1). Inter-
estingly, as with the ABGD and PTP analyses, the 4× ratio 
also separated sub-clade A and B in lineage 2 as distinct

Inter‑relationship between geographical isolates 
of Tarebia granifera
Population genetic analyses of T. granifera showed there to 
be high levels of variation in the COX1 of individuals from 
Malaysia and Thailand, with the highest haplotype diversity 
seen in Malaysia. The snails collected across South Africa 
appear to be genetically identical with a single haplotype 
shared across all sites (Table 2). To account for the fact that 
several of the geographical isolates, especially those from 
Malaysia and Thailand, would indeed represent a mixture 
of species, each of the lineages were also analysed as large 
independent populations. Lineage 1 appeared to have the 
lowest level of genetic variation with π = 0.0022. Sub-clade 
B had the highest nucleotide diversity with π  = 0.0059 and 
the highest number of haplotypes as well as the greatest 
haplotype diversity (h  = 9, Hd 0.81). Sub-clade A had the 
lowest number of haplotypes and haplotype diversity (h = 4, 
Hd = 0.24) despite not having the lowest nucleotide diversity 
at π = 0.00471 (Table 2).
Haplotype network analyses also confirmed the occur -
rence of two major lineages, with lineage 2 being separated 
into two sub-lineages and haplotypes considered to be gener-
ated from invasive snail populations only occurring within 
sub-clade A (Fig. 4). Haplotypes from Thailand and Malay-
sia were scattered across the haplotype network, occurring 
in each of the lineages. However, there was an absence of 
haplotypes from Thailand within the invasive sub-clade A. 
The analyses also illustrate that the sole haplotype found in 
South Africa is shared with snail populations from Malaysia 
and China (Fig. 3A, B) and shared a predicted recent com-
mon ancestor with a haplotype found in invasive T. granifera 
populations from the USA, Moorea and Tahiti.

Demographic history of Tarebia granifera
Owing to the identification of distinct lineages that could 
represent putative species, genetic demographic analyses 
were performed at the lineage level. These analyses revealed 
conflict between the Fu’s F and Tajima’s D, with lineage 1 
having a negative Fu’s F but a positive Tajima’s D, and the 
reverse was seen in both sub-clades in lineage 2, with no sig-
nificant scores found. Pairwise mismatch analyses revealed 
lineage 1 to be unimodal, indicating a high frequency of 
haplotypes with few mutations (Fig.  5). Both sub-lineages 
A and B of lineage 2 appeared to be multimodal but still 
with low frequency of highly divergent haplotypes with high 
pairwise differences (Fig.  5). The raggedness index (r), the 
comparison of experimental distribution of mismatch pairs, 
was not significantly different from observed distributions, 
with each of the lineages indicating the potential of popula-
tion expansion events (Table 2).
Discussion
The success of an invasive species is ultimately linked to 
its adaptability, including its tolerance of a wide range of 
environments, reproductive rate and high levels of pheno -
typic and genetic diversity, all of which can mediate success. 
Crucially, the ability to identify distinct cryptic lineages and 
species is paramount, as genetically distinct populations of 
exotic and/or pest species may differ substantially in char -
acteristics that enable tolerance of a range of environmental 
conditions, affect fecundity and ultimately establish new 
populations in naive ecosystems (Xuan et al. 2025). This is a 
particular problem for the Thairidae, especially T. granifera, 
which is taxonomically challenging even in its native range 
throughout Southeast Asia (Veeravechsukij et al. 2018). The 
phylogenetic and haplotype network analyses presented in 
this current study confirmed the existence of the two discrete 
lineages that had already been identified by Veeravechsukij 
et al. (2018) and further resolved lineage 2 into two sub-
clades A and B.
All haplotypes included within this study had previously 
been characterised in Veeravechsukij et al. (2018), and the 
phylogenetic analyses presented in this study are also con-
gruent with that of Veeravechsukij et al. (2018), providing 
reassurance in analytical methods and validity in approach. 
However, unlike the Veeravechsukij et al. (2018) study, the 
analyses presented here suggests that the two sub-clades of 
lineage 2 may represent distinct MOTUs and could be con-
sidered as putative distinct species within the T. granifera 
species complex. The separation of these two clusters was 
also documented by Veeravechsukij et al. (2018); however, 
their analyses focused predominantly on the separation 
between the two major clades and not between the sub-
clades within them. In this current study, the authors argue 
that sub-clade A and B within lineage 2 could be repre -
sentative of two distinct species owing to the congruence of 
the species delimitation approaches applied here: inter-clade 
p-distances exceeding the 3% COX1 barcode threshold sug-
gested by Hebert et al. (2003), with 13.5–13.7% divergence 
between lineage 1 and each sub-clade, and 3.18% between 
clades A and B; ABGD partitioning into three recursive par-
titions at P  = 0.001; and the Birky et al. (2010) 4× ratio, 
with K/ϴ values of 7.624, 61.037 and 57.168 for the sub-
clade A/B, sub-clade A/lineage 1 and sub-clade B/lineage 1 
comparisons, respectively. The PTP analyses likewise recov-

be considered as putative until further studies have been able 
to verify them as distinct species.
Cryptic species are common in snail taxa with highly 
polymorphic morphology, where distinct species with over-
lapping characteristics are frequently confused as a single 
species, and alternatively morphological plasticity has led 
to the erection of multiple species from a single one. Both 
cases have been seen in a variety of snail families including 
but not limited to the Physidae (Albrecht et al. 2025; Lawton 
et al. 2018), the Lymnaeidae (Lawton et al. 2015; Mirfen-
dereski et al. 2021 ) and the Littorinidae (Xu et al. 2024 ), 
and, in each case, this is attributed to a lack of a molecular 
phylogenetic framework to underpin the systematics of these 
mollusc families. Thiaridae has an estimated 200 species,

although there are many more taxa named owing to a high 
level of redundancy with approximately 70% of all named 
species being inaccurately identified on morphology alone, 
with every phenotype being described as a distinct spe-
cies (Glaubrecht 2009; Veeravechsukij et al. 2018). Subse-

and further highlights the need for comprehensive molecular 
systematic analyses to allow detailed revision of the thiarids 
(Glaubrecht 2009; Veeravechsukij et al. 2018).
Despite these caveats, the concordance between the spe-
ciation delimitation analyses and the population genetic 
approaches suggests three putative species within the T. 
granifera complex, and this would have direct implications 
for the invasion biology of the group. Only sub-clade A 
was found to contain invasive populations both from South 
Africa and globally, supporting the hypotheses that invasive 
populations outside the native range of T. granifera represent 
a single lineage of the complex, allowing the rejection of 
the hypotheses that multiple lineages were able to establish 
in South Africa. This also raises the further hypotheses that 
invasion success may be lineage specific rather than a char-
acteristic of the T. granifera complex as a whole. Lineage-
specific invasion histories have been reported in other mol-
lusc species in which mitochondrial phylogeographic studies 
have repeatedly shown that only a subset of lineages within 
a species complex participates in invasions and that these 
lineages can differ immensely in their spread and ecological 
impact. The invasive parthenogenic thiarid snail Melanoides 
tuberculata provides the closet comparison with T. granif-
era with genetically and morphologically distinct lineages 
which coexist within a species complex (Facon et al. 2003). 
Phylogeographic analyses of mitochondrial genes indicated 
that there were at least six independent introductions of M. 
tuberculata in the New World, with invasive clones origi-
nating from across the native phylogeny rather than a single 
region of origin (Facon et al. 2003). Similarly, eight ran-
domly distributed COX1 haplotypes found in Florida indi-
cated multiple invasion events by diverse representatives of 
the species complex (Tolley-Jordan et al. 2023). Molecular 
phylogenetic analyses of African populations of M. tubercu-
lata identified independent invasion events of Asian isolates 
into Lakes Malawi and Tanganyika, the Congo River, Nige-
ria and Cameroon, with invasive populations overlapping 
native African forms in morphospace and thereby masking 
their spread (Van Bocxlaer et al. 2015). Crucially, each of 
these studies demonstrated that individual clonal lineages 
from within the M. truncatula complex possess distinct inva-
sion dynamics and that introduced clones may have com -
petitively excluded one another rather than merging into a 
single invasive population (Van Bocxlaer et al. 2015; Facon 
et al. 2003; Tolley-Jordan et al. 2023). This phenomenon is 
not confined to gastropods and has been reported in other 
molluscs also. The freshwater clam Corbicula consists of 
multiple morphologically identical clonal lineages, denoted 
into forms A–D, with invasive distributions which are line-
age specific. Within Corbicula, form A is globally distrib-
uted with form B restricted to western North America, form 
C occurring in European and South American watersheds 
and the recently confirmed New World form D appears to be 
confined to the Illinois River (Haponski and Foighil 2019). 
The distinct lineages of Corbicula which can be separated 
by mitochondrial markers are able to hybridise where their 
populations overlap (Pfenninger et al. 2002 ; Renard et al. 
2000), and, in Argentina, two mitochondrial lineages exhibit 
virtually segregated distributions despite similar invasion 
histories and origins (Hünicken et al. 2024). In both M. 
tuberculata and Corbicula , it was the genetic lineage that 
was crucial for invasion biology rather than the morpho 
species, and this study argues this to be the case for the T. 
granifera complex too. However, the authors agree that test-
ing whether sub-clade A of lineage 2 is intrinsically more 
invasive than the others with the T. granifera complex will 
require extensive experimental and genomic approaches that 
are beyond the scope of the present single-marker dataset.
The South African T. granifera shared a single COX1 
haplotype across all localities sampled, which was also 
shared with snail populations from China and Malaysia 
and appeared to be a sister to a haplotype that was shared 
between snail populations from the USA, Tahiti and Moorea, 
illustrating the occurrence of only two invasive haplotypes 
which exist within the same sub-clade. It is important to 
note that diversity between haplotypes does exist elsewhere 
within the sub-lineage, illustrating that although there is 
diversity within sub-clade A, there is a complete lack of 
haplotype variation across all sampled South African locali-
ties. The data presented here would suggest that the South 
African population of T. granifera is derived from a single 
population from the mainland of Southeast Asia (Veeravech-
sukij et al. 2018). Within invasive molluscs, the presence of 
low genetic diversity or a single mitochondrial haplotype 
across an entire non-native range is indicative of either a 
major bottleneck event or that the founding population was 
small because of a single invasion, and although bottlenecks 
are expected to reduce establishment probability, some of 
the most successful invaders have exhibited dramatically 
reduced genetic diversity (Therriault et al. 2005). As T. 
granifera is parthenogenetic, only a single individual would 
have been required to establish a new population in a naive

environment. The complete lack of genetic diversity in the 
South African T. granifera population shares parallels with 
the invasion of the western USA by a single clonal lineage 
of the New Zealand mud snail Potamphyrgus antipodarum 
where limited introductions were followed by rapid expan-
sion events (Dybdahl and Drown 2011). Similarly, single 
haplotype invasions have been documented in other aquatic 
molluscs such as the European populations of the freshwa-
ter mussel Sinanodonta woodiana (Konečný et al., 2018) 
and the Hawaiian populations of the apple snail Pomacea 
canaliculata (Yang et al., 2018). Both species were shown 
to have high levels of genetic diversity within their native 
ranges and with only single haplotypes found within the 
non-native environments, this was interpreted as evidence of 
a single colonisation event as the source of the entire popula-
tion which led to the rapid expansion of these species across 
the non-native ecosystems (Konečný et al. 2018; Yang et al. 
2018). The data presented in this current study would there-
fore suggest a single invasion event into South Africa by T. 
granifera which subsequently led to the rapid expansion of 
the snail into new environments across the country. How -
ever, owing to the parthenogenic nature of the T. granifera 
and the mechanisms by which it has been introduced into 
the new locations, there is a caveat that applies and needs 
to be considered. The aquarium trade is considered the 
most likely mechanism for the introduction of non-native 
freshwater molluscs, responsible for at least 41% of species 
introduced into new ecosystems (Preston et al. 2022). Small 
snails are frequently imported and released as unintended 
hitchhikers of traded plants and ornamental species (Preston 
et al. 2022), and aquaculture facilities and aquarium releases 
are recognised as long-standing conduits for non-native gas-
tropod introductions worldwide (Young et al. 2021). This 
mechanism of movement can readily distribute single mito-
chondrial haplotypes across vast distances; for example; 
the European aquarium populations of the planorbid snail 
Gyraulus chinenisis carry haplotypes which are genetically 
close to wild populations in Italy and Albania, and COX1 
sequences from UK aquaria are identical to sequences from 
Taiwan (Saito et al. 2023). Therefore, the authors suggest 
that it could be possible that T. granifera individuals from 
the same source population, and thus carrying the same 
mitochondrial haplotype, could have entered South Africa 
through multiple invasion events. This could account for the 
wide distribution of the snail throughout South Africa, as 
well as the rapid impacts these snails have had on local bio-
diversity and ecology, replacing most of the molluscan fauna 
within each habitat (Gerber et al. 2024; Roux et al. 2025). 
The occurrence of cryptic invasion events by aquatic snails 
is not uncommon, as stated previously, Asian genotypes 
of M. tuberculata replaced native populations of snail in 
African freshwater ecosystems through multiple independ-
ent invasion events (Van Bocxlaer et al. 2015). Such events 
went undetected owing to a lack of morphological differ -
ences between populations and often owing to a lack of dif-
ferentiation in the genetic markers used (Van Van Bocxlaer 
et al. 2015). Also, the pairwise mismatch analyses and the 
assessment of raggedness support sub-clade A represent-
ing a rapidly expanding population of a highly successful 
invasive species, conforming to patterns seen in other inva-
sive freshwater snails with a global distribution (Dybdahl 
and Drown 2011). However, the results of the current study 
have revealed that single DNA barcoding approaches are 
not sufficient to truly disentangle the invasion biology of 
T. granifera. To thoroughly address the cryptic invasion 
concept for these snails’, genome-wide markers would be 
crucial to compare populations from across native and non-
native ranges; however, to date, no genome resources are 
readily available but will become crucial for monitoring the 
spread of these snails. It is likely that the putative species 
represented by sub-clade A from lineage 2 of T. granifera 
will continue its spread across the tropics, and it is vital that 
molecular and genomic tools are developed and employed to 
not only monitor its spread but also to be able to distinguish 
between the different cryptic species within T. granifera. 
This will be crucial for assessing the invasive capability of 
the different cryptic species should any other snails from 
the different lineages become invasive. This is an essential 
part of ecological impact assessment and more so for dis-
ease surveillance and monitoring of exotic trematode spread, 
highlighting once again that invasions of T. granifera are 
truly a One Health issue.
