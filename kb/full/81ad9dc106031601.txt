IntroductionThe 
global compound feed industry produces over one billion tonnes of feed 
annually, representing the single largest cost component in modern animal production. 
Feed manufacturing technology – the applied science of transforming raw agricultural 
commodities into safe, nutritionally balanced, and physically acceptable animal feeds – 
therefore occupies a central position at the intersection of nutritional science, food 
safety, and engineering. Every processing decision, from the choice of grinding 
technology to the parameters of steam conditioning, either adds to or subtracts from 
the ultimate goal: delivering bioavailable nutrients efficiently, safely, and sustainably to 
the consuming animal.1

1.1 Principles of feed formulation and mixing
The foundation of every compound feed manufacturing operation is the diet 
formulation – the quantitative recipe that specifies which raw materials must be 
combined, and in what proportions, to meet the physiological requirements of the 
target animal class. Formulation is not merely arithmetic; it is the practical 
translation of nutritional science, agricultural economics, and processing 
engineering into a single, manufacturable blueprint. A precisely formulated diet that 
is poorly mixed, ground, or pelleted will fail to deliver its intended nutritional value. 
Conversely, excellent processing technology cannot compensate for 
a fundamentally incorrect or imbalanced formulation. This section examines the 
science and methods of feed formulation, followed by the equipment and principles 
governing the mixing step that transforms a formula into a homogeneous physical 
blend.
1.1.1 Introduction to feed formulation: The blueprint for nutrition
Feed formulation is defined as the process of combining feed ingredients in 
specified quantities to create a compound feed that satisfies the nutrient 
requirements of a defined animal class for a specified productive purpose – whether 
maintenance, growth, egg production, lactation, or reproduction – at the lowest 
practicable cost. The formulator must simultaneously satisfy biological, economic, 
and physical constraints.
Nutrient requirement standards are the quantitative benchmarks against which 
formulations are validated. The most widely used published standards include the 
National Research Council (NRC) series for poultry (1994), swine (2012), dairy 
cattle (2001), beef cattle (2016), and aquatic species (2011); the INRA (Institut 
National de la Recherche Agronomique, France) feeding systems; and the CVB 
(Centraal Veevoederbureau, Netherlands) feed tables. These standards express 
requirements for metabolisable energy, crude protein, standardised ileal digestible 
(SID) amino acids, available phosphorus, calcium, and trace minerals and vitamins. 
The shift from total nutrient values to digestible or bioavailable equivalents has 
been one of the most significant advances in precision feeding, as it directly accounts 
for the incomplete and variable utilisation of dietary nutrients that depends on both 
ingredient type and processing history [1–8].Animal

The Ideal Protein concept, developed from research by [ 9] and elaborated by 
Cole, Lenis, and colleagues, provides the theoretical framework for amino acid 
formulation in monogastric nutrition. The Ideal Protein defines the optimal ratio of 
all indispensable amino acids relative to lysine – the reference amino acid used 
because its sole metabolic function is protein synthesis. For a growing pig, the ideal 
ratio of methionine + cystine: lysine is approximately 60:100, threonine: lysine is 
65:100, and tryptophan: lysine is 18:100. Formulating to these digestible amino acid 
ratios allows the nutritionist to minimise dietary crude protein while maintaining 
or improving growth performance, with the important co-benefit of substantially 
reducing urinary nitrogen excretion and environmental ammonia emissions 
[10–12].

formulating under real-world ingredient variability. Because the nutrient content of 
agricultural commodities varies around published mean values – due to cultivar, 
geographic origin, crop year, and processing differences – a diet formulated at the 
mean will violate its most critical constraints approximately 50% of the time in 
practice. Stochastic formulation models express nutrient content as probability 
distributions and optimise diets to meet constraints at a specified probability level 
(e.g., 90th or 95th percentile), building an economically optimal safety margin into 
each nutrient specification that accounts for the variance of each ingredient’s 
composition in the mill’s current supply [ 19]. An illustrative least-cost formulation

1.1.3 The art and science of mixing
The formulation specifies the intended composition of the diet; mixing is the 
physical process that makes that intention a reality in every kilogram of feed 
delivered to the animal. The objective of mixing is to create a statistically 
homogeneous dispersion of all ingredients – including trace minerals and vitamins 
present at parts-per-million concentrations – throughout the entire batch. Failures of 
mixing uniformity are consequential: an animal receiving a vitamin-deficient portion 
from a poorly mixed batch will suffer sub-clinical deficiency regardless of how 
accurately the premix was weighed.Animal

1.1.3.1
Types of mixers
Horizontal ribbon mixers are the most common type in commercial compound 
feed mills. A central shaft carries inner and outer helical ribbons that convey material 
in opposing axial directions, creating three-dimensional circulation that promotes 
rapid, thorough blending. They accommodate liquid additions through spray 
nozzles, discharge cleanly through a full-length bottom gate, and achieve typical 
mixing times of 3–6 minutes for dry ingredients.
Vertical screw mixers use one or two vertical augers inside a conical vessel to lift 
material from the base and cascade it over the top. Their lower capital and floor- 
space requirements make them suitable for smaller mills and on-farm mixing 
operations, but they require mixing times of 10–20 minutes and are more susceptible 
to segregation.
Paddle mixers – particularly twin-shaft paddle mixers – achieve mixing times of 
60–90 seconds through intense fluidising action. They are preferred for high- 
throughput mills, liquid-intensive formulas, and total mixed ration (TMR) 
preparation.
Continuous mixers blend streams of pre-metered ingredients as they traverse 
a screw or paddle conveyor. They suit high-volume, single-formula production lines 
but offer limited flexibility for formula changes.

1.4.1 Why reduce particle size?
The primary biological rationale for particle size reduction is the consequent 
increase in specific surface area exposed to digestive secretions. For spherical 
particles, surface area per unit mass is inversely proportional to diameter: halving 
the diameter doubles the accessible surface area. In the pig and broiler small 
intestine, endogenous enzymes contact the feed surface, and faster enzymatic 
hydrolysis means more complete nutrient absorption. Research consistently 
demonstrates that reducing maize particle size from 1,200 µm to 400 µm improves 
apparent total tract digestibility of energy by 2–5 percentage points and 
standardised ileal digestibility of amino acids by 1–3 percentage points in growing 
pigs [23, 25–29].
Beyond digestibility, finer particle size improves mixing uniformity by reducing 
segregation tendencies. It also dramatically improves pellet durability: smaller 
particles create greater inter-particle contact area during die compression, 
strengthening starch gelatinisation bonds. Studies routinely show that 
reducing GMD from 900 µm to 600 µm increases PDI by 5–15 percentage units [ 28, 
30–35].
Handling and flowability considerations provide a further dimension. Moderately 
fine particles (400–900 µm) flow well through pneumatic conveyers. Excessively 
fine fractions (below ~ 150 µm) become cohesive due to van der Waals and 
electrostatic forces, causing hopper bridging, erratic flow, and dust-explosion 
hazards requiring comprehensive electrical earthing, explosion-relief venting, and 
spark-detection and suppression systems.
1.4.2 Grinding technologies
1.4.2.1 Hammer mills
The hammer mill is the dominant grinding technology in compound feed 
manufacturing worldwide, valued for versatility, ability to process fibrous materials, 
relatively low capital cost, and ease of screen-size adjustment. The operating 
principle is impact and attrition: feed material enters the grinding chamber and is 
struck by a series of swinging or fixed hammers attached to a high-speed rotor 
(2,800–3,600 rpm; tip speeds 80–110 m/s). Ground material exits through 
a perforated screen; only particles fine enough to pass through the perforations are 
discharged [21, 24].
The principal control variable is screen hole diameter (2–12 mm). Higher tip 
speeds produce finer grinding but increase heat generation (5–15 °C rise) and 
accelerate hammer and screen wear. The relationship between screen hole diameter 
and product GMD is not linear and requires empirical characterisation.
Disadvantages: wide, irregular particle-size distributions (high GSD); significant 
heat and dust; lower energy efficiency than roller mills for dry cereal-grain grinding.Perspective

1.4.2.2
Roller mills
Roller mills fracture grain between pairs of counter-rotating cylindrical rolls using 
compressive force and controlled shear. One roll typically rotates faster than the other 
(differential 1.5:1 to 2.5:1), creating a shearing action at the nip that efficiently fractures 
the endosperm while leaving the bran relatively intact. Roll gap, roll surface geometry 
(smooth or corrugated), and speed differential are the primary control variables.
Roller mills consume approximately 40–50% less energy per tonnethan hammer 
mills for dry cereal grain at comparable particle sizes [22]; actual savings range from 
20% to 60% depending on target fineness and moisture content. They generate 
less heat and dust, and produce a narrower particle size distribution (lower GSD). 
Disadvantages include unsuitability for fibrous or sticky materials, higher capital 
costs, and less flexibility for wide-range particle-size adjustment [21, 23].
Crimping passes high-moisture grain (24–30% moisture) between smooth or 
lightly corrugated rolls to produce a fissured kernel for ensiling. Crushing applies 
greater compressive force for a coarser product used in beef cattle rations.

1.5 Thermal processing
Thermal processing – the application of heat through dry convective heating, 
steam, or the thermomechanical work of extrusion – is the most transformative 
group of unit operations in feed manufacturing. No other class of processes so 
profoundly reshapes the chemical, physical, and biological properties of feed 
ingredients. The nutritional consequences are dual-natured: at appropriate Animal

destroys heat-labile vitamins, reduces amino acid bioavailability through Maillard 
reactions, and promotes lipid oxidation. The goals are to modify starch granule 
structure, denature proteins, inactivate ANFs, destroy Salmonella and other 
pathogens, and confer physical handling characteristics.

1.5.1.2 Extrusion
Extrusion is a high-temperature, short-time (HTST) continuous process in which 
feed is heated, pressurised, and sheared as it is conveyed through a barrel by rotating 
screws. Friction and direct steam injection raise the temperature to 100–180 °C and 
the pressure to 10–60 bar. As the melt exits the die, moisture vaporises, causing 
expansion and a porous structure (floating aquatic feeds, pet foods) [ 40, 41].
Single-screw extruders are simpler but offer limited independent control of 
shear and thermal input. Twin-screw extruders provide superior mixing and 
greater processing flexibility; they are preferred for premium aquaculture feeds and 
functional pet foods [40–43].
Nutritional effects: starch gelatinisation at 85–95% (high-shear HTST), trypsin 
inhibitors are completely inactivated above 120 °C, microbial destruction is 
effectively complete. Trade-off: Vitamin C losses of 50–80%, Vitamin E losses of 
30–50% – necessitating post-extrusion coating with encapsulated vitamins [40, 41].
1.5.2 Steam processing: Conditioning, flaking, and pelleting
1.5.2.1 Conditioning
Conditioning is the step in which dry mash is exposed to steam to increase 
temperature and moisture before pelleting. In a standard single-pass conditioner, 
saturated steam condenses on feed particles, raising temperature to 75–95 °C 
and moisture to 15–17% within 15–45 seconds [44, 45]. Dry saturated steam 
(100% dryness fraction) is critical. Longer-retention conditioners (4–8 minutes at 
85–95 °C) achieve greater starch gelatinisation and Salmonella kill rates of 3–5 
log10 cfu/g [30–35].
1.5.2.2 Flaking
Steam-flaking is preferred for beef feedlot and dairy cattle. Grain is steamed for 
20–45 minutes to hydrate and gelatinise starch, then passed through large-diameter 
smooth rolls to produce a flake at a target density of 280–320 g/litre for corn 
(vs. 720 g/litre for whole grain). Rumen starch degradability of steam-flaked corn 
reaches 65–75% versus 45–55% for dry-rolled corn [ 39, 46].

counter-flow cooler draws ambient air upward through a moving bed of pellets 
while pellets flow downward. Properly cooled pellets exit at ≤ 5°C above ambient, 
with moisture below 12.5%. Inadequate cooling leads to condensation, mould

1.5.4 Effects on nutrient availability and anti-nutritional factors
Positive effects: Starch gelatinisation (30–50% more digestible), protein 
denaturation (5–15 percentage unit improvement in ileal digestibility of legume 
proteins), inactivation of trypsin inhibitors, gossypol binding, and myrosinase 
inactivation [30, 38, 40, 41].
Negative effects (over-processing): Maillard reaction (reduces available lysine), 
vitamin destruction (A, D₃, K, B₁, and C losses of 30–80% during pelleting, near- 
complete during extrusion), and oxidation of cysteine and methionine [30, 40, 41].
1.5.4.1 The thermal processing window
Every feed formula has a thermal “window” where beneficial effects outweigh 
harmful ones. Practical management requires simultaneous monitoring of urease 
activity (ANF inactivation), available lysine (Maillard damage), vitamin potency, 
and PDI. Trending these parameters allows optimisation for each formula type.
1.6 Wet processing: Ensiling principles and management
Ensiling is the most widely practised method of conserving high-moisture forage 
crops and grains for livestock feeding. Unlike drying, ensiling preserves feed by 
reducing pH through controlled anaerobic fermentation – creating an acidic 
environment that inhibits spoilage microorganisms while retaining moisture essential 
for rumen fermentation. Ensiling is an exercise in applied microbial ecology.
1.6.1 The science of ensiling: A controlled fermentation
Ensiling proceeds through four sequential phases [ 50–55].
1.6.1.1 Phase 1: The aerobic phase (1–3 days)
Plant cells and aerobic microorganisms consume oxygen and water-soluble 
carbohydrates (WSCs), producing CO₂, heat, and dry matter loss. Minimising time 
between harvest and oxygen exclusion is critical.
1.6.1.2 Phase 2: The fermentation phase (5–21 days)
As oxygen is depleted, lactic acid bacteria (LAB) dominate. Homofermentative 
LAB (L. plantarum, P. acidilactici) convert glucose to lactic acid with a 98–99% DM 
recovery. Heterofermentative LAB (L. buchneri, Leuconostoc) produce lactic acid, 
acetic acid, ethanol, and CO₂; DM recovery is lower (~ 75%), but acetic acid 
improves aerobic stability.Animal

Phase 3: The stable phase (weeks to years)
Low pH, anaerobic conditions, and high organic acids inhibit microbial growth. 
Silage can be stored for long periods if the seal remains intact.
1.6.1.4 Phase 4: The feed-out phase
When the silo face is opened, oxygen re-enters. Acid-tolerant yeasts metabolise 
residual sugars and lactic acid (not acetic acid), raising pH and temperature. Moulds 
then proliferate. High acetic acid content (from L. buchneri) inhibits yeasts and 
improves aerobic stability. The four phases of ensiling and the associated changes in

1.6.2 Key factors for successful ensiling
Dry matter (DM) content: Optimum 30–40% DM. Below 25% DM: clostridial 
fermentation, butyric acid, effluent loss. Above 65% DM: poor consolidation, 
heating, mould.
Water-soluble carbohydrates (WSCs): Minimum ~ 3% fresh weight, target 5– 
8%. Legumes are WSC-poor and require wilting or additives.
Inoculants: Homofermentative LAB improve fermentation efficiency; 
L. buchneri (heterofermentative) improves aerobic stability by 24–100 + hours 
[51–54, 56].
Packing and sealing: Target packing density ≥ 225 kg DM/m 3. Oxygen-barrier 
films (OTR < 10 cm3/m2/day) reduce surface mould depth from 20–40 cm to 
2–5 cm.
1.6.3 Ensiling high-moisture grains
High-moisture grain (HMG) ensiling (22–30% moisture) eliminates drying costs 
and enhances rumen digestibility. Grain must be ground or rolled before ensiling. 
After 90–180 days, rumen starch digestibility of HMG corn approaches 90–95% 
versus 45–55% for dry-rolled corn [51–55].

1.6.4
Managing silage for quality and minimising losses
Total DM losses range from 5% (well-managed tower silos) to > 30% (poor 
bunkers). Major loss sources: fermentation (2–5%), aerobic spoilage (1–15% – largest 
controllable), effluent (3–8%). Aerobic stability (hours before temperature rises > 
2–3°C above ambient) can exceed 200 hours for L. buchneri-treated silage. Face 
management: advance at least 15 cm/day in summer [ 51–55].
Mycotoxin contamination: Field moulds (Fusarium, Aspergillus) produce pre- 
harvest toxins stable through ensiling. Aerobic spoilage allows Penicillium roqueforti 
growth, producing storage mycotoxins. Prevention of aerobic spoilage is key. 
Binders (aluminosilicates, yeast cell wall MOS) are more effective against aflatoxins 
than against deoxynivalenol [55].
1.7 Quality control during processing
Quality control (QC) in feed manufacturing is a continuously operating system of 
monitoring, measurement, corrective action, and documentation that encompasses 
every stage from incoming ingredient receipt to finished feed traceability. 
A proactive, process-oriented QC culture is both a commercial imperative and a legal 
obligation [57–63].
1.7.1 The pillars of a quality control programme
Ingredient receiving: Visual inspection, probe sampling (5–10 insertions per 25 
tonnes), NIRS analysis for moisture, CP, fat, fibre, and starch. Mycotoxin screening 
(lateral flow, ELISA, LC-MS/MS) [59, 62, 63].
In-process control: Grinding (GMD/GSD per shift), mixing (CV quarterly), 
conditioning (continuous temperature logging), pelleting (hot pellet temp/moisture, 
PDI per batch), cooling (cooled-pellet temp/moisture).
Finished product testing: Proximate analysis, amino acids, calcium, 
phosphorus; PDI, hardness, fines; Salmonella enumeration; mycotoxin analysis 
based on risk profile.

[60, 62]:
1. Hazard analysis
2. Identify critical control points (CCPs)
3. Establish critical limits
4. Establish monitoring procedures

steam conditioner (Salmonella kill), (3) post-pellet liquid application (uniformity, 
carry-over). The principal critical control points in a typical compound feed mill are

1.7.3 Key hazards and mitigation strategies
Physical hazards: Ferrous metal (rare-earth magnets, cleaned daily), non- 
ferrous metal (electromagnetic detectors), stones (destoners).
Chemical hazards: Mycotoxins (supplier spec, screening, binders); pesticide 
residues; heavy metals (ICP-OES); veterinary drug carry-over (sequencing, 
flushing, equipment design, analytical verification) [63].
Microbiological hazards: Salmonella – thermal kill step (≥ 81°C), prevention 
of post-process recontamination (segregation, positive pressure, cleanable 
surfaces, rodent control, environmental swabbing, organic acid treatments) [59, 62].
1.7.4 Traceability and recall
Feed traceability – ability to follow feed forward and backward – is a legal 
requirement [58, 65]. Four information layers: ingredient lot traceability, batch 
production records, finished product lot assignment, despatch records. Required 
response time: 4 hours from incident identification. Retained samples are stored 
sealed for 2–3 years [57, 61–63].
1.7.5 Digitalisation and smart feed mills
Modern feed manufacturing increasingly integrates digital process control, 
machine learning, and sensor-based monitoring. The smart feed mill 
combines automation, continuous data acquisition, and AI-driven optimisation 
[66–69].

1.7.6 Sustainability and environmental management
The feed industry faces pressure to reduce its environmental footprint. 
Sustainability strategies encompass energy efficiency, waste reduction, water 
conservation, and circular economy principles [72, 73].
Energy efficiency: Grinding accounts for 30–60% of electrical energy. 
Switching from hammer mills to roller mills can reduce energy consumption by 
40–50% [22]. Heat recovery from conditioners and coolers can pre-heat boiler 
feed water. Variable-frequency drives on fans and motors further reduce energy 
use [21, 72, 73].

fly larvae), single-cell proteins (from yeast or bacteria), and algae are emerging

ConclusionFeed 
manufacturing is not a series of mechanical steps applied to agricultural raw 
materials. It is an integrated biological and engineering process whose every stage – 
from least-cost formulation, through grinding, thermal processing, and ensiling, to 
quality control and traceability – is designed in service of a single goal: the precise 
delivery of bioavailable nutrients to the consuming animal in a form that optimises 
health, productive performance, and sustainability.
The feed industry faces future opportunities and challenges: alternative 
proteins (insect meal, single-cell proteins), artificial intelligence and digital twin 
process control, precision livestock feeding systems demanding smaller, 
customised batches, and the imperative to reduce environmental footprint. 
Meeting these challenges will demand the integrated scientific, engineering, 
and biological understanding that this chapter has sought to articulate 
[12, 67–69, 71, 74].
Case Study 6.1: Roller mill retrofit reduces energy use by 35%
A medium-sized feed mill in Iowa, producing 150,000 tonnes/year of grower- 
finisher pig feed, replaced two hammer mills (250 kW each) with a single roller mill 
(160 kW) for grinding maize (target GMD 600 µm). Hammer mill energy 
consumption: 12.5 kWh/tonne; roller mill: 8.1 kWh/tonne – 35% reduction. GSD 
decreased from 2.7 to 2.1; PDI rose from 84% to 89%. Payback period: 14 months 
(based on [21–24]).
Case Study 6.2: Aerobic spoilage outbreak in maize silage
A dairy farm in Wisconsin reported reduced intake and milk production. 
Investigation revealed a bunker silo of maize silage (32% DM) inadequately sealed. 
The silage face temperature was 38°C (ambient 5°C); yeast counts > 10⁶ cfu/g; pH 
5.2. No L. buchneri inoculant had been used. Corrective actions: discarded the top 
1 m, applied L. buchneri, and installed oxygen-barrier film. Subsequent silage 
remained stable for 4 months (adapted from [50–55]).3.
