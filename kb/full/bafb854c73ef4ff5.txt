Abstract 
Across the rapidly urbanizing Global South, wetlands continue to experience intense socio-
ecological pressures despite their numerous benefits to humans and wildlife. Protecting wetlands 
and other ecological resources requires a renewed sense of ecological stewardship, espec ially in 
endangered regions. This study contributes to this call by (1) analyze the drivers of land-use change 
and ecological vulnerability of the Ada-Songhor wetland area (2) explore the various conservation 
practices deployed and how they support livelihoods and wetland protection (3) examine the role 
of commons-based approaches (i.e. trust, community participation and social cohesion) in 
mediating the outcome of wetlands governance. Using a mixed-methods approach that combines 
satellite imagery analysis with stakeholder interviews, the study finds significant spatial 
transformations over the past three decades, including a 10% loss of vegetation cover and a 15% 
increase in bare land, largely due to anthropogenic activities. The findings further show that state-
community collaborations have been useful in co-producing convivial conservation practices and 
community-based approaches to safeguard the wetlands. Overall, the study ’s findings highlight 
the socio-ecological and governance complexities of wetlands management and underscore the 
benefits of collaborative, well-coordinated, and socially attuned management practices for 
protecting ecologically sensitive areas. 
 
Keywords: conviviality, wetlands, conservation, commons, livelihood 
 
1. Introduction 
Wetlands are dynamic ecosystems essential to the socioecological functioning of both the natural 
and the built environments (Maietta et al., 2020; Roy et al., 2025). They regulate hydrological 
processes, improve water quality through natural filtration, and provide habitat for a wide diversity 
of species (Shrestha et al, 2025). Wetlands are also well-known for their socioeconomic and 
cultural significance, which support rural and peri-urban livelihoods and sustain local economies 
through fishing, farming, and the extraction of raw materials (Alikhani et al., 2021; Atiim et al., 
2022). (Ferreira et al., 2023; He et al., 2025). However, in sub-Saharan Africa, wetland ecosystems 
face mounting threats in cities and regions due to expansive urban development and poorly planned 
land-use and property management regimes (Kuusaana et al., 2021; Kundu et al., 2024; Ntiamoa-
Baidu and Taye, 2025). Wetland conservation is further threatened by resource extraction, 
hydrological alteration, and pollution (Kundu et al, 2024). These threats occur amid growing 
socioeconomic challenges in many natural resource-dependent communities, including increasing 
poverty, food insecurity, and a lack of alternative livelihood opportunities (Matovu et al. 2024). 
The convergence of these challenges amplifies socioecological stressors for the more than 100 
million wetland-dependent population in sub-Saharan Africa (Duku et al., 2022). 
3 
 
Additionally, recent evidence suggests that about 35 per cent of wetlands have been lost globally 
since the 1970s (Ramsar Convention, 2021). These trends have prompted governments and 
environmental institutions to introduce regulatory measures such as Ramsar site designations, 
protected area regimes, land-use planning controls, and restrictions on activities including sand 
winning, mangrove harvesting, and settlement development in wetland buffer zones (Kuusaana et 
al., 2021; Cobbinah et al., 2020). Despite these interventions, wetland degradation continues, 
raising concerns about the effectiveness of predominantly top-down conservation approaches. 
While the need for wetland protection is widely acknowledged, the debate often remains lopsided 
(Kundu et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2024), with strong emphasis on regulation and protection but 
limited attention to how conservation measures intersect with local livelihoods, access rights, and 
collective resource management. Evidence from Africa illustrates these tensions. In South Africa, 
Maseko and Moyo (2022) report conflicts between Simangaliso Wetland Park managers and 
surrounding communities due to livelihood restrictions. Similarly, Frimpong et al. (2025) highlight 
contestations among residents, planners, and developers over the management of the Sakumono 
Ramsar Site in Tema, Ghana, while Asante et al. (2024) describe “wetland gentrification” driven 
by rising property values and speculative development. These experiences point to the limitations 
of exclusionary conservation and highlight the need for more inclusive approaches, such as 
convivial conservation , which emphasize co-existence, participation, and equitable access to 
ecological resources. 
Despite growing contributions to wetlands conservation, there remains a limited understanding of 
how these interventions shape the everyday livelihoods of local communities, and how state –
community relations, trust, community participation, and social cohesion mediate wetland 
governance outcomes in Ghana. Consequently, this paper seeks to (1) analyze the drivers of land-
use change and ecological vulnerability of the Ada-Songhor wetland area (2) explore the various 
conservation practices deployed and how they support livelihoods and wetland protection (3) 
examine the role of commons-based approaches (i.e. trust, community participation and social 
cohesion) in mediating the outcome of wetlands governance. These objectives are unpacked 
through the integrated theoretical lenses of Convivial Conservation (CC) and the Common-Pool 
Resource (CPR) to show how state-community collaborations are advancing wetland conservation 
despite glaring anthropogenic threats. 
The findings of this study contribute to emerging knowledge on wetland management and 
community livelihood outcomes in regions experiencing expansive urban development without 
strategic land use planning and management. The findings also emphasise the practical value of 
wetland co-management approaches, protecting ecological resources while safeguarding people’s 
right to livelihoods. Finally, it offers practical implications for wetland policy and planning by 
identifying institutional, social, and ecological conditions that can support more equitable and 
sustainable co-management approaches in Ghana, Africa and beyond.

2. Theoretical underpinning of the study 
Combining the theoretical perspectives of CC and CPR offers a potent analytical framework for 
addressing socio-environmental challenges by promoting locally engaged resource governance 
approaches and realising a global vision of human-nature coexistence in a Global South context. 
In this study, we extend the original thinking of Illich (1973) and Ostrom (1980) to provincialise 
how convivial conservation practices and community-based approaches are contributing to the 
sustainable and collective management of wetland resources in Ada, Ghana. This contributes to 
the broader global, regional, and local goals of realising the cohabitation of human and non-
humans (wetlands) in overlapping spaces (Toncheva et al., 2022). Below, we advance deeper into 
these two theoretical perspectives. 
2.1 Convivial Conservation: towards a just and participatory approach to wetland management 
Convivial conservation is rooted in Illich’s conception of conviviality, which emphasises 
participation, justice, interdependence, and the need for societies to live within ecological limits 
(Krauss, 2021). Krauss (2021) further describes conviviality as a unifying philosophy that 
promotes democratic decision-making and ensures that marginalised and indigenous groups have 
a meaningful voice in the use and distribution of natural resources. Such a logical disposition 
contrasts with market-based or industrial paradigms of conservation (Bryant, 2015). In practice, 
Illich’s view calls for transforma tive approaches that prioritise equity, non-exploitative 
relationships with nature, and collective well-being. Entering the debate, Fletcher et al. (2020) 
argue that convivial conservation sits between two dominant conservation ideologies —market-
driven conservation and strict ecological protection. At the centre of these two extremes, convivial 
conservation seeks to reconcile social and ecological priorities, acknowledging the political –
economic systems within which conservation occurs while advocating for pragmatic pathways that 
enhance fairness and justice in ecological governance (Massarella et al, 2022). 
 
Though evolving, the core principles of CC have gained prominence in conservation literature. 
Notably, there is an emphasis on justice and on recognising ecological resources for their ethica l 
and intrinsic value rather than solely for their economic utility (Giarracca, 2010). This perspective 
encourages more responsible, participatory, and morally grounded stewardship of ecosystems. 
Another principle centres on grassroots decision-making and consultative processes, which 
foreground the needs, knowledge, and rights of marginalised and indigenous communities that are 
closely connected to ecological systems. In realising these principles, Büscher and Fletcher (2019) 
advocate for a conservation practice regime that pushes for shifting from “protected areas” to 
“promoted areas,”— a practice where nature is conserved not from people but through and with 
people. The effects of this relational framing view human presence not as a threat but as integral 
to sustaining ecological systems, reframing activities such as ecotourism through a sustainability 
standpoint rather than capitalist logic (Büscher & Fletcher, 2020). Büscher and Fletcher (2019) 
further call for moving away from a technocratic, top-down conservation management regime 
toward commons-based or engagement-oriented approaches. Asserting such logic positions local 
5 
 
communities as co-managers of ecological resources and helps counter the alienation and 
dispossession that often accompany conservation interventions. 
 
Despite its transformative potential, CC faces several practical and political challenges in 
implementation. Hajjar et al. (2021) note that policies promoting community-based conservation 
sometimes reproduce existing bureaucratic controls, thereby limiting the devolution of power and 
restricting community rights to ecological spaces. This creates opportunities for elite capture and 
continued state dominance (Noe & Kangalawe, 2015). It also becomes difficult in engaging 
multiple actors with diverse political interests towards a collective conservation management 
regime. In response, Büscher and Fletcher (2019) argue that effective implementation re quires 
navigating competing agendas, deciding when to apply pressure or negotiate, and ensuring that 
decisions ultimately prioritize ecological sustainability and community well-being. Another 
challenge concerns the pervasive influence of capital, which tends to commodify nature and shape 
power relations at multiple scales (Büscher & Fletcher, 2019). Responding to this requires 
collective organization, resistance, and transparency to prevent unequal power dynamics from 
undermining conservation goals and to minimize conflict in ecological management—an approach 
that can be likened to “the new politics of conviviality”, which emphasizes the practices of mutual 
adjustment and learning to live together (by humans and nature) (Büscher and Fletcher 2020). 
 
Against these shortcomings, CC has proven analytically valuable in studies seeking to promote 
human–nature coexistence and to design transformative conservation practices. For instance, 
Toncheva et al (2022) and Pettersson et al. (2022) have deployed CC as an analytical tool to 
understand and resolve human –wildlife conflicts in the Rodopi Mountains of Bulgaria and in 
northwestern Spain. Büscher et al. (2022) have also employed CC to examine how different actors 
shape biodiversity outcomes and to identify structural reforms necessary for meaningful 
conservation transformation. We build on these studies to extend the empirical rigor and the spatial 
application of CC by shifting the analytical gaze from Global North experiences and highlighting 
a Global South reality of how co-produced conservation practices are helping to address the 
challenges of wetland conservation. In doing so, this study contributes to sustainable mechanisms 
for addressing the biodiversity crisis ignited by the expansion of human activities (Ceballos et al., 
2017). The study also extends the idea of a stewardship-based co-management model that 
emphasizes working with a range of stakeholders in the enforcement of environmental and 
conservation regulations (Frimpong et al., 2025). 
2.2 Common-pool resource: collective management of ecological resources 
A crucial element of the wetland management regime in the Global South, loosely addressed by 
the CC perspective, has to do with the contested logics over ownership and the collective 
management of wetlands. Here, differential ownership and access to land as a common pool 
resource on which wetland sit determines the effectiveness of management approaches. We deepen 
our understanding of this relevant but obscured area by deploying the theoretical lens of CPRs, 
6 
 
which refers to shared natural or human-made resource systems —such as fisheries, forests, 
irrigation networks, rangelands, and groundwater basins —that are characterized by non-
excludability and rivalry in use, where individuals can access them at relatively low cost, but one 
user’s consumption diminishes what is available to others (Saijo and Kobayashi, 2017). Hinging 
on these characteristics, CPRs occupy a conceptual space between public and private goods and 
are highly vulnerable to depletion. A classic example of this vulnerability is demonstrated in 
Hardin’s depiction of the “tragedy of the commons,” where rational individuals, acting in pursuit 
of individual gain, collectively generate resource collapse (Wall, 2014). Although influential, 
Hardin’s model has been critiqued for overstating the inevitability of overuse and underestimating 
the capacity of users to cooperate. Subsequently, influential scholars such as Ostrom have 
demonstrated that many communities around the world have long prevented tragedies through 
locally evolved norms, monitoring systems, and rule enforcement (Ostrom, 2015). This sparked 
the foundation for a new institutionalist perspective, emphasizing how both formal and informal 
institutions structure behavior, reduce uncertainty, and lower transaction costs in CPR governance 
(Tucker et al, 2023). In fact, in some developing countries, where customary land norms often 
carry more authority than statutory law, these institutional dynamics are particularly significant 
for understanding resource-use decisions and collective stewardship (Tucker et al, 2023). 
The extant CPR scholarship has evolved from diagnosing failure to identifying conditions for 
sustainable collective action. For instance, earlier comparative studies have highlighted that se lf-
governing communities tend to succeed when they are relatively small, socially cohesive, able to 
communicate, and possess strong social capital that supports long-term goals over short-term 
benefits (Steins et al 1999; Berge and van Laerhoven, 2011). Other studies have also systematically 
mapped the diverse rule systems—boundary, authority, position, scope, aggregation, information, 
and payoff rules —through which communities align individual incentives with collective 
outcomes (Dunlop et al, 2021; Neef et al, 2022). In realizing the tenets of collective resource 
governance, Ostrom (1980) outlines eight core design principles, namely: clearly defined 
boundaries; context-specific rules; inclusive decision-making; effective monitoring; graduated 
sanctions; accessible conflict resolution; recognition of local rights to self-organize; and nesting 
of local institutions within broader governance systems (Robert et al, 2021). In essence, the CPR 
literature rejects universal, top-down policy prescriptions and argues that no single institutional 
blueprint can fit in all settings. Thus, successful governance depends on adaptive rulemaking, 
autonomy to revise arrangements, and alignment with local ecological and social contexts (Weston 
and Bollier, 2013). This recognition has driven a broader policy shift toward decentralization, 
participatory governance, and community-based management as viable alternatives to centralized 
control in many developing-country resource systems (Weston and Bollier, 2013). For instance, 
Slough et al. (2021) have found across some developing countries, including Brazil, Peru and 
Liberia, that community monitoring can improve CPR management even in contexts that belittle 
community participation. 
7 
 
The application of the CPR theory extends to contemporary systems such as fisheries, groundwater, 
rangelands, and large river catchments. Within these contexts, Ostrom’s (1980) principles have 
been used to assess why some governance arrangements remain effective amid rising ecological 
and social pressures (Korol and Korol, 2025; Nsoh, 2022). Studies largely focus on polycentric 
and multi-level governance, where local institutions must interact with state agencies across 
broader and more diverse user groups (Armitage, 2008; Basurto, 2013). These modern contexts 
often feature heterogeneous actors with unequal power, as seen in catchments where expanding 
urban centers can appropriate resources at the expense of rural or downstream users (van Klingeren 
and de Graaf, 2021). Overall, while the CPR framework remains widely applicable, its 
implementation today requires navigating greater complexity, larger scales, and deeper 
distributional conflicts than those emphasized in earlier community-based studies (see e.g., Korol 
and Korol, 2025). Specifically, the application of CPR to understand the contested logic over 
ownership and collective management of wetlands remains underexplored. By tackling such 
crucial issues, we contend that adopting a ‘commons’ approach, awareness and understanding of 
the issues surrounding ownership and collective management will contribute to the sustainable 
conservation of communal resources such as wetlands in especially in the African context (Mombo 
et al., 2013). 
Consequently, we deploy the CPR as a diagnostic tool to examine how shared ecological resources, 
such as wetlands, are shaped by contested logics over ownership, patterns of use, social cohesion 
and the broader collective management arrangements (Fleischman et al, 2014). Wetlands are 
ecological resources, yet contestations over who owns the land may result in land –use disputes. 
Hence, clarifying issues over ownership contestations contributes to identifying the pressures 
arising from competing livelihood needs and ecological constraints. It further enables an 
assessment of how existing conservation policies either enable or hinder convivial human –nature 
interactions (Díaz-Pinzón et al, 2024). In the analysis section, we make the case that the use of 
common-based approaches via multi-stakeholder collaborations has been significant in addressing 
issues of ownership disputes and contestations. An insight that demonstrates harmonized 
institutional governance and environmental education on the roles of both primary and secondary 
users of wetland resources (Mombo et al., 2013). 
3. Institutional and regulatory framework for wetland management in Ghana 
Wetland management in Ghana is centred on the Wildlife Division of the Forestry Commission, 
which is responsible for protecting wildlife and conserving habitats across the country (Ministry 
of Lands and Forestry, 1999). The Division works with local communities and the public to 
promote the wise use of natural resources and manages protected areas in ways that support 
education, research, and nature-based tourism. Several other institutions play complementary roles 
within this governance system. The Lands Commission supports wetland protection by 
administering public and vested lands, maintaining land records, and providing mapping and 
survey services that guide national land-use decisions (Frimpong et al, 2025). At the district level, 
8 
 
the Physical Planning Departments (PPDs) of the MMDAs regulate spatial development, prepare 
land-use plans, and issue building permits. Their ability to zone land places them in a strategic 
position to identify wetlands as ecological areas that require protection (Frimpong et al, 2025). 
The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) adds another layer of oversight by regulating 
environmental quality, conducting impact assessments, and ensuring that proposed developments 
do not degrade wetland ecosystems (Donatus et al, 2022). These institutions collectively form a 
multi-layered governance structure, although coordination among them remains an ongoing 
challenge. 
Several policies and laws guide how wetlands are used and protected in Ghana. The National 
Wetlands Conservation Strategy (1999) outlines the country’s approach to sustainable wetland use, 
encouraging livelihoods such as farming, fishing, and salt production as long as they do not 
undermine ecological health (Ministry of Lands and Forestry, 1999). Key objectives of the strategy 
include promoting community participation, maintaining the ecological and cultural values of 
wetlands, integrating local knowledge into management practices, and strengthening national 
capacity for conservation. These principles are reinforced by the National Wetland Policy, which 
aims to safeguard the life-supporting functions of wetlands and enhance public awareness of their 
importance (Ministry of Lands and Forestry, 1999). On the legal front, the Wetlands (Ramsar 
Sites) Regulations, 1999 provide specific protection for designated wetlands and prohibit activities 
such as mining, settlement, waste disposal, and actions that alter wetland hydrology (Government 
of Ghana, 1999). Broader support comes from the Wildlife and Forestry Act (Act 571), which 
offers a legal basis for sustainable forest and wildlife management and emphasizes community 
involvement in protecting sensitive ecosystems (Forestry Commission, 1999). These policies and 
legislative instruments together create a strong framework for wetland conservation, even though 
enforcement and resource constraints continue to limit their full effectiveness. 
4. Methodology 
4.1 Study Area: Ada, Ghana 
Ada East District is one of the 29 Metropolitan, Municipal, and District Assemblies (MMDAs) in 
the Greater Accra Region of Ghana. It is in the eastern part of the region, sharing boundaries with 
the Central and South Tongu Districts to the north and east, respectively, Ada West District to the 
west, and the Gulf of Guinea to the south (MOFEP, 2023). Geographically, the District lies 
between latitudes 5°45′ N and 6°00′ N and longitudes 0°20′ E and 0°35′ E (MOFEP, 2023). In 
terms of physical geography, Ada East is characterized by a generally low-lying and relatively flat 
terrain, situated within the coastal –riverine transition zone where the Volta River meets the Gulf 
of Guinea (see Figure 1) (Ada East District, 2014). The riverine and estuarine features make the 
district an important tourism and recreational destination within the region (Ada East District, 
2014). The landscape is further shaped by lagoon systems, swamps, wetlands, and significant 
mangrove stands, which contribute to the ecological uniqueness of the area (Ofori-Danso, 1999). 
9 
 
The district is also endowed with substantial salt deposits —an important natural resource that 
supports local livelihoods and has historically shaped the area’s economic activities (MOFEP, 
2023). 
 
Figure 1: Map of Ada East District 
 
From a socio-demographic and economic background, Ada East District has a population of 
approximately 76,411 and a land area of 268.7 km† (MOFEP, 2025). Females constitute 51.5% of 
the population, while males account for 48.5% (MOFEP, 2025). Like many districts in Ghana, the 
population structure is predominantly youthful, with about 60% of residents falling within the 15–
64 age bracket (MOFEP, 2025). Spatially, the District is largely rural, with around 65% of the 
population living in settlements classified as rural. Farming and fishing remain dominant 
livelihood activities, complemented by salt mining as a significant economic pursuit (MOFEP, 
2025). In recent years, tourism has expanded economic opportunities for residents, particularly 
through the growth of the hospitality industry. Ada-Foah, the District capital, has experienced a 
marked increase in tourist arrivals, especially to the Volta estuary and the surrounding wetlands, 
which have also attracted researchers and conservation stakeholders (MOFEP, 2025). 
Land tenure arrangements constitute a critical dimension of social, economic, and political life in 
the district, as they determine ownership, access, resource use, and the negotiation of competing 
interests among local and state actors (NDPC, 2014). Land ownership in Ada East is notably 
complex and often contested, reflecting longstanding tensions between the Government of Ghana 
and local communities. Portions of land in the district are vested in the President of Ghana in trust 
for the people of Ada, enabling the state to regulate, lease, or manage these lands in accordance 
10 
 
with customary rights and the interests of clans and families who are the traditional custodians 1. 
The vesting of ecologically sensitive lands—such as the Songor Lagoon and associated wetlands—
in the state is intended to ensure coherent and sustainable management, although such management 
is legally expected to occur in collaboration with local stakeholders (Ofori-Danso, 1999). In 
contrast, lands located farther from these ecologically fragile zones are owned by clans and 
families and are not under direct state control. Nevertheless, such lands are subject to statutory 
zoning regulations and require development permits for any form of construction or land-use 
change (NDPC, 2014). 
Over the years, tensions have intensified between the state and local communities, particularly 
following the leasing of approximately 41,000 acres of land—including virtually the entire Songor 
Lagoon—to a private salt mining company in 2020 (Agbove, 2023). Many residents perceived this 
decision as a breach of communal ownership principles, arguing that they were not adequately 
consulted and that the concession threatened their long-standing livelihoods in artisanal salt 
production (Agbove, 2023). This dispute underscores the broader contestations around resource 
governance, community rights, and state authority in Ghana’s coastal districts. 
 
4.2 Research design 
The qualitative case study research design was employed to interrogate issues of convivial 
conservation practices and commons-based approaches to wetland conservation. The case study 
research design was considered appropriate because it allowed for the combination of multiple 
data sources to examine the study objectives (Creswell, 2014). The study involved both geographic 
information systems and remote sensing analysis (GIS/RS), and qualitative interviews with 
community residents, community leaders and key institutional actors whose work relates to 
wetlands management. 
4.3 Data Collection and Participant Characteristics 
The data collection process commenced with a familiarization visit to the study area to build 
rapport with the potential research participants. They included institutional representatives from 
the Lands Commission, Forestry Commission, the District Assembly, the community residents, 
and their local representatives. It was also to ensure that we identify respondents who are well-
positioned to provide appropriate data and responses adequate to address the study objectives. 
Upon request, copies of the interview guides were given to the respondents to study so they can 
access any relevant information that could better position them to speak to the research objectives 
meaningfully during the actual data collection sessions. The entire data collection exercise 
occurred over a period of 3 months. This period included the time of preliminary community visits, 
the actual data collection exercise, and follow-ups with study respondents for clarifications. A total

4.4 Data Analysis 
The interview transcripts were imported into NVIVO 12 and assigned codes for analysis. 
Following this, the data analytical procedure followed the six-step process of thematic analysis, 
12 
 
which is widely accepted and utilized for qualitative data analysis (see Braun and Clarke, 2006). 
The first step involved detailed reading and coding of every interview transcript, which allowed 
familiarization and themes to be derived from the data. This process was appropriate as it allowed 
for grounded themes to emerge from the data analysis (Manning, 2017). In the second step, initial 
codes regarding vulnerabilities, convivial conservation practices and community-based 
approaches to wetland management were generated. In the third step, the codes were compared to 
ensure they appropriately fit the initial themes generated. The fourth step involved a review of the 
themes to ensure that they appropriately address the study objectives. In the fifth stage, the themes 
were defined and finalized. The sixth step involved the writing and discussion of the study findings. 
The results and discussion were underpinned by the principle of sense-making, where 
interpretative narratives and quotations were implied from the codes and the themes as used in 
other qualitative studies (see Thapa, 2017; Frimpong et al., 2022; Pedrosa et al., 2021). The 
GIS/RS analysis involved LULC analysis to estimate the extent of wetland encroachment over the 
years. 
5. Results 
5.1 Vulnerabilities of the wetland due to anthropogenic activities 
This part of the results lays the premise for appreciating some of the challenges facing authorities 
in protecting the wetlands. It is out of these anthropogenic threats that convivial strategies and 
communal mechanisms have become necessary for securing the ecological integrity of the 
wetland; these issues are presented later in the results. Indiscriminate sand and mangrove 
harvesting have altered the natural boundary of the wetland, leading to massive land-use and land-
cover transformations over the past three decades despite its legally protected status. Figure 2 
shows that approximately 10% of the vegetation cover has been lost between 1990 and 2020, while 
bare land increased by about 15% within the same period (see Table 1). This ecological loss raises 
serious issues about community attitude and conservatory powers of institutions mandated to 
render ecological stewardship over the wetlands.

one estate developer who wanted to put up a hotel… but where he was putting up the building is 
within the core conservation boundary… we intervened and halted it and up till now he has not 
been able to put up the project” ( Forestry Commission official) . Such cases not only show 
disregard for ecological stewardship but also undermine convivial conservation goals, especially 
in realizing the practice of mutual adjustment and peace of human-nature coexistence. 
Beyond physical encroachment, we also find that ecological (wetland) stewardship is under siege 
through the undertaking of harmful and unsustainable environmental practices occurring within 
the wetland landscape. Such practices include improper disposal of waste directly into water 
bodies, sand mining along the beaches, and the cutting of mangroves for fuelwood. We observed 
that there is sloppiness in realizing ecological stewardship because these practices are deeply 
embedded in local cultural and livelihood systems, making behavioral change difficult. For 
instance, “Most of their liquid waste is directed into the wetland” (Municipal Planner). Officials 
also further shared that: 
“Residents hold the view that their grandfathers have been winning sand since time 
immemorial… so when you talk to them to discontinue, they have a problem with that… 
Some people will also cut the mangroves… They hold a traditionally informed view that 
when they use it to smoke fish, it gives a good taste, hence they prefer cutting mangroves 
as a source of firewood” (Community Leader) 
While some of the community’s residents recognized the ecological value of wetlands, they also 
admitted contributing to the ongoing wetland degradation. For some residents, this was due to 
“inadequate public education and limited community engagement by state agencies” (Community 
resident). A blame denied by the informant as they claimed: “residents often lack adequate 
understanding of the wetland’s ecological functions and long -term benefits, leading to misuse of 
the resource” (Forestry Commission official). Other residents corroborated this by stating that: 
“Most residents don't appreciate the ecological value of the wetlands… so they would 
rather go in to cut the trees or try to fill it and use it for residential or commercial 
development” (Community Resident) 
Residents’ lack of appreciation for the intrinsic and ecological value of wetlands contravenes the 
intent of convivial conservation and highlights a deeper challenge of residents ’ (dis)engagement 
in ensuring ecological stewardship. The contrasting perspectives on the ecological value of 
15 
 
wetlands also illustrate weak institutional enforcement, growing development pressures, 
entrenched cultural practices, and socio-economic vulnerabilities in wetland dispossession. 
5.2 Convivial conservation practices 
Though authorities found it challenging to ensure ecological (wetland) stewardship behaviors, 
several strategies that aligned with convivial conservation goals were being introduced to restore 
the integrity of the wetlands. The paper contends that such conservation practices should be 
encouraged to safeguard the natural ecosystem of the wetlands. Notably, an informant shared that: 
“the main conservation practices involve mangrove planting and pruning, particularly at the 
edges of the boundary near the core zone and within the core zone itself ” (Forestry Commission 
official). Mangrove planting contributed to convivial conservation by ensuring that the goals of 
realizing ecological integrity are tied to community well-being. For instance, it helped to realize a 
symbiotic relationship where the mangroves helped in coastal protection and discouraged 
encroachment into the wetlands. This highlights a mutually beneficial endeavor on how planting 
mangroves benefits both the ecosystem and the community. An official explained that: 
“...for instance, if you plant mangroves and the fish come to lay eggs when it rains, the 
water moves the fish into the other lagoon. When they go fishing, they get more fish from 
the Lagoon. They know that the work we are doing is for community purposes, so they have 
embraced it” (Forestry Commission Official). 
Other officials corroborated this, highlighting that “wetland conservation is treated as a 
development priority of the municipality, that is why we have extended a significant support to the 
Forestry Commission.” (Municipal Planning Officer). NGOs also encourage community members 
to participate in mangrove planting, reinforcing conservation awareness and community 
engagement. 
Beyond reforestation, officials also ensured the maximum enforcement of wetlands regulations. 
Livelihood activities such as salt mining, unauthorized farming, and overfishing undertaken in and 
around the wetland were strictly controlled, sometimes requiring collaboration with the Police 
when individuals fail to comply with notices and warnings. Education and awareness campaigns 
were also integral to the conservation efforts, often facilitated by traditional leaders who help 
bridge communication with the local communities. An official mentioned that: 
“The Forestry Commission has been engaging in community education and sensitization 
on the dangers of encroaching on the wetland, and we have been supporting local leaders. 
They educated people about the importance of these sites to us as human beings and the 
world, and how we can also plant trees to protect them .” (Representative of Traditional 
Authority) 
The traditional authority largely facilitated community engagement by “...assisting the forestry 
commission to strengthen bonds with the communities for community education programs to be 
successful” (Representative of Traditional Authority). These community-based approaches 
16 
 
conducted in collaboration with traditional authorities are crucial for wetland conservation through 
the integrated application of indigenous knowledge and empowering community-engaged 
stewards to ensure ecological protection. 
Conservation practices were reported by residents to have positive outcomes, albeit with some 
challenges. Positive impacts included enhanced fish breeding and yield due to mangrove protection, 
as well as employment opportunities through ecotourism. One of the Officials noted that, “...when 
they go fishing, they get more fish from the Lagoon, because of some of the restriction s we have 
placed on the lagoon to allow for spawning. The residents appreciate that the work we are doing 
is for community purpose” (Forestry Commission Official). It was also noted that “...the wetlands 
attract endangered species of birds and animals, creating tourism-related employment that trickles 
down to the common person in the community ” (Lands Commission Official) . In terms of the 
challenges, some residents occasionally attempt to encroach on the core conservation zones of the 
wetlands, even though farming and other livelihood activities such as salt mining are allowed 
within the buffer and transitional zones. An official mentioned that: 
“...we don’t encourage or allow them to farm in the core conservation zone. But some of 
them are trying to come and farm within the protected areas… we allow them to farm in 
the buffer and the transition area so that they can also benefit from the wetland. (Lands 
Commission Official) 
“Salt mining in the lagoon areas close to the wetland poses potential threats to the 
protected ecosystem, especially given that leasing arrangements by the central government 
could allow mining in sensitive zones” (Forestry Commission Official). 
There were also reports of conservation as a source of inconvenience. For some residents, the 
planting of mangroves resulted in the breeding of snakes and mosquitoes. This was corroborated 
by community informants. The participants mentioned that: 
“...we have complained that the dense mangroves have been breeding mosquitoes and 
snakes, and restricted access to water bodies, which affects our livelihood activities in the 
wetland” (Community Resident). 
“Some of the residents complained that mosquitoes have been invading their 
communities...I liaised with the forestry officials and went to cut down some of the 
mangroves” (Assembly Member) 
Officials responded by ensuring that conservation practices do not end up displacing livelihoods. 
They introduced mechanisms such as the pruning of mangroves and controlled mosquito 
infestations. It can be deduced that while regulations and conservation activities impose certain 
restrictions, proactive engagement, communication, and adaptive management can effectively 
mediate tensions and promote coexistence between humans (the community) and ecological 
systems (wetlands).

5.3 Contested logics over ownership and the commons approach to wetland management 
Multiple actors make competing claims over the ownership of the wetland. While institutions such 
as the Forestry Commission and the District Assembly are sponsored by the state and some NGOs 
to safeguard the ecological integrity of the wetlands, Traditional authorities and family heads, on 
the other hand, hold significant overlapping influence over land use patterns because some portions 
of the wetland fall under their control. This is due to the plural land tenure system in Ghana, where 
lands are owned by the state, clan or the family, with inherent contestations that often generate 
land disputes. In the case of Ada, the land is owned by the clan. An official remarked that 
“...here in Ada, the lands are owned by clans or chiefs, who sell portions of the wetlands 
to developers. The problem is that, as an individual, I don’t have any right to go and stop 
the people [developers], who are sometimes wealthy and powerful” (Assembly Member). 
“One of the challenges in managing the wetlands has to do with the inappropriate [illegal] 
sale of the land [wetland], by the traditional authorities, without our permission ” 
(Municipal Planning Officer). 
“The people believe that the wetland is their land and the trees planted there belong to 
them. Meanwhile, state institutions think otherwise …this has resulted in a 
misunderstanding over the use of the trees and other ecological resources ” (Community 
Leader). 
In the wake of the contested logics over land ownership between Traditional authorities, residents, 
and state institutions, officials have utilized several commons approaches to mitigate negative 
outcomes. Notably, they sought the support of the community leaders and the residents to ensure 
the timely enforcement of the wetland rules and regulations: “Yes, because if you don’t act quickly, 
more people will come in and then try to convert these areas [wetlands] into saltpans…We need 
to enforce the rules and regulations together so that we can protect the wetlands (Municipal 
Planning Officer).” 
Through this state and non-state collaboration, both interpersonal and institutional trust is built, a 
virtue that stimulates a unified approach to wetland management. This involved multiple 
stakeholders like traditional authorities, community committees, state agencies, and NGOs. For 
instance, interpersonal trust is fostered through fair mediation, particularly around land and 
seasonal farming disputes. An informant mentioned that: “when there is a land use dispute 
between multiple families and clans over portions of the wetland, we rely on the collaboration and 
the trust we have established over the years to resolve it” (Municipal Planning Officer). 
Authorities also dwell on community participation as a commons approach to wetland 
management; it ranges from active engagement in management to tokenistic involvement in 
decision-making, such as the introduction of site management committees, training programmes, 
eco-tourism initiatives, and mangrove monitoring to enable communities apply indigenous 
knowledge in support of state-led initiatives. The officials explained that: 
18 
 
“…one of the NGOs came and we selected some communities for training on how to 
identify and protect endangered birds…this has helped protect birds on the site.” (Forestry 
Commission official) 
 “We have established a committee comprised of different stakeholders from the 
communities, state institutions [e.g. the forestry commission] and NGOs to develop 
community-based strategies for sustainable wetlands management…we meet every quarter” 
(Municipal Planning Officer).” 
The interviews also revealed the significance of social cohesion in wetland management, 
particularly among families and local communities. This helped to reduce disputes around land 
access and allocation, which also significantly enhanced conservation efforts. Respect for 
traditional leadership ensures compliance with their directives and reduces disputes among 
competing family factions on the use of the wetland. 
“The respect that we have for our leaders [traditional authorities] has helped in resolving 
some of the misunderstandings between the communities on the wetlands. Some 
communities are of the view that they have control over the wetland, and others disag ree, 
but our chiefs have always brought us together.” 
 
6. Discussion 
6.1 Land-use and land-cover change and the drivers of wetland transformation 
The land-use and land-cover changes documented in the Ada –Songhor wetland, particularly 
vegetation decline and the expansion of bare land, reflect governance and institutional failures 
rather than population growth alone. Although the wetland is legally protected, weak monitoring, 
fragmented institutional mandates, and limited enforcement capacity undermine effective 
boundary control. From a Common-Pool Resource (CPR) perspective, this condition approximates 
de facto open access, where formal rules exist but are insufficiently enforced, weakening 
incentives for collective restraint (Ostrom, 2015; Tucker et al., 2023). The resulting pattern of 
gradual encroachment aligns with studies of African wetlands and urban ecological commons 
where formal protection coexists with persistent conversion due to institutional fragmentation 
(Cobbinah et al., 2020; Kuusaana et al., 2021). 
Tourism-led development emerges as a particularly powerful driver of wetland transformation. 
The influx of residential and recreational infrastructure reflects rising land values and speculative 
investment in coastal spaces. These pressures reconfigure local land markets and enable developers 
to negotiate access to ecologically sensitive areas, sometimes through informal transactions. 
Similar dynamics have been observed in other Ghanaian Ramsar sites, where tourism and real 
estate development accelerate wetland loss despite regulatory frameworks (Asante et al., 2024; 
Frimpong et al., 2025). CPR theory explains this outcome as a breakdown of rule legitimacy: when 
influential actors can bypass regulations without meaningful sanctions, rule compliance among 
other users deteriorates. 
19 
 
Culturally embedded practices such as sand winning and mangrove harvesting also contribute to 
wetland degradation, but their persistence is better understood as a governance failure rather than 
a purely cultural problem. The results show that residents’ li mited appreciation of wetland 
ecological functions coexists with claims of inadequate public education and weak engagement. 
This reinforces evidence from other contexts that behavioral change is unlikely where conservation 
messaging is sporadic and disconnected from everyday livelihood realities (Kundu et al., 2024). 
Thus, wetland vulnerability in Ada is produced through the interaction of development pressure, 
weak enforcement, limited ecological awareness, and inadequate community engagement, rather 
than livelihood dependence alone. 
6.2 Conservation practices, livelihoods, and community access 
The results indicate that convivial conservation practices in Ada generate clear ecological benefits 
while simultaneously producing localized tensions around access and inconvenience. Mangrove 
restoration and protection enhance fish breeding and lagoon productivity, confirming established 
links between mangrove ecosystems and fisheries sustainability (Duku et al., 2022). These 
outcomes demonstrate that conservation can support livelihoods when ecological improvements 
translate into tangible benefits. However, tensions arise where conservation interventions alter 
everyday interactions with wetland spaces. Complaints about mosquito breeding, snakes, and 
restricted access to water bodies illustrate how conservation measures can generate perceived costs 
even when they are ecologically justified. Importantly, these tensions do not suggest that 
conservation inherently undermines livelihoods. Rather, they point to the need for adaptive 
management that acknowledges unintended side effects and responds to community concerns. 
Convivial conservation emphasizes precisely this principle: coexistence depends on continuous 
negotiation, mutual adjustment, and responsiveness (Büscher & Fletcher, 2019; Massarella et al., 
2022). 
 
The results further show that regulatory dynamics do not completely deny livelihood access. 
Farming and salt mining are permitted in buffer and transition zones, indicating an attempt to 
balance protection and use. The more fundamental challenge lies in weak spatial enforcement and 
blurred zoning boundaries, which allow some actors to push activities into core conservation areas. 
From a CPR perspective, unclear boundary rules and inconsistent enforcement undermine 
collective action by creating uncertainty over legitimate access (Robert et al., 2021). Consequently, 
vulnerability arises less from conservation restrictions themselves and more from governance 
inconsistencies and weak integration between conservation, spatial planning, and livelihood 
support. 
6.3 Tenure complexity, trust-building, and collective management 
The results show that wetland governance in Ada operates within a context of tenure complexity 
and overlapping institutional roles, where clans, traditional authorities, state agencies, and NGOs 
all claim some degree of influence over wetland spaces. Such plural tenure systems are widely 
recognised in African land governance and often generate ambiguity over authority, access, and 
enforcement (Cobbinah et al., 2020; Kuusaana et al., 2021). From a CPR perspective, these 
conditions weaken the clarity of authority and boundary rules, which are essential for effective 
commons management (Ostrom, 2015). However, rather than resulting in a complete governance 
20 
 
breakdown, the Ada case demonstrates how actors actively mobilise trust-building and 
participatory arrangements to sustain collective management. 
The reliance on collaboration between state institutions and traditional authorities reflects a 
pragmatic response to tenure complexity. Officials’ engagement with chiefs and clan leaders to 
mediate land disputes and enforce wetland rules illustrates how relational trust substitutes for 
formal legal clarity. CPR scholarship recognises such negotiated governance as a functional 
pathway in contexts where statutory authority alone is insufficient (Robert et al., 2021). Trust in 
Ada is therefore not abstract, but operational: it is built through repeated interaction, joint problem-
solving, and shared responsibility for enforcement. 
Community participation further strengthens this relational governance system. The establishment 
of site management committees, training programmes, community-based monitoring, and NGO-
supported conservation activities positions residents as co-managers of the wetland rather than 
passive rule followers. These practices align with CPR design principles on collective-choice 
arrangements and local involvement in rule implementation. At the same time, participation is 
uneven and often mediated through chiefs, opinion leaders, and selected representatives. From a 
convivial conservation perspective, this partial participation may limit deeper democratization of 
decision-making but still represents an important step toward coexistence-oriented governance 
(Büscher & Fletcher, 2019; Massarella et al., 2022). 
Overall, the Ada case suggests that wetland governance is sustained not through clear-cut authority 
structures, but through negotiated cooperation built around trust, participation, and shared 
problem-solving. Collective management thus emerges as a coping strategy within institutional 
complexity, rather than a product of institutional coherence. Strengthening these relational and 
participatory arrangements offers a more realistic pathway for improving wetland governance than 
reliance on enforcement alone. 
7. Conclusion and Policy/Governance Implications 
This study examined land-use change, conservation –livelihood interactions, and governance 
dynamics in the Ada –Songhor wetland using the combined lenses of CPR theory and convivial 
conservation. The findings demonstrate that wetland vulnerability persists despite formal 
protection because governance arrangements are fragmented, enforcement is weak, and 
conservation is poorly integrated with spatial planning and livelihood support. Therefore, 
Wetlands in Ghana constitute not only ecological systems under threat but also contested social 
spaces where access, authority, and survival are continuously negotiated. Theoretically, the study 
extends CPR scholarship by highlighting how contested tenure arrangements and weak boundary 
enforcement undermine collective action even where community norms exist. It also advances 
convivial conservation by showing that coexistence between humans and wetlands is most likely 
when conservation visibly supports community wellbeing and when participation enforces shared 
decision-making. 
There are several policy ramifications. First, wetland governance should move beyond 
enforcement-centered approaches toward strengthened co-management arrangements that 
institutionalize community roles in decision-making, monitoring, and benefit-sharing. Second, 
21 
 
spatial planning agencies must more firmly embed wetland protection within local development 
and environmental planning. Third, conservation programmes should explicitly support livelihood 
diversification strategies linked to environmentally supportive activities such as sustainable 
fisheries, eco-tourism services, and wetland-friendly enterprises. Finally, clearer protocols are 
needed to align customary authority with statutory conservation objectives and reduce 
opportunities for informal wetland dispossession. Aligning CPR design principles with convivial 
conservation ideals offers a pathway for balancing ecological sustainability with social justice in 
Ghana’s wetland governance. 
8. Limitations of the Study 
This study is based on a single case study, which limits the generalisability of the findings to other 
wetlands in Ghana and beyond. Although the mixed-methods approach strengthens analytical 
depth, the remote sensing analysis relies on available temporal imagery that may not capture short-
term land-use fluctuations. In addition, while diverse stakeholders were interviewed, private 
developers and national-level policy actors were underrepresented, potentially limiting insights 
into elite decision-making processes. Nonetheless, the range of stakeholders engaged as part of the 
research process provided valuable insights on wetland management and conservation in Ghana. 
Future research could adopt comparative multi-site designs, incorporate longitudinal livelihood 
data, and include a broader range of private- and national-level actors to deepen understanding of 
wetland governance dynamics.
