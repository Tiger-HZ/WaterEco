This article provides a critical and comprehensive analysis of the legislative frameworks governing wildlife protection in Britain and Europe, highlighting both their strengths and persistent shortcomings. At the European level, the analysis considers the Habitats Directive and the recently adopted EU Nature Restoration Law, which imposes legally binding restoration targets for ecosystems and aims to address the ongoing biodiversity crisis. The article also explores the regulatory environment surrounding wildlife trade, including the implementation of CITES through EU regulations and the challenges posed by illegal trafficking and moves on to examine the foundational statutes in Britain, notably the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, which serves as the principal mechanism for the protection of wild species and habitats, and its integration of key European directives such as the Birds and Habitats Directives. Despite the existence of robust legislative instruments and the establishment of the Natura 2000 network—the largest coordinated protected area network globally—significant gaps remain. These include inconsistent implementation at the domestic level, enforcement challenges, and the dilution of original European standards during national transposition. The analysis reveals that the effectiveness of these frameworks is often undermined by political, economic, and social pressures. Ultimately, the article argues that while recent legislative developments represent positive steps, the protection of wildlife in Britain and Europe requires stronger enforcement mechanisms, greater harmonization between policy and practice, and enhanced cross-border cooperation to ensure the survival of threatened species and habitats.
