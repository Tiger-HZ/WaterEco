Early Mobilization and Return to Physical Activity Following Common Pediatric 
Abdominal Surgery: A Narrative Review 
2.1. Study population 
The studies included in this review primarily involved pediatric patients younger than 18 years 
of age, including neonates undergoing abdominal surgical procedures. The analyzed literature 
encompassed patients undergoing appendectomy, inguinal hernia repai r, colorectal surgery, 
neonatal intestinal surgery, and surgery for congenital duodenal obstruction. Both single-center 
and multicenter studies, together with evidence syntheses and clinical practice guidelines, were 
included to provide a comprehensive ove rview of postoperative recovery, early mobilization 
strategies, postoperative activity restrictions, and recommendations for return to physical 
activity following common pediatric abdominal surgery. 
3. Research results 
3.1. Why Early Mobilization Matters 
Early mobilization has become one of the fundamental principles of modern perioperative care 
and an essential component of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols. Rather 
than focusing solely on early ambulation, it encompasses a multimodal strategy aimed at 
reducing the physiological stress response to surgery, promoting faster functional recovery, 
and facilitating an earlier return to normal daily activities. In pediatric patients, early 
mobilization is integrated with other ERAS interventions, including optimized perioperative 
nutrition, opioid-sparing analgesia, minimally invasive surgical techniques, and early removal 
of drains and urinary catheters, creating a comprehe nsive approach to postoperative recovery 
[4,5,6,7,9]. 
Prolonged postoperative immobilization contributes to delayed recovery through several 
mechanisms. Reduced physical activity may impair gastrointestinal motility, delay restoration 
of bowel function, increase muscle deconditioning, and prolong hospitalizat ion. In contrast, 
early mobilization stimulates gastrointestinal recovery, promotes earlier oral feeding, and 
supports the return of normal physiological function. Together with optimized pain 
management and appropriate fluid therapy, it enables children to resume normal activities more 
rapidly while minimizing postoperative discomfort [4,5,7,9]. 
The available evidence consistently demonstrates that ERAS pathways incorporating early 
mobilization improve postoperative recovery without compromising patient safety. In a 
prospective cohort of children undergoing various gastrointestinal procedures, implementation 
of ERAS protocols reduced the time to first flatus and defecation, shortened postoperative 
intravenous nutrition and hospital stay, and lowered hospitalization costs without increasing 
postoperative complications [4]. Similarly, a systematic review and meta-analysis including 16 
studies and 1723 pediatric patients found that ERAS protocols significantly reduced the time 
to initiation of enteral feeding, accelerated achievement of full enteral nutrition, decreased 
opioid requirements, and shortened hospital stay by an average of 2.5 days, while complication 
and readmission rates remained unchanged [7]. Comparable findings were reported in another 
meta-analysis, which demonstrated shorter length of stay, earlier oral intake, faster return of 
bowel function, and reductions in postoperative complications and 30 -day readmission rates 
among children undergoing abdominal surgery [9]. 
Structured early mobility programmes have also been implemented after major pediatric 
procedures, including liver transplantation, and were associated with earlier independent 
ambulation and shorter hospital stay [10]. 
Overall, current evidence supports early mobilization as a safe and effective component of 
pediatric perioperative care. When incorporated into comprehensive ERAS pathways, it 
contributes to faster functional recovery, earlier restoration of gastrointestinal function, shorter 
hospitalization, and improved postoperative outcomes without increasing the risk of 
complications [4,5,6,7,9]. 
 
3.2. Return to Physical Activity after Common Pediatric Abdominal Procedures 
3.2.1. Appendectomy 
Appendectomy is one of the most frequently performed emergency surgical procedures in 
children and is generally associated with rapid postoperative recovery, particularly in cases of 
uncomplicated appendicitis treated laparoscopically. Despite the widespread use of minimally 
invasive techniques, recommendations regarding the timing of return to unrestricted physical 
activity remain inconsistent and are often based on surgeon preference rather than high-quality 
evidence [3,11,12,13,14]. 
Recent prospective evidence suggests that postoperative activity restrictions may be more 
conservative than necessary. Harvey et al. compared physician -directed activity restrictions 
(PDAR), consisting of a routine recommendation to avoid full activity for two weeks, with 
self-directed activity restrictions (SDAR), in which children resumed activities whenever they 
and their caregivers felt comfortable. Although the difference did not reach statistical 
significance, patients in the SDAR group returned to un restricted activity approximately two 
days earlier than those following physician -directed recommendations (9.5 vs. 11.9 days), 
without an increase in postoperative complications or a reduction in patient and caregiver 
satisfaction [ 3]. Importantly, nearly all caregivers (97.7%) reported feeling comfortable 
determining when their child could safely resume normal activities, supporting greater family 
involvement in postoperative decision-making [3]. 
Additional insight into postoperative recovery was provided by Reynolds et al., who evaluated 
recovery following several common pediatric surgical procedures. After uncomplicated 
laparoscopic appendectomy, children returned to baseline behaviour after a me an of 7.1 
postoperative days, discontinued analgesics after approximately 2.9 days, and returned to 
school after an average of 7.6 days. These findings suggest that functional recovery generally 
occurs within the first postoperative week, although return t o school may be delayed slightly 
beyond the resolution of symptoms [ 13]. The authors emphasized that evidence -based 
counselling may better prepare families for postoperative recovery and help standardize 
recommendations regarding school attendance and daily activities [13]. 
The benefits of minimally invasive surgery are also reflected in the speed of functional 
recovery. In a prospective randomized trial, patients undergoing laparoscopic appendectomy 
resumed full activities significantly earlier than those treated with an open approach (14 versus 
25 days) and required a shorter duration of postoperative analgesic use [ 11]. Consistent with 
these findings, Ionescu et al. reported that junior athletes treated laparoscopically for 
complicated appendicitis were able to resume sports participation within 10 –12 days after 
surgery, suggesting that minimally invasive surgery may facilitate an early return to intensive 
physical activity even after more advanced disease [15]. 
More recently, improvements in perioperative care have further accelerated recovery after 
appendectomy. A systematic review and meta -analysis evaluating same -day discharge after 
laparoscopic appendectomy for uncomplicated appendicitis found no increase in readmissions, 
emergency department visits, reoperations, or overall complications compared with overnight 
hospitalization. Moreover, same-day discharge was associated with lower healthcare costs and 
a reduced risk of wound-related complications, suggesting that carefully selected children can 
safely recover at home immediately after surgery [ 12]. Similarly, the implementation of 
Enhanced Recovery Concepts (ERCs) in pediatric appendectomy resulted in significantly 
shorter hospital stays without increasing postoperative pain, readmissions, reoperations, 
emergency department visits, or hospital cos ts, highlighting the value of structured 
perioperative care pathways in promoting faster recovery [14]. 
Although precise timelines for unrestricted exercise and sport remain insufficiently defined, 
current evidence supports an individualized return based on functional recovery, symptom 
resolution, and the absence of postoperative complications rather than fi xed time -based 
restrictions. 
3.2.2. Inguinal Hernia Repair 
Inguinal hernia repair is one of the most common elective surgical procedures in children and 
is usually followed by a short and uncomplicated recovery. Nevertheless, recommendations 
concerning return to unrestricted physical activity remain variable, and prolonged restrictions 
are often based more on traditional practice than on procedure-specific evidence [3,13,16]. 
Available data indicate that functional recovery after laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair is 
generally rapid. In a retrospective study of 40 children, Reynolds et al. reported that patients 
returned to baseline behaviour after a mean of 4.8 postoperative days, stopped analgesic 
medication after approximately 2.7 days, and returned to school after a mean of 5.8 days [13]. 
These findings suggest that most children regain normal daily functioning within the first 
postoperative week. The authors consequently r ecommended that children undergoing 
laparoscopic procedures should generally be expected to return to school within approximately 
five to seven days, provided that recovery is uncomplicated [13]. 
The necessity of fixed postoperative activity restrictions has also been questioned. In the 
prospective Let Them Play study, children undergoing routine laparoscopic appendectomy or 
inguinal hernia repair were managed either with physician-directed restrictions for two weeks 
or with a self-directed approach, allowing return to full activity when the child and family felt 
comfortable [3]. Across the combined study population, children in the self -directed group 
resumed full activity approximately two days ea rlier on average than those following 
physician-directed restrictions, although this difference was not statistically significant. No 
increase in postoperative complications, wound or fascial dehiscence, or short -term hernia 
recurrence was observed [3]. However, these results were not reported separately for inguinal 
hernia repair, and only 12 children undergoing this procedure completed the postoperative 
survey. Therefore, the findings support the potential safety of symptom -guided recovery but 
should not be interpreted as definitive hernia-specific evidence. 
The choice between laparoscopic and open repair does not appear to substantially influence the 
speed of recovery. A meta -analysis of eight randomized controlled trials involving 733 
pediatric patients found no significant differences between laparoscopic a nd open inguinal 
hernia repair in overall complication rates, recurrence, hospital stay, or time to full recovery 
[16]. Pooled analysis of four trials assessing return to full or normal daily activity showed 
comparable recovery times between the two approaches [16]. Thus, current evidence does not 
support selecting a surgical technique solely to accelerate return to physical activity. 
Most children resume normal daily functioning within several days and return to school within 
approximately one week after uncomplicated repair. In the absence of robust sport -specific 
data, progression of activity may reasonably be guided by pain resoluti on, normal mobility, 
and uncomplicated wound healing. 
3.2.3. Colorectal Surgery 
Pediatric colorectal procedures, including operations performed for inflammatory bowel 
disease, Hirschsprung disease, anorectal malformations, and colonic dysmotility, are among 
the most extensive abdominal surgeries in children. Compared with routine procedures such as 
appendectomy or inguinal hernia repair, these operations are associated with greater surgical 
stress, prolonged postoperative ileus, delayed oral feeding, and longer hospitalization. 
Consequently, enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) proto cols have been increasingly 
adopted to facilitate postoperative rehabilitation through multimodal perioperative care, with 
early mobilization representing one of their fundamental components [4,17,18]. 
Several studies have demonstrated that ERAS pathways improve postoperative recovery in 
pediatric colorectal surgery without increasing the overall risk of complications. In a 
retrospective cohort study including 194 pediatric colorectal surgery patients, implementation 
of an ERAS protocol resulted in significantly shorter Foley catheter duration (1.0 vs. 1.9 days), 
reduced postoperative opioid requirements, lower postoperative intravenous fluid 
administration, and a shorter postoperative length of stay (4.2 vs. 5.9 days) compared with 
conventional care [ 17]. Importantly, despite faster recovery and earlier discharge, ERAS 
implementation was not associated with higher rates of overall complications, emergency 
department visits, or hospital readmissions within 30 days. Although the adjusted analysis 
demonstrated an increased likelihood of reoperation within 30 days, the absolute number of 
reoperations remained very low, and the authors emphasized that the retrospective design, 
heterogeneous patient population, and limited event rate precluded establishing a causal 
relationship between ERAS implementation and this outcome [17]. 
Evidence supporting ERAS in pediatric colorectal surgery has been strengthened by a recent 
meta-analysis including 10 studies and 1,298 patients [ 18]. Compared with conventional 
perioperative care, ERAS protocols significantly accelerated several indicators of 
postoperative recovery, including earlier return of bowel function, shorter time to first enteral 
feeding, and earlier oral intake. Patients ma naged according to ERAS pathways also required 
less postoperative opioid analgesia and received lower volume s of intraoperative intravenous 
fluids. Furthermore, ERAS was associated with a reduction in hospital length of stay, lower 
healthcare costs, and a lower 30 -day readmission rate. Notably, no significant increase in 
overall postoperative complications was observed. However, sensitivity analysis demonstrated 
that the apparent reduction in readmission rates was not robust after exclusion of one study, 
while power analysis indicated insufficient statistical power for both readmission and 
complication outcomes. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, 
highlighting the need for larger prospective studies [18]. 
Additional evidence comes from a prospective study evaluating ERAS implementation across 
pediatric gastrointestinal procedures, including transabdominal Soave procedures. Children 
treated within the ERAS pathway experienced significantly faster gastrointes tinal recovery, 
with earlier first flatus, first bowel movement, initiation of oral feeding, removal of gastric 
tubes and urinary catheters, and shorter postoperative intravenous nutrition compared with 
conventional care [4]. These improvements translated into a substantially shorter postoperative 
hospital stay (4.8 vs. 7.7 days) and lower hospitalization costs without increasing postoperative 
complications [4]. These benefits were attributed to the combined effects of early feeding, 
optimized fluid therapy, reduced use of tubes and drains, opioid -sparing analgesia, and 
mobilization [4]. 
3.3. Role of ERAS in Early Mobilization and Postoperative Recovery 
3.3.1. Principles and Mechanisms of Pediatric ERAS 
Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) is a multidisciplinary, evidence-based perioperative 
care pathway designed to optimize recovery through the coordinated implementation of 
multiple interventions across the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperati ve periods 
[5,6,8]. Unlike traditional approaches focused primarily on the surgical procedure itself, ERAS 
emphasizes the standardization of perioperative care, integration of best available evidence into 
clinical practice, and active collaboration among s urgeons, anesthesiologists, nurses, allied 
healthcare professionals, patients, and their families [ 5,8]. The underlying principle of ERAS 
is that combining several evidence -based interventions produces synergistic effects that are 
greater than those achieved by any single measure alone, thereby reducing the physiological 
stress response to surgery and prom oting faster functional recovery [ 5]. Core components of 
pediatric ERAS pathways include preoperative education and family engagement, 
minimization of fasting, minimally invasive surgical techniques whenever feasible, multimodal 
opioid-sparing analgesia, optimized fluid management, avoidance or early removal of tubes 
and drains, early enteral nutrition, and early postoperative mobilization [5,6,8]. Although these 
principles were originally established in adult colorectal surgery, increasing efforts have 
focused on adapting ERAS pathways to pediatric populations. Such adaptation is necessary 
because children, particularly neonates, differ substantially from adults with regard to 
physiology, nutritional requirements, developmental stage, communication abilities, and 
dependence on caregivers. Consequently, pediatric ERAS pathways incorporate child-specific 
and family-centered elements while maintaining the central objective of accelerating recovery 
through standardized, evidence-based perioperative care [5,6,8]. 
Effective multimodal analgesia minimizes postoperative pain while reducing opioid -related 
adverse effects, facilitating earlier mobilization and recovery. Optimized fluid therapy helps 
maintain physiological homeostasis without fluid overload, whereas earl y enteral nutrition 
supports gastrointestinal function, limits postoperative catabolism, and enables faster 
progression to a normal diet [4,5]. In addition, minimizing the use of nasogastric tubes, urinary 
catheters, and surgical drains reduces discomfort and removes barriers to early ambulation. 
Together, these interventions promote restoration of bowel function, prevent physical 
deconditioning associated with prolonged bed rest, and facilitate an earlier return to functional 
independence [ 4,5]. Evidence from pediatric intensive care settings further suggests that 
structured early mobility programmes can safely improve functional recovery, with children 
achieving independent ambulation sooner following major abdominal procedures such as liver 
transplantation [10]. 
3.3.2. Clinical Benefits of ERAS 
Clinical evidence indicates that ERAS pathways improve several short -term postoperative 
outcomes in pediatric abdominal surgery. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 studies 
involving 1,723 children found that ERAS reduced the time to initiation of enteral feeding by 
approximately 21 hours, shortened the time to full enteral nutrition by 2.2 days, decreased 
postoperative opioid requirements, and reduced hospital stay by 2.54 days without increasing 
overall complication or readmission rates [ 7]. Simil ar benefits were reported in pediatric 
colorectal surgery, where ERAS was associated with earlier return of bowel function and oral 
intake, lower opioid and intravenous fluid use, shorter hospitalization, and reduced in-hospital 
costs [18]. In a broader meta-analysis of abdominal procedures, ERAS shortened hospital stay 
and accelerated oral intake and bowel recovery, while also being associated with lower 
postoperative complication and 30 -day readmission rates [ 9]. However, these safety findings 
are not ent irely uniform. In a single -center colorectal cohort, ERAS reduced opioid use, 
intravenous fluid administration, urinary catheter duration, and postoperative length of stay, 
but was associated with higher adjusted odds of reoperation within 30 days, althoug h the 
absolute number of reoperations was small and no differences were observed in overall 
complications, emergency department visits, or readmissions [ 17]. Overall, the available 
evidence supports ERAS as an effective strategy for accelerating early postoperative recovery 
while maintaining an acceptable safety profile. In addition to improving recovery parameters, 
ERAS implementation has also been associated with a reduction in postoperative surgical site 
infections in pediatric surgical patients [1 9]. Neverthe less, the heterogeneity and 
predominantly retrospective design of existing studies warrant cautious interpretation of less 
frequent safety outcomes [7,9,17,18]. 
3.3.3. ERAS Across Different Pediatric Procedures 
ERAS principles have been applied across a broad spectrum of pediatric abdominal surgery, 
ranging from appendectomy in older children to complex neonatal intestinal procedures. In 
pediatric appendectomy, the use of enhanced recovery concepts, including early oral intake and 
mobilization, was associated with a shorter hospital stay without significant differences in 
readmissions, reoperations, or emergency department visits [1 4]. In neonates with congenital 
upper gastrointestinal obstruction, an adapted ERAS pathway incorporating parental 
involvement, thermal management, regional analgesia, and passive early activity promoted 
earlier feeding and bowel recovery and reduced the duration of parenteral nutrition and 
hospitalization [ 20]. The development of an internationally endorsed ERAS guideline for 
neonatal intestinal surgery further demonstrates that these principles can be adapted to the 
physiological and developmental needs of even the youngest surgical patients [8]. 
3.3.4. Limitations and Future Directions 
Interpretation of the available evidence is limited by the predominance of retrospective designs, 
heterogeneous patient populations, and substantial variation in ERAS protocol composition. 
Because most studies assess entire care pathways, the independent c ontribution of early 
mobilization remains uncertain [5,7,8,9,18]. 
4. Discussion 
The available evidence indicates that early mobilization should be considered within a broader, 
multimodal approach to postoperative recovery. Pediatric ERAS pathways appear feasible and 
promote faster recovery through coordinated measures such as early or al feeding, multimodal 
analgesia, and avoidance of unnecessary postoperative interventions, without a consistent 
increase in postoperative complications [4,5,6,7,9,14]. 
These findings also suggest that early mobilization should not be viewed solely as an inpatient 
intervention but rather as the first stage of a gradual return to normal physical activity. Although 
the reviewed studies rarely evaluated return to sport directly, improved postoperative recovery 
may facilitate an earlier return to age-appropriate daily activities when clinically appropriate. 
As restoration of normal mobility is a fundamental component of functional recovery, 
postoperative recovery strategies should aim not only to reduce hospital stay but also to 
facilitate a safe return to everyday physical activity [4,6,14]. 
The available evidence has important implications for postoperative activity recommendations 
in pediatric surgery. Current practice remains highly variable, with activity restrictions often 
based on surgeon preference and clinical experience rather than ro bust evidence. Recent 
prospective data suggest that allowing children and their families to guide the timing of return 
to normal activities may be safe following routine procedures, without increasing postoperative 
complications, while also reducing the ps ychosocial burden associated with prolonged 
restrictions [3]. Furthermore, procedure -specific recovery data indicate that evidence -based 
preoperative counselling can provide more realistic expectations regarding return to school, 
pain management, and functional recovery, thereby supporting individualized postopera tive 
recommendations and improving communication between healthcare providers and families 
[3,13]. 
Despite the encouraging results reported across the available studies, several important 
limitations remain. Most evidence focuses on short -term perioperative outcomes, such as 
hospital stay, gastrointestinal recovery, or early postoperative complications, whereas data on 
return to sport, long-term physical function, and quality of life remain limited [ 18]. Although 
long-term follow-up studies have identified persistent differences in motor function and health-
related quality of life in selected pediatric surgical populations, the clinical significance of these 
findings remains uncertain and requires further investigation [ 21]. Future research should 
prioritize well-designed prospective, multicenter studies evaluating standardized postoperative 
activity recommendations and long -term functional outcomes to support the development of 
evidence-based guidelines for return to physi cal activity after pediatric abdominal surgery 
[8,18]. 
 
 
5. Conclusions 
Early mobilization is an important component of postoperative recovery following common 
pediatric abdominal surgery, particularly when incorporated into comprehensive, multimodal 
ERAS pathways. Available evidence indicates that such pathways can accelerate 
gastrointestinal recovery, reduce opioid requirements, shorten hospital stay, and support an 
earlier return to normal daily functioning without a consistent increase in postoperative 
complications. However, early mobilization should not be considered an isolated intervention, 
as its benefits are closely linked to coordinated perioperative measures, including effective 
analgesia, early enteral nutrition, optimized fluid management, and timely removal of tubes 
and catheters. 
Despite favourable short-term outcomes, evidence concerning the optimal timing of return to 
unrestricted physical activity and sport remains limited and procedure -specific 
recommendations are still lacking. Current data support an individualized, symptom -guided 
approach to postoperative activity after routine procedures, while ERAS protocols appear 
beneficial in promoting recovery after more complex operations [ 3,18]. Further prospective, 
multicentre studies are required to establish standardized recommenda tions for safe return to 
physical activity and to evaluate long-term functional outcomes in pediatric surgical patients.
