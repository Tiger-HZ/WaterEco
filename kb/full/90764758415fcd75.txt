Abstract
Heavy metal contamination in river basins poses a global challenge to vegetation health, crop productivity, and long-term ecological security. Existing
vegetation ecological risk assessment methods typically assume uniform vegetation exposure and often overlook the inﬂuence of contamination sources
and hydrological pathways on vegetation vulnerability. This study develops a vegetation ecological risk assessment framework using a source-pathway-
receptor approach to quantify spatiotemporal risk patterns at the root level. An integrated environmental risk assessment was ﬁrst conducted for the pre-
and post-monsoon seasons of 2023. Subsequently, contamination source intensity, environmental pathway intensity, and vegetation receptor vulnerability
were combined using the entropy weighting method to estimate vegetation ecological risk within shallow-root (S-VER) and deep-root (D-VER) systems. The
derived risk patterns were validated using vegetation response obtained from a normalized difference vegetation index.
Findings revealed a moderate level of S-VER inﬂuenced by surface hazards in 49% and 43% of sub-basins during the pre- and post-monsoon season.
Seasonal surface hydrological processes regulated these patterns within the ecological function and agricultural production zones. Conversely, D-VER
exhibited relatively stable patterns with low-to-moderate risk observed in 51% and 46% of the sub-basins due to persistent subsurface contamination and
limited seasonal variability. The lower Hindon Basin exhibits elevated ecological risk due to cumulative upstream contamination loading, intensiﬁed
hydrological forcing, and reduced vegetation resilience. A negative relationship between vegetation response and estimated ecological risk further supports
these patterns. The study provides a scientiﬁc basis for evaluating vegetation ecological risk in contaminated river basins and underscores the need for
targeted monitoring and sustainable watershed management strategies.
1. Introduction
Vegetations are the primary ecological receptor that regulates the hydrological cycle, enhances soil stability, ﬁlters environmental pollutants, and maintains
agricultural productivity and long-term ecological balance. However, the health of these ecological receptors is increasingly threatened by heavy metal inputs
associated with rapid expansion of small- and large-scale industrial activities, intensiﬁed agricultural practices, conversion of forest and barren lands into
agricultural and built-up areas, and the construction of artiﬁcial structures along river corridors (Duan et al., 2024). In particular, the discharge of untreated
industrial eﬄuents, inﬁltration of contaminated wastewater, and agrochemical runoff have impaired the suitability of irrigation water and soil systems. Once
introduced, contaminants enter the soil through seepage and percolation processes. These contaminants are subsequently absorbed by plant roots and
transported to plant tissues, where they interfere with normal metabolic functions (Liang et al., 2026; Mishra et al., 2023). The study by Uslu et al. (2025)
reported a marked reduction in seed germination when irrigation water enriched with heavy metals was used. In addition, drip and ﬂood irrigation practices
can result in direct deposition of heavy metals onto vegetation surfaces. Consequently, across diverse climatic and agricultural regions worldwide, hazardous
metals such as cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), chromium (Cr), copper (Cu), and nickel (Ni) have been identiﬁed as detrimental to food crops, tree species,
plantation, and grasses, manifesting as chlorosis, stunted growth, reduced biomass, and premature senescence (Alegbe et al., 2025; Rai et al., 2019).
According to the previous studies, ecological risk from heavy metal contamination is shaped by multiple environmental controlling factors such as industrial
density, proximity to discharge points, land-use pattern, soil properties, terrain characteristics, and seasonal hydrological variability (Zahid et al., 2025; Das
and Behera, 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Tariq et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023). Together, these factors control the contaminant mobility, redistribution, and
persistence across the river basins. Despite this recognition, most existing assessments do not explicitly link surface and subsurface processes with
vegetation vulnerability, nor do they identify which landscape zones are most susceptible and require prioritized monitoring and intervention (Ma et al., 2026).
Methodologically, earlier research has focused on quantifying heavy metal concentrations in soil and water to delineate contamination zones (Chen et al.,
2025). While such approaches provide valuable information on pollution status, the combined inﬂuence of environmental drivers on vegetation response has
remained insuﬃciently explored. Conventional ecological risk assessment frameworks assume vegetation as a uniform ecological receptor with similar
exposure and response across vegetative organs (Zhao et. al., 2025; Adams and Garman, 2024). In reality, vegetation responses to heavy metal
contamination are strongly dependent on root depth and exposure pathways. Shallow-root vegetation (e.g., annual crops) is primarily inﬂuenced by heavy
metals that are migrated through surface runoff, irrigation return ﬂows, and topsoil deposition (Romeh et al., 2025). In contrast, deep-root vegetation (e.g.,
trees and perennial shrubs) is exposed to contaminants by vertical percolation and shallow groundwater recharge processes (Lv et al., 2025). These
contrasting exposure mechanisms necessitate the development of vegetation ecological risk using a source-pathway-receptor (SPR) conceptual approach.
However, its application has rarely been extended to resolve root depth-speciﬁc vegetation risk (Yu et al., 2025). Similarly, crop growth and hydrological
models, such as the Agricultural Production Systems sIMulator (APSIM), AquaCrop, and the Soil and Water Assessment Tool (SWAT), simulate vegetation
responses to water and nutrient dynamics but do not account for the cumulative effect of multisource environmental parameters (Gavasso-Rita et al., 2024).
The selection of a method for determining parameter weights is also a crucial step in ecological risk estimation. Method based on equal weighting neglects
the relative contribution of parameters, while expert-based approaches introduce subjectivity and inconsistencies in spatially heterogeneous environments.
Beyond these methodological uncertainties, evaluating vegetation responses remains challenging due to limited ﬁeld observations and the indirect nature of
vegetation indicators. These methodological gaps are more pronounced in agro-industrial basins where ﬁeld monitoring is sparse and hydrological
observations are poorly gauged. Similar challenges have been reported in mixed agricultural-industrial landscapes across North America, Europe, and Asia
(Niroula et al., 2023).
To address these knowledge gaps, this study develops a vegetation-based ecological risk (VER) assessment framework to evaluate and map root depth-
speciﬁc vulnerability to heavy metal contamination in a polluted agro-industrial river basin. The framework follows a multistep structure, beginning with sub-
basin scale assessment of integrated environmental risk (IER) as an initial screening indicator of cumulative environmental stress. Subsequently, VER is
quantiﬁed following the source-pathway-receptor conceptual approach by integrating contaminant source intensity (CSI), environmental pathway intensity
(EPI), and vegetation receptor vulnerability (VRV) using the entropy weighting method (EWM). The resulting risk patterns are evaluated against observed
vegetation response indicators to assess their consistency and ecological relevance. Accordingly, this study addresses the following research questions: (a)

A key strength of the proposed framework is its ability to distinguish shallow- and deep-root vegetation vulnerability to dynamic heavy metal exposure

management challenges. This study will also inform environmental management strategies aimed at balancing water and soil pollution control with
ecological function restoration.
2. Methodological framework for vegetation-based ecological risk assessment
The whole study is conducted using a systematic, multi-stage methodological framework designed to quantify vegetation-based ecological risk under heavy
metal contamination (Fig. 1). The overall workﬂow is organized into six steps, beginning with spatial zone delineation and data preparation, followed by sub-
basin scale environmental risk screening, vegetation-based ecological risk formulation, spatiotemporal analysis, validation, and management
recommendation.
In this study, vegetation-based ecological risk (VER) is conceptualized as the likelihood and magnitude of adverse ecological effects on vegetation arising
from the combined inﬂuence of contaminant sources, environmental transport pathways, and receptor-speciﬁc vulnerability. In agro-industrial river basins,
seasonal hydrological variability, landscape heterogeneity, and contrasting root depth govern the exposure of vegetation to surface and subsurface
contamination (Liu et al., 2022). To explicitly capture these interacting processes, the present study adopts the source-pathway-receptor (SPR) conceptual
approach to represent the causal linkages between hazard generation, transport mechanisms, and ecological response. Following the SPR structure shown
in Fig. 2, we have restructured VER from three interacting components: (i) contamination source intensity (CSI); (ii) environmental pathway intensity (EPI);
and (iii) vegetation receptor vulnerability (VRV). Nevertheless, the computation of these components required the integration of multi-source environmental
datasets, including heavy metal concentrations collected in-situ and analyzed under laboratory conditions. The sampling and measurement of heavy metals
in surface water and soil are detailed in Supplementary Material 1. Subsequently, all environmental parameters were normalized and aggregated within
ecologically meaningful sub-basins delineated using a digital elevation model (DEM).
2.1 Basin-scale screening using integrated environmental risk
Before the quantiﬁcation of vegetation-based ecological risk, an integrated environmental risk (IER) was assessed at the sub-basin scale to characterize the
cumulative magnitude and spatial heterogeneity of environmental disturbance (Bartan et al., 2023). Thus, IER functions as an initial screening layer to
capture the combined effects of multiple environmental stressors. Parameters representing anthropogenic pressure, land-use characteristics, soil and terrain
properties, hydrological variables, and heavy-metal concentrations were integrated using the entropy weighting method (EWM) to derive the IER. The
resulting IER map identiﬁes zones with dominant environmental effects and provides insight into the key drivers inﬂuencing the ecological security.
2.2 Quantiﬁcation of source contamination intensity, pathway forcing, and receptor
vulnerability
CSI deﬁnes the severity of heavy metal contamination from industrial eﬄuents, wastewater discharges, agricultural runoff, and polluted irrigation inputs.
These sources were divided into surface and subsurface hazards depending on their presence in the environment. EPI describes the surface and subsurface
processes by which contaminants are mobilized and transported from sources to different vegetation root depths. VRV captures the sensitivity and adaptive
capacity of vegetation to contaminant exposure. This incorporates vegetation characteristics such as rooting depth, physiological tolerance, land-cover
conditions, and seasonal dynamics. The grouping of environmental parameters into the SCI, EPI, and VRV components was performed using multivariate
analysis, as detailed in Supplementary Material 2.

2.4 Validation of vegetation-based ecological risk
Heavy metal exposure develops physiological changes that inﬂuence the absorption and reﬂectance characteristics of vegetation leaves in the visible and
near infrared region of the electromagnetic spectrum. Such spectral responses are effectively detected and quantiﬁed using remotely sensed satellite
imagery. Among various vegetation indices, the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) is a widely accepted indicator for characterizing vegetation
response due to its sensitivity to near infrared (NIR) and red (RED) reﬂectance bands. Previous studies by Mirzaei et al. (2019) and Li et al. (2015) reported
that heavy metal accumulation alters vegetation spectral signatures, thereby reducing NDVI values in the vegetation. In the present study, validation of root

3. Application
The developed framework of vegetation-based ecological risk assessment was applied to the Hindon River Basin (HRB) located in western Uttar Pradesh,
Northern India (Fig. 3). The HRB, a sub-basin covering a catchment area of 5870 km2 of the Yamuna River system, extends between latitudes 28°20′-29°30′ N
and longitudes 77°20′-78°20′ E. The Hindon River ﬂows southward for 394 km before joining the Yamuna River near Gautam Buddha Nagar. This river is
hydrologically supported by major tributaries such as the Kali and Krishni rivers, along with minor tributaries including the Dhamola and Nagdehi.
Over the past two decades, HRB has witnessed a substantial loss of aquatic and human lives through heavy metal contamination in water and soil (Golui et
al., 2022). Concurrently, rapid socio-economic development has profoundly altered the dynamics of regional natural resources. Agricultural expansion now
dominates the landscape at the expense of forest cover, with nearly 68% of the basin area under water-intensive cash crops (Singh et al., 2024). This land-
use transformation has resulted in excessive fertilizer and pesticide application, irrigation demand, large-scale surface water diversion, and intensive
groundwater abstraction. In parallel, the agricultural growth has supported the proliferation of small- and large-scale industries, including sugar mills,
distilleries, paper mills, tanneries, and electroplating industries (Gupta et al., 2023). Inadequate enforcement of wastewater treatment regulations has led to
widespread illegal practices. The release of untreated eﬄuents through concealed pipelines into canals, drains, and river stretches is one such persistent
issue. Collectively, these activities have contributed to ecological and human health risks, manifested through declining crop productivity, ﬁsh mortality, and
an increasing incidence of carcinogenic health disorders (Jadav and Yadav, 2025). Hydrologically, the HRB is experiencing a challenge of water availability
driven by declining rainfall, rising temperatures, and sustained surface and groundwater withdrawals. The drying of the river stretches, and the depleting
groundwater level are eye-witnessed examples in the recent site survey. The regional hydrology is further controlled by terrain slope and the diversion of
water from the Upper Ganga and the Eastern Yamuna canal network (Dwivedi and Yadav, 2025). Considering the spatial heterogeneity in land use, socio-
economic functions, and environmental pressure, a scientiﬁcally robust identiﬁcation of vegetation ecological risk zones is required. Accordingly, the HRB
was classiﬁed into major function-oriented zones (MFOZs), namely the Economic Development Zone (EDZ), Agricultural Production Zone (APZ), and
Ecological Function Zone (EFZ). In addition, the HRB was subdivided into Upper (UHB), Middle (MHB), and Lower (LHB) regions based on terrain
characteristics to capture downstream variations in hydrological processes, contaminant transport, and accumulation behavior.

4. Results
4.1 Statistical characterization of environmental parameters
Descriptive statistics of 36 environmental parameters used in this study are summarized in Table S1. Among the environmental pollution parameters, the
elevated mean concentrations of heavy metals in surface water during the pre-monsoon season follow the order Cr > Pb > Ni > Cd > Hg > As. With reference to
water quality guidelines prescribed by the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS, 2012), we found 71.43% of the sub-basins (4069.88 km²) were impacted by Cr,
while 65.71% (4002.71 km²) and 62.86% (3487.01 km²) were affected by Pb and Cd, respectively. Following the monsoon, mean metal concentrations
declined substantially and followed the order Cr > Ni > Pb > As > Cd > Hg. Correspondingly, the proportion of affected sub-basins decreased to 2.86% (211.25
km²) for Cr, 11.43% (592.45 km²) for Ni, and 14.29% (736.03 km²) for Hg. In contrast to surface water contamination, soil heavy metal concentrations (Fig. 4)
exhibited a consistent dominating pattern of Cr > Ni > Pb > As > Cd > Hg across both seasons. Using the agricultural soil quality guidelines proposed by Kumar
et al. (2019), we found nearly all sub-basins (5818.06 km²) to be unsafe for Cr, Ni, Cd, and Pb during the pre-monsoon season. In contrast, 22.86% (1526.82
km²) and 20% (1281.23 km²) of sub-basins were affected by As and Hg. A similar contamination pattern was recorded during the post-monsoon season, with
Cr, Ni, Cd, and Pb consistently impacted all sub-basins, while only 8.57% (358.27 km²) and 5.71% (357.17 km²) of sub-basins were unsafe for As and Hg. The
observed spatial distribution of each heavy metal concentration in surface water and soil during pre- and post-monsoon season are shown in Fig. S3 (a,b).
Further analysis of coeﬃcients of variation (CV) in heavy metals conﬁrmed signiﬁcant spatial heterogeneity rather than uniform distribution. In surface water,
we observed extremely high CV values (CV > 1) for Ni, Cd, and Hg during the pre-monsoon season. However, soil heavy metals exhibited moderate CV in the
range of 0.24–0.57. Interestingly, Hg displayed exceptionally high spatial variability with CV > 3 in both soil and water media during the pre-monsoon season.
The regional scale analysis of all heavy metals and the inﬂuence of environmental parameters on their spatial variability are detailed in Supplementary
Material 4.
4.2 Integrated environmental risk assessment
Figure 5 (a,b) shows the spatial distribution of IER across the HRB during pre- and post-monsoon seasons. Overall, IER levels exhibited a declining trend from
pre-monsoon to post-monsoon season. However, despite this reduction, the areal extent of moderate and high-level IER increased during the post-monsoon
period, with a corresponding decrease in low-level areas. The proportion of the sub-basin classiﬁed as low-level declined from 60% during the pre-monsoon
to 43% in the post-monsoon season, corresponding to a cumulative reduction of 982.87 km². Conversely, the sub-basin extent of moderate- and high-level IER
increased substantially, rising from 31% to 46% and from 3% to 11%, respectively, with cumulative increases of 807.76 km² and 175.11 km². Seasonally, IER
levels exhibited pronounced variability across 60% of sub-basins. This variability was spatially heterogeneous, with signiﬁcant seasonal transitions observed
in 75%, 77%, and 20% of sub-basins within the upper, middle, and lower regions, respectively (Fig. 6a). An analysis of IER distribution across the regionally
subdivided HRB revealed a clear seasonal transition from low-level to moderate- and high-level IER. Low-level IER remained dominant in 92% (1985.65 km²)
and 83% (1908.02 km²) of the sub-basins of the UHB during the pre- and post-monsoon seasons. Only 9% (77.63 km²) of the low-level sub-basin area
transitioned to moderate-level IER during the seasonal shift. In contrast, the MHB exhibited a pronounced decline in low-level IER from 54% (1418.46 km²)
during the pre-monsoon season to 15% (513.22 km²) during the post-monsoon season. This shift was driven by strong seasonal effects, resulting in
increases of moderate- and high-level IER by 24% and 15%, corresponding to conversions of 672.18 km² and 233.10 km² of low-level sub-basin area,
respectively. The LHB showed comparatively limited seasonal variability, with low-level IER remaining constant at 30% (178.52 km²) of sub-basins in both
seasons, primarily due to 10% (57.96 km²) of the sub-basins transitioning from high- to moderate-level IER. Across the MFOZs, low-level IER dominated in
63% (944.94 km²), 55% (1442.72 km²), and 83% (1194.97 km²) of sub-basins within the EDZ, APZ, and EFZ during the pre-monsoon season (Fig. 5c,d). The
number of sub-basins contributing to seasonally varied IER levels across the different HRB regions and MFOZs is shown in Fig. 8(a). Under post-monsoon
conditions, moderate IER became dominant in the EDZ and APZ, covering 63% (1291.02 km²) and 50% (1356.23 km²) of the total sub-basin area. In contrast,
low-level IER remained prevalent within the EFZ, occupying 67% of the sub-basins.

4.4 Environmental pathway intensity

km2) and low-level SHF in 51% (2670.74 km2) of the sub-basin during pre- and post-monsoon seasons (Fig. S5). The observed change was attributed to a
reduction of cumulative area of 980.88 km2 in high-level SHF during the post-monsoon. Similarly, we observed moderate-level SSHF in 46% (2604.17 km2)

4.5 Vegetation receptor vulnerability
VRV was estimated by integrating root depth-speciﬁc (shallow versus deep root) and seasonal (pre-monsoon versus post-monsoon) vegetation vulnerability.
On analyzing the root depth-speciﬁc vegetation across the HRB, we found a moderate-level of shallow-root and a low-level of deep-root dominated in 77%
(4847.67 km2) and 66% (3564.76 km2) of the sub-basin (Fig. S6). In contrast, only 6% and 17% of sub-basins were observed with high shallow- and deep-root
vegetation. Regionally, shallow-root vegetation of moderate-level was observed in 100% (2055.96 km2), 67% (1576.46 km2), and 60% (1215.26 km2) of sub-
basin of MHB, UHB, and LHB, respectively. In contrast, shallow-root vegetation of high-level was also noted in 20% (143.88 km2) of the sub-basin of LHB.
Similar analysis was extended to deep-root vegetation, where high-levels were recorded in 33% (828.05 km2) and 20% (117.73 km2) of sub-basins in UHB and
LHB. Nevertheless, a low-level of deep-root vegetation was also recorded in 42% (765.39 km2) and 77% (1444.38 km2) of the sub-basins of UHB and MHB.
Across the MFOZs, shallow-root vegetation of moderate-level was reported in 90% (2234.75 km2) and 75% (1746.28 km2) of sub-basins within APZ and EDZ.

we observed that EFZ alone exhibited dominant high-level deep-root vegetation in 50% (499.07 km2) of sub-basins. However, APZ and EDZ were noted with

covered with high-levels of pre- and post-monsoon vegetation in 50% of the sub-basins.
4.6 Spatiotemporal patterns of shallow and deep root vegetation-based ecological risk
We observed a dominant moderate-level S-VER in 49% (2437.10 km2) and 43% (2762.05 km2) of the sub-basin during the pre- and post-monsoon seasons.
Moreover, sub-basins under high- level (610.22 km2) were reportedly transitioned to low (285.27 km2) and moderate-level (324.95 km2) after the monsoon
season (Table 2). The observed transition was due to the seasonal shift (change < 0) in 94.28% of sub-basins across the HRB. A similar pattern remained
regionally consistent across 100% of sub-basins in the UHB, 92.31% in the MHB, and 90% in the LHB (Fig. 6e).
We further analyzed the S-VER distribution regionally, and the results suggested that LHB exhibits dominant moderate-level in 70% (729.76 km2) and 40%
(872.78 km2) of the sub-basin during pre- and post-monsoon seasons (Fig. 7). Moreover, 20% of sub-basins under pre-monsoon high-level S-VER within LHB
were completely changed to low-levels during the post-monsoon season. In contrast, MHB and UHB were observed with a strong low- (46% in pre-monsoon
and 54% in post-monsoon) and moderate-level (33% in pre-monsoon and 67% in post-monsoon) S-VER, respectively. Across the MFOZs, moderate- and low-
level S-VER were dominant in the APZ and EFZ, whereas moderate- and high-level S-VER were dominant in the EDZ. In APZ, moderate- and low-level S-VER
were represented by 55% (1298.22 km2) and 45% (1080.41 km2) of sub-basins during the pre-monsoon season, and 35% (785.86 km2) and 60% (1440.36
km2) of sub-basins during the post-monsoon season. Similarly, in EFZ, moderate- and low-level S-VER were observed in 50% (499.07 km2) and 50% (751.52
km2) of the sub-basins during the pre-monsoon season, and 50% (651.44 km2) and 50% (599.15 km2) of the sub-basins during the post-monsoon season. In
EDZ, moderate- and high-level S-VER were noted in 38% (639.81 km2) and 50% (1021.01 km2) of the sub-basins during the pre-monsoon season, and 50%
(1021.01 km2) and 38% (562.11 km2) of the sub-basins during the post-monsoon season. The number of sub-basins contributing to seasonally varied S-VER
and D-VER levels across the different HRB regions and MFOZs is shown in Fig. 8(e).
Similar analyses were conducted in D-VER, and results indicated dominant low- (51% (2689.66 km2) and 46% (2432.41 km2) of sub-basins) to moderate-
(46% (3074.98 km2) and 51% (3332.22 km2) of sub-basins) levels of D-VER during the pre- and post-monsoon seasons (Fig. 7d). Moreover, 5% (257.24 km2)

further analyzed the regional distribution of the D-VER pattern across the HRB. The result suggested strong low-level D-VER distributed in 70% (688.18 km2)
and 50% (496.25 km2) of the sub-basins of LHB during the pre- and post-monsoon seasons. Similarly, moderate-level D-VER was reported in 58% (1602.32
km2) and 75% (1807.39 km2) of the sub-basins of UHB. In contrast, MHB exhibited only 8% sub-basins with a high-level of D-VER in both seasons. Across
MFOZs, EDZ and APZ were found with strong low-level D-VER in 63% (1075.33 km2) and 50% (1149.10 km2) of the sub-basins during both seasons. However,
EFZ exhibited a signiﬁcant moderate level of D-VER in 83% (1042.60 km2) of the sub-basins during both seasons.
4.7 Validation of shallow and deep-rooted vegetation-based ecological risk
Figure 9 shows the Spearman's rank correlation between estimated root depth-speciﬁc vegetation risk and vegetation response from NDVI. The result
revealed a negative correlation of ρ = -0.29 between S-VER and S-NDVI during the pre-monsoon season. This correlation was found to be stronger during the
post-monsoon season with ρ = -0.36. We further examined the cross-seasonal correlation between S-VER (pre) and S-NDVI (post), and yet the result exhibited
a negative correlation with ρ = -0.20. In contrast, D-VER showed high buffering capacity during the pre-monsoon season, with weak to negligible correlation
observed with NDVI (ρ = -0.11). In contrast, the post-monsoon season improved vegetation greenness, and therefore, a moderate positive association of ρ = 
0.76 was found with D-NDVI. On examining the correlation of D-VER across the season, we observed high temporal stability with a correlation ranging
between 0.83 and 0.91.
5. Discussion
This study reveals the distinct exposure mechanisms for ecological risk in shallow- and deep-root vegetation through interaction of contamination source,
environmental pathways, and vegetation receptor characteristics across the HRB. The contrasting spatiotemporal variability in S-VER and D-VER shows that
the vegetation response to contamination is depth-dependent and seasonally asymmetric. Shallow-root vegetation is sensitive to strong seasonal variability
driven by surface contamination dynamics and hydrological forcing. Conversely, deep-root vegetation responds to persistent ecological stress driven by long-
term subsurface metal accumulation. These patterns are further supported by declining vegetation greenness indicated by lower NDVI values within high S-
VER zones, whereas deep-root vegetation exhibits delayed and buffered spectral responses.

5.1 Inﬂuence of contamination sources and landscape characteristics on VER
The integrated environmental risk identiﬁes hydrological connectivity and land-use intensity as the dominant basin-scale drivers controlling the spatial
redistribution of environmental stress and the resulting vegetation ecological risks (Fig. 10e). In practice, this inﬂuence is reﬂected across the HRB, where
expanding agricultural activities for water-intensive cash crops such as sugarcane, wheat, rice, mustard, and edible vegetables have promoted the
proliferation of sugar mills, distilleries, and agro-processing industries. Eﬄuent discharges from these industries, together with domestic wastewater and
agricultural return ﬂows, have resulted in increased heavy metal loading in rivers, canals, and soils (Sharma et al., 2021). Moreover, the use of contaminated
surface water for irrigation, together with return ﬂows from the topsoil layer, develops a recurring source-sink contamination cycle. This mechanism strongly
exposes shallow-root vegetation to Hg, Cd, and Pb originating from built-up, agriculture, and nearby industrial areas (Fig. 10a-i). Soils enriched in clay and silt
further intensify this exposure by promoting surface adsorption and retention of metals (Xie et al., 2024; Selim et al., 2023). Hu et al. (2023) also reported
high heavy metal accumulation in peri-urban soils and cultivated vegetables, which causes potential human health risks. Consequently, elevated S-VER is
predominantly observed in intensely cultivated sub-basins within the MHB and LHB (Fig. 10d-i). Within these regions, rabi and zaid, including double- and
triple-crop experience the highest levels of exposure (Fig. 10c-i). In contrast, sub-basins dominated by coarser soil texture facilitate the downward leaching of
Hg, Cd, and As into deeper subsurface layers (Fig. 10a-ii). This persistent subsurface contamination contributes to relatively stable low-to-moderate D-VER
patterns observed under plantation and forest cover across the HRB, as shown in Fig. 10c-ii and Fig. 10d-ii.
5.2 Inﬂuence of hydrological pathways in regulating metal mobility
Seasonal monsoonal rainfall and localized contamination inputs from concealed and open drains alter surface runoff, water yield, evapotranspiration, and
percolation processes, thereby modifying heavy metal mobility and exposure pathways. In 2023, water availability across the HRB was higher during the pre-
monsoon season compared to the post-monsoon season (Dwivedi and Yadav, 2025; Jadav and Yadav, 2025). High surface runoff and river ﬂows periodically
reduce heavy metal concentrations through the dilution process; however, they also lead to expansion of contaminated zones via downstream redistribution
depending on terrain slope (Aziz et al., 2023) (Fig. 10b-i). Intensive irrigation practices using contaminated surface water further enhance the wet-dry cycle,
enabling the accumulation and migration of heavy metals (Tudi et al., 2025). Under these conditions, MHB with ﬂatter terrain favours metal accumulation,
whereas steeper reaches of LHB facilitate rapid transport and convergence of upstream contaminants. These coupled processes explain the observed
seasonal transition from high to moderate S-VER during the post-monsoon season (Li et al., 2025). At the subsurface level, percolation and water yield exert
dominant control over vertically downward metal movement in both seasons, while evapotranspiration plays a comparatively minor role (Fig. 10b-ii). These
processes enhance the exposure of deep-root vegetation to long-term storage within the soil proﬁle (Hu et al., 2021). Unlike surface pathways, subsurface
contamination remains buffered from short-term hydrological ﬂuctuations as indicated by relatively stable D-VER patterns across seasons.
5.3 Spatiotemporal behaviour of VER across regions and functional zones
The UHB exhibited strong seasonal variability in both S-VER and D-VER due to dominant hydrological forcing and comparatively low contamination intensity.
In contrast, the MHB serves as a transition zone, where mixed agricultural and built-up areas interact with moderate hydrological forcing, resulting in
heterogeneous risk patterns within EDZ and APZ. The LHB has been consistently observed as a long-term ecological risk hotspot due to cumulative
upstream contaminant migration, intensiﬁed surface hydrological forcing, and reduced dilution capacity. As a result, this region observed high to moderate S-
VER and persistent D-VER across seasons. Across MFOZs, EDZ and APZ were dominated by shallow-root vegetation risk due to frequent irrigation and
Page 8/11
surface exposure. In contrast, EFZ within UHB exhibited long-term soil metal accumulation in deep-root vegetation due to subsurface contamination
migration processes (Deng et al., 2025).
5.4 Implications for VER management
The identiﬁcation of root depth-speciﬁc ecological risk zones provides scientiﬁc guidance for targeted management strategies. Sub-basins exhibiting high S-
VER should be regulated for irrigation water quality, interception of agricultural runoff pathways, and strict control of untreated wastewater discharge (Ferri
and Bolelli, 2025). Areas dominated by D-VER require interventions such as phytostabilization, vegetative barriers, and long-term monitoring of subsurface
contamination (Calmon et al., 2026). Priority should be given to low-risk sub-basins to create ecological buffers through restricting industrial expansion and
promoting sustainable agricultural practices. Importantly, downstream EDZ and APZ require integrated basin-scale management to prevent continuous
accumulation and ecological intensiﬁcation.
5.5 Limitations and future research
Despite the scientiﬁc contribution, this study has a few limitations. Seasonal mean concentrations may not capture short-term contamination pulses during
extreme rainfall and episodic discharges. Sampling density was limited to one representative site per sub-basin, and vegetation exposure was inferred
through root-zone contamination rather than direct plant tissue analysis. Future studies should incorporate multi-site sampling, plant tissue measurements,
sub-basin level crop yield validation, high-frequency monitoring, and coupled reactive transport modelling to better quantify metal migration and long-term
storage within the vadose zone. Integration with geophysical techniques such as electrical resistivity tomography could further strengthen subsurface risk
characterization.
6. Conclusions
The assessment of seasonal ecological risk due to heavy metal dynamics in shallow- and deep- root vegetation proven to be an effective approach to identify
priority conservation areas and strengthen ecological security. Previous studies had simply evaluated the ecological risk using soil and water quality
parameters, while the implications of contaminant transfer to vegetation roots remained insuﬃciently addressed. In contrast, the present study
systematically combined the SCI, EPI, and VRV within a SPR approach. Preliminary investigation through IER revealed that environmental risk was not
uniform across the HRB but dynamically transitioned between low- and high-level under changing hydrological conditions. These ﬁndings underscore the
necessity of root depth-speciﬁc ecological risk rather than uniform vegetation assessments. From pre- to post-monsoon season, a moderate-level S-VER in
49% (1324.75 km²) and 43% (2762.05 km²) of the sub-basins was concentrated in agriculturally and hydrologically connected LHB. However, UHB and MHB
were largely dominated by low-risk conditions. The observed risk levels also underwent seasonal redistribution due to the strong sensitivity of shallow-root
vegetation to hydrological variability in nearly 94.28% of sub-basins. In contrast, D-VER was dominated by low-risk conditions with 51% (2689.66 km²) and
46% (2432.41 km²) of the sub-basins during pre- and post-monsoon season. Although 65.71% of sub-basins exhibited seasonal transitions, the overall D-VER
pattern remained spatially stable. Low-levels of D-VER were concentrated in the LHB, whereas moderate and high-level was observed in the UHB and MHB.
Following monsoonal rainfall, the decline in high S-VER sub-basins and the expansion of low-risk conditions in 80% (4534.45 km²) of the sub-basins indicate
the redistribution of surface contamination by surface hydrological forcing. Similarly, increased subsurface hazard, together with enhanced subsurface
hydrological forcing during recharge periods, led to persistent moderate-level D-VER exposure in deep-rooted vegetation. The dominance of shallow-root
vegetation in more than 77% (4847.67 km²) of the sub-basins within APZ indicates high susceptibility to surface contamination and elevated S-VER.
Conversely, the higher proportion of deep-root vegetation in the EFZ led to prolonged subsurface exposure and persistent moderate D-VER conditions.
Among the MFOZs, EDZ were primarily controlled by SH and SHF, whereas APZ exhibited a strong interaction between shallow root vegetation vulnerability
and SHF. In contrast, EFZ were dominantly inﬂuenced by SSH and SSHF. The overall ﬁndings emphasize that root depth-speciﬁc seasonal ecological risk
assessment should be adopted in place of a uniform vegetation risk assessment framework for targeted pollution control, adaptive agricultural practices,
and long-term subsurface monitoring and restoration measures in agro-industrial river basins.
