Abstract
Measuring growth dynamics has been fundamental to understanding lichen biology and ecology. In this integrative analy -
sis, I synthesize and reanalyze published data on three-dimensional growth in the foliose cephalolichen Lobaria pulmo-
naria transplanted in pristine North American forests, encompassing thallus area expansion, biomass accumulation, thallus 
thickening, and reproductive investment across multiple environmental gradients. Best subset regression selection and 
linear mixed models were applied to identify the principal internal and external factors influencing growth, specifically 
thallus area expansion and thickening, quantified as increases in specific thallus mass. The analyses reveal pronounced 
intrathalline growth heterogeneity, significant trade-offs between reproductive investment and growth, and the multifacto -
rial influences of bark pH, elevation, light regime, and phosphorus availability. Light was often a limiting growth factor 
within forest interiors, whereas excessive stand openness caused chlorophyll bleaching and reduced growth. Initial phos -
phorus enrichment enhanced thallus thickness but did not consistently promote area or biomass expansion. Taken together, 
these finding highlight the habitat specialization of L. pulmonaria and the importance of bark pH, light, hydration, and 
reproductive investment in shaping growth dynamics. The study advances the understanding of growth strategies in L. 
pulmonaria and provides a foundation for predicting responses to environmental change and guiding conservation efforts.

Beschel 1958; Armstrong and Bradwell 2011). Early stud -

the main modes of thallus growth (Hale 1970; Hill 1981).

The study has three specific objectives: (1) to charac -
terize the intrathalline pattern of area expansion within L. 
pulmonaria; (2) to test for trade-offs between reproduction 
and growth by examining relationships between thallus area 
and mass increments and recent measures of reproductive 
investment, with the hypothesis that marginal soralia, by 
disrupting marginal meristems, would be associated with 
stronger growth reductions than laminal soralia; and (3) 
to develop predictive models of thallus area growth and 
thickening using available lichen traits and environmental 
variables from studies in pristine North American forests 
(Gauslaa and Goward 2012; Bidussi et al. 2013b; Gauslaa 
2026).
Materials and methods
Measurements of growth
Historically, lichen thallus growth has often been quanti -
fied as the percentage mass increase, linear extension of 
individual lobes, or diameter increment in circular thalli, 
as reviewed by Hale ( 1973) and Armstrong ( 1976). With 
the development of digital photography and computerized 
image analysis, measurements of thallus area increment 
have become increasingly common. However, area-based 
methods require standardization of hydration status at the 
beginning and end of study periods, because thallus area can 
shrink substantially during desiccation.
Relative growth rates, originally developed for plants 
(Evans, 1972), have since been adapted for lichens. Rela -
tive growth rate (RGR) expresses the increase in dry mass 
(DM) in mg g− 1 day− 1, while the relative thallus area growth 
rate (RTAGR) quantifies the increase in thallus area (A) in 
mm2 cm− 2 day− 1. When time (t) is measured in days, these 
metrics are calculated as: RGR = (ln (DM end/DMstart)) * 
1000/Δt, and RTAGR = (ln (Aend/Astart)) * 100/Δt.
Lichen thickness typically varies substantially across a 
thallus. However, specific thallus mass, defined as DM per 
unit fully hydrated area (STM = DM/A), serves as a practi -
cal proxy for mean thallus thickness (Gauslaa and Coxson 
2011). Growth in thallus thickness is expressed as: Percent 
change in STM = (STMend - STMstart) * 100/STMstart.
Dataset compilation and experimental overview
A comprehensive dataset was assembled by integrating 
published 14-month growth data (RGR, RT AGR, and per -
cent change in STM) for L. pulmonaria thalli transplanted 
into pristine inland forests of British Columbia (Gauslaa 
and Goward 2012; N = 208; Bidussi et al. 2013b; N = 94), 
together with recent quantifications of soralia for all trans -
plants included in those studies (Gauslaa 2026). One study 
(Gauslaa and Goward 2012) transplanted thalli onto pairs of 
neighboring, similar-sized spruce trees in dripzone and non-
dripzone position, either beneath or outside Populus cano-
pies, on a gently undulating valley floor. The other study 
(Bidussi et al. 2013b) used spruce trees distributed along an 
adjacent mountain slope ranging from 550 to 1650 m a.s.l. 
This expanded dataset enables more robust assessment of 
growth and facilitates analyses of how reproductive traits, 
such as soralia, influence lichen growth rates.
Both transplantation experiments used L. pulmonaria 
transplants collected simultaneously from a single humid, 
partially shaded source stand dominated by juvenile and 
early-reproducing thalli. These studies were conducted con-
currently from May 2010 to August 2011, thereby eliminat-
ing confounding effects of study and year or differing source 
material. This is particularly important because lichen 
growth rates can vary substantially among years (e.g., Val -
lot 1896; Phillips 1969). The studies spanned environmental 
gradients in pristine forests that regenerated after a stand-
replacing wildfire in 1926, including bark pH, elevation, 
and site factors reflecting canopy openness. Indirect site fac-
tor (ISF) and direct site factor (DSF) describe the proportion 
of diffuse and direct solar radiation, respectively, reaching a 
lichen transplant (Anderson 1964).
Transplants were randomly assigned between the two 
experiments and subjected to similar methodologies. As 
a phosphorus (P) pretreatment, 50% of all transplants, 
selected at random, were submerged for 5 min in a 500 mg 
L− 1 K2PO4, whereas the remaining 50% served as controls 
and were submerged in distilled water. This treatment added 
both P and K to the transplants, but Gauslaa and Goward 
(2020) found no growth-stimulating effect of K in the local 
canopy throughfall on L. pulmonaria , suggesting that K 
was not a limiting factor. On each tree, lichen transplants

Statistical methods
Data from five thalli were excluded due to partial or com -
plete loss. Growth rates could not be calculated for ten thalli 
(3.3%) because of loss, fragmentation, or outlying STM val-
ues attributed to measurement or labeling errors. Means are 
reported ± standard error.
Model selection was performed using best subset regres-
sions in Minitab Statistical Software 22, with variables log-
transformed where appropriate. For analyses of variation in 
RTAGR, RGR, and percent change in STM, the following 
explanatory variables were initially included: initial thal -
lus area (A Start; mean = 13.76 ± 0.25 cm 2, range = 5.7–29.9 
cm2); initial dry mass (DM Start; mean = 118.1 ± 2.5 mg, 
range = 40–321 mg); initial specific thallus mass (STM -
Start; mean = 8.5 ± 0.5 mg cm− 2, range = 6.1–11.7 mg cm− 2); 
change in STM (mean = 17.5 ± 0.5%, range = -2.5–44.5%); 
bark pH (mean = 4.88 ± 0.04, range = 3.95–6.77); indirect 
site factor (ISF; mean = 0.286 ± 0.003, range = 0.179–0.457); 
direct site factor (DSF; mean = 0.230 ± 0.006, range = 0.041–
0.467); elevation (mean = 829 ± 16 m, range = 550–1650 m); 
number of soralia at the end of the experiment per cm 2 
thallus area (used as proxy for reproductive investments; 
mean = 3.36 ± 0.15, range = 0–13.6); number of laminal sora-
lia at the end of the experiment per cm 2 thallus area (best 
correlated with % increase in STM; mean = 0.43 ± 0.05, 
range = 0–6.3); and chlorophyll bleaching at the end of the 
experiment (mean = 3.2 ± 0.5%, range = 0–70%).
After identifying the optimal subset of predictors, the 
resulting model was further evaluated using a linear mixed 
model in Jamovi 2.6.26 with tree ID (the recipient of trans -
plants) included as a cluster variable to account for potential 
non-independence of samples within trees.
Results and discussion
Intrathalline variability in growth rates
A typical L. pulmonaria thallus (Fig. 1) exhibited both api-
cal/marginal and intercalary growth as proposed by Honeg-
ger (1993). In the illustrated specimen, the percent increase 
in mass (104%) exceeded the percent increase in area 
(83.9%) over the 14-month transplantation period, resulting 
in an 11.1% increase in STM and thus in thallus thickness. 
Expressed as relative growth, the RGR was 1.63 mg g− 1 d− 1 
and the corresponding RTAGR was 0.139 mm2 cm− 2 d− 1.
Growth was highly heterogeneous within the thallus, 
with individual lobes expanding markedly at different rates 
(Fig. 1). This pattern aligns with earlier reports of decoupled 
growth rates among separate lobes within a thallus (Linkola 
1918; Phillips 1969). In this individual, lobe width increased 
more than lobe length, and the greatest expansion occurred 
in submarginal depressions between reticulated ridges in 
rapidly growing lobes (Fig. 1).
Species of Lobaria are recognized for their higher 
growth rates relative to other foliose lichens (Phillips 1969; 
Hale 1973), even when growing alongside them in favor -
able habitats such as open forests (Gauslaa and Goward 
2012; Bidussi et al. 2013b) and forest gaps (Bidussi and 
Gauslaa 2015). The combination of pronounced intercalary 
growth and strong intrathalline variation, as visualized in 
Fig. 1, likely contributes to the fast growth observed in L. 
pulmonaria.
Balancing growth and reproduction: patterns and 
trade-offs
Over the full 14-month observation period, the mean 
RTAGR for all L. pulmonaria transplants was 0.077 ± 0.002 
mm2 cm − 2 d − 1 (range = -0.007–0.158 mm 2 cm − 2 d − 1, 
N = 298), corresponding to 40.5 ± 1.2% area growth (range 
= -3–100%), and the mean RGR was 1.13 ± 0.02 mg g− 1 d− 1 
(range = 0.14–1.88 mg g− 1 d− 1), equivalent to a 64.6 ± 1.4% 
increase in dry mass (range = 6–128%). Greater reproduc -
tive investment was associated with lower RT AGR and 
RGR (Table 1). Thallus area expansion was slightly more 
negatively affected by reproductive traits than mass growth 
(Table 1). Marginal soralia were more strongly associated 
with reduced growth rates than laminal soralia (Table 1), 
possibly because their formation disrupts apical and mar -
ginal meristems. However, 99% of transplants still showed 
positive RTAGR, indicating that marginal soralia did not 
fully prevent thallus area expansion. Overall, total (mar -
ginal + laminal soralia) reproductive investments showed 
stronger negative correlation coefficients with growth rates 
than either soralia category alone (Table 1). At the end of 
experiment, marginal soralia were 6.8 times more abundant 
and covered 11.6 times more thallus area than laminal sora-
lia (Gauslaa 2026), making it difficult to attribute stronger 
growth-retarding effects solely to their marginal position. 
Thus, the hypothesis that marginal soralia exert a stronger 
inhibitory effect on growth than laminal soralia received 
limited support.
Recognition of a trade-off between reproduction and 
growth in lichens dates back to early studies (e.g., Beschel 
1958). In the present study, the final total number of sora -
lia per thallus area showed the strongest relationship with 
RTAGR (Fig. 2), although the effect was weak despite being

Open-shade habitats, rich in diffuse skylight but low in direct

Site-specific water availability was not measured. 
Although rainfall is relatively consistent across the study 
area, variation in rainfall strongly influences lichen growth 
rates at broader spatial scales (Kärenlampi 1970; Gauslaa 
et al. 2007). At local scales, tree architecture redistributes 
water within the canopy. Taller trees channel throughfall 
more efficiently away from lichen sites on lower branches 
than smaller trees, as observed during rainy days (Gauslaa 
and Goward 2012). At the same time, tall canopies reduce 
nocturnal reradiation from lower branches, thereby dimin -
ishing the nocturnal cooling needed for dew formation. Air 
humidity and dewfall, both of which are at least as impor -
tant for lichens as rainfall (Gauslaa 2014), vary strongly at 
small spatial scales (Bidussi et al. 2013b). Nocturnal dew -
fall promotes lichen growth by ensuring full turgor pressure 
and facilitating thallus area expansion (Bidussi et al. 2013a). 
Where assessed, the height of trees used for transplantation 
negatively affected growth of L. pulmonaria (Gauslaa and 
Goward 2012, 2020). Tree height and canopy density likely 
account for much of the variance associated with the ran -
dom tree effect through their influence on hydration. Includ-
ing direct measures of hydration sources would probably 
further improve future growth models.

Determinants and patterns of thallus thickening
Both increased lobe thickness (Gauslaa and Coxson 2011) 
and a deeper reticulate morphology (Phinney 2019) increase 
STM in L. pulmonaria . STM is a flexible functional trait 
that shapes the ecologically important internal water-hold -
ing capacity (WHC internal), expressed either as mm water 
or as water mass per unit thallus area, across macrolichen 
growth forms (Gauslaa 2014; Esseen et al. 2015; Phinney 
et al. 2022). Higher STM prolongs active hydration periods 
in lichens (Gauslaa and Solhaug 1998; Gauslaa et al. 2017; 
Ås Hovind et al. 2020), while a more pronounced reticu -
late pattern forms external pockets that retain rainwater 
and thereby extend hydration into subsequent dry periods 
(Phinney 2019). It should be noted, however, that greater 
reproductive investment is also associated with higher STM 
(Gauslaa 2026).
Thallus thickness generally increases with growth and 
size (Dahlman et al. 2002; Chinnery and Ellis 2023), sug-
gesting size-related acclimatization to hydration sources 
(Merinero et al. 2014). In the present study, however, no 
significant relationship was found between initial transplant 
size and change in STM (P = 0.727). Within the limited size 
range of transplants, the relationship between area and STM

strongly along environmental gradients. For example, STM 
is nearly twice as high in Hypogymnia physodes on sun-
exposed tree trunks and cliffs as on shaded forest trunks 
(Solhaug et al. 2009), and STM increases with canopy open-
ness in Lobaria species (Gauslaa and Coxson 2011). Thallus 
thickening is typically triggered by transplantation to more 
light-exposed habitats (Gauslaa 2014; Merinero et al. 2015; 
Longinotti et al. 2017; Chinnery and Ellis 2023; Di Nuzzo 
et al. 2026). In the present study, transplants showed slightly 
increased browning at the end of the experiment (Fig. 1), 
indicative of melanin formation as a response to higher 
UV-B levels (Gauslaa and Solhaug 2001; Solhaug et al. 
2003). This change is consistent with higher light exposure 
at transplant sites relative to the source habitat. Seasonal 
studies have shown that STM reaches the highest values 
during the driest part of the year in both L. pulmonaria 
(Gauslaa et al. 2013; Di Nuzzo et al. 2026) and Xanthoria 
aureola (Gauslaa and McEvoy 2005), likely because low 
turgor pressure in fungal hyphae then prevents area expan -
sion while still allowing net photosynthetic gain (Larsson et 
al. 2012). STM also increases with temperature, as demon -
strated in a 14-days growth chamber experiment (Bidussi et 
al. 2013a), probably because higher temperature increases 
evaporative demands.
The present study also revealed patterns that contrast 
with general expectations. A decline in STM with increas -
ing DSF (Fig. 5; P < 0.001) was an unexpected finding. 
This seemingly contradictory response may reflect spatial 
variations in nocturnal dew formation. On clear nights, cold 
air from the surrounding mountains tends to accumulate in 
topographic depressions such as the valley floor, and stronger 
nocturnal cooling may therefore generate sufficient morning 
dew to hydrate lichens at sites with high DSF, where sparse 
canopy cover provides little protection against long-wave 
reradiation (Y . Gauslaa, pers. obs.). Hydration to full tur -
gor promotes thallus area growth during the night and early 
morning (Bidussi et al. 2013a), whereas rapid sun-driven 
desiccation later in the day shortens the photosynthetically 
active period and thereby limits further thickening (Larsson 
et al. 2012; Bidussi et al. 2013a). STM may increase most 
rapidly when hydration supports prolonged net photosyn -
thesis below the threshold needed for full turgor.
Finally, STM increased with elevation along the steep 
forested mountainside studied here (Table 3). Higher eleva-
tions in the study area are characterized by cooler temper -
atures and greater rainfall (Bidussi et al. 2013b), whereas 
stronger nocturnal cooling at lower elevations frequently 
promotes the formation of heavy dew. Nocturnal dew and 
humid air tend to favor thallus area extension, resulting in 
thinner thalli (Bidussi et al. 2013a; Gauslaa 2014), while 
lichens in habitats where rain is the main hydration source 
may invest more in thallus thickness to improve water stor-
age and compensate for less frequent hydration events. 
An elevational increase in STM has also been reported for 
Nephroma arcticum (McEvoy et al. 2007). In that case, 
however, the gradient extended above the alpine timber -
line, suggesting that the increase in STM may instead have 
reflected greater light exposure and correspondingly shorter 
periods of full-turgor hydration in alpine environments.

Phosphorus-induced increases in thallus thickness 
and water storage
Phosphorus addition was a strong positive predictor for the 
percentage increase in thallus thickness (P < 0.001; Table 3). 
Its effect was most pronounced in transplants at shaded sites 
and diminished in more open stands with high DSF, as indi-
cated by overlapping confidence intervals at the highest 
DSF values (≥ 0.38; Fig. 5). Transplants receiving the initial 
P-fertilization exhibited a significantly greater increase in 
STM (20.3 ± 0.7%) than controls (14.7 ± 0.7%; P < 0.001; 
Table 4). P-treated transplants also had slightly higher final 
STM ( P = 0.002) and, correspondingly, higher WHC internal 
(P = 0.018; Table 4). In contrast, RGR was unaffected by 
the P treatment (P = 0.193), and RTAGR showed a marginal 
tendency to be lower in P-fertilized transplants ( P = 0.056; 
Table 4). Phosphorus addition did not contribute to models 
of RTAGR (P = 0.99) or RGR (P = 0.96).
Within the low to intermediate pH-range typical of tree 
bark and forest canopy throughfall, increasing pH is often 
associated with elevated levels of P and base cations, 
reflecting a shift from oligotrophic to more mesotrophic 
conditions (Penn and Camberato 2019; Gauslaa et al. 2020). 
Although both higher bark pH and P addition were associ -
ated with enhanced STM (Table 3), their low variance infla-
tion factors (1.04 and 1.13) indicate that these factors were 
largely independent. This lack of confounding may reflect 
the fact that oligotrophic conditions—characterized by low 
bark and soil pH, as well as less favorable throughfall chem-
istry (Gauslaa et al. 2021)—cannot sustain dense forests, 
resulting in greater stand openness and higher evaporating 
demand. Thus, the positive effect of pH on STM (Table 3) 
is not necessarily atrbutable to increased P availability, but 
rather to broader habitat and chemical conditions associated 
wth site fertility and structure.
Lichen are known to rapidly take up P from solutions 
(Smith 1960; Hyvärinen and Crittenden 1998), and the 
initial P treatment likely resulted in substantial uptake, 
potentially lowering the lichen N/P ratio. To compensate 
for this imbalance (Crittenden et al. 2023; Crittenden and 
Thornton 2024), L. pulmonaria may have increased invest-
ment in cephalodia or enlarged existing ones, possibly at the 
expense of green algal thallus tissues. Although increased 
cephalodia in P-treated transplants could have contributed 
to greater STM, cephalodia occupy only a small portion 
of total thallus volume (Pentecost 2021; Gerasimova et 
al. 2021), despite their large size (Millbank and Kershaw 
1970). Previous studies have reported mixed effects of P-fer-
tilization. For example P addition did not influence STM 
or cephalodia mass in Peltigera aphthosa (Palmqvist et al. 
2017), likely because this ground-dwelling lichen genus 
shares hydration with thick bryophyte carpets and therefore 
does not hydrologically benefit from increased thallus thick-
ness in the same way as epiphytic lichens. Another possible 
response to an N/P imbalance is a shift in the photobiont/
mycobiont ratio. Notably, P-treatment in Cladonia stellaris 
increased fungal hyphal growth relative to algal cell num -
bers (Makkonen et al. 2007), and a similar effect may have 
occurred in L. pulmonaria transplants examined here.
At a broader scale, P-fertilization has been associated 
with increased abundance of cyanolichens in a Hawai -
ian montane forest (Benner and Vitousek 2007) and with 
higher N fixation in Pseudocyphellaria crocata (Benner et 
al. 2007). In one study, an initial P dose doubled biomass 
growth in L. pulmonaria transplants (McCune and Caldwell 
2009), whereas in another it had no effect (Marks et al. 
2015), highlighting the importance of background P avail -
ability and local environmental conditions. The bedrock 
in the present study area consists of young, P-rich basalts 
(Hickson 1986; Porder and Ramachandran 2012), which 
may explain why added P did not stimulate further growth 
in area or biomass.
Overall, these findings indicate that P enrichment can 
enhance thallus thickening and water storage capacity in 
L. pulmonaria, especially in shaded habitats, but does not 
necessarily promote area or biomass expansion. P-induced 
changes in STM may introduce additional noise into models 
relating STM to hydration sources.

Conclusion
Rather than following a single growth strategy, L. pulmo-
naria appears to balance area expansion, thallus thicken -
ing, and reproduction in ways that depend strongly on 
local environmental conditions. This helps explain both 
the marked variability in growth among transplants and the 
species’ close association with special forest habitats. The 
results suggest that successful persistence of L. pulmonaria 
depends not only on suitable substrate and light conditions, 
but also on hydration regimes and nutrient context at fine 
spatial scales. From a conservation perspective, this under -
lines the importance of maintaining heterogenous, structur-
ally intact forest habitats that provide the microclimate and 
chemical conditions required for sustained growth.
