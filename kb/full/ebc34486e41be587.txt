Patient‑reported foot dysfunction and quality of life in psoriasis: 
the independent association of psoriatic arthritis beyond skin disease 
severity

Abstract
Objective This study aimed to compare patient-reported foot-related pain, disability, and activity limitation, assessed using 
the Foot Function Index (FFI), and health-related quality of life, assessed using the Short Form-36 (SF-36), between par -
ticipants with psoriasis and healthy controls, and to examine the independent associations of psoriatic arthritis (PsA), skin 
disease severity, cutaneous foot involvement, nail involvement, and body mass index (BMI) with these outcomes.
Materials and methods This cross-sectional study included 114 participants with psoriasis and 142 healthy controls. 
Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and BMI-stratified sensitivity analyses were 
conducted. Factors independently associated with total FFI were examined using multiple linear regression with variance 
inflation factor (VIF) assessment and 95% confidence intervals. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Ben-
jamini–Hochberg method.
Results Participants with psoriasis had significantly higher FFI scores and lower SF-36 scores across all subscales than 
healthy controls (all FDR-adjusted p < 0.001; Cohen's d: 2.57–3.46). For total FFI, between-group differences persisted 
across BMI strata (d = 3.30–3.93; all p < 0.001). In multiple regression analysis, PsA showed the strongest independent 
association with total FFI score (β = 0.385, p < 0.001). BMI (β = 0.270, p = 0.003) and PASI (β = 0.217, p = 0.044) were also 
independently associated with total FFI (R2 = 0.302), whereas cutaneous foot involvement was not (p = 0.982).
Conclusion People with psoriasis reported substantially greater foot-related pain, disability, and activity limitation and poorer 
health-related quality of life than healthy controls. PsA status showed the strongest independent association with worse FFI 
scores, while BMI and PASI were also associated. Because FFI is a patient-reported outcome, these findings reflect perceived 
foot-related burden rather than objective evidence of specific joint, entheseal, structural, or gait abnormalities.
Key Points
• Participants with psoriasis reported substantially greater foot-related pain, disability, and activity limitation and poorer health-related qual-
ity of life than healthy controls.
• Psoriatic arthritis showed the strongest independent association with worse total FFI scores after adjustment for skin disease severity and 
other covariates.
• Higher BMI and PASI were independently associated with worse patient-reported foot-related function.
• FFI may provide complementary information on foot-related patient burden, but it should not be interpreted as an objective measure of 
specific musculoskeletal pathology.

Introduction
Psoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory dis-
ease characterized by epidermal hyperproliferation, abnor-
mal keratinocyte differentiation, and persistent immune 
activation, with prevalence estimates varying substantially 
according to age and geographic region [1 , 2]. Its burden 
extends beyond the skin and includes musculoskeletal, car-
diovascular, and metabolic manifestations associated with 
systemic inflammation [3]. The IL-23/IL-17 axis is central 
to psoriatic skin inflammation [4].
Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a heterogeneous musculoskel-
etal disease occurring in a substantial proportion of peo-
ple with psoriasis. Contemporary GRAPPA recommenda-
tions emphasize a domain-based framework that includes 
peripheral arthritis, axial disease, enthesitis, dactylitis, and 
skin and nail psoriasis [5]. The foot can be affected through 
several of these domains. Qualitative studies describe foot-
wear problems, limitations in walking and daily activities, 
and broader social and occupational consequences of PsA-
related foot problems [6, 7]. Enthesitis at the Achilles ten-
don and plantar fascia may contribute to pain and functional 
difficulty and these sites are among the most commonly 
affected entheses in PsA [8].
Objective biomechanical studies further indicate that foot 
involvement in PsA may be accompanied by altered gait and 
loading patterns. Three-dimensional gait and observational 
studies have reported abnormalities in Achilles tendon bio-
mechanics, spatiotemporal parameters, and gait variability 
in people with PsA [9, 10]. These findings are clinically rele-
vant but are distinct from patient-reported functional burden; 
questionnaires cannot identify which specific joint, enthe-
seal, structural, or biomechanical abnormality is responsible 
for an individual's symptoms.
Dermatologic involvement of the feet may add a separate 
regional burden. Palmoplantar psoriasis has been associated 
with greater mobility limitations, discomfort, and health-
related quality-of-life impairment despite sometimes lim-
ited overall skin involvement [11]. Similarly, regional foot 
involvement can contribute disproportionately to discord-
ance between global skin severity measures and patient-
reported quality of life [12]. Thus, both musculoskeletal 
disease and cutaneous disease at a weight-bearing site may 
be relevant when evaluating foot-related burden in psoriasis.
The Foot Function Index (FFI) is a patient-reported 
outcome measure developed to assess foot pain, disability, 
and activity limitation [13]. A Turkish version has demon-
strated reliability and validity in people with foot disorders 
[14]. The FFI was selected for the present study because its 
three domains correspond to the patient-experienced con -
sequences of foot symptoms that were of interest; it was not 
intended to substitute for a rheumatologic foot examination, 
enthesitis or dactylitis scoring, structural assessment, or 
instrumented gait analysis. Prior work linking PsA-related 
foot experiences to the International Classification of Func-
tioning, Disability and Health also supports the multidimen-
sional nature of foot-related disability [7].
Health-related quality of life was evaluated using the 
SF-36, a generic patient-reported health-status instrument 
encompassing physical, pain, social, emotional, vitality, men-
tal-health, and general-health domains [15]. A Turkish ver -
sion of the SF-36 has previously been evaluated for reliability 
and validity [16]. Using FFI together with SF-36 therefore 
allowed the study to examine a foot-specific patient-reported 
burden alongside broader health-related quality of life.
The explanatory variables were chosen to represent clini-
cally distinct components potentially associated with foot-
related burden: PASI for overall cutaneous severity, PsA 
status for the presence of musculoskeletal disease, cutane-
ous foot involvement for regional skin disease, nail involve-
ment for the nail domain, and BMI for potential mechani-
cal and metabolic burden. Age and sex were included as 
demographic covariates. This framework was intended to 
test defined clinical associations rather than to anatomically 
phenotype individual foot pathologies.
Accordingly, the study addressed three specific ques-
tions: (1) whether participants with psoriasis reported 
worse FFI and SF-36 scores than healthy controls; (2) 
whether PsA was associated with total FFI after adjustment 
for PASI, cutaneous foot involvement, nail involvement, 
BMI, age, and sex; and (3) whether greater patient-reported 
foot impairment was associated with poorer health-related 
quality of life. We hypothesized that FFI scores would be 
worse in the psoriasis group and that PsA would remain 
independently associated with total FFI beyond skin dis-
ease severity.
Materials and methods
This cross-sectional, observational study included 114 par-
ticipants with psoriasis aged ≥ 18 years and 142 healthy 
controls. Participants with psoriasis were consecutively 
recruited at Dicle University and Istinye University. Healthy 
controls were recruited from healthcare personnel and 
screened using the predefined eligibility criteria below. 
Individuals in the control group with known dermatologic, 
rheumatologic, or orthopedic disease, peripheral neuropathy, 
diabetes mellitus, plantar fasciitis, hallux valgus, or any evi-
dent foot deformity were excluded.
The study protocol approved by the ethics committee 
specified a minimum total sample size of 120 participants 
across the two groups. Recruitment continued beyond this 
minimum, resulting in a final sample of 114 participants

with psoriasis and 142 healthy controls. A sensitivity 
power analysis using a two-sample standardized mean-
difference framework, based on the achieved sample, 
indicated approximately 80% power, at a two-sided α of 
0.05, to detect a standardized between-group difference of 
d = 0.35. For the multivariable analysis, 114 participants 
with psoriasis were available for a seven-predictor linear 
regression model, corresponding to approximately 16 
observations per predictor.
PASI was calculated by an experienced dermatologist; the 
lower-extremity component of PASI includes the foot region. 
Cutaneous foot involvement was defined as the presence of 
active psoriatic plaques on the plantar and/or dorsal foot on 
dermatologic examination. Nail involvement was recorded 
as psoriatic nail changes, including pitting, onycholysis, sub-
ungual hyperkeratosis, and dystrophy. PsA was diagnosed 
by a rheumatologist, and participants with PsA fulfilled the 
CASPAR classification criteria. Treatments were classified 
as biologic therapy (guselkumab, secukinumab, bimeki-
zumab, ixekizumab, and risankizumab; n = 72) or conven-
tional therapy (methotrexate; n = 42).
Patient-reported foot-related impairment was assessed 
using the Turkish-validated Foot Function Index (FFI) 
(12,13). The FFI contains three domains-pain, disability, and 
activity limitation-and is scored from 0 to 100, with higher 
scores indicating worse foot-related status. Health-related 
quality of life was evaluated using the Turkish version of 
the Short Form-36 (SF-36) [15, 16]. The SF-36 comprises 
eight domains: physical functioning, role limitations due to 
physical health, role limitations due to emotional problems, 
vitality, mental health, social functioning, bodily pain, and 
general health. Each domain is scored from 0 to 100, with 
higher scores indicating better health status. The FFI was 
interpreted as a patient-reported outcome and not as an 
objective clinical measure of joint, entheseal, structural, or 
gait pathology.
Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 
version 21.0. The distribution of continuous variables 
was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For non-nor-
mally distributed variables, between-group comparisons 
were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, while 
categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square 
test. Associations among FFI, PASI, and SF-36 scores 
were evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis. To 
control the type I error rate in multiple comparisons, 
the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) cor-
rection was applied. For comparisons among the con-
trol, PsA-negative psoriasis, and PsA-positive psoriasis 
groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used, followed by 
Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests 
when appropriate. For descriptive effect-size reporting, 
standardized between-group differences were expressed 
as Cohen's d.
To identify factors independently associated with total 
FFI score within the psoriasis group, multiple linear regres-
sion analysis was performed using the forced-entry method. 
PASI, PsA status, cutaneous foot involvement, nail involve-
ment, BMI, age, and sex were entered simultaneously 
because they represented the clinical and demographic 
domains defined above. Multicollinearity was assessed 
using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Secondary multi-
variable models examined SF-36 bodily pain and physical 
functioning using the same clinical framework. For BMI-
stratified sensitivity analyses, participants were categorized 
as BMI < 25.0 kg/m2, 25.0–29.9 kg/m2, and ≥ 30.0 kg/m2. 
A two-sided p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically 
significant.

Discussion
In this cross-sectional study, participants with psoriasis 
reported substantially greater foot-related pain, disability, 
and activity limitation and poorer health-related quality of 
life than healthy controls. These differences remained large 
within BMI strata. The findings therefore identify a con-
siderable patient-reported foot burden in this cohort; how -
ever, the FFI does not establish the presence or severity of 
specific articular, entheseal, structural, or biomechanical 
abnormalities.
PsA was the variable most strongly associated with total 
FFI in the multivariable model. This finding is clinically 
plausible within the GRAPPA domain-based framework, 
because PsA can involve peripheral joints, entheses, and dig-
its, all of which may affect the foot [5]. Qualitative and clini-
cal literature also indicates that PsA-related foot problems 
can restrict walking, footwear use, work, and daily activi-
ties [6, 17]. Nevertheless, our analysis used PsA status as a 
diagnostic-level variable and did not quantify active joint or 
entheseal disease; therefore, the association should not be 
interpreted as evidence that any specific PsA foot pathology 
caused the observed FFI scores.
The descriptive gradient in total FFI across controls, 
PsA-negative psoriasis, and PsA-positive psoriasis is 
consistent with reports that foot pain and disability are 
prominent in PsA. Gait studies have documented altered 
spatiotemporal parameters and variability in people with 
PsA [10], and a recent clinical study found disabling foot 
pain to be associated with difficulty in daily activities, with 
rearfoot enthesitis frequently identified among sympto-
matic participants [18]. Our data complement, rather than 
replicate, these studies because we measured patient-
reported foot-related impairment without objective gait, 
joint, or enthesitis assessment.
BMI was independently associated with total FFI and 
SF-36 physical functioning. Obesity is well established as 
a comorbidity of psoriasis [19], and greater body mass may 
plausibly increase mechanical demand on a weight-bearing 
region. However, the present cross-sectional analysis cannot 
distinguish mechanical loading from inflammatory, behavio-
ral, or other pathways. The persistence of psoriasis-control 
differences within BMI strata indicates that BMI differences 
alone did not account for the observed group separation.
An important clarification concerns the variable termed 
'foot involvement' in the original manuscript. In this study, 
this variable represented cutaneous psoriatic plaques on the 
plantar and/or dorsal foot, not arthritis, enthesitis, dactylitis, 
deformity, or other musculoskeletal foot pathology. Its lack 
of an independent association with total FFI after adjustment 
therefore should not be interpreted as evidence that clini-
cally assessed musculoskeletal foot involvement is unimpor-
tant. Previous work has shown that regional psoriatic foot

involvement may contribute disproportionately to discord-

The choice of FFI was intended to capture patient-
reported pain, disability, and activity limitation. This is 
aligned with qualitative and ICF-linking work showing that 
PsA-related foot problems affect multiple dimensions of 
functioning and participation [6, 7]. At the same time, FFI 
is not PsA-specific and does not localize pathology. It should 
therefore be viewed as a complementary patient-reported 
measure rather than a substitute for disease-specific rheu-
matologic examination or biomechanical testing.
PASI showed only modest relationships with FFI and 
selected SF-36 domains, supporting a distinction between 
overall skin severity and patient-experienced functional 
burden. Palmoplantar psoriasis has been associated with 
impairments in mobility and health-related quality of life 
[11]. However, a systematic review found no consistent evi-
dence that the magnitude of quality-of-life impairment dif-
fers according to the anatomical region affected [20]. The 
present data do not establish that foot skin disease itself 
caused the observed impairment; rather, they indicate that 
global cutaneous severity alone did not account for all vari-
ation in patient-reported outcomes.
The exploratory treatment comparison should also be 
interpreted cautiously. Although the biologic group had 
lower cross-sectional PASI values, total FFI did not differ 
significantly between treatment groups. Because treatment 
was not randomized, the groups differed in PsA and cutane-
ous foot involvement, and baseline FFI, disease duration, 
and treatment duration were unavailable, no inference can 
be made regarding treatment effects on foot function or the 
mechanisms underlying persistent symptoms. Longitudinal 
studies with baseline and follow-up foot-specific measures 
are needed. Marked health-related quality-of-life impairment 
in PsA has also been documented using generic health-status 
and disability measures [21].
The strengths of this study include a healthy control 
group, use of a foot-specific patient-reported outcome, 
BMI-stratified sensitivity analyses, and multivariable 
assessment of clinically relevant covariates. Several limi-
tations are important. First, the cross-sectional design pre-
cludes causal inference. Second, tender and swollen joint 
counts, dactylitis measures, validated enthesitis scores, 
structural foot deformity, imaging, plantar-pressure assess-
ment, and instrumented gait analysis were not systemati-
cally collected. Consequently, the FFI findings should be 
interpreted as patient-reported foot-related pain and dis-
ability rather than objective evidence of specific muscu-
loskeletal pathology. Third, disease duration, physical 
activity, footwear characteristics, psychosocial variables, 
and baseline treatment-related measures were unavailable. 
Fourth, exclusion of controls with several common foot and 
systemic conditions may have increased contrast between 
groups and could have contributed to the very large effect 
sizes. The groups also differed in sex distribution, and 
residual confounding related to sex cannot be excluded. 
Finally, the FFI is not a PsA-specific instrument. These 
limitations should be addressed in future longitudinal stud-
ies combining patient-reported outcomes with standardized 
rheumatologic and biomechanical foot assessment.
Conclusion
In this cross-sectional study, participants with psoriasis 
reported substantially greater foot-related pain, disability, and 
activity limitation and poorer health-related quality of life than 
healthy controls. BMI-stratified analyses showed that these 
between-group differences persisted across BMI categories.
Within the psoriasis group, PsA showed the strongest 
independent association with total FFI, while BMI and 
PASI were also independently associated. Cutaneous 
foot involvement, defined specifically as active psoriatic 
plaques on the foot, was not independently associated with 
total FFI after adjustment. These results concern patient-
reported foot-related burden and should not be interpreted 
as objective evidence of specific articular, entheseal, struc-
tural, or gait abnormalities.
The findings support further evaluation of foot-specific 
patient-reported outcomes as a complement to standard 
dermatologic and rheumatologic assessment, particularly 
when foot-related symptoms or PsA are present. Prospec-
tive studies that incorporate joint counts, dactylitis and 
enthesitis measures, structural examination, and gait or 
other biomechanical assessments are required before 
stronger clinical recommendations can be made.
The observed associations are correlational, and their 
direction and clinical significance should be confirmed in 
longitudinal and interventional studies.
