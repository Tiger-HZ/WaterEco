Primary and secondary nutrient status of salt affected 
soils of Hospet Taluk in TBP irrigation command 
area, Karnataka 
 
Shridhar and Vishwanath J 
 
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/26174693.2026.v10.i9Si.9904 
 
Abstract 
A study was conducted to assess the status of primary nutrients (available N, P₂O₅, and K₂O) and 
secondary nutrients (exchangeable Ca, exchangeable Mg, and available S) and their relationship with 
soil properties in Hospet Taluk of Bellary district, Karnataka. Soil samples were collected during 
summer 2017 using GPS and topographic sheets at a scale of 1:50,000. A total of 82 soil samples were 
collected from 41 sampling points representing 15 grids on a 5′ × 5′ grid basis at two soil depths, 
namely 0-15 and 15-30 cm, and analyzed for primary and secondary nutrient status. 
Among the primary nutrients, the majority of soil samples had low available nitrogen (95.12-97.60%) 
(<280 kg ha⁻¹), high available phosphorus (90.20-100%) (>56.3 kg ha⁻¹), and high available potassium 
(48.90-68.30%) (>336 kg ha⁻¹) at the surface and subsurface depths, respectively. Among the 
secondary nutrients, the majority of samples had low exchangeable magnesium (63.41-78.05%) (<10.3 
meq 100 g⁻¹) at both depths. For exchangeable calcium, the highest proportion of surface soil sa mples 
(51.20%) was in the medium category (12.5- 25 meq 100 g⁻¹), whereas the highest proportion of 
subsurface samples (48.80%) was in the low category (<12.5 meq 100 g⁻¹). Regarding available 
sulphur, the highest proportion of surface soil samples (65.85%) was in the high category (>50 ppm), 
while the majority of subsurface samples (70.73%) were in the medium category (10-50 ppm). 
 
Keywords: Primary nutrients, secondary nutrients, nutrient distribution, distribution mapping , salt-
affected soils 
 
1. Introduction 
Soil is one of the most valuable natural resources upon which agricultural productivity 
largely depends. Salt -affected soils constitute an important category of degraded soils in 
which productivity is reduced because of excessive soluble salts, sodici ty, or a combination 
of both. Salinity is generally associated with an electrical conductivity of the soil saturation 
extract (ECe) greater than 4 dS m⁻¹, whereas sodicity is commonly characterized by an 
exchangeable sodium percentage (ESP) greater than 15 . Although all soils contain varying 
amounts of soluble salts, excessive accumulation of salts in the active root zone can 
adversely affect plant growth, crop yield, and produce quality. Salt- affected soils are 
generally distinguished from normal soils by higher concentrations of soluble electrolytes 
and/or exchangeable sodium (Szaboles, 1989) [33]. 
The accumulation of salts in soil can induce several degradation processes, including soil 
dispersion, surface crust formation, deterioration of soil structure, reduced aggregate 
stability, and compaction. High concentrations of soluble salts may also interfere with 
nutrient availability and uptake, resulting in nutrient imbalances and, in severe cases, the 
accumulation of potentially toxic ions in plants (Priyad arshini, 2011) [17]. Based on the 
dominant constraint, salt -affected soils are broadly classified as saline, sodic, and saline-
sodic soils. In India, these soils are generally categorized as saline and sodic (alkali) soils. 
According to the reconciled esti mates of CSSRI, Karnal, NRSA, and NBSS&LUP (2006) 
[13], approximately 6.73 million hectares of land were affected by salinity and sodicity, 
comprising about 3.77 million hectares of sodic soils and 2.96 million hectares of saline 
soils. Earlier projections estimated that approximately 11.7 million hectares could be affected 
by salinity and alkalinity by 2025.

However, estimates of salt -affected areas may vary 
considerably because of differences in assessment methods, 
including the use of satellite imagery , which can be 
influenced by soil moisture, parent material, topography, 
and other surface characteristics (Mandal and Sharma, 2001) 
[11]. 
Salt-affected soils exhibit considerable variation in their 
physical, chemical, and biological properties, which can 
influence soil fertility and crop productivity. Therefore, 
characterization of their nutrient status is essential for 
developing appropriate soil and crop management strategies. 
In particular, the availability of primary nutrients, namely 
nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K), and 
secondary nutrients, namely calcium (Ca), magnesium 
(Mg), and sulphur (S), is important for evaluating the 
fertility status and productive potential of salt -affected soils. 
Excessive concentrations of soluble salts and s odium can 
influence nutrient transformations, availability, and plant 
uptake, while deficiencies or imbalances of essential 
nutrients may further restrict crop growth. Assessment of 
the distribution of these nutrients at different soil depths can 
therefore provide valuable information for nutrient 
management in salt-affected agricultural soils. 
The Tungabhadra Project (TBP) command area is an 
important irrigated agricultural region where soil salinity 
and waterlogging have emerged as major constraints, 
particularly because of inappropriate land and water 
management practices and deviations from recommended 
cropping patterns over several decades. Approximately 
96,215 ha, accounting for about 26.5% of the total command 
area, has been reported to be salt affect ed (Anonymous, 
2012) [1], with an additional 1,000 ha reportedly becoming 
salt affected annually (IDNP, 2002) [7]. The continued 
expansion of salt -affected areas highlights the need for 
systematic assessment and monitoring of soil fertility and 
nutrient availability to support appropriate management and 
reclamation practices. 
In this context, the present study was undertaken to assess 
the status and distribution of primary and secondary 
nutrients in the salt -affected soils of Hospet Taluk in the 
TBP irrigation command area. The study evaluated available 
nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, along with 
exchangeable calcium, exchangeable magnesium, and 
available sulphur at surface and subsurface soil depths. The 
findings provide information on the spatial and depth -wise 
distribution of essential nutrients and can serve as a basis for 
developing appropriate nutrient management strategies for 
improving soil fertility and sustaining agricultural 
productivity in the salt -affected soils of the TBP command 
area. 
In salt-affected soils, the availability and uptake of essential 
plant nutrients are strongly influenced by the concentration 
and composition of soluble salts. Excessive salinity can 
modify soil chemical conditions, alter nutrient solubility and 
mobility, and i nterfere with nutrient uptake by plants, 
thereby resulting in nutrient deficiencies or imbalances. 
High concentrations of sodium, for insta nce, can interfere 
with potassium uptake because of competition between Na⁺ 
and K⁺ at the root surface, potentially leading to potassium 
deficiency (Shahin et al ., 2009) [26]. Excessive sodium may 
also contribute to the deterioration of soil structure through 
clay dispersion, reducing aggregate stability, water 
infiltration, and soil permeability and thereby aggravating 
salinity-related constraints (Munns and Tester, 2008) [12]. 
Nitrogen availability in saline soils may be affected by 
changes in microbial activity and the processes involved in 
nitrogen cycling (Yusuf et al ., 2008) [41]. High salt 
concentrations can also suppress biological nitrogen fixation 
by rhizobia, particularly in soils supporting leguminous 
crops. Phosphorus availability may likewise be altered under 
saline and sodic conditions because its solubility and 
retention are influenced by soil pH and interactions with 
calcium, magnesium, iron, and aluminium. Under certain 
soil conditions, these interactions can reduce the 
concentration of readily available phosphorus in the soil 
solution (Zhao et al ., 2009) [43]. Potassium, which plays an 
essential role in osmotic regulation, enzyme activation, and 
plant tolerance to abiotic stress, is particularly vulnerable to 
ionic competition from Na⁺, which can restrict K⁺ uptake by 
plant roots (Shahin et al., 2009) [26]. 
The availability of the secondary nutrients calcium and 
magnesium can also be influenced by salinity and sodicity. 
Excessive Na⁺ in the soil exchange complex can alter the 
relative proportions of exchangeable Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ and may 
contribute to their displac ement under sodic conditions. 
Calcium is particularly important for maintaining soil 
aggregation and structural stability, while both calcium and 
magnesium are essential for plant growth and physiological 
processes. Consequently, an imbalance in these nutr ients 
may further limit crop productivity in salt-affected soils. 
Effective management of nutrient status in salt -affected 
agricultural soils requires integrated approaches that 
consider both nutrient availability and the underlying 
salinity or sodicity co nstraints. Regular soil testing, 
balanced fertilization, appropriate irrigation and drainage 
management, and the use of suitable organic amendments 
can help improve nutrient availability and soil condition. 
Where appropriate, the use of salt-tolerant crop varieties can 
further enhance crop establishment and productivity under 
saline conditions. Thus, assessment of the status and 
distribution of primary and secondary nutrients is essential 
for developing site -specific nutrient management strategies 
and improving the productive potential of salt -affected soils 
in irrigation command areas. 
 
2. Material and methods 
2.1 Study Area 
The Tungabhadra irrigation command area in Karnataka 
provides irrigation facilities for agricultural crops in Bellary, 
Koppal, and par ts of Raichur districts. The present study 
was conducted in Hospet Taluk of Bellary district, which is 
situated in the eastern part of Karnataka and extends broadly 
from southwest to northeast. Bellary district lies between 
15°30′ and 15°50′ N latitude and 75°40′ and 77°11′ E 
longitude, covering a geographical area of approximately 
8,447 km². 
Hospet Taluk is located at approximately 15°16′00″ N 
latitude and 76°24′00″ E longitude, with an average 
elevation of 479 m (1,572 ft) and a geographical area of 934 
km² (Fig. 1). The major soil types occurring in the study 
area include medium -deep red gravelly clay soils, deep 
black clayey soils, deep black calcareous clayey soils, and 
deep alluvial clayey soils. The average annual rainfall of 
Hospet Taluk is approxima tely 658.3 mm. The major crops 
cultivated in the taluk include paddy, sorghum (jowar), 
maize, cotton, and sugarcane.

Collection of Soil Samples and Mapping of Primary and 
Secondary Nutrients 
Soil samples were collected during April -May 2017 using a 
Global Positioning System (GPS) and topographic sheets at 
a scale of 1:50,000. Sampling was conducted on a 5′ × 5′ 
grid basis, covering 15 grids in Hospet Taluk. The sampling 
sites were selected from agricultural areas within the TBP 
command area receiving irrigation water from the Left Bank 
Canal. 
Soil samples were collected using an auger from four depth 
intervals, namely 0-15, 15-30, 30-60, and >60 cm (60+ cm). 
The collected samples were air -dried under shade, gently 
powdered using a pestle and mortar, passed t hrough a 2 mm 
sieve, and stored in clean polythene bags until analysis. The 
samples were analyzed for primary nutrients (available 
nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium) and secondary 
nutrients (exchangeable calcium, exchangeable magnesium, 
and available sulphur) using standard analytical procedures. 
The spatial distribution of primary and secondary nutrients 
was assessed using the nutrient status data, and distribution 
maps were prepared to delineate the variability of nutrient 
availability within the study area. An overview of the salt -
affected soils and soil -sampling locations in the TBP 
command area is presented in Plates 1a and 1b. 
 
3. Results and discussion 
Characterization of soil samples of the study area 
The soil pH, EC (dS m⁻¹), organic carbon content, and per 
cent distribution of soil samples are presented in Tables 1 
and 2. 
 
Soil pH(1:2.5) 
The data on soil pH (1:2.5) at 0-15, 15-30, 30-60 and 60+ cm 
varied from 5.72 to 8.74, 6.20 to 8.60, 6.72 to 8.69 and 7.06 
to 9.15 with a mean value of 7.51, 7.60, 7.89 and 8.07 
respectively. The mean soil pH increased slightly with soil 
depth. Generally, the mean soil pH (Table 1) at all the 
depths of Hospet taluk was in the category of mildly 
alkaline (7.5 to 8.0) (Br ady and Well, 2007). However, 
Slight alkalinity of these soils could be attributed to 
semiarid climate, presence of basic parent material ( viz., 
Gneissic complex), restricted drainage, high calcium 
carbonate and exchangeable bases (Ravi Kumar et al., 2007) 
[25]. As far as per cent distribution of pH is considered, in 
general about 56.10 (15 -30 cm) to 70.83 (30 -60 cm) in 
Hospet taluk were moderately to mildly alkaline in reaction 
(Table 2). Priyadarshini (2011) [17] reported that about 45, 11 
and 12 per cent of surface soil samples were mildly alkaline, 
moderately alkaline and strongly alkaline (> 8.5) 
respectively in soil reaction of salt affected soils of Nellore 
and Gudur divisions of Nellore district, Andhra Pradesh). 
Release of several organic acids duri ng decomposition of 
organic matter could lower the soil pH values in surface soil 
samples compared to sub -soils (Bhadrapur and Seshagiri 
Rao, 1977) [5]. Further, the increase in pH with depth could 
be attributed to leaching and accumulation of exchangeable 
sodium and calcium carbonate salts at lower depths with the 
formation of sodium carbonate under low CO 2 conditions 
leading to high soil pH (Shivakumar et al . 2017 and Sharma 
et al . 2008) [31, 27] . Similar results were observed by 
Yeresheemi et al. (1997) [39] in salt affected soils of Upper 
Krishna project of Karnataka, Balpande et al . (1996) [2] in 
degraded Vertisols of Purna valley in Maharashtra and 
Mahendra (2007) [10] in soils at Main Agriculture Research 
Station, Dharwad, Karnataka. 
 
Soil EC(1:2.5) 
Soil Electrical Conductivity 
The soil electrical conductivity (EC, 1:2.5) at 0 -15, 15 -30, 
30-60, and >60 cm depths ranged from 0.15 to 3.40, 0.20 to 
12.40, 0.20 to 8.80, and 0.19 to 6.50 dS m⁻¹, respectively, 
with corresponding mean values of 0.91, 1.10, 1.16, and 
1.11 dS m⁻¹. Unlike soil pH, the mean EC did not show a 
consistent trend with depth, although slightly higher values 
were generally observed in the subsurface l ayers. The mean 
EC values were below 4.0 dS m⁻¹ at all depths, indicating 
that the soils were, on average, within the non -saline range 
at the time of sampling. However, the distribution of 
individual EC values (Table 2) showed that 2.40 to 8.33% of 
the sam ples, depending on soil depth, had EC values 
exceeding 4.0 dS m⁻¹, indicating the presence of saline 
conditions in these samples at the time of sampling. 
 
Soil Organic Carbon 
The mean soil organic carbon (OC) content was 
considerably higher in the surface s oil (1.13%) than in the 
subsurface soil (0.60%) of Hospet Taluk (Table 1). 
According to the soil -test fertility rating proposed by 
Tandon (2017) [35], approximately 92.7% of surface soil 
samples and 29.3% of subsurface soil samples had OC 
contents in the h igh category (>0.75%) (Table 2). The 
higher OC content in surface soil may be attributed to the 
accumulation of root biomass, decomposition of crop 
residues, and the regular application of farmyard manure 
(FYM) and other organic amendments over the years. These 
organic inputs are predominantly incorporated into the 
surface layer, resulting in greater organic carbon 
accumulation compared with deeper soil layers. 
The present findings are consistent with observations 
reported for the soils of Gangavathi Taluk, where medium to 
high OC contents were recorded and attributed to the 
incorporation of paddy stubbles and the addition of organic 
manures (Ravi Kumar et al ., 2007; Wijebandara et al ., 
2011) [25, 37]. The marked decline in OC with depth observed 
in the pres ent study is also consistent with the greater 
biological activity and continuous addition of organic 
residues in the surface soil. 
 
Available Nitrogen, Phosphorus and Potassium 
The contents of available N, P₂O₅, and K₂O, along with their 
percentage distribution in surface and subsurface soil 
samples of Hospet Taluk, are presented in Tables 3 and 4, 
respectively. 
 
Available Nitrogen 
Similar to soil organic carbon, the mean available nitrogen 
(N) content was higher in the surface soil (218.4 kg ha⁻¹) 
than in t he subsurface soil (165.4 kg ha⁻¹) of Hospet Taluk 
(Table 3). However, the mean available N content at both 
depths fell within the low category (<280 kg ha⁻¹) according 
to the soil -test fertility ratings of Tandon (2017) [35]. 
Approximately 95.12% of the s urface soil samples were 
classified as low in available N (Table 4). Despite the 
generally high organic carbon status of these soils, the low 
available N content may be attributed to the susceptibility of 
nitrogen to various loss processes, including leach ing, 
volatilization, and denitrification (Rao and Batra, 1983) [19].

In addition, the relatively lower microbial activity and 
organic carbon content in subsurface layers may contribute 
to reduced nitrogen availability at greater depths. The 
present findings are consistent with those of Patel et al. 
(2017) [15], who reported low available N contents in salt -
affected soils of Dholka Taluka in the Bhal region of 
Gujarat. 
 
Available Phosphorus 
Unlike available N, there was little difference in the mean 
available P₂O₅ content between the surface (266.2 kg ha⁻¹) 
and subsurface (270.5 kg ha⁻¹) soils of Hospet Taluk (Table 
3). Although organic matter and crop residues in the surface 
layer may promote the mineralization and solubilization of 
relatively unavailable pho sphorus compounds, thereby 
increasing phosphorus availability (Chaudhary and Ghosh, 
2006) [6], the observed difference between the two depths 
was small. 
The distribution of samples showed that 100% of the surface 
and 90.2% of the subsurface samples had available P₂O₅ 
contents in the high category (>56.3 kg ha⁻¹) (Table 4), 
according to the soil -test fertility ratings of Tandon (2017) 
[35]. This indicates that the soils were generally well supplied 
with available phosphorus. The long- term application of di-
ammonium phosphate (DAP) and poultry manure under the 
prevailing paddy -paddy cropping system may have 
contributed to the accumulation and maintenance of 
relatively high available phosphorus levels in these soils. 
 
Available Potassium 
Similar to available N, the mean available K₂O content was 
higher in the surface soil (454.1 kg ha⁻¹) than in the 
subsurface soil (353.0 kg ha⁻¹) of Hospet Taluk (Table 3). 
According to the soil -test fertility ratings of Tandon (2017) 
[35], the highest proportion of samples in both surface 
(68.3%) and subsurface ( 48.9%) soils had available K₂O 
contents in the high category (>336 kg ha⁻¹) (Table 4). 
The relatively high available K₂O status of these soils may 
be associated with the occurrence of K-bearing micaceous 
minerals, which a re common in soils of arid and semi -arid 
regions (Kapoor et al., 1981; Ravi Kumar et al., 2007) [9, 25]. 
The release of potassium through the weathering or 
dissolution of K- bearing minerals such as muscovite under 
sodic conditions may also contribute to th e available K pool 
(Pal, 1985) [14]. The higher K₂O content in surface soil may 
additionally be associated with the regular return of crop 
residues and organic inputs to the surface layer. 
In general, the lower contents of available N and K₂O in 
subsurface soils compared with surface soils may be 
associated with the lower organic carbon content, reduced 
biological activity, and limited accumulation of organic 
residues at greater depths. However, the small difference in 
mean available P₂O₅ between the two depths indicates that 
phosphorus dist ribution was comparatively less variable 
within the soil profile. 
The contents of exchangeable calcium (Ca), exchangeable 
magnesium (Mg), and available sulphur (S) and their
percentage distribution in surface and subsurface soil 
samples of Hospet Taluk are presented in Tables 3 and 4 , 
respectively. 
 
Exchangeable Calcium 
Similar to available N, the mean exchangeable calcium (Ca) 
content was higher in the surface soil (19.02 meq 100 g⁻¹) 
than in the subsurface soil (16.10 meq 100 g⁻¹) of Hospet 
Taluk (Table 3). The distribution of soil samples showed 
that the maximum proportion of surface ( 51.20%) and 
subsurface (48.80%) samples fell within the medium (12.5 -
25 meq 100 g ⁻¹) and low (<12.5 meq 100 g⁻¹) categories, 
respectively (Table 4), according to the soil -test fertility 
ratings of Tandon (2017) [35]. 
Calcium generally dominates the exchange complex in soils 
of semi -arid regions (Sharma et al ., 1996) [30]. Similar 
observations were reported by Surekha et al. (1997) [32] in 
the semi -arid region of Andhra Pradesh, Sharma et al . 
(2004) [28] in salt -affected soils of southern Rajasthan, and 
Ram et al . (2010) [18] in flood -prone soils of eastern 
Rajasthan. The relatively higher exchangeable Ca content in 
the surface soil may be associated with the accumulation of 
organic residues and exchangeable bases in the upper soil 
layer. In irrigated soils, the distribution of soluble and 
exchangeable calcium may also be influenced by 
dissolution, movement, and redistribution within the soil 
profile (Pathak and Patel, 1980) [16]. 
 
Exchangeable Magnesium 
The mean exchangeable magnesium (Mg) content was 8.63 
meq 100 g⁻¹ in the surface soil and 9.30 meq 100 g⁻¹ in the 
subsurface soil of Hospet Taluk (Table 3), indicating a slight 
increase with depth. According to the soil -test fertility 
ratings of Tandon (2017) [35], the majority of surface 
(78.05%) and subsurface ( 63.41%) soil samples had 
exchangeable Mg contents below 10.3 meq 100 g⁻¹ and 
were therefore classified in the low category (Table 4). The 
relatively higher mean Mg content in the subsurface soil 
may be associated with the downward movement and 
redistribution of soluble and exchangeable magnesium 
within the soil profile. 
 
Available Sulphur 
The mean available sulphur (S) content was higher in the 
surface soil (198.0 ppm) than in the subsurface soil (145.3 
ppm) of Hospet Taluk (Table 3). Based on the soil -test 
fertility ratings of Tandon (2017) [35], the highest proportion 
of surface ( 65.85%) and subsurface ( 70.73%) soil samples 
fell within the high (>50 ppm) to medium (10- 50 ppm) 
categories (Table 4). The relatively high available S content 
in these soils may be associated with the presence of soluble 
sulphate salts and the addition of sulphur through fertilizers 
and other agricultural inputs. Similar observations were 
reported by Shivakumar et al . (2017) [31] in salt -affected 
soils of the Vani Vilas Command area of Hiriy ur Taluk, 
Chitradurga district.

Conclusion 
The study revealed that the soils of Hospet Taluk were 
predominantly mildly alkaline , with mean soil pH values 
ranging from 7.51 to 8.07 across the investigated depths. 
The mean EC values were below 4.0 dS m⁻¹ at all depths, 
indicating that the soils were generally non- saline at the 
time of sampling. However, a proportion of individual 
samples had EC values exceeding 4.0 dS m⁻¹ , indicating 
localized saline conditions. Specifically, 2.40% of surface 
(0-15 cm) samples and 7.32% of subsurface (15- 30 cm) 
samples had EC values >4.0 dS m⁻¹. The soils generally had 
medium to high organic carbon contents. 
With respect to primary nutrients, the mean available N 
content was in the low category (<280 kg ha⁻¹) , whereas 
available P₂O₅ and K₂O were in the high categories (>56.3 
and >336 kg ha⁻¹, respectively) . Among the secondary 
nutrients, the mean exchangeable Mg content was in the low 
category (<10.3 meq 100 g⁻¹) , while exchangeable Ca was 
in the medium category (12.5-25 meq 100 g⁻¹) and available 
S was in the high category (>50 ppm) . Overall, the results 
indicate that nitrogen and magnesium were the major 
nutrient constraints, whereas phosphorus, potassium, 
calcium, and sulphur were generally present at adequate to 
high levels. These findings highlight the need for balanced
