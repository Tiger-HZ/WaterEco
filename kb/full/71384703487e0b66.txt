Abstract 14 
The ecological risks of microplastics (MPs) are increasingly recognized. However, the 15 
role of the MP aging process on risks in freshwater ecosystems remains unknown. In 16 
this study, 500 nm polystyrene microplastics (PS-MPs) were subjected to artificial UVC 17 
(254 nm) irradiation for six months to induce accelerated photo-oxidation. Adult 18 
zebrafish were exposed to pristine and UVC-aged PS-MPs for 21 days to evaluate 19 
health related indicators. UVC photodegradation significantly altered the 20 
physicochemical properties of PS-MPs, including roughening of surfaces and 21 
generation of new oxygen-containing functional groups. Exposure to aged PS-MPs 22 
produced stronger biological responses than pristine particles, as indicated by 23 
integrated biomarker response (IBR) analysis, with elevated oxidative stress and 24 
changes in humoral immune indicators. Multi-omics analysis revealed that both pristine 25 
and aged PS-MPs induced gut microbiota dysbiosis and hepatic metabolic disorder, 26 
while the aged PS-MP exposure group showed broader metabolic perturbations in 27 
female zebrafish, including alterations in glycolipid and amino-acid metabolism 28 
pathways and enhanced oxidative stress responses. Correlation analysis indicated close 29 
associations among gut microbial shifts and metabolic alterations. Our results highlight 30 
the enhanced toxicity of aged PS-MPs on freshwater organisms and emphasize the 31 
importance of considering the aging process in toxicological assessments of MPs in 32 
freshwater systems. 33

1. Introduction 36 
Microplastic (MP) pollution in freshwater ecosystems is attracting increasing 37 
concern. MPs can be ingested by various freshwater organisms directly, including fish, 38 
cladocerans and mussels, leading to adverse impacts [1, 2]. 39 
MPs can affect freshwater organism health at the individual, cellular, and molecular 40 
levels [3]. For example, the accumulation of MPs has been observed to block the 41 
digestive system, hindering nutrient absorption and damaging organism health [4]. MPs 42 
could induce oxidative stress, inflammation, DNA damage and immune inhibition [5-43 
8]. Furthermore, there is the potential for ingested MPs to be transferred through the 44 
food chain and even to the human body, posing a threat to wider ecosystem stability 45 
and human health [9]. A comprehensive assessment of health effects and risks of MPs 46 
in freshwater ecosystems, accounting for potential changes in the properties and 47 
behavior of MPs during environmental exposure, is therefore necessary. 48 
After being released into freshwater, MPs will undergo many biological and abiotic 49 
aging processes, such as via light, heat and oxidation, that can alter their properties and 50 
behavior [10]. Aging processes have the potential to affect the toxicity of MPs to 51 
freshwater organisms, but little is known about these processes . Previous studies have 52 
demonstrated that aged polyvinyl chloride (PVC) MPs showed greater toxicity on algae 53 
Chlamydomonas reinhardtii due to formation of carbonyl groups and increased zeta 54 
potential [11]. Michailidou et al. [12] found that aged polyethylene MPs (compared to 55 
pristine) caused greater health damage to freshwater fish Perca fluviatilis by inducing 56 
oxidative stress and affecting cellular health. Conversely, some studies have 57 
demonstrated aging of MPs did not increase the toxicity to algae, daphnids and 58 
duckweed [13, 14]. Thus, it remains uncertain about the impacts of aging processes on 59 
the toxicity of MPs, and further study is needed. While solar radiation reaching 60 
freshwater systems is dominated by UV A and UVB wavelengths, accelerated UVC (254 61 
nm) irradiation is frequently used in laboratory studies as a controlled photo-oxidation 62 
model to induce surface functionalization and carbonyl formation. 63 
After exposure to MPs, ingestion is the major uptake pathway, however, MPs can 64 
also adhere to fish skin and transfer to other parts of the body [15, 16]. As an ideal 65 
model for toxicology and risk assessment, existing research has shown that MPs can 66 
cause damage to the health of zebrafish based on these interaction routes, including the 67 
gut-liver axis, metabolism, the nervous system, reproductive development, and humoral 68 
immune system [17-19]. Our previous study also demonstrated a range of sex-specific 69 
toxicity effects of polystyrene MPs (PS-MPs) on zebrafish [20]. Furthermore, there has 70 
been some research on the impacts of aged MPs on zebrafish larvae, indicating 71 
aggravated neurotoxicity, gut damage, lipid malabsorption, immune dysfunction and 72 
growth inhibition [21, 22]. However, there remains a need for research on the impacts 73 
of aged MPs on adult zebrafish health. 74 
Accordingly, this study aims to investigate whether PS-MPs aging process 75 
influences the ir health impacts on adult female and male zebrafish over a 21-day 76 
exposure period. Specifically, we considered: (i) characterization of aged PS-MPs; (ii) 77 
the toxicity and mechanisms of influence for pristine and aged PS-MPs on zebrafish of 78 
both sexes. The toxicity on zebrafish health was evaluated by measuring humoral 79 
indicators and oxidative stress. Multi-omics analysis was used to reveal the microbial 80 
and metabolic responses of zebrafish along gut-liver axis. 81 
2. Materials and methods 82 
2.1 PS-MPs preparation, aging treatment and characterization 83 
Pristine PS-MPs (500 nm) were synthesized in the lab according to methods used 84 
in previous studies [23, 24]. The pristine PS-MPs were placed in a controlled aging 85 
chamber and subjected to continuous and uninterrupted UVC irradiation (254 nm; 4 × 86 
20 W; surface intensity: 75 μW/cm²) for a total duration of 180 days. UVC irradiation 87 
represents a controlled laboratory aging method and was designed to accelerate photo-88 
oxidative functionalization and carbonyl formation. After aging, particles were 89 
characterized by transmission electron microscope, Fourier Transform infrared 90 
spectroscopy (FTIR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The carbonyl index 91 
(CI) was determined to characterize the aging degree [25]. 92

2.2 Zebrafish culture and experimental setup 95 
Zebrafish (5 months old) were supplied by the Institute of Hydrobiology, Chinese 96 
Academy of Sciences (Wuhan, China). The zebrafish were kept in ultraviolet sterilized 97 
and aerated water at 24 ± 1 ◦C with a 14:10 h light/dark cycle and were fed twice daily 98 
brine shrimp. After acclimation, zebrafish were randomly assigned to six experimental 99 
groups: female control group (F-control), female pristine PS-MPs treatment group (FP-100 
PS-MP), female aged PS-MPs treatment group (FA-PS-MP), male control group (M-101 
control), male pristine PS-MPs treatment group (MP- PS-MP), male aged PS-MPs 102 
treatment group (MA- PS-MP). Each group contained four replicate glass tanks (n=4 103 
tanks per group): 25 zebrafish were included in each tank. The exposure concentration 104 
(2.5 mg/L) used in this study is higher than most reported environmental concentrations. 105 
This elevated concentration was selected to serve as a controlled mechanistic stress-test, 106 
enabling the detection of biological responses and identification of sensitive endpoints 107 
under intensified exposure conditions, rather than to directly simulate environmental 108 
scenarios. Particles were kept in suspension through continuous gentle aeration. A 50% 109 
water change with fresh MP-spiked medium was performed every 48 hours to maintain 110 
concentration stability and water quality. After 21 days of exposure, zebrafish were 111 
anesthetized and sacrificed in ice water before obtaining the tissues. 112 
2.3 Determination of Immunoglobulin M (IgM) in mucus, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) 113 
and triglyceride (TG) in blood serum and oxidative stress in liver 114 
Skin mucus was collected according to methods outlined in a previous study [26] 115 
(Text S1). IgM content in mucus, LPS and TG level in zebrafish blood, reactive oxygen 116 
species (ROS) level, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and malondialdehyde (MDA) 117 
content in liver tissue homogenates (10 %) were measured by commercial kits 118 
(Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). 119 
2.4 Integrated biomarker responses (IBR) analysis 120 
 Integrated biomarker response version 2 (IBRv2) analysis was conducted to 121 
summarize measured health-related indicator responses into one general index [27]. 122 
The health-related indicators included ROS, SOD, MDA, LPS, TG, and IgM. The 123 
calculation formulae are listed in Text S2. 124 
2.5 Gut microbiome analysis 125 
The gut samples were collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen and then preserved at -126 
80 ℃. The details of DNA extraction, 16s rRNA gene sequencing, and data analysis are 127 
listed in Text S3. 128 
2.6 Metabolomic analysis in the gut and the liver and correlation analysis 129 
 The gut and liver samples were collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen and then 130 
preserved at -80 ℃ for further analysis. The details of sample preparation and extraction, 131 
UPLC conditions, LC-MS/MS analysis, data analysis and interpretation and 132 
bioinformatics analysis are provided in Text S4. Correlation analysis (Pearson analysis 133 
and Mantel test) was performed to evaluate the relationship between gut microbiota and 134 
gut differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs), gut microbiota and liver DEMs, and 135 
gut DEMs and liver DEMs by the OmicStudio tools at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool 136 
(R package stats and ggcor, p<0.05, rho>0.6 or <-0.6). 137 
2.7 Data analysis 138 
 The data are expressed as mean ± SD. One-way analysis of variance (ANOV A) 139 
with IBM SPSS Statistics 22 was used to evaluate the differences among different 140 
groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. 141 
3. Results and Discussion 142 
3.1 Characterization of pristine and aged PS-MPs 143 
The hydrodynamic diameter and PDI of pristine PS-MPs in exposure water were 144 
584.3 ± 2.0 nm and 0.023 ± 0.021, respectively, indicating relatively good dispersion 145 
in the exposure medium. Smooth surfaces were observed in pristine PS-MPs while aged 146 
PS-MPs had rough surface and bulges (Fig. 1). PS-MPs was observed to change color 147 
from white to yellow after the UV aging process. XPS spectra showed that the O 1s 148 
proportion of aged PS-MPs increased from 8.03% to 19.92%, while the C 1s proportion 149 
of aged PS-MPs decreased from 91.97% to 80.08%. FTIR results (Fig. 1D) showed that 150 
new functional groups were found in the regions of 3100-3700 cm-1, 1630-1840 cm-1, 151 
1340-1200 cm-1, identified as -OH and C=O groups. The CI value of pristine and aged 152 
PS-MPs was 0.43 ± 0.01 and 3.08 ± 0.003, respectively. 153 
Several studies have demonstrated that laboratory accelerated oxidation of MPs 154 
could simulate the long-term aging process of MPs in the natural environment, and UV 155 
photodegradation can accelerate the aging process and alter physicochemical properties 156 
of MPs [28-30]. As with our study, Xiang et al. [31] also observed a wrinkled surface 157 
of aged PS-MPs; Chen et al. [32] found that UV radiation could change the color and 158 
increase O 1s proportion of PS-MPs, indicating that PS-MPs had been oxidized . The 159 
generation of new oxygen-containing functional groups from FTIR analysis further 160 
demonstrated this process had occurr ed. While surface free radical content was not 161 
directly quantified, their role in the degradation process is strongly inferred from these 162 
oxygen-containing transformations, which are the formation of peroxy radicals and 163 
hydroperoxide intermediates, resulting in the peroxy radicals undergoing chain scission 164 
to form carbonyl compounds [33]. Combined, our results show that UV 165 
photodegradation altered the physicochemical properties of PS-MPs. 166 
3.2 Humoral indicators, oxidative stress and IBR analysis 167 
Humoral indicators were determined in blood serum and skin mucus. As shown in 168 
Fig. 2A, both pristine and aged PS-MPs significantly increased IgM level in females. 169 
The increase of IgM level in the FA-PS-MP group is greater than that in the FP-PS-MP 170 
group. The skin mucus of fish is a barrier against various chemical, physical and 171 
biological stressors, regarded as the first line of defense against infection [34]. As one 172 
of the immune parameters in mucus, IgM serves as an immune effector in the humoral 173 
immune system of fish [35]. To our knowledge, there is little research focusing on the 174 
effects of MPs on zebrafish mucus immunity. Yang et al. [36] showed that PS-MPs had 175 
no effect on IgM level in larval zebrafish. However, our study on adult zebrafish found 176 
that IgM level increased in female mucus in both PS-MP groups, and the FA- PS-MP 177 
group showed a greater increase in mucus compared to the FP-PS-MP group, indicating 178 
PS-MPs might induce immunostimulatory effects on female zebrafish and aged PS-179 
MPs induced more of these effects. 180 
Fig. 2B and C show that aged PS-MPs raised LPS and TG content significantly in 181 
both sexes. However, no differences were found between controls and pristine PS- MP 182 
groups. Aged PS-MPs increased ROS level and SOD activity in females, induced SOD 183 
activity and MDA content in males, while there was no change between controls and 184 
pristine PS-MPs treatments (Fig. 2D-F). 185 
The release of LPS is related to changes in the composition of gut microbiota [37]. 186 
Luan et al. [38] similarly found that polyglycolic acid MPs could induce gut microbiota 187 
dysbiosis in zebrafish, leading to the overproduction of LPS and adverse effects on liver 188 
health by the gut-liver-brain axis. In our study, the increased relative abundance of 189 
gram-negative bacteria and upregulation of LPS biosynthesis could increase LPS level 190 
in the gut (section 3.3.1), potentially inducing gut inflammatory responses [39]. The 191 
increased serum LPS level in aged PS- MP groups suggested a compromised integrity 192 
of the gut barrier, leading to the discharge of gut LPS to the bloodstream [40], which 193 
may further induce immune and inflammatory responses and oxidative stress in the 194 
liver [41, 42]. 195 
Oxidative damage is one of the major toxicity mechanisms of MPs to zebrafish 196 
[43]. In our study, aged PS-MPs induced oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in the 197 
zebrafish liver, which may be explained by their changed surface characteristics, 198 
including the increase of oxygen-containing functional groups and free radicals [44]. 199 
The oxidative imbalance could damage hepatic cells and disrupt the homeostasis of 200 
liver lipid metabolism [45, 46]. As a widely present lipid compound in organisms and 201 
one of the most important forms of energy storage, the primary site of TG synthesis is 202 
the liver [47]. The increase in serum TG levels indicated an increase in liver lipid levels 203 
and their release into the systemic circulation. The accumulation of TG in the liver will 204 
induce liver dysfunction and damage to liver cells, leading to liver lipid metabolism 205 
disorders [48], and therefore the increased content of TG, ROS, SOD and MDA 206 
observed in our study in response to aged MP exposure suggests lipid metabolism 207 
dysbiosis and oxidative damage occurred in the liver. 208 
 The IBR analysis integrated multi-indicator responses (Fig. 2G and H). The value 209 
of the IBRv2 index in the aged PS- MP treatment group was higher than that in the 210 
pristine PS-MPs treatment in both sexes. Specifically, the values increased from 6.38 211 
(FP-PS-MP) to 11.15 (FA- PS-MP) in females, and from 6.72 (MP- PS-MP) to 12.10 212 
(MA-PS-MP) in males (Table S1). A high IBR value usually indicates an increase in 213 
toxic stress in the test organism [49]. The higher IBR value in the aged PS-MP treatment 214 
groups further demonstrated that UVC-aged PS-MPs showed larger biomarker 215 
perturbations to zebrafish than pristine PS-MPs under these experimental conditions. 216 
3.3 The adverse health impact on zebrafish induced by pristine and aged PS-MPs 217 
through the gut-liver axis 218 
By inducing oxidative stress and the disruption of humoral indicators in zebrafish, 219 
aged PS-MPs induced more adverse effects than pristine PS-MPs. To clarify the impact 220 
of PS-MPs on zebrafish health and the toxicity differences between pristine and aged 221 
PS-MPs, it is necessary to further analyze molecular processes along gut-liver axis to 222 
explain the changes in gut microbiome and metabolism. 223 
3.3.1 Gut microbiota composition and predicted function 224 
The Venn diagram (Fig. S1 A and B) showed that PS-MPs changed the number of 225 
OTUs of gut microbiota compared to the control group. To quantify the overall 226 
community shift, beta-diversity was assessed using UniFrac distances. Aged PS-MP 227 
exposure induced a significant shift in the female intestinal community (Unweighted 228 
UniFrac, p = 0.033), whereas the male group showed no such significant divergence 229 
(p > 0.05) (Fig S1E and F). Consistent results were found by PLS-DA analysis (Fig. 230 
S1C and D). 231 
The relative abundance of gut microbiota composition in the control, pristine and 232 
aged PS-MP treatment groups was exhibited at the phylum level in Fig 3A, 3B, S2A 233 
and S2B. Firmicutes and Bacteroidota increased while Proteobacteria and 234 
Actinobacteriota decreased after exposure to pristine PS-MPs. Aged PS-MPs increased 235 
Proteobacteria and Bacteroidota and reduced Firmicutes and Actinobacteriota. In male 236 
zebrafish, pristine and aged PS-MPs both increased Firmicutes, Actinobacteriota and 237 
Bacteroidota while decreasing Proteobacteria. 238 
As Proteobacteria are often associated with gut inflammation [39], our results 239 
showed an upward trend in their relative abundance specifically in female zebrafish 240 
following exposure to aged PS-MPs. Although this enrichment did not reach statistical 241 
significance after FDR correction, this compositional shift is noteworthy, as microbial 242 
dysbiosis in the gut is frequently linked to increased gut permeability and the 243 
subsequent translocation of bacterial endotoxins, such as LPS into the bloodstream [40, 244 
50]. This exploratory finding is consistent with the significantly elevated serum LPS 245 
levels and oxidative stress biomarkers observed in the female FA- PS-MP group 246 
(Section 3.2), suggesting that aged particles may trigger a more pronounced 247 
inflammatory response via the gut-liver axis in females. The sex-dependent increase of 248 
Proteobacteria may also be related to physiological differences between male and 249 
female fish. Sex-dependent host-microbiome interactions have been reported in 250 
zebrafish and other aquatic organisms, where differences in metabolism, immune 251 
regulation, and endocrine signaling can influence microbial responses to environmental 252 
stressors [51-53]. In addition, the gut microbiota plays an important role in regulating 253 
host metabolism and immune responses in zebrafish, and disturbances in microbial 254 
composition can influence metabolic homeostasis [54, 55]. Therefore, physiological 255 
differences between males and females may create distinct intestinal 256 
microenvironments that influence microbial susceptibility to MPs and may create gut 257 
conditions that preferentially favor the proliferation of opportunistic Proteobacteria in 258 
females. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms underlying this sex-dependent 259 
microbial response require further investigation. At the genus level, the FA- PS-MP 260 
group exhibited an upward trend in the relative abundance of Pseudomonas and 261 
Aeromonas (Fig. S2C). Although these changes did not achieve statistical significance 262 
after FDR correction, their increased presence is consistent with the overall expansion 263 
of the Proteobacteria phylum observed in females exposed to aged particles. These 264 
genera are fish pathogens and can cause several fish diseases, such as lung infections, 265 
enteritis, and ulcer disease [56, 57]. Their elevated levels suggest a stressed gut 266 
environment, which is consistent with the elevated LPS levels and oxidative stress 267 
biomarkers in the female FA-PS-MP (Fig. 2B). 268 
The Firmicutes are associated with immune function and digestive health and the 269 
Bacteroidetes are associated with the digestion of cellulose and monounsaturated fatty 270 
acids in fish [58, 59] . In contrast to several studies [60-62], Firmicutes and 271 
Bacteroidetes increased and Proteobacteria reduced in the FP-PS-MP, MP-PS-MP, and 272 
MA-PS-MP groups compared to controls. Gu et al. [63] found a similar outcome in the 273 
gut of large yellow croaker Larimichthys crocea after PS nanoplastics exposure, and 274 
suggested that an increase in gut beneficial bacteria would help the species to cope with 275 
the growth of potential pathogenic bacteria. Considering the complexity and 276 
interactions of gut microbiota [64], our results highlight the potential for dysbiosis in 277 
the gut microbiota by PS-MPs, which may further result in oxidative stress and 278 
inflammatory responses. 279 
As the FA-PS-MP group exhibited the most distinct alterations in both microbial 280 
composition and liver metabolism (section 3.3.4), predicted functions of gut microbiota 281 
in the FA-PS-MP group was explored by PICRUSt2. Several pathways exhibited 282 
significant upregulations compared to the F-control group, including energy 283 
metabolism, membrane transport, metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, and glycan 284 
biosynthesis and metabolism (Fig. S2E). 285 
We found that aged PS-MPs could affect element metabolism (C, N, S, and P) of 286 
female gut microbiota. The phosphotransferase system is related to the transport and 287 
phosphorylation of carbohydrates [65]. Several studies also demonstrated the potential 288 
modification of microbial element metabolism functions induced by MPs, including 289 
C/N/S/P metabolism [66, 67]. In addition, upregulation of LPS biosynthesis of gut 290 
microbiota paralleled an increased level of LPS in the blood. The increase of LPS level 291 
could induce gut inflammation by activation of innate immunity and promote pro-292 
inflammatory gene expression [68]. Thus, the upregulation of LPS biosynthesis may 293 
indicate a more invasive gut microbiota. 294 
3.3.2 Gut metabolomic analysis and KEGG pathway analysis 295 
While no metabolites passed the stringent False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction 296 
(q < 0.05), an exploratory analysis based on nominal p-values (p < 0.05) revealed a 297 
distinct metabolic signature in the FA- PS-MP group. Although Principal Component 298 
Analysis (PCA) showed that the FA- PS-MP group exhibited a more pronounced 299 
separation from the control compared to the FP- PS-MP group (Fig. S3A), these shifts 300 
represent exploratory trends rather than statistically significant deviations. In the FA-301 
PS-MP group, 129 DEMs were identified (nominal p < 0.05), with a majority being up-302 
regulated. In contrast, fewer DEMs were observed in the FP- PS-MP group (Fig. S3B 303 
and C). Hierarchical clustering (Fig. S4) further indicated that the aged PS-MPs exerted 304 
a broader influence on the metabolome than pristine particles, particularly affecting 305 
lipids, nucleosides, and organic acids. 306 
 Functional enrichment analysis revealed that aged PS-MPs induced a broader and 307 
more complex metabolic disruption in female zebrafish compared to pristine PS-MPs. 308 
As shown in the KEGG category overview (Fig. 3C and D), the FA- PS-MP group 309 
exhibited perturbations across a higher number of metabolic categories, particularly 310 
within nucleotide metabolism and human disease-related pathways, which were less 311 
prominent in the FP-PS-MP group. As visualized in the enrichment bubble plots (Fig. 312 
S3D and E), the FA- PS-MP group was associated with a wider array of metabolic 313 
perturbations. Notably, glycerophospholipid and ether lipid metabolism emerged as the 314 
enriched pathways in the FA-PS-MP group. Conversely, the metabolic influence of the 315 
FP-PS-MP group was more restricted, primarily centering on the PPAR signaling 316 
pathway and arachidonic acid metabolism. These results suggest that UVC aging 317 
process significantly expands the metabolic footprint of PS-MPs, intensifying the strain 318 
on hepatic lipid and nucleotide homeostasis. 319 
 Based on the KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. S3D and E), the lipid metabolism 320 
category emerged as a prominent metabolic target in the FA-PS-MP group, including 321 
glycerophospholipid metabolism and ether lipid metabolism. As one of the fundamental 322 
gut functions, lipid metabolism is important for energy production and metabolism [39]. 323 
Similarly, several studies have shown that MPs can induce disruptions in gut lipid 324 
metabolism in fish, causing adverse health impacts [5, 69]. Rossi et al. [70] 325 
demonstrated that PS nanoplastics could readily permeate into lipid membranes, 326 
altering their structure. Cedervall et al. [71] found that PS nanoplastics can bind to 327 
apolipoprotein A-I in fish to interfere with lipid metabolism. 328 
3.3.3 Correlation between influenced gut microbiota and gut DEMs in female zebrafish 329 
 Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the association between gut 330 
microbiota and gut DEMs. In the FP-PS-MP group, only Actinobacteriota was related 331 
to 8-hydroxy-5Z, 9E, 11Z, 14Z-eicosatetraenoic acid and 17-aminogeldanamycin (Fig. 332 
S5). In the FA-PS-MP group, gut microbiota was closely related to lipid metabolites. 333 
Specifically, Proteobacteria was closely related to larsucosterol, delta-24-cholesterol, 334 
Sphingomyelin ( SM) (d16:2(4E, 8Z)/PGJ2), and Phosphatidylethanolamine ( PE) 335 
(18:2/0:0). Bacteroidota was significantly associated with glycerophosphocholine and 336 
larsucosterol. These results indicate the potential association between gut microbial 337 
alteration and gut lipid metabolism. 338 
3.3.4 Hepatic metabolomic alterations induced by pristine and aged PS-MPs and KEGG 339 
pathway analysis 340 
V olcano plots (Fig. 4) highlight several candidate metabolites with substantial 341 
effect sizes, particularly within the glycerophospholipid category. In the female FA-PS-342 
MP group, 95 DEMs were identified, with a predominant trend toward up-regulation 343 
(70 up-regulated and 25 down-regulated). In contrast, the FP-PS-MP group exhibited a 344 
much more restricted metabolic response, with only 27 DEMs (8 up-regulated and 19 345 
down-regulated). Regarding the male groups, exposure to pristine PS-MPs resulted in 346 
67 DEMs (30 up-regulated and 37 down-regulated), while the MA- PS-MP group 347 
showed 64 altered metabolites (24 up-regulated and 40 down-regulated). Hierarchical 348 
clustering (Fig. S7) further corroborated that aged PS-MPs induced a more extensive 349 
redistribution of the metabolome in females than in males. 350 
Among all treatment groups, the FA- PS-MP group displayed the highest number 351 
of metabolic perturbations, suggesting a distinct sex-specific sensitivity and a more 352 
profound impact of aged particles on hepatic homeostasis compared to pristine PS-MPs. 353 
In addition, most of the DEMs in the FA- PS-MP group and the MA- PS-MP group 354 
belonged to lipids, including glycerophospholipids, fatty acyls, steroids and steroid 355 
derivatives and prenol lipids (Fig. S9). Glycerophospholipids are the main components 356 
of biological membranes and precursors to lipid mediators, associated with numerous 357 
metabolic diseases [72]; fatty acyls are important sources of energy and play a pivotal 358 
role in lipid composition [73]; and steroid hormones play a regulatory role in regulating 359 
lipid metabolism and inflammation. [74]. The changes in these metabolites suggested 360 
potential liver damage and dysregulation in lipid metabolism induced by aged PS-MPs. 361 
Impacts on hepatic lipid metabolism were previously demonstrated as a component of 362 
hepatotoxicity of MPs [38, 72, 75]. The disruption of lipid metabolism can be induced 363 
by oxidative stress [76], which was observed in the aged PS- MP treatment groups in 364 
our study (Fig. 2). Thus, the metabolome analysis provides further evidence that 365 
exposure to aged PS-MPs could result in metabolic disorders, particularly affecting 366 
lipid metabolism. 367 
 In the female groups, pristine PS-MPs altered carbohydrate metabolism and amino 368 
acid metabolism; while aged PS-MPs altered more pathways, including membrane 369 
transport, digestive system, carbohydrate metabolism, endocrine system, amino acid 370 
metabolism, metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, and signal transduction (Fig. 4 and 371 
S10). In the male groups, pristine and aged PS-MPs both altered global and overview 372 
maps, biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites, amino acid metabolism, and 373 
nucleotide metabolism. 374 
MPs were demonstrated to disrupt carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism in fish, 375 
leading to energy insufficiency and disorder which is a finding consistent with previous 376 
research [77-79]. Specifically, our results showed that pristine and aged PS-MPs both 377 
influenced amino acids metabolism and carbohydrate metabolism in both sexes, which 378 
are basic biological processes for maintaining liver function and overall metabolic 379 
homeostasis [80, 81]. Among differential amino acids, tryptophan and phenylalanine 380 
are related to immunity and stress response [82]. Choi et al. [83] also found the exposure 381 
to polyethylene terephthalate MPs affected tryptophan and phenylalanine metabolism, 382 
accompanied by inflammatory response and oxidative stress in juvenile rockfish. The 383 
disruptions in these amino acid metabolism pathways can lead to potential damage to 384 
protein synthesis and immunity. 385 
In addition, there were some unique ly influenced metabolic pathways (e.g. 386 
membrane transport, digestive system, endocrine system) in the FA-PS-MP group 387 
compared to the F-control and the MA-PS-MP group, indicating that exposure to 388 
pristine and aged PS-MPs may disrupt different basic metabolic mechanisms as well as 389 
sex-specific impacts of aged PS-MPs. In our study, betaine, enriched in the ABC 390 
transporter pathway, was down-regulated in the FA- PS-MP group. This indicated that 391 
aged PS-MPs might disrupt the balance of the substrate transport system and affect 392 
energy metabolism [84], which is consistent with a previous study [85]. Meanwhile, 393 
betaine has strong antioxidant properties and plays an important role in resisting 394 
oxidative damage [86]. The significant decrease of betaine level indicated its 395 
contribution to defend against oxidative damage and repair ROS-induced damaged 396 
membranes. Bile secretion and pancreatic secretion are involved in the digestive 397 
process, and the secreted bile acids, hormone and digestive enzymes can regulate lipid 398 
and glucose metabolism, contributing to immunity and glucose homeostasis [87, 88]. 399 
Thus, the effects of aged PS-MPs on the digestive system indicated potential gut 400 
function disorder in female zebrafish. 401 
3.3.5 Correlation between influenced gut microbiota and liver DEMs 402 
The correlation analysis revealed the complicated association between gut 403 
microbiota and hepatic DEM s. As shown in Fig. 5A, in the FP-PS-MP group, 404 
Bacteroidota had a significant positive correlation with glycerophosphoserine (GPSer) 405 
(22:6/18:0), and Actinobacteriota had a significant positive correlation with GPSer 406 
(22:6/18:0) and glycerophosphocholine (GPCho) (17:1/17:1), which belong to 407 
glycerophospholipids. In the FA-PS-MP group, the associations were more complex . 408 
Proteobacteria, Firmicutes and Bacteroidota were positively correlated with lipids , 409 
amino acids and carbohydrates metabolites (Fig. 5 B). Among them, lipid-related 410 
metabolites accounted for the majority, including glycerophospholipids, fatty acyls, 411 
steroids and steroids derivatives, and prenol lipids. Meanwhile, the Pearson analysis 412 
showed that the DEMs were interrelated with each other. In the MP-PS-MP group (Fig. 413 
S11A), only Bacteroidota had a significant positive correlation with organooxygen 414 
compounds and lipids metabolites, while Proteobacteria was positively correlated with 415 
lipids metabolites and negatively correlated with D-valine in the MA-PS-MP group (Fig. 416 
S11B). 417 
Changes in gut microbiota can impact the gut barrier function, leading to the 418 
transmission of endogenous and exogenous substances produced by microbiota to the 419 
liver through the gut-liver axis, affecting zebrafish health [89]. The gut-liver axis 420 
provides a conceptual framework for understanding interactions between intestinal 421 
microbiota and host metabolism. In this study, the observed correlations between gut 422 
microbiota and liver metabolites suggest coordinated responses between gut microbial 423 
composition and hepatic metabolic profiles following PS-MPs exposure. Previous 424 
studies have also reported associations between microplastic exposure, microbiota 425 
dysbiosis, and metabolic disturbances [90, 91] , which is consistent with our findings. 426 
Notably, compared to the FP- PS-MP group, the FA- PS-MP group exhibited more 427 
complex and extensive associations, particularly involving lipid metabolites such as 428 
glycerophospholipids (e.g., GPSer, GPCho, and Phosphatidylcholine (PC)), as well as 429 
amino acids and carbohydrates. These findings suggest the associations between 430 
microbial variation and alterations in hepatic glycolipid metabolism in females exposed 431 
to aged PS-MPs. However, these correlation-based results may be inherently 432 
associative and the metabolomic results are derived from untargeted profiling and 433 
therefore require further biological validation. Future studies should include targeted 434 
quantitative validation of representative metabolites (e.g., lipid-related metabolites) as 435 
well as functional experiments to clarify the potential links between gut microbiota 436 
dynamics and hepatic metabolic alterations. Approaches such as tracer-based 437 
metabolite tracking, intestinal permeability assays, LPS neutralization experiments, or 438 
germ-free/antibiotic-mediated microbiome modulation would help further elucidate 439 
these relationships. 440 
3.3.6 Correlation between influenced gut and liver lipid metabolites in the FA- PS-MP 441 
group and potential toxicity mechanism of aged PS-MPs on female zebrafish 442 
 Based on the results from gut and liver metabolomics, lipid metabolism was 443 
considered as the significantly influenced pathway in both the gut and liver metabolome. 444 
Meanwhile, gut microbiota was closely related to multiple lipid metabolites in both the 445 
gut and the liver. A correlation network analysis was conducted to identify the 446 
association and metabolic interactions across the two tissues, including 9 lipid 447 
metabolites from the gut and 12 lipid metabolites from the liver. Lipid metabolites in 448 
the gut were closely associated with lipid metabolites in the liver (Fig. 6A). 449 
Glycerophospholipids products (PC, GPSer, GPCho in the liver; PE, 450 
glycerophosphocholine, glyceryl phosphorylcholine in the gut) appeared to weigh high 451 
in the correlation network, indicating glycerophospholipid metabolism disorder and the 452 
interplay between the two tissues. While pristine MPs have been documented to disturb 453 
general lipid homeostasis in zebrafish [92], our findings reveal that mechanical aging 454 
potentially intensifies this disruption. 455 
Because aged PS-MPs induced greater negative impact on gut microbiota 456 
composition and metabolic profiles in female zebrafish, further attention was given to 457 
the potential biological responses associated with aged PS-MPs exposure in female 458 
zebrafish. Based on the combined observations from microbiome profiling, 459 
metabolomic analysis, and oxidative stress biomarkers, aged PS-MPs exposure was 460 
associated with shifts in gut microbial composition, alterations in glycolipid- and amino 461 
acid-related metabolic pathways, and increased oxidative stress (Fig. 6B). These 462 
patterns suggest a possible interaction between gut microbiota dynamics, host 463 
metabolic responses, and oxidative stress under aged PS-MPs exposure. However, these 464 
relationships are based on associative evidence and should be interpreted cautiously, as 465 
additional functional and mechanistic experiments are required to confirm the 466 
underlying toxicity pathways. 467 
3.4 Limitations and future work 468 
This study provides novel insights into the toxicity mechanisms of pristine and 469 
aged PS-MPs on zebrafish health. However, there are still some aspects that require 470 
further investigation to comprehensively understand the toxicological impacts and 471 
potential risks of pristine and aged MPs to freshwater ecosystems. In-depth research 472 
should be conducted on the physicochemical property changes of aged MPs to better 473 
understand the mechanism of the toxicity changes. Targeted metabolite quantification 474 
and functional experiments are needed to validate the proposed pathways and clarify 475 
the mechanistic links between gut microbiota and host metabolism under PS-MPs 476 
exposure. Moreover, future studies should incorporate environmentally relevant aging 477 
processes (e.g., UV A/UVB exposure, mechanical abrasion) and lower, field-realistic 478 
concentrations to better assess ecological relevance and refine risk assessment. Finally, 479 
future studies could investigate the toxicity of MPs on freshwater-sediment ecosystem 480 
instead of individual species, studying the interactions between plants, animals, water 481 
and sediment. 482 
4. Conclusions 483 
This study revealed that aged PS-MPs exert ed more adverse effects than pristine 484 
PS-MPs, causing a stronger negative impact on zebrafish health. Exposure to aged PS-485 
MPs increased the IgM, LPS and TG level and induced oxidative stress in zebrafish. 486 
The potential toxicity mechanism could be driven by the changed physicochemical 487 
properties of aged MPs, including the increase of oxygen-containing functional groups 488 
and free radicals. Meanwhile, female zebrafish showed greater changes in gut 489 
microbiota composition and metabolic profiles than males, suggesting potential sex-490 
dependent responses to aged PS-MPs exposure. Both pristine and aged PS-MPs were 491 
associated with shifts in gut microbial composition and alterations in hepatic metabolic 492 
profiles. In particular, aged PS-MP exposure were accompanied by increased relative 493 
abundance of Proteobacteria in female zebrafish and broader changes in metabolites 494 
related to lipid, amino acid, and carbohydrate metabolism. Correlation analyses further 495 
indicated coordinated patterns between microbial composition and host metabolic 496 
responses in the gut and liver. Together, these results suggest that aged PS-MPs may 497 
influence host physiology through combined changes in microbial composition, 498 
metabolic responses, and oxidative stress. Overall, our findings provide new insights 499 
into the potential health implications of aged MPs in freshwater organisms. 500
