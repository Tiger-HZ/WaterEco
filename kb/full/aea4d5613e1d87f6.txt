Introduction 
Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is a critical staple food crop for a significant 
global population. It meets more than 80 % of their dietary needs. 
However, it typically has a low phosphorus use efficiency (PUE) of 
around 25 %. Because of this inefficiency, plants exhibit signs of P 
deficiency, such as stunted growth, fewer branches (tillering) and 
narrow, short, dark green leaves. Additionally, affected plants may 
produce fewer panicles and grains and develop thin stems (1). Plants 
have developed diverse adaptive mechanisms to obtain P from soil, 
especially in reaction to P scarcity. These changes can be in the form 
of anatomical, morphological, physiological, biochemical, or genetic 
changes (2). Some plants can change P that is not soluble into forms 
that are bioavailable, which makes P more efficient. Under P 
deficient conditions, the release of nucleases, organic acids, protons 
and acid phosphatase (APase) makes it easier for P to dissolve, 
mineralise and desorb, which in turn improves phosphorus 
acquisition efficiency (PAE) (3). In addition, plants adjust their root 
system architecture (RSA) such as the root-to-shoot biomass ratio 
(RSR), lateral root growth and root hair proliferation to improve PAE 
by extracting P from decomposing organic matter in the soil (4). 
Phosphorus is among the seventeen essential nutrients 
required for plant growth. However, it limits crop productivity on 
over 40 % of the world’s arable land. Global P reserves are being 
consumed rapidly and some predictions suggest that natural soil P 
reserves may be exhausted by 2050, posing a serious risk to 
sustainable agriculture (5). A large proportion of P fertilisers applied 
to soil often become adsorbed, making them unavailable to plants 
that lack specific adaptive traits. Even so, the right use of P-based 
fertilisers can increase the amount of P available in the soil and, as a 
result, the number of crops that can be grown to meet the needs of 
the growing world population. Phosphorus may also be recycled 
through crop residues by putting them back into the soil. When crop 
residues used as livestock feed, some of the P goes back into the soil 
through manure or bone meal. Plants have evolved diverse adaptive 
mechanisms, called P-efficiency strategies, to get enough P when 
conditions are unfavourable. These mechanisms involve both 
enhanced uptake efficiency, the plant's capacity to absorb more P 
under deficient conditions and increased utilisation efficiency, which 
is the ability to produce a greater biomass yield per unit of absorbed 
P. Plants enhance P uptake efficiency through multiple mechanisms,

Abstract 
Phosphorus (P) is a primary limiting macronutrient in tropical and subtropical rice production. While total soil P may be high, over 50 % of 
global agricultural soils suffer from low bioavailable orthophosphate due to strong chemical fixation with aluminium (Al) and iron (Fe) oxides 
in acidic soils, or calcium (Ca) in alkaline soils. Developing and deploying P-efficient rice genotypes capable of maintaining growth and yield 
under low soil P availability is a key priority for sustainable agriculture. A pot culture experiment was carried out at the Radio Isotope 
Laboratory, Department of Soil Science and Agricultural Chemistry, Tamil Nadu Agricultural University, Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu . T he 
treatment comprised factor A: rice genotypes viz.,TNRH-180, CB08504, CB06732 and ADT 47 and factor B: P levels viz., 0, 25 and 50 kg P ha-1. 
Phosphorus was applied in the form of the radioisotope ³²P, the experiment aimed to evaluate and quantify yield performance, phosphorus 
use efficiency (PUE) and the native P supplying capacity of low P soils. The findings indicated that the rice genotypes viz.,TNRH-180 recorded 
higher yield attributes, yield and PUE viz., productive tillers (8.4 nos.), panicle length (24.1 cm), grains per panicle (167.7 nos.), test weight 
(17.2 g), grain yield (22.3 g/pot) and PUE (23.72 %) and was followed by CB06732 respectively.

 Radioisotopes undergo radioactive decay by emitting 
energy and radiation, which have been extensively used in 
numerous agricultural applications. The use of radioisotopes has 
been applied in the development of high-yielding crops to enhance 
productivity and for studying the dynamics of fertilisers in different 
plants. In this way, research on agricultural problems has become 
more efficient and faster. Additionally, the mass production of 
radioisotopes in nuclear reactors and atomic plants has led to 
increased applications of radioisotopes in agriculture (7). Since 
fertilisers comprise an economically important input, efficient 
fertiliser application plays a very important role in reducing costs 
associated with crop production. There must be an effort to increase 
plant nutrient uptake efficiency and reduce nutrient losses to the 
environment. In this connection, radioisotopes become a valuable 
means of measuring initial P and nitrogen (N) content in the soil, 
which is extremely important when determining optimal fertiliser 
application amounts (8). Therefore, the study aimed to quantify the 
yield, nutrient uptake and PUE using the radioisotope material in rice 
crops under the pot culture method. 
 
Materials and Methods 
Experimental location and initial study 
The first field experiment was conducted in Paddy Breeding Station, 
Coimbatore with 22 rice genotypes viz., CB06732, TNRH-180, 
AS06034, CB08536, CB08513, CB08504, CB08509, CB06535, TP08010, 
AD07038, TM07333, AS06035, AD08010, TM07278, ACM07001, 
AS6016, CORH-3, ADT-47, ADT-43, ASD-16, Co-47 and ADT -45 
(varieties, pre-release cultures and landraces) at 0 and 50 kg P2O5 ha-1 
in P deficient soil of Noyyal series. Based on P uptake efficiency, four 
rice genotypes viz., TNRH-180, CB08504, CB06732 and ADT-47 were 
selected from the field experiment. 
Pot culture experiment 
The pot culture experiment was executed at Radio Isotope (Tracer) 
Laboratory, Department of Soil Science and Agricultural Chemistry, 
Tamil Nadu Agricultural University, Coimbatore. The experiment soil 
was filled in earthen pots at 10 kg pot-1, lined with polythene sheets. 
The treatment consisted factor A: rice genotypes viz., TNRH-18, 
CB08504, CB06732 and ADT- 47 and factor B: fertiliser levels viz., 0, 25 
and 50 kg P. This lab experiment was laid out in a completely 
randomised block design (CRBD) with 3 replications (Fig. 1). Four hills 
were maintained in pot culture and duration of the experiment was 
130 days. Fertilisers were applied as per the ratio of 150 : 50 for N and 
potassium (K), whereas P was provided in the form of a radioactive 
isotope of 32P. Before commencing the experiment, initial soil 
samples were collected from the field for various physico-chemical 
properties. After the rice crop was harvested, both grain and straw 
samples were prepared and subjected to a radio assay. The P from 
the plant samples, extracted using a triacid mixture, was precipitated 
as magnesium pyrophosphate (9). 
Nature of phosphorus32 
Carrier-free radioactive 32P was procured from the Board of 
Radiation and Isotope Technology (BRIT), Mumbai, in the form of 
orthophosphoric acid dissolved in dilute hydrochloric acid and used 
for the experiments. The isotope has a physical half life of 14.3 days 
and undergoes β-emission (negatron release) with a maximum 
energy of 1.71 MeV, transforming into sulphur.

 Preparation of pots and tagging of phosphorus32 
Soil samples with low available P content were collected from the 
Paddy Breeding Station and used for a pot culture experiment. Each 
pot was filled with 10 kg of wet soil. All pots received a uniform 
supply of N (as urea) and K (as muriate of potash), which were 
applied in four equal splits at the basal, active tillering, panicle 
initiation and flowering stages. Phosphorus32 was supplied as 
labelled single superphosphate (SSP), applied basally. The labelled 
SSP was prepared by thoroughly mixing rock phosphate, the 32P 
stock solution and concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4) in calculated 
proportions with constant stirring. 
 The prepared material was dried under an infrared lamp and 
then finely ground using a mortar and pestle. The radioactive 
fertilisers were applied to the pots following all prescribed safety 
measures for handling isotopes. After application, the pots were 
irrigated and maintained in a submerged condition and seedlings 
raised in the nursery for 22 days were transplanted into each pot at 
of 2–3 seedlings per pot. 
Radioisotope handling and safety protocol 
Radioisotope handling must follow strict radiation safety principles 
to minimise radiation exposure and prevent contamination. Since 
ionising radiation can cause biological harm, exposure should be 
maintained as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA) by minimising 
the quantity of radioactive material handled, reducing exposure 
time, maximising the distance from the radiation source and using 
appropriate shielding. Radioisotopes should be classified according 
to their toxicity based on the type and energy of radiation, physical 
and biological half-life, absorption pattern and the quantity and 
chemical form handled. Radiation safety practices should comply 
with the recommendations of the international commission on 
radiological protection (ICRP), including justification of use, 
optimisation of protection and adherence to prescribed dose limits 
for occupational workers and the public. Personnel handling 
radioisotopes should wear appropriate personal protective 
equipment, including laboratory coats, disposable gloves, closed 
footwear or shoe covers and other protective clothing as required. 
Personal radiation exposure should be monitored using 
thermoluminescent dosimeter (TLD) badges or film badges, which 
are typically evaluated at regular intervals (commonly once every 
three months) by authorised radiation safety personnel to ensure 
that occupational dose limits are not exceeded. 
 Radiation exposure and contamination should be 
continuously monitored using pocket ionisation chambers, survey 
meters, Geiger counters, scintillation counters and contamination 
monitors. Airborne contamination should be assessed using static 
air samplers, while surface contamination should be monitored 
either by direct measurement with portable contamination 
monitors or by indirect smear tests using filter paper or cotton 
swabs. In the event of contamination, immediate decontamination 
measures should be undertaken by removing contaminated 
clothing, gently washing the affected skin with soap and water and 
cleaning laboratory equipment and work surfaces with water 
followed by suitable detergents or approved decontaminating 
agents such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), chromic 
acid, or dilute mineral acids. Radioactive spills should be contained 
using absorbent materials and all contaminated materials should be 
treated as radioactive waste until contamination levels are reduced 
to permissible limits. All radiation work should be performed only by 
trained personnel following approved radiation safety protocols and 
ALARA principles to ensure the protection of personnel, the 
laboratory environment and the surrounding ecosystem. 
Waste disposal of radioisotope materials 
The disposal of radioisotope materials should be carried out in 
accordance with the Atomic Energy (Safe Disposal of Radioactive 
Wastes) Rules, 1987, with the primary objective of ensuring that 
radiation exposure to personnel and the environment remains 
within prescribed limits. Radioactive waste generated during 
research activities should be segregated at the point of generation 
based on its physical form, activity level, half-life and chemical 
characteristics. The guiding principles of radioactive waste 
management include concentrate and contain, delay and discharge 
and dilute and discharge, with the disposal method selected 
according to the characteristics and hazard associated with each 
radionuclide. Short-lived solid radioactive wastes should be 
collected in designated containers and disposed of through 
controlled burial in approved pits, whereas small quantities of 
short-lived liquid wastes that are soluble or dispersible in water may 
be discharged into the sanitary sewage system within the prescribed 
regulatory limits. Liquid scintillation cocktails and other organic 
radioactive waste should be managed as chemical waste rather 
than discharged into sewage systems. Radioactive animal carcasses 
and contaminated plant materials should be securely wrapped in 
polythene bags and disposed of using the same controlled burial 
procedures as solid radioactive waste. Incineration of combustible 
radioactive waste may be undertaken only in suitable facilities under 
controlled conditions, with the resulting ash collected and disposed 
of as solid radioactive waste. Long -lived and non -dispersible 
radioactive wastes should not be disposed of locally and must be 
managed under the guidance and authorisation of the Radiological 
Safety Division of the Atomic Energy Regulatory Board (AERB). 
Throughout the waste management process, proper segregation, 
documentation, monitoring and compliance with regulatory limits 
are essential to ensure safe and environmentally responsible 
disposal of radioactive materials. 
 Short-lived solid radioactive waste must be separated from 
normal trash right at the worktable and buried locally in 
custom-made dirt pits. Pits must be exactly 120 × 120 cm. When 
filled, the waste must be covered by a top layer of compact soil that 
is at least 120 cm thick. If multiple pits are needed, they must be 
spaced at least 180 cm apart. A facility is allowed to dig a maximum 
of 12 pits per year. The area must be locked, fenced off and labelled 
with warning signs. Each pit has a strict radioactive limit depending 
on the material. For example, a single pit can hold up to 9250 MBq of 
tritium 3(H), 370 MBq of 32P and 37 MBq of iodine-13. After waiting for 
7 to 10 half-lives, the radioactivity decreases enough for the waste to 
be excavated and safely disposed of in a normal municipal dump. 
This allows the pit to be reused.

air oven at 65 °C, after which their dry weight was recorded. The 
oven-dried materials were chopped, finely ground using a Willey mill 
and stored in wide-mouthed, stoppered bottles. Representative 
subsamples were then taken and analysed for N, P, K, calcium (Ca), 
magnesium (Mg) and micronutrients following standard analytical 
methods. Phosphorus present in the plant’s triacid extract was 
determined by precipitating it as magnesium pyrophosphate,

Interpretation of radio assay data 
Radio assay data were converted into different parameters by using 
following formula: 
Absolute activity (disintegrations per second) 
The corrected count rate (counts per second, cps) is converted to 
Becquerels (Bq) or disintegrations per second (dps) by adjusting 
detector efficiency: 
Activity (Bq or dps) = Corrected count rate (cps)/Detector efficiency 
(%) × 100 
Specific activity 
Specific activity represents the radioactive decay rate per unit mass 
of total P present in the digested tissue: 
Specific activity (dps mg-1 P) = Sample disintegration rate (dps)/Total 
P mass in sample (mg) 
Phosphorus derived from fertiliser (% Pdff) 
The proportion of total plant P translocated directly from the applied 
32P labelled fertiliser standard is calculated as follows: 
Phosphorus derived from fertiliser (%) = Specific activity of plant 
sample/Specific activity of fertiliser standard × 100 
Phosphorus derived from soil (% Pdfs) 
The fraction of plant P sourced from native soil pools is determined 
by subtracting the fertiliser-derived fraction from the total P content. 
Phosphorus derived from soil (%) = 100 – % Pdff 
Where, Pdff is phosphorus derived from fertiliser. 
Fertiliser phosphorus uptake 
The absolute mass of P accumulated by the plant from the 
exogenous fertiliser source per experimental unit (pot) is expressed 
as follows: 
Fertiliser P uptake (mg P pot-1 = % Pdff/100 × Total P uptake 
(mg P pot -1) 
Where, Pdff is phosphorus derived from fertiliser. 
Fertiliser phosphorus utilisation efficiency (%) 
The percentage of applied fertiliser P successfully recovered by the 
plant tissue is calculated using the following ratio: 
% P utilisation = Fertiliser P uptake (mg P pot -1)/Total P applied via 
fertiliser (mg P pot -1) × 100 
Statistical analysis 
The observations recorded for different traits in the study were 
subjected to statistical analysis (10). The experimental data were 
further analysed using the procedure described earlier (11). Whenever 
significant differences among treatments were detected, critical 
differences (CD) were calculated at the 5 % probability level and 
reported accordingly. Genotypic clustering in the field experiment 
was performed using MINITAB software (version 22.5) (12). 
 
Results and Discussion 
Yield attributes 
Productive tillers per plant in ric e genotypes revealed significant 
differences between the genotypes. Among the rice genotypes, 
productive tillers per plant showed gradual increase when applied 
with higher level of P application (6.1–8.4). The mean value of 
productive tillers per plant was higher in TNRH -180 (7.9 nos.) 
followed by ADT-47 (7.9) and the lowest value recorded in CB06732 
(6.6). Even at omission P plots, TNRH-180 and ADT-47 performed 
better under P stress soil (Fig. 2). This may be attributed to variations 
in enzyme and hormone activity, leading to improved production of 
productive tillers (8). Rice genotypes can be able to use both available 
and bound forms of native P by releasing organic acids from their 
roots, which help dissolve the nutrient. Consequently, the identified 
genotypes have the capacity to improve PUE in soil ecosystems 
under P stress (13). 
 Low P soil significantly increased the number of 
productive tillers, root biomass, root-shoot ratio, root bleeding rate and 
root acid phosphat ase (RAP) activity . T ot al root length, 
root oxidation activity (ROA) and root tot al and active absorbing surf ace 
areas were significantly increased in strong-toleranc e varieties under 
low P , whereas the opposite responses were observed in weak 
tolerant varieties. T otal root length, ROA, root bleeding rate, root 
active absorbing surface area and RAP activity were positively and 
significantly correlated with grain yield, P translocation efficiency and 
internal P efficiency. These results suggest that the tolerance of rice 
varieties to a low-P growth condition is closely associated with root 
growth with higher biomass and activity (14). 
 The study was to screen 25 rice cultivars at low, medium and 
high levels of soil P . Number of tillers, root length, plant height, root 
dry weight and shoot dry weight were related to tissue P 
concentrations, P uptake and P-use efficiency. Shoot weight was 
found to be the plant parameter most sensitive to P deficiency. 
Significant cultivar differences in P use efficiency were found. 
Phosphorus use efficiency was higher in roots than shoots and 
decreased with increasing levels of soil P . Positive correlations were 
found among growth parameters such as plant height, tillers, root 
and shoot weight and P content of roots and shoots. These results 
indicate selection of rice cultivars for satisfactory performance under 
low P availability can be carried out using shoot and root dry weight 
as criteria (15). 
 Among the genotypes, TNRH-180 recorded the highest 
mean panicle length (24.1 cm), while ADT-47 registered the lowest 
(21.8 cm). The interaction effect indicated that TNRH-180 produced 
the maximum panicle length (24.5 cm), followed by 24.1 cm when 
supplied with 25 kg P . This effect may be due to the development of 
longer panicles bearing a greater number of grains per panicle under 
higher P application, supported by increased uptake of major 
nutrients during the reproductive stage (16). 
 The higher mean number of grains per panicle recorded in 
rice genotype TNRH-180 (159.1 nos.) and it was followed by CB08504. 
Among the various P levels, a higher number of grains per panicle 
was registered in TNRH-180 at 50 kg P . The interaction between 
genotypes and P levels was significant. This may be attributed to 
spikelet formation into well-filled grains because of the application of 
P fertiliser in conjunction with organic manure, enabling the crops to 
stay green even during the late stages of their life cycle. This would 
enable photosynthetic assimilation to be more effective, resulting in 
an increased number of filled grains per panicle (17). Adequate 
supply of micronutrients was essential for efficient utilisation of 
major plant nutrients that would react on yield components and

Grain and straw yield 
Among the rice genotypes, grain yield showed a gradual increase 
when applied with higher level of P (19.1 to 25.0 g pot-1). The mean 
value of grain yield was higher in TNRH-180 (23.1 g pot-1) followed by 
CB06732 (22.3 g pot-1) and the lowest value was recorded in CB08504 
(18.7 g pot-1). Comparatively, when P was applied at different doses 
viz., 0, 25 and 50 kg P the rice genotypes like TNRH-180, CB06732 and 
CB08504 found superior in grain yield under different P conditions 
(Fig. 3). It might be due to the enhancement of yield parameters like 
panicles per metre, panicle length, panicle weight, grains per panicle 
and filled grain percentage, consequently increasing grain yield (22). 
Even the plot received 0 kg P TNRH-180 recorded higher grain yield 
(21.0 g pot-1) followed by CB06732 respectively. It may be due to rice 
genotypes that can use both available and bound forms of native P 
by releasing organic acids from their roots, which help dissolve the 
nutrient. Consequently, the identified genotypes have the capacity to 
improve P utilisation efficiency under P soil ecosystem. Low P 
tolerant rice genotypes (TNRH-180) possess "metabolic flexibility" 
and adapt to P omission by altering root physiological traits and 
exudation patterns to mobilise bound rhizosphere P . 
 Straw yield increased with higher P application, ranging from 
24.0–32.3 g pot-1. The highest mean straw yield was observed in 
CB06732 (30.1 g pot -¹), while the lowest was recorded in 
CB08504 (26.5g pot-1). The interaction between genotypes and P 
levels was significant, with CB06732 producing the maximum straw 
yield (34.0g pot-1), followed by ADT-47 (33.1g pot-1), whereas the 
lowest yield (22.9 g pot-1) was noted in CB08504 under P omission 
conditions. The rise in grain and straw yield could be attributed to the 
overall enhancement of plant growth, as P plays a crucial role in 
plant metabolism, ultimately leading to improved yield (23).

Potassium uptake 
Potassium uptake in rice genotypes showed significant variation 
across different genotypes and P levels. The K uptake showed a 
gradual increase with higher level of P application and the range 
varied from 270–628 mg pot-1. The mean value of K uptake was 
higher in TNRH-180 (469 mg pot-1) followed by CB06732 460 mg pot-1 
and the lowest value was recorded in CB08504 (412 mg pot-1) (Fig. 3). 
The increased uptake of K in rice genotypes may be due to efficient 
nutrient uptake by combining the application of organic fertiliser 
together with N, P and K (29). The application of increased nutrient 
levels led to an increase in nutrient availability, leading to improved 
yields and quality (30). Phosphorus and Fe nutrition had a significant 
influence on K uptake by the crop. Application of 80 kg P with 50 kg 
FeSO4 ha-1 registered higher K uptake at all the stages. The higher K 
recovery might be due to higher drymatter production (DMP) 
particularly in straw, bulk of the absorbed K is retained (31). The K 
uptake by rice increased with advancement of crop growth. The 
highest uptake of K was observed when 100 % P was applied. This 
might be due to the increased root activity resulting in the increased 
dry matter accumulation and high utilisation of K (32). 
 
Correlation matrix 
The significant relationships among nutrient uptakes (N, P and K) 
implies that proper fertilisation and nutrient balance in the soil is 
necessary for optimal crop production. The significant positive 
association between grain and straw yields and nutrient uptake 
indicates the need for nutrients such as N and P to determine 
economic and biomass yields. The moderate relationship between 
productive tillers and nutrient uptake indicates that nutrients play a 
key role in vegetative growth and branching, which could affect the 
yield potential. On the contrary, the insignificant relationships 
between panicle length and other variables imply that it might not 
be an important factor in yield determination (Fig. 5). 
 The moderate correlation between test weight and nutrient 
uptakes suggests that the filling and quality of the grain are affected by 
the nutrient availability but not as much as the total yield. It is worth 
noting that the low correlation between the panicle length and 
number of grains per panicle and the grain yield shows that the grain 
yield is more dependent on the number of productive tillers and 
nutrient availability than on panicle properties. Nutrient management 
and integration are important to optimise the grain yield. 
Percent phosphorus utilisation 
There was variation in the mean PUE in the different genotypes of 
rice, with the highest percentage of P utilisation being observed in 
TNRH-180 (24 %) when P was applied at two concentrations 
followed by CB06732. When P was applied at the higher 
concentration (50 kg P), the genotype ADT-47 (21 %) showed a higher 
percentage of PUE than the others (Fig. 6). At the lower concentration 
(25 kg P), the genotype TNRH-180 (29 %) had the highest PUE 
compared to the others. The decrease in percent phosphorus 
utilisation (PPU) at 50 kg ha-1 can be attributed to the increased 
mineralisation of P from the soil (33).

Conclusion 
This study demonstrates that phosphorus (P) application 
significantly enhances the yield attributes, biomass production and 
macronutrient (nitrogen (N), P and potassium (K)) uptake of rice 
genotypes. Among the evaluated cultivars, TNRH-180 emerged as 
the most superior genotype, consistently recording the highest grain 
yield, nutrient uptake and phosphorus utilisation efficiency (PUE) 
achieving a peak percent phosphorus utilisation (PPU) of 29 % at the 
25 kg P ha-1 level. Notably, both TNRH-180 and ADT-47 exhibited 
robust performance and tolerance under P-deficient (omission) 
conditions, which is closely linked to adaptive root morphology and 
enhanced physiological activity. These genotypes proved a strong 
capacity to efficiently utilise both native soil P and applied P from 
soil. Therefore, they are recommended for cultivation in low P 
regions of soil ecosystem. Thus, utilising such scientific approaches 
will enable farmers to cultivate rice genotypes suitable for P stress, 
which will reduce P fertiliser usage and prevent soil pollution. Future 
research, cultivar development can be accelerated by using 
metabolomics to isolate the specific organic acids responsible for 
unlocking bound native P, alongside molecular screening to map 
the underlying high affinity phosphate transporter genes.
