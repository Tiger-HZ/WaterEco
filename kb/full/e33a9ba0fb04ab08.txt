Abstract Due to their environmental ubiquity, persis-
tence, and potential effects on living organisms, micro-
plastic pollution has become a global concern. Urban 
wetlands, catchment basins within cities, are one of the 
final sinks of these pollutants. We studied the occurrence, 
abundance, distribution, and physical and chemical char-
acteristics of MPs to determine the impact of anthropo-
genic activities on the microplastic pollution status in an 
urbanized wetland. Surface water samples from seven 
sites inside the urbanized wetland Laguna del Camarón 
were collected during two climatic seasons (rainy and 
dry), followed by organic-matter digestion, filtration, 
microscopic counting and classification by shape and 
color. Subsequently, the polymers were identified with 
Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. MPs 
concentration was significantly higher in the dry season 
(2.05 particles/L) than in rainy season (1.17 particles/L), 
with the higher values occurring in the small inner 
lagoon (LC6 and LC7), which, according to the Pollution 
Load Index (PLI), has been considered as unpolluted to 
moderately polluted and highly polluted with MPs. Fiber 
shape and black and blue colors are predominant, with 
polyester (PES), polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), 
and polystyrene (PS) polymers mainly occurring in this 
ecosystem consistent with nearby recreational and con-
struction activities. This study revealed spatial and sea-
sonal variations in the MP pollution status, elucidated 
potential pollution sources, and identified hotspots linked 
to visitor use and construction activities for targeted inter-
vention (improve onsite waste collection, visitor surveil-
lance, and the placement of bins and informative signs).

Introduction
Throughout history, wetlands have offered a wide 
range of ecosystem services, from nutrient retention 
to recreation and aesthetics (Boyer & Polasky, 2004). 
They function as protective zones, areas for the 
growth and reproduction of wildlife, and to reduce the 
effects of urban heat islands (Alikhani et  al., 2021). 
However, they are also highly fragile and suscepti-
ble to negative impacts such as contamination from 
hydrocarbons, metals, plastic waste, and microplas-
tics (MPs) (Joshi et al., 2021; Sharley et al., 2017).
Plastics, since their invention, have revolutionized 
daily life and industrial processes, offering comfort, 
safety, and hygiene. They are moldable, resistant, 
durable, inert, lightweight, and low-cost materials. 
Unfortunately, global demand and production have 
outpaced the world´s capacity for their reduction, 
recovery, repair, reuse, and recycling. It is estimated 
that only 9% of plastic waste is adequately recycled 
(d’ Ambrières, 2019); the rest ends up dispersed in 
various environmental compartments, where it frag-
ments through processes such as weathering and ero-
sion, eventually forming microplastics.
Microplastics (MPs) are defined as plastic micro-
particles ranging in size from 1 to 5000 µm. They are 
classified as primary and secondary. Primary MPs 
are intentionally manufactured with specific prop-
erties to fulfill certain functions and are commonly 
found in personal care products such as toothpaste, 
exfoliants, and cosmetics. Some primary MPs are 
also used as raw materials in manufacturing. In con-
trast, secondary MPs result from the weathering and 
degradation of macroplastics, as they are exposed to 
environmental factors (UV radiation, wind, surface 
runoff, chemical and biological reactions), which 
promote their aging, fragmentation, and dispersion 
(Dawson et  al., 2018; Miranda et  al., 2021; Stride 
et al., 2024).
Research on MPs has surged in recent years, 
driven by their environmental ubiquity, persistence, 
and potential effects on living organisms. MPs have 
been found in diverse environments, from Antarc-
tic ice to deep ocean trenches (Kelly et  al., 2020; 
Van Cauwenberghe et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2020). 
Their abundance in different environments varies 
depending on the type of human activities in the 
area, seasonality, and environmental factors that 
influence their mobility and transport (Dai et  al., 
2018; Dey et al., 2023; Warrier et al., 2022). MPs 
are often ingested by organisms that mistake them 
for food, and several studies have demonstrated 
their transfer along the food chain (Kataria et  al., 
2024; Saeedi, 2024).
Monitoring microplastic pollution has focused pri-
marily on marine environments, as these are one of the 
main destinations for land-based runoff. However, micro-
plastics are ubiquitous pollutants, and their presence in 
non-marine environments, such as rivers, streams, and 
lagoons is significant. Rivers are recognized as key vec-
tors for transporting microplastics from inland areas to 
coastal zones and, ultimately, to the ocean. However, 
they are not merely passive transporters; they also act as 
deposition sites, with rates varying depending on poly-
mer density and changes in the energy of the river sys-
tem. Interactions between riparian flora and fauna popu-
lations and these contaminants have also been observed 
(He et al., 2021; O’Connor et al., 2022; Owowenu et al., 
2025). Furthermore, lagoon environments have been 
classified as microplastics sinks. However, factors such 
as the buoyancy of polymers, their interaction with ben-
thic and planktonic fauna and vertical mixing processes, 
in which wind, temperature and changes in relevant phys-
icochemical parameters play a significant role, result in 
complex dynamics within these environments; therefore, 
classifying them solely as sinks is an oversimplification 
(Bhutto et al., 2023; Doto et al., 2025; Stride et al., 2024). 
In all cases, the authors attributed the presence of MPs 
to human influence. In this regard, urban wetlands are 
highly vulnerable because they are in constant and close 
contact with human populations that generate waste. This 
can enter these systems through the air, runoff, wastewa-
ter discharge, and the disposal of plastic waste, among 
other means (Lei et al., 2023). In this context, the present 
study is the first to report MPs’ presence in the surface 
waters of the urban wetland “Laguna del Camarón”, 
Sinaloa, Mexico. The main objectives were: 1) to iden-
tify possibles main sources of this type of pollution in the 
system through the morphological and chemical charac-
terization of the microplastics found; and 2) to assess the 
variability in abundance and distribution of microplastics 
in surface waters during two seasons, considering that, 
their release rate from potential sources, as well as the

Materials and methods
Study area and sampling method
The wetland Laguna del Camarón, from now on 
called LC, is located within the limits of the Central 
Park Modernization Project, in the city of Mazatlán, 
Sinaloa, in northwestern Mexico (Fig.  1), and it has 
an area of approximately 5.64  ha. LC maintains a 
permanent underground connection with the sea; 
it is a highly intervened wetland that still provides 
various ecosystem services, such as flood regu-
lation, species refuge (local and migratory), and 
provides landscape, cultural, and tourist benefits. 
However, like many water bodies worldwide, it has 
been vulnerable to anthropogenic impacts. Mazatlán

is one of Mexico’s most prominent tourist destina-
tions in recent years, and the current surge in hotel 
demand has led to large-scale development along its 
coastal zone.
The LC system is directly affected by tourism-
related activities, including hotels, condominiums, 
restaurants, ongoing construction, vehicular traffic, 
and recreational areas. Following the park’s moderni-
zation, LC system was divided into two distinct water 
bodies: (a) the main lagoon, and (b) a smaller internal 
lagoon, situated in the area with the highest visitor 
concentration.
Seven sampling sites were selected along the 
LC system —five in the main lagoon and two in the 
internal lagoon (Fig.  1). Sampling was conducted in 
October 2022 and March 2023, corresponding to the 
rainy and dry seasons, respectively. At each site, three 
replicate samples were collected. All sampling was 
carried out from a small kayak-type vessel. Approxi-
mately, thirty liters of water were collected by repli-
cate at each site, 15–20 cm depth, using a pre-cleaned 
glass container of 2 L. After pouring the samples 
through the sieve, the sampling jar was rinsed with fil-
tered water, and sample plus rinse water were filtered 
through a stainless-steel mesh sieve with a pore size of 
63 µm, which was rinsed with filtered water between 
replicate and sampling site, therefore, only the frac-
tion from 63 to 5000 µm was considered in this work. 
After filtration, the sieve mesh was rinsed with filtered 
triple-distilled water, and the rinse water was collected 
in glass jars. All samples were transported and stored 
at 4 °C in the laboratory until further analysis.
Sample treatment
In the laboratory, all samples were removed from 
refrigeration and placed inside a laminar flow hood, 
where they remained sealed until reaching room tem-
perature. To extract microplastics, a digestion process 
was carried out, as organic matter can interfere with 
microplastic recovery. A volume of 100 mL (1:1 v/v) 
of filtered hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂, 30%) was added 
to each sample. The mixture was stirred at 80  rpm 
and maintained at 60  °C overnight. In this regard, 
Masura et  al. (2015)  and Prata et  al. (2019) have 
shown that 30% peroxide and the application of tem-
perature remove excess organic matter; which facili-
tates the release of microplastics and does not affect 
the polymeric structure of the particles.
After digestion, the sample was decanted and fil-
tered through glass fiber filters (Whatman™ GF/F), 
with a pore size of 0.7  µm, using a vacuum pump 
(Rocker 300). To ensure total particle extraction and 
remove any particles adhering to the flask, these were 
washed with filtered water and then incorporated into 
the filter. The filters were then placed in glass Petri 
dishes and dried at 40 °C for 2 h. Once dried, the fil-
ters were sealed in the Petri dishes and wrapped in 
aluminum foil for storage until further observation.
Observation and morphological characterization of 
microplastics
Each Petri dish was carefully unwrapped under a 
stereomicroscope to isolate the MPs. The filters were 
examined horizontally at a maximum magnification 
of 5X. Potential MPs were isolated onto a new, clean 
paper filter. Each paper filter was folded in half and 
wrapped in aluminum foil for storage until chemical 
analysis. Although handling was minimized, folding 
the filters may have caused particle loss or transfer, 
which could have biased the reported results. During 
the observation phase, the number of potential MPs, 
along with their morphological characteristics—
such as color and shape—were recorded, follow -
ing Hidalgo-Ruz et  al. (2012). MPs retained by the 
stainless-steel mesh (63 µm) were counted and char -
acterized according to their shape, color, and polymer 
composition. Microparticle size measurements and 
classification into size intervals were not included in 
the scope of the present study.
Chemical characterization of microplastics
The polymer identification of MPs was performed 
using Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectros-
copy (Thermo Scientific™ Nicolet™ iN10 MX), 
equipped with a µATR accessory with a germanium 
tip and an MCT detector cooled with liquid nitrogen, 
capable of measuring particles down to 10 µm. Each 
spectrum was acquired in the wavenumber range of 
600–4000  cm⁻1, at a resolution of 4  cm⁻ 1, with 16 
scans per sample. Due to the total number of particles 
isolated as potential microplastics, as well as ana-
lytical and budgetary constraints, it was not feasible 
to identify the polymer composition of 100% of the 
particles. Ten percent of the particles, per site, (200

All spectra were visually inspected and analyzed

coefficient of the µFTIR analysis (< 1%; n = 6).

Statistical data analysis
Differences in the mean concentration of MPs among 
the seven sampling sites (LC1–LC7) and between the 
two climatic seasons (rainy and dry) were evaluated 
by means of a two-way factorial ANOVA, consider -
ing site, season, and their interaction as fixed factors. 
Because microplastic concentration data are typically 
right-skewed and, in this case, displayed heterogene-
ous variances among cells, the values were square-
root  transformed (√x) prior to analysis to reduce 
the skewness of the distribution and to stabilize the 
variance, thereby meeting the assumptions of the 
parametric model. The homogeneity of variances of 
the transformed data was subsequently verified with 
Cochran’s C test for each source of variation (site, 
season, and the site × season interaction). When the 
ANOVA detected significant differences, pairwise 
comparisons of the means were carried out using 
Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test 
for equal sample sizes. The level of significance was 
set at α = 0.05, and exact probabilities are reported 
throughout. All analysis were performed in STATIS-
TICA version 12 (StatSoft Inc., 2014).
Quality control
To ensure the quality of the results and prevent sample 
contamination throughout all steps of the procedure, 
only glass and stainless steel were used, all materials 
employed were pre-washed and cleaned with filtered 
triple-distilled water, wrapped in aluminum foil, and 
stored until use. In addition, the laboratory staff wore 
cotton clothing and lab coats. To avoid airborne con-
tamination, the entire workflow was performed in an 
isolated area and under a flow cabinet. The lab rea-
gents (e.g., water, H2O2) were filtered (0.7-μm pore 
size Whatman™ glass fiber filter grade GF/F) to be 
microplastic-free. All workbenches and surfaces 
were cleaned with isopropyl alcohol. Additionally, 
laboratory controls consisting of filters placed in open 
Petri dishes were distributed throughout the work 
areas to account for possible contamination of the

Results and discussion
Microplastics abundance
A total of 740 MPs particles were found in the surface 
water of the entire LC during the rainy season, with 
an average concentration of 1.17 ± 0.56 particles/L. 
In contrast, significantly higher concentrations were 
observed during the dry season, averaging 2.05 ± 2.41 
particles/L. The minimum, maximum, and mean val-
ues of MPs concentrations at each sampling site are 
shown in Table  1. These values ranged from 0.00 
(LC1) to 3.30 (LC6) particles/L during the rainy sea-
son and from 0.00 (LC4) to 7.63 (LC7) particles/L 
during the dry season. The spatial distribution of MPs 
concentrations in LC, during both climatic seasons, is 
shown in Fig. 2.
After the square-root transformation, Cochran’s 
C test confirmed that the variances were homoge-
neous for all sources of variation (site: C = 0.317, 
critical value = 0.435; season: C = 0.804, critical 
value = 0.999; site × season interaction: C = 0.108, 
critical value = 0.162; p > 0.05 in every case), so the 
assumptions required by the parametric model were 
satisfied and the two-way ANOVA could be applied 
to the transformed data.
Results from the two-way ANOVA revealed sig-
nificant differences in MP concentration among sam-
pling sites (F 6, 28 = 11.97, p < 0.001), between cli-
matic seasons (F 1, 28 = 4.38, p = 0.046), and for the 
site × season interaction (F 6, 28 = 6.44, p < 0.001) 
(Supp. Table  A). The Tukey HSD post hoc test 
showed that this pattern was driven almost entirely 
by the two sites of the inner lagoon (LC6 and LC7) 
during the dry season. Considering the site factor 
(pooled across seasons), LC6 and LC7, which are 
more isolated but connected to the main lagoon, dif-
fered significantly from each of the five sites of the 
main lagoon (LC1–LC5; p ≤ 0.016 in all compari-
sons) but not from each other (p = 0.927), whereas the 
five main-lagoon sites were statistically indistinguish-
able among themselves (p > 0.86 in all comparisons). 
For the season factor, the mean concentration was 
significantly higher in the dry season than in the rainy 
season (p = 0.046).
During the dry season, the mean concentration in 
sites LC6 and LC7 was significantly higher, with the 
mean concentration in site LC7 being the highest. 
The highest concentration, recorded at LC7 during 
the dry season, differed significantly from every other 
site–season combination (p ≤ 0.009) except LC6 dur -
ing the dry season (p = 0.239), whereas LC6 in the

Aquatic vegetation can act as a barrier to microplas-
tics, which can reduce their transport across the water 
body, as indicated by de Los Santos et al. (2021) and 
Martuscelli et al. (2025) in previous studies. This could 
explain why there is a relatively higher abundance of 
microplastics in LC6 and LC7 during the dry season.
It is also important to note that the inner lagoon, 
where sites LC6 and LC7 are located, is very close 
to the area with the most significant visitor influ-
ence, including dining areas, the main outdoor stage 
for artistic events, as well as cycling, skating, and 
athletics paths. This area is a potential source of 
plastic waste and subsequently MPs. High MP con-
centrations have been reported, attributing them to 
recreational activities, tourism, and other activities 
with poor disposal and, consequently, accumulation 
of plastic debris (Bayo et al., 2019; Chandrakanthan 
et al., 2024). Therefore, one might expect greater air -
borne particle deposition; however, further studies in 
this area taking this into account would need to be 
conducted. Lei et  al. (2023) studied MPs contami-
nation in wetlands with different land uses, and they 
observed a direct relation between MPs concentra-
tions and the distance with urban economic centers. 
They mentioned that this is mainly due to increased 
accumulation of plastic waste, vehicle transport 
routes, urbanization level, population density, and 
other human activities.
Furthermore, slightly higher concentrations of 
MPs were observed in the rainy season at sites LC4 
and LC5, which are located in the main freshwa-
ter inflow zone of the main lagoon. This is due to a 
slope that causes greater runoff, carrying out debris 
from nearby streets during meteorological events. At 
the remaining sites, the distribution of MP concentra-
tions was similar in the rainy and dry seasons. While 
several studies have demonstrated the influence of 
surface runoff following meteorological events on 
the accumulation of plastic debris (Wang et al., 2022; 
Cho et  al., 2023; Shafi et  al., 2024), this trend was 
only observed in LC4 and LC5 in this study. Gerolin

Microplastic pollution in wetlands has been docu-
mented worldwide in numerous studies. The concen-
trations found in LC are higher than those reported for 
some Colombian wetlands (Table 2). Garcés-Ordonez 
et al. (2022) investigated microplastic pollution in sur-
face waters and reported concentrations between 0 and 
0.30 particles/L, in a wetland from Colombian Carib-
bean, they found that the highest microplastic pollution 
occurs near urban areas and river mouths (Table  2). 
Another investigation in Colombia reported consist-
ently low concentrations; Porras-Rojas et  al. (2023) 
investigated microplastics in water from Gualí Wet-
land, and they report concentrations between 0.17–0.31 
particles/L, in three sites under the influence of differ-
ent anthropogenic activities.
By contrast, there are studies reporting results of 
an order of magnitude higher than those of the present 
study. Kadir et  al. (2025), in Australia, studied water 
from the Centennial Parklands pond catchment and 
found concentrations ranging from 3.1 to 11.9 particles/L 
(Table 2). Similar results were reported by Zhang et al. 
(2025) in surface waters from an urban wetland in China; 
they report concentrations of 2.4–14.9 particles/L.
Forero-López et al. (2021) analyzed MPs in a highly 
impacted estuarine wetland in Argentina; they collect 
the water samples using a Nansen net of 60 µm, unlike 
the other studies mentioned earlier, and found concen-
trations from 0.182–33.733 particles/L, which is higher 
than all the compared studies.
Microplastic characterization
Color of microplastics
In LC, a wide range of colors was observed in MPs 
particles, as shown in Fig.  3. Predominantly, black, 
brown, blue, white, and yellow were the most com-
mon colors. In contrast, other colors such as trans-
parent, green, red, gray, orange, pink, and purple 
were observed in lower proportions during both the 
rainy and dry seasons. We also found several parti-
cles showing a multicolor pattern, that is, they have 
two or more colors in their structure.

Similar results were reported by Ganesan et  al. 
(2019) in their study on MPs rates and transport in dif-
ferent water types, and by Ronda et al. (2023) in their 
work on the effect of tourism and coastal recreation 
on MPs pollution in beaches of the southern Buenos 
Aires province. The authors found that white, green, 
yellow, brown, blue, and black were the predominant 
colors. The predominance of black and blue microfib-
ers may be related to the deposition of textile micro-
particles from the clothing of visitors frequenting 
LC. Turner et al. (2019) mentioned that the presence 
of black and blue microparticles corresponds to the 
indigo dye used in textile staining, however, this must 
be corroborated using other chemical techniques that 
allow for the identification of textile dyes.
In general, a higher relative abundance of black par-
ticles was observed at all sites during the rainy season 
respect to the dry season. This is the only consistent pat-
tern across all sampling sites in relation to the colours 
detected. Therefore, it can be assumed that the influx of 
black particles is linked to runoff. Several authors have 
indicated that the color of MPs is a parameter influenc-
ing their accumulation in marine organisms, as dark and 
bright-colored MPs can be mistaken for food (Alvarado-
Zambrano et al., 2023; Manikanda et al., 2021).
Today, plastic debris of various colors can be 
found in the environment, however, color alone 
does not provide much information about the origin 
of the microparticles; it is necessary to find a way 
to integrate color and chemical composition data in 
order to identify pollution sources more accurately.
Shape of microplastics
Fibers, foam, fragments, and film were the shapes 
observed among the MPs found in LC during both 
climatic seasons. Fibers accounted for more than 
60%, followed by foam with approximately 20%, 
fragments with around 15%, and film with less than 
5% (Fig.  4). The predominance of fibers, as previ-
ously mentioned, can be associated with the con-
tribution of microfibers originating from visitors’ 
clothing (Ronda et  al., 2023), which can be easily 
transported by wind (Xiao et al., 2023).
At sites LC6 and LC7, the presence of microfibers 
exceeded 75%, which may reflect the high influx of 
visitors to the study area. Meanwhile, the occurrence 
of foam and fragments can be associated with 1) the 
accumulation of single-use plastic waste derived from 
food packaging and recreational activities and 2) the 
intense construction activity of hotels, condominium 
buildings, and family recreational facilities along the 
reservoir’s margins. In the present study, a tendency 
of increase in microparticles with foam and frag-
ments shapes in the main lagoon (from LC5 to LC1) 
was observed (Fig.  4), and further studies should be 
conducted to describe this behavior. It is important to 
mention that construction activities were taking place

Polymeric composition
Overall, in both seasons, polymer identification 
showed the presence of polyester (PES, 48% and 
62%,), polypropylene (PP, 19% and 23%), polyethyl-
ene (PE, 10% and 2%), and polystyrene (PS, 5% and 
5%), which were the most dominant polymer types 
(Fig. 5) in dry and rainy seasons, respectively. Other 
polymers such as polyacrylonitrile (PAN), polyvi-
nyl chloride (PVC), phenolic resin (PR), alkyd resin 
(AR), acrylic, and polydialyl phthalate (DAP) were 
also detected in low proportions (≤ 5%). Natural pol-
ymers were also identified in surface water, notably 
cotton (32 particles across both seasons) and cellu-
lose (29 particles across both seasons); these counts 
were not included in the frequency calculations or the 
comparison with other investigations.
Similar results were observed by Suteja et  al. 
(2021), in their study on the temporal and spatial 
distribution of MPs in the waters of Benoa Bay, 
Indonesia, and by Rios-Mendoza et  al. (2021) in 
surface water from Mazatlán Bay, Mexico, both of 
which highlighted PE, PP, and PS as the dominant 
polymers.
LC is a recreational and leisure area surrounded by 
new real estate developments; therefore, the origin of 
the identified polymers may stem from the accumu-
lation and improper management of waste generated 
by activities carried out within the park, in addition 
to shedding synthetic fibers from polyester and cot-
ton from the sports and everyday clothing worn by 
visitors.
The textile industry primarily uses synthetic fib-
ers of PES, or polyethylene terephthalate (PET), and 
natural polymers such as cotton in garment produc-
tion. De Falco et al. (2020) report that direct release

PE is the most used plastic polymer (Qiang

total plastic polymers’ demand (Plastics Europe,

bags, bottles, bottle caps, and toys; once in the 
environment, the wind, rain, and solar UV break -
down the exposed debris to microplastics (Qiang 
et al., 2023). Meanwhile, PP and PS are polymers 
commonly used in the production of utensils such 
as spoons and plates, product packaging, food stor -
age, and construction materials.
Pollution load index
The pollution load index calculated for each site var -
ied from 1.12 to 2.30 in the rainy season, and from 
0.94 to 4.01 in the dry season. During both seasons, 
sites LC6 and LC7showed the highest values. In 
rainy seasons LC6 and LC7 showed values of 2.30 
and 1.73, classifying them as moderately polluted 
and unpolluted to moderately polluted, respectively; 
in the dry season LC6 and LC7 had 2.92 and 4.01 
values, classifying them as moderately polluted and 
highly polluted, respectively. Sites LC6 and LC7 
were initially identified as potential hotspots for 
microplastic pollution, which was later confirmed 
by statistical analysis, as they are located in the area 
most exposed to recreational activities, as described 
in Section "Microplastics abundance". In terms of the 
entire zone, for both seasons, LC can be classified as 
unpolluted to moderately polluted.
These results should be considered by local author-
ities or managers at these sites to strengthen regula-
tions and increase visitor surveillance.
Limitations
It should be noted that this study includes only sur -
face water measurements; sediments and organisms 
were not sampled, and quantitative source apportion-
ment was not performed. The polymeric analysis was 
performed in 10% of the particles. Therefore, our 
results should be interpreted as a baseline assess-
ment of microplastic contamination and the potential 
sources of this pollutant in this ecosystem.
Conclusions
The spatial and temporal distribution of MPs in the 
surface waters of Laguna del Camarón was evalu-
ated. The input of microplastics to this water body 
may be a result of different processes, such as the 
aerial deposition during the dry season due to the 
proximity of LC to roads, hotels, and residential

particles/L); this seasonal difference may reflect 
higher recreational use and reduced dilution effect 
during the dry season. The highest concentrations 
of MPs were observed in the inner lagoon during 
both climatic seasons, identifying it as a hotspot of 
MPs pollution. This distribution is likely influenced 
by the proximity of the wetland to waste accumu-
lation areas resulting from recreational activities 
within the Central Park and the plant barrier (Najas 
marina), that isolates it from the main lagoon. The 
shape of the MPs indicated the presence of micro-
fibers (> 60%), likely originating from visitors’ 
clothing, as well as fragments and foam, which may 
be linked to nearby construction activities. The pre-
dominant polymers identified were PES, PP, PE, 
and PS—materials widely used in textiles, bottle 
caps, food containers, packaging, and storage. Tak -
ing together, the spatial patterns and chemical anal-
ysis of the polymers suggest that visitor activities 
and textile derived microfibers are major contribu-
tors to the pollution status of the wetland.
This study constitutes the first assessment of MPs 
in this area and provides comparable data to stud-
ies conducted in similar environments. The find-
ings underscore the urgent need for implementing 
effective waste management (improve onsite waste 
collection and the placement of bins and informa-
tive signs) and control strategies across all human 
activities to reduce plastic pollution in aquatic 
ecosystems, which contribute to the formation of 
microplastics.
