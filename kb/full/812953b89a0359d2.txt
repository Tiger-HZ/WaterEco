Abstract
Safe drinking water remains essential for public health, particularly in developing 
regions where sachet water is widely consumed. This study assessed the 
physicochemical properties, bacteriological quality, antibiotic resistance, heavy 
metal contamination, and potential health risks of 8) selected sachet water samples. 
Physicochemical parameters were measured using APHA standard methods. Bacterial 
counts were determined by serial dilution and plate count techniques. Escherichia 
coli isolates were identified through colonial morphology, Gram staining, and 
biochemical tests. Antibiotic susceptibility was assessed by Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion, 
while MAR index, heavy metals, correlations, CF, HPI, and HRI were calculated. 
Physicochemical parameters showed moderate variation, with pH ranging from 
5.80 to 7.37, EC (0.20–1.20 mS/cm), TDS (100.00–600.00 ppm), turbidity (0.90–1.90 
NTU), and DO (6.10–7.20 mg/L). Organic pollution indicators were highest in W3 
(BOD: 1.80 mg/L, COD: 8.50 mg/L, TOD: 11.50 mg/L). Total heterotrophic counts 
ranged from 0.00 to 110.00 CFU/ml, exceeding permissible limits, while coliform 
counts peaked at 6.00 CFU/ml. Isolates identified as Escherichia coli exhibited high 
susceptibility to ofloxacin (26.00–34.00 mm) but complete resistance (0.00 mm) to 
several antibiotics. MAR indices ranged from 0.63 to 0.75, indicating high resistance. 
Heavy metals were within permissible limits, with Pb up to 0.006 ppm and Fe up to 
0.160 ppm. Risk indices confirmed low contamination (CF < 1; HPI: 34.65–52.15; HRI: 
0.159–0.220). Despite acceptable chemical quality, bacteria contamination and multi-
drug resistance pose significant public health concerns.

 1 Introduction
The consumption of safe drinking water is essential for maintaining public health and 
preventing the spread of waterborne diseases. In many developing countries, including 
Nigeria, packaged drinking water such as sachet water has become a widely consumed 
alternative to municipal water supplies due to its affordability and accessibility [ 1, 2]. 
Sachet water, often referred to as “pure water, ” is commonly sold by street vendors and 
small-scale producers across urban and rural communities [ 3]. However, concerns have 
been raised regarding the microbiological and physicochemical quality of these prod -
ucts, particularly where production and distribution are not adequately monitored by 
regulatory authorities such as the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration 
and Control [4].
One of the major bacterial contaminants associated with drinking water is Escherichia 
coli, which belongs to the family Enterobacteriaceae, a Gram-negative bacterium com -
monly found in the intestinal tract of humans and warm-blooded animals [ 5, 6]. The 
presence of this organism in drinking water is widely used as an indicator of faecal con -
tamination and poor sanitary conditions during water processing, storage, or handling 
[7]. Contamination of sachet water with Escherichia coli may occur through untreated 
water sources, unhygienic production practices, or improper storage conditions [ 8, 9]. 
Consumption of water contaminated with pathogenic strains of Escherichia coli can lead 
to gastrointestinal infections, diarrhea, urinary tract infections, and other serious health 
complications [10].
In recent years, the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria in environmen -
tal and drinking water sources has become a major global public health concern [11–13]. 
Multidrug resistance occurs when bacterial strains develop resistance to multiple classes 
of antibiotics, thereby reducing the effectiveness of commonly used antimicrobial thera -
pies [14]. Among waterborne bacteria, antibiotic-resistant Escherichia coli is particularly 
significant because it serves as both an indicator of faecal contamination and a reser -
voir of antimicrobial resistance genes that can be transferred to other microorganisms 
through horizontal gene transfer [ 15]. Environmental water systems have increasingly 
been recognized as important reservoirs of the environmental resistome, facilitating 
the persistence, evolution, and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance genes among 
bacterial populations. The continuous exposure of environmental bacteria to antibiotic 
residues, heavy metals, and other pollutants may promote co-selection mechanisms that 
enhance the spread of resistance determinants, thereby contributing significantly to the 
global antimicrobial resistance crisis recognized by the World Health Organization [16].
The contamination of drinking water with MDR Escherichia coli poses substantial 
health risks, particularly in developing countries where sanitation infrastructure, water 
treatment systems, and regulatory oversight may be inadequate [ 17]. In Nigeria, sachet 
water has become one of the most accessible and affordable sources of potable water 
due to deficiencies in public water supply systems [18]. Although sachet water consump-
tion has improved access to drinking water, concerns remain regarding bacterial con -
tamination, physicochemical quality, and the presence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria 
in commercially available products [ 19]. Previous studies in Nigeria have documented 
variations in physicochemical and bacteriological water quality across different loca -
tions and seasons. Studies conducted in Awka, southeastern Nigeria, reported signifi -
cant fluctuations in bacterial loads and physicochemical parameters in drinking water

sources, emphasizing the need for continuous surveillance and quality assessment [ 20, 
21]. Despite these findings, there remains a paucity of data regarding the occurrence and 
antibiotic resistance profiles of Escherichia coli isolated from sachet water sold in Ago-
Iwoye and Oru communities of Ogun State, southwestern Nigeria. This knowledge gap 
limits effective risk assessment and evidence-based interventions aimed at protecting 
public health in these rapidly growing communities.
Beyond bacterial contamination, water quality assessments are increasingly being 
framed within the broader context of both legacy pollutants and emerging contami -
nants. While traditional concerns such as heavy metals remain important, recent stud -
ies have highlighted the growing threats posed by contaminant mixtures including 
microplastics, pharmaceutical residues, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), and 
other emerging pollutants in groundwater and drinking water systems worldwide [ 22, 
23]. Recent reviews have emphasized that heavy metal contamination remains a side -
lined but persistent environmental challenge despite the increasing attention given to 
emerging contaminants [ 24, 25]. Furthermore, groundwater systems are increasingly 
threatened by complex contaminant cocktails involving microplastics, chemical pollut -
ants, and resistant microorganisms that may interact synergistically to affect ecosystem 
and human health [ 24]. Emerging contaminants such as PFAS have also been identified 
as persistent pollutants capable of compromising groundwater quality and posing long-
term health risks even at low concentrations [26].
The presence of heavy metals and other physicochemical contaminants in drinking 
water may further exacerbate environmental and human health risks. Heavy metals 
such as lead, cadmium, nickel, and iron can enter water systems through natural geo -
logical weathering processes and anthropogenic activities including agricultural runoff, 
industrial discharge, and improper waste disposal [ 27]. Chronic exposure to elevated 
concentrations of these metals has been associated with neurological disorders, kid -
ney dysfunction, developmental abnormalities, carcinogenic effects, and other adverse 
health outcomes [ 28]. Moreover, increasing evidence suggests that heavy metals may 
contribute to the selection and maintenance of antibiotic resistance genes in environ -
mental microbial communities through co-selection mechanisms, thereby linking chem-
ical pollution with antimicrobial resistance development [29].
Given the widespread consumption of sachet water in Ago-Iwoye and Oru and the 
limited information available on its bacteriological safety, physicochemical quality, heavy 
metal burden, and antibiotic resistance characteristics, this study was designed to evalu -
ate the occurrence and multidrug resistance patterns of Escherichia coli isolates recov -
ered from selected sachet water brands. The findings will contribute to filling existing 
knowledge gaps regarding drinking water quality in southwestern Nigeria and provide 
baseline data for public health monitoring, regulatory enforcement, and sustainable 
water resource management.
Risk assessment approaches are widely used to evaluate the potential health implica -
tions of contaminated water [ 30]. These approaches include the determination of con -
tamination indices, heavy metal pollution indices, and health risk indices, which help 
quantify the level of exposure and potential toxicity associated with environmental con -
taminants [31]. When combined with antibiotic susceptibility testing, these assessments 
provide valuable insight into the potential public health threats associated with contami-
nated drinking water, particularly in communities that rely heavily on packaged water

2 Materials and methods
2.1 Study area
This study was conducted in Ago-Iwoye, Oru, and Awa communities located within 
Ijebu North Local Government Area of Ogun State, southwestern Nigeria. Ogun State 
lies within the tropical rainforest zone and experiences distinct wet and dry seasons, 
which may influence water quality characteristics. Ago-Iwoye is a rapidly urbanizing 
university town that hosts a large transient and resident population due to the presence 
of Olabisi Onabanjo University, commercial establishments, and growing residential 
developments [33]. Oru is a neighbouring semi-urban community characterized by resi -
dential settlements, markets, educational institutions, and commercial activities [33, 34]. 
Both communities depend heavily on commercially packaged sachet water as a major 
source of drinking water due to increasing population growth and variable access to 
public water supplies. The widespread consumption of sachet water in these commu -
nities makes them suitable locations for assessing physicochemical quality, heavy metal 
contamination, bacteriological status, and antimicrobial resistance patterns associated 
with packaged drinking water.
2.2 Sample collection
A preliminary market survey was conducted to identify the sachet water brands most 
commonly available and consumed within Ago-Iwoye and Oru communities. Based 
on the frequency of availability across major distribution points, 8 sachet water brands 
were selected for analysis (Fig.  1). The selected brands represented the most frequently 
encountered products among vendors, retail shops, and street hawkers at the time of 
sampling and were considered representative of the dominant brands consumed by resi-
dents in the study area. Sachet water samples were purchased in their original sealed 
condition to avoid external contamination in February, 2026. Each sample was care -
fully labelled according to location and brand before transportation to the laboratory. 
Samples were transported in sterile containers using ice-packed coolers and analysed 
within 24 h of collection to preserve sample integrity and ensure reliable bacteriological 
results. Standard aseptic procedures were maintained throughout sample handling and 
processing.
2.3 Study limitation
Although several sachet water brands are distributed within Ago-Iwoye and Oru, only 
eight widely available brands were included in this study due to logistical and resource 
constraints. Consequently, the findings may not fully represent the quality characteris -
tics of all sachet water brands marketed within the study area.
2.4 Physicochemical analysis of sachet water samples
The physicochemical parameters of the water samples were determined using stan -
dard procedures recommended by [ 35, 36]. Colour, odour, and taste were assessed

organoleptically by visual observation and sensory evaluation immediately after sam -
ple collection. The pH and temperature were measured in situ using a digital pH meter 
(Hanna Instruments HI98107 pH Meter, Hanna Instruments, Romania). Electrical con -
ductivity (EC) and total dissolved solids (TDS) were determined using a conductivity/
TDS meter (Hanna Instruments HI98303 DiST 3 Conductivity/TDS Meter, Hanna 
Instruments, Romania). Turbidity was measured using a turbidity meter (Hach 2100Q 
Portable Turbidimeter, Hach Company, USA). Dissolved oxygen (DO) was determined 
using a portable DO meter (Hanna Instruments HI9146 Dissolved Oxygen Meter, Hanna 
Instruments, Romania). Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) concentration was determined using a 
spectrophotometric method with a UV–Visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800 
Spectrophotometer, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan). All measurements were conducted 
in triplicate to ensure accuracy and reliability of the results.
2.4.1 Determination of biochemical oxygen demand (BOD)
Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD₅) was determined using the standard 5-day incu -
bation method according to the procedures described by the American Public Health 
Association [37, 38] Water samples were collected in sterile BOD bottles and analyzed 
immediately to prevent changes in oxygen concentration. The initial dissolved oxygen 
(DO₁) concentration of each sample was measured using a calibrated dissolved oxy -
gen meter (Hanna Instruments HI9146 Dissolved Oxygen Meter, Hanna Instruments, 
Romania). Where necessary, samples were diluted with oxygen-saturated dilution water 
containing phosphate buffer, magnesium sulfate, calcium chloride, and ferric chloride 
solutions to provide suitable conditions for microbial activity. The bottles were then 
incubated in the dark at 20 ± 1 °C for five days using a BOD incubator (Memmert BE500 
BOD Incubator, Memmert GmbH + Co. KG, Germany) to prevent photosynthetic oxy -
gen production. After incubation, the final dissolved oxygen concentration (DO₅) was

2.4.2 Determination of chemical oxygen demand (COD)
Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD) was determined using the closed reflux colorimet -
ric method following Standard Methods for the Examination of Water and Wastewater 
[38]. Approximately 2 mL of homogenized water sample was transferred into a COD 
digestion vial containing a standardized acidic potassium dichromate digestion solution, 
silver sulfate catalyst, and mercuric sulfate for chloride interference suppression. The 
vials were sealed and digested at 150 °C for two hours in a COD reactor (Hach DRB200 
COD Reactor, Hach Company, USA). During digestion, oxidizable organic compounds 
present in the sample were chemically oxidized by potassium dichromate under strongly 
acidic conditions. Following digestion, the vials were allowed to cool to room tempera -
ture and analyzed calorimetrically using a spectrophotometer according to the manu -
facturer’s instructions. COD concentrations were determined by comparing absorbance 
values against calibration standards prepared from potassium hydrogen phthalate solu -
tions. Results were expressed as mg O₂/L. COD represents the total oxygen equiva -
lent required for the chemical oxidation of both biodegradable and non-biodegradable 
organic matter present in water. All analyses were performed in triplicate to ensure 
accuracy and reliability [37, 40].
2.4.3 Determination of total oxygen demand (TOD)
Total Oxygen Demand (TOD) was determined using a Hach BioTector B3500 Analyzer 
(Hach Company, USA) based on the catalytic combustion oxidation principle. Prior 
to analysis, water samples were homogenized and filtered where necessary to remove 
large suspended particles that could interfere with the analytical system. A measured 
volume of sample was automatically introduced into the analyzer, where all oxidizable 
substances, including organic and inorganic compounds, were subjected to high-tem -
perature catalytic oxidation in the presence of oxygen. During oxidation, the analyzer 
continuously measured the amount of oxygen consumed by the sample. The differ -
ence between the oxygen concentration entering and leaving the reaction chamber was 
used to calculate the total oxygen demand. Results were automatically generated by the 
instrument and expressed as mg O₂/L. Unlike BOD and COD, which estimate biode -
gradable and chemically oxidizable fractions respectively, TOD measures the total oxy -
gen requirement for complete oxidation of all oxidizable constituents in a sample [41].
2.5 Bacteriological analysis of water samples using plate count agar (PCA) and eosin 
methylene blue agar (EMB)
The bacteriological quality of the water samples was determined using the standard plate 
count method [ 42]. Prior to analysis, all culture media, glassware, and equipment were 
sterilised using an autoclave at 121 °C for 15 min. For the total heterotrophic bacterial 
count, Plate Count Agar (PCA) and Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) agar were used as the 
culture medium. Approximately 1 mL of the direct water sample was aseptically trans -
ferred into sterile Petri dishes, after which molten PCA and EMB agar were poured into 
the plates using the pour plate technique. The plates were gently swirled to ensure even 
distribution of the inoculum and allowed to solidify. The inoculated plates were then

Samson et al. Discover Chemistry (2026) 3:523 
incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h. After incubation, visible bacterial colonies were counted 
and recorded. The number of colonies observed was expressed as colony-forming units 
per milliliter (CFU/ml) of the water sample. On EMB agar, Escherichia coli typically pro-
duces colonies with a metallic green sheen, while other coliform bacteria may produce 
pink or purple colonies. These colonial characteristics helped in the preliminary identifi-
cation of potential coliform organisms present in the water samples [43].
2.6 Gram staining and biochemical identification of bacteria
Bacterial isolates obtained from the culture plates were further identified using Gram 
staining and biochemical tests. Gram staining was performed to determine the Gram 
reaction and morphological characteristics of the bacterial cells. A smear of each bac -
terial isolate was prepared on a clean glass slide and heat-fixed. The smear was then 
stained using crystal violet, followed by the addition of Gram’s iodine, which acted as 
a mordant. The slide was then decolorized using alcohol and counterstained with saf -
ranin. The stained slides were examined under a light microscope using oil immersion 
(100× objective lens) to observe the cellular morphology and Gram reaction. Gram-pos -
itive bacteria appeared purple, while Gram-negative bacteria appeared pink or red [ 44]. 
Following Gram staining, further identification of the bacterial isolates was carried out 
using standard biochemical tests. These included the catalase test, oxidase test, indole 
test, citrate utilization test, methyl red test, Voges–Proskauer test, and sugar fermen -
tation tests. Each test was performed according to standard bacteriological procedures 
[45].
2.7 Antibiotic susceptibility test of Escherichia coli isolates
The antibiotic susceptibility profiles of the Escherichia coli isolates recovered from sachet 
water samples were determined using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method on Muel -
ler–Hinton agar following the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Insti -
tute [46, 47]. Pure bacterial isolates were subcultured on nutrient agar and incubated at 
37 °C for 18–24 h to obtain fresh cultures. A bacterial suspension was prepared in sterile 
normal saline and adjusted to the turbidity of a 0.5 McFarland standard (approximately 
1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL). Sterile cotton swabs were used to evenly inoculate the surface of 
Mueller–Hinton agar plates. Commercial antibiotic discs (Oxoid, UK) comprising cip -
rofloxacin (5 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), amoxicillin–clavulanic acid 
(20/10 µg), azithromycin (15 µg), cefuroxime (30 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), and van -
comycin (30 µg) were aseptically placed on the inoculated agar surface. The plates were 
incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 18–24 h, after which the diameters of inhibition zones 
were measured in millimetres using a transparent ruler. The inhibition zone diameters 
were interpreted according to the breakpoint criteria of Clinical and Laboratory Stan -
dards Institute [46]. Isolates tested against ciprofloxacin (5 µg) were classified as suscep -
tible at inhibition zones ≥ 26.00 mm, intermediate at 22.00–25.00 mm, and resistant at ≤ 
21 mm. For gentamicin (10 µg), susceptibility, intermediate susceptibility, and resistance 
were defined as ≥ 15.00 mm, 13.00–14.00 mm, and ≤ 12.00 mm, respectively. Ceftriax -
one (30 µg) results were interpreted as susceptible at ≥ 26.00 mm, intermediate at 23.00–
25.00 mm, and resistant at ≤ 22.00 mm. For amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (20/10 µg), 
inhibition zones of ≥ 18 mm were considered susceptible, 14.00–17.00 mm intermediate, 
and ≤ 13.00 mm resistant. Cefuroxime (30 µg) susceptibility was defined as ≥ 23.00 mm,

intermediate susceptibility as 15.00–22.00 mm, and resistance as ≤ 14.00 mm. Although 
azithromycin (15 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), and vancomycin (30 µg) were included in 
the antibiotic panel [ 46], does not provide routine interpretive criteria for erythromy -
cin and vancomycin against Escherichia coli because members of the Enterobacterales 
exhibit intrinsic resistance to these agents. Similarly, azithromycin breakpoints are lim -
ited to specific clinical applications and selected Enterobacterales species. Therefore, the 
zone diameters obtained for these antibiotics were recorded and reported for compara -

2.8 Heavy metal analysis using atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS)
Heavy metal concentrations in the water samples are Cu, Zn, Pb, Ni, Co, Fe, and Cd were 
determined using Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometry following standard methods 
[48]. Water samples were first acid-digested with concentrated nitric acid and filtered 
prior to analysis. Quantification of the metals was carried out using a Buck Scientific 
Model 210 VGP Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer manufactured by Buck Scien -
tific, USA. The instrument was calibrated with appropriate standard solutions prepared 
for each metal, and absorbance readings were taken at their respective wavelengths. 
Metal concentrations were expressed in parts per million (ppm), and all analyses were 
performed in triplicate to ensure accuracy and reproducibility of the results.
2.9 Potential risk assessment analysis (PRA)
Potential Risk Assessment (PRA) was determined using Contamination Factor (CF), 
Heavy Metal Pollution Index (HPI), and Health Risk Index (HRI) [49]. After heavy metal 
concentrations in water samples were determined and evaluated against World Health 
Organization (WHO) permissible standards, the Contamination Factor (CF) was cal -
culated for each metal at every sampling site using the formula CF = C ₘ/Cₛ, where C ₘ 
represents the measured metal concentration and C ₛ denotes the WHO standard value. 
This index quantifies the degree to which individual metal concentrations deviate from 
acceptable limits, with values classified as low (CF < 1), moderate (1–3), considerable 
(3–6), or very high (≥ 6).
The Heavy Metal Pollution Index (HPI) was computed to provide an aggregate mea -
sure of overall water quality with respect to multiple metals simultaneously. Unit weight-
ages (Wi) were assigned to each metal as the inverse of its WHO standard, normalised so 
that the sum of all weightages equalled unity. The HPI was then derived as HPI = Σ(Wi × 
Ci/Si) × 100, with values below 100 indicating low pollution and values at or above 100 
signifying high pollution.
The Health Risk Index (HRI) was employed to assess potential non-carcinogenic risk 
to adult consumers (Table  1). HRI was calculated as HRI = (C × IR × EF × ED) / (BW 
× AT × RfD), assuming a daily water intake of 2 L, body weight of 70 kg, exposure fre -
quency of 365 days per year, exposure duration of 70 years, and an averaging time of 
25,550 days. Reference dose (RfD) values were sourced from established toxicological 
databases. An HRI below 1.0 indicates acceptable risk, while values at or exceeding 1.0 
denote potential health concern.

2.10 Statistical analysis
Data obtained from physicochemical, bacteriological, heavy metal, and antibiotic sus -
ceptibility analyses were subjected to statistical analysis using IBM SPSS Statistics ver -
sion 24. Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way 
analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range post hoc test was 
used to determine significant differences among the sampling locations, with statistical 
significance set at p < 0.05. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate rela -
tionships among physicochemical and heavy metal parameters. Contamination Factor 
(CF), Heavy Metal Pollution Index (HPI), Health Risk Index (HRI), and Multiple Anti -
biotic Resistance (MAR) index were calculated using established equations to assess 
contamination levels, potential health risks, and antibiotic resistance patterns of the bac-
terial isolates.

3.2 Discussion
The present study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the physicochemical quality, 
bacteriological status, antibiotic resistance profile, and heavy metal risk of sachet water, 
revealing important insights into its safety for human consumption within the commu -
nities concerned. Sachet water remains one of the most widely consumed sources of 
drinking water in many developing countries because of its affordability and accessibility, 
especially in urban and peri-urban communities [ 8]. However, concerns regarding pro -
duction standards, storage conditions, bacterial contamination, and environmental pol -
lutants have continued to raise questions about its public health safety [51]. The findings 
of this study therefore contribute valuable evidence on the bacteriological and chemi -
cal integrity of sachet water sold within the study area, while also highlighting emerg -
ing antibiotic resistance concerns associated with contaminated drinking water sources. 
Similar broad concerns on packaged drinking water quality have been discussed by [29].
In this current study, the physicochemical characteristics of the sachet water samples 
indicate generally acceptable water quality with slight spatial variability. The pH range of 
5.80–7.37 suggests mildly acidic to neutral conditions and falls close to the acceptable 
guideline range for potable water. Samples W2 and W5 were near neutrality, indicating 
better buffering capacity and lower acidity than W1. These findings are comparable to 
those reported by [ 29], who observed similar pH fluctuations in packaged water brands 
sold within the Samaru community, Northwest Nigeria. The relatively low electrical con-

minimal ionic and mineral content, which is characteristic of treated or filtered drinking 
water. Such low dissolved solids may indicate effective purification processes, including 
reverse osmosis or demineralization steps often used in sachet water production [ 52]. 
Comparable observations were documented by [53] in treated drinking water systems.

Bacteriological analysis revealed variable contamination levels among the sachet water 
samples. Total heterotrophic bacterial counts ranged from 0.00 to 110.00 CFU/ml, with 
SOA-5 exceeding the recommended permissible limit of 100 CFU/mL. Elevated hetero -
trophic counts in packaged water often indicate inadequate treatment, poor sanitation 
during processing, contaminated filling machines, or unhygienic storage environments. 
Similar trends have been reported in sachet water studies across Nigeria, supporting the 
findings of [ 9]. The presence of coliform bacteria, with counts reaching 6.00 CFU/mL, 
further suggests possible faecal contamination or post-treatment contamination. Coli -
form detection in drinking water is a key sanitary indicator because these organisms are 
associated with contamination pathways that may also introduce enteric pathogens [56]. 
This aligns with warnings by the World Health Organization on the risks associated with 
inadequately treated drinking water [57].
The predominance of Escherichia coli based on colonial morphology and biochemi -
cal characterization is particularly significant because Escherichia coli is a recognized 
indicator of faecal pollution. All isolates were Gram-negative rods, positive for indole, 
methyl red, catalase, and motility, while negative for citrate, urease, oxidase, and Voges–
Proskauer reactions, consistent with standard identification features of Escherichia coli. 
Similar findings have been documented by [ 58, 59]. The detection of Escherichia coli in 
sachet water indicates serious sanitary lapses, as potable water should ideally be free of 
faecal coliforms. Even low-level contamination can create a pathway for gastrointestinal 
infections, especially among immunocompromised individuals, children, and the elderly 
[17].
The antibiotic susceptibility profile highlights a concerning resistance pattern among 
the recovered Escherichia coli isolates. High susceptibility to ofloxacin (26.00–34.00 
mm) and cefoperazone (30.00–36.00 mm) indicates that these antibiotics remain effec -
tive treatment options against the tested isolates. Nitrofurantoin also showed selective 
activity in some isolates, particularly those with inhibition zones of 34.00 mm. These 
findings are consistent with studies by [ 60, 61]. However, widespread resistance was 
observed against augmentin, ampicillin, ceftazidime, cefuroxime, and gentamicin. This 
pattern reflects growing antimicrobial pressure in the environment, where misuse or 
overuse of antibiotics in human medicine, livestock production, and improper waste 
disposal may select for resistant strains. The observations also align with reports by 
[62] who linked cephalosporin use with increasing extended-spectrum beta-lactamase 
prevalence.
Comparable multidrug resistance trends have been reported in environmental Esch-
erichia coli isolates, suggesting persistent selective pressure from antibiotic misuse. The 
Multiple Antibiotic Resistance (MAR) index values of 0.63–0.75 far exceeded the thresh-
old of 0.2, indicating that these isolates originated from high-risk environments where 
antibiotics are frequently encountered. Such environments may include sewage systems, 
hospital effluents, animal waste runoff, and polluted surface waters. Similar conclusions

were drawn by the [63], as well as recent studies by [ 64, 65]. The presence of multidrug-
resistant Escherichia coli in consumable water is a major public health concern because 
it limits treatment options if infections occur and may facilitate horizontal transfer of 
resistance genes to other pathogens [66].
This study also reported heavy metal analysis that revealed low concentrations of all 
tested metals, including copper, zinc, lead, nickel, cobalt, iron, and cadmium, with values 
below recommended safety limits. This contrasts with the findings of [ 67] who reported 
excessive heavy metal contamination in groundwater. The strong positive correla -
tions among BOD, COD, and TOD ( r = 0.99–1.00) indicate common organic pollution 
sources, while negative correlations between dissolved oxygen and some metals suggest 
oxidative or redox processes influencing water chemistry. Contamination factor values 
below 1, heavy metal pollution index values of 34.65–52.15, and health risk index values 
of 0.159–0.220 confirm minimal ecological and human health risks from metal exposure 
through sachet water consumption.
Nevertheless, lead contributed the greatest share of the risk burden despite its low 
concentration because of its low reference dose and known toxicity, as also emphasized 
by [68, 69]. The findings suggest that while the chemical quality of the sachet water was 
largely acceptable, microbiological contamination and antibiotic resistance remain sig -
nificant public health concerns. Regulatory agencies and producers should therefore 
strengthen hygienic production practices, routine microbiological screening, proper 
storage systems, and antibacterial resistance surveillance to ensure safer sachet water for 
consumers.
4 Conclusion
This study demonstrated that the examined sachet water samples were generally within 
acceptable physicochemical and heavy metal quality limits, although slight variations 
existed among sampling locations. Parameters such as pH, turbidity, dissolved oxygen, 
conductivity, and total dissolved solids indicated relatively safe water quality, while cop -
per, zinc, lead, nickel, cobalt, iron, and cadmium concentrations remained below recom-
mended guideline values. The contamination factor (CF), heavy metal pollution index 
(HPI), and health risk index (HRI) further confirmed low contamination status and 
negligible non-carcinogenic risk to adult consumers. However, bacteriological assess -
ment revealed notable concerns. Some samples exceeded recommended heterotrophic 
bacterial counts, and coliform contamination was detected in selected sachet water 
brands, indicating lapses in hygiene, treatment efficiency, or post-production handling. 
Morphological and biochemical characterisation identified the isolates as Escherichia 
coli, confirming faecal contamination and potential public health implications. Antibi -
otic susceptibility testing showed high sensitivity to ofloxacin and moderate activity of 
cefoperazone and nitrofurantoin, whereas widespread resistance was observed against 
augmentin, ampicillin, ceftazidime, cefuroxime, and gentamicin. Elevated MAR indi -
ces (0.63–0.75) indicated exposure of the isolates to high-risk environments with fre -
quent antibiotic pressure. Although chemical safety was satisfactory, microbiological 
contamination and multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli pose significant health risks. 
Continuous regulatory monitoring, improved production hygiene, and antimicrobial 
resistance surveillance are therefore essential to safeguard public health. Future studies 
will employ membrane filtration, API 20E and molecular identification techniques. It

of brands to improve representativeness and strengthen risk assessment.
