 A B S T R A C T 
Traditional preservation of raw hides and skins typically relies on high concentrations of 
sodium chloride (NaCl), which contributes to increased salinity and pollution in tannery 
effluents. This study explores a more sustainable alternative by incorporati ng Justicia 
gendarussa leaf powder with a significantly reduced amount of salt. Initial trials using 
varying proportions of the leaf powder (5% to 20%) identified the 15% leaf powder with 
10% salt formulation as the most effective based on physical conditi on and organoleptic 
evaluation. This optimized combination was applied in a 30 -day preservation trial and 
compared against the standard method using 40% salt. The experimental skins remained 
stable throughout the preservation period, exhibiting no odor, ha ir slip, or microbial 
damage, and retained their softness and flexibility. Analytical results—including moisture 
content, shrinkage temperature, bacterial count, FTIR spectroscopy, SEM and DSC 
thermograms—confirmed effective collagen preservation and structural stability. Notably, 
the soaking liquor from the experimental method showed a substantial reduction in 
pollution indicators, with TDS, electrical conductivity, and NaCl levels significantly lower 
than the conventional process. Mechanical testing of the resulting leather showed that the 
strength and performance characteristics fulfilled or surpassed the standard criteria for 
shoe upper leather. These findings support the potential of Justicia gendarussa as a viable 
and eco -friendly approach for raw hide preservation, contributing to reduced 
environmental load and aligning with key sustainable development objectives (SDGs 6 and 
12).

INTRODUCTION 
 
The leather industry stands as one of the oldest and most 
traditional sectors globally, primarily dependent on the 
processing of animal hides and skins [1]. This transformation 
of raw hides/skins into leather involves a sequence of highly 
resource-intensive operations, relying on raw animal hides, a 
wide array of chemicals, skilled labor, and complex 
machinery [2]. The principal raw material, animal hide or 
skin, is a by -product of the meat processing sector, ensuring 
a continuous supply stream as long as the meat indu stry 
persists [3]. Despite its substantial contribution to national 
and international markets, the leather industry is recognized 
for generating significant environmental pollution, especially 
from centuries-old tanning practices [4]. In response, modern 
tanneries have emerged with enhanced technological 
capabilities aimed at better resource utilization and 
minimizing environmental harm. 
As animals are usually slaughtered in abattoirs separate from 
tanneries, rapid transportation and proper preservation of 
hides/skins are crucial [2]. The intrinsic nature of rawhide—

comprising approximately 65% moisture, 33% proteins, 
0.5% minerals, and 2 –6% lipids —renders it highly 
susceptible to bacterial degradation [3]. Studies have shown 
that putrefaction can begin within just 5 to 6 hours post -
mortem if preventive measures are not applied, and within 8–
12 hours, bacteria can penetrate the corium, the functional 
collagen layer of the skin [5, 6] . Hence, preservation is an 
essential pre-tanning process that directly influences the final 
leather quality. Traditionally, preservation is achieved 
through salting using sodium chloride (40 –50% w/w), 
primarily due to its low cost and ready avail ability [3]. 
However, this method poses serious environmental 
challenges by contributing significantly to the pollution load 
in tannery efflue nt, particularly in the form of COD, Total 
Dissolved Solids (TDS) and chlorides [7]. Excessive salt 
release into the environment leads to elevated total dissolved 
solids (TDS) and chloride concentrations in tannery effluents, 
which, when discharged, degrade surface and groundwater 
quality. The accumulation of salts in surrounding soils causes 
soil salinization, which dimini shes soil fertility and reduces 
agricultural productivity in nearby areas. Furthermore, high

salinity levels inhibit microbial activity in biological 
treatment systems, thereby reducing the efficiency of effluent 
treatment plants (ETPs) and increasing the cost and 
complexity of wastewater management [8, 9] .This practice 
accounts for more than 50% of COD, 40% of TDS and over 
55% of chloride conten t in composite tannery wastewater 
[9]. With increasing regulatory scrutiny and rising 
operational costs of effluent treatment, the leather sector is 
compelled to explore sustainable alternatives, aligning with 
SDG 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation) and SDG 12 (Responsible 
Consumption and Production) [10].This growing concern has 
prompted researchers to investigate eco -friendly and 
reversible preservation methods that minimize salt usage. 
Another existing approach to minimize salt usage is the direct 
processing of fresh hides without any curing. While this 
method completely avoids salt discharge, it poses logistical 
and microbiological challenges, such as the need for 
immediate processing and difficulty in long -distance 
transport, which limit its large-scale applicability [11]. Beside 
this, alternatives tested include both chemical and physical 
approaches—such as boric acid, potass ium chloride, gamma 
irradiation, and controlled drying —as well as phyto -based 
compounds derived from various plants [11-16]. Among 
these, interest has been steadily increasing in the use of 
botanical extracts, which are biodegradable, less hazardous, 
and often exhibit inherent antimicrobial pr operties suitable 
for skin preservation [17-23]. Botanical -based approaches, 
although promising, often show inconsistent preservation 
efficacy, limited antimicrobial spectra, and poor compatibility 
with standard leather processing steps. Moreover, there has 
been minimal exploration of underutilized, regionally 
abundant plants with ric h bioactive profiles, such as Justicia 
gendarussa. 
In light of these efforts, the present study explores the use of 
Justicia gendarussa leaves as a plant -based preservation 
medium in conjunction with a minimal quantity of common 
salt. Justicia gendarussa , locally known as "Jagat Madan" or 
"Gandoross" in various regions of Bangladesh and South Asia, 
is a tropical medicinal shrub widely recognized for its 
therapeutic applications [24-26]. Native to Southeast Asia, 
Justicia gendarussa is rich in several bioactive co nstituents, 
including alkaloids, flavonoids, saponins, tannins, and 
phenolic compounds, each contributing synergistically to its 
antibacterial efficacy [27-31]. The alkaloids interfere with 
microbial DNA replication and protein synthesis, leading to 
inhibition of bacterial growth. Flavonoids and phenolics act 
as powerful antioxidants and disrupt bacterial cell 
membranes by forming complexes with extra cellular and 
soluble proteins, ultimately causing cell lysis [32]. Saponins 
possess surface -active properties that increase membrane 
permeability, resulting in leakage of intracellular components 
[33]. Tannins, on the other hand, precipitate microbial 
proteins and inhibit key enzymes essential for bacterial 
metabolism [34]. Through these combined mechanisms, 
Justicia genda russa effectively suppresses bacterial 
proliferation on hides, thereby preventing putrefaction and 
reducing the need for excessive salt during preservation. 
Despite its traditional use in medicine, there has been no 
reported application of J. gendarussa for the preservation of 
goatskin or rawhide, making this study a novel initiative in 
this field. 
The objective of this work was to examine the effectiveness of 
J. gendarussa in preserving goatskin while reducing the 
dependency on sodium chloride, thereby lowering the 
salinity and TDS levels in tannery effluents. The efficacy of 
preservation was evaluated based on physical and 
microbiological parameters including moisture rete ntion, 
hydrothermal stability, bacterial count, hair slip, odor, surface 
texture, and any signs of fungal or worm infestation over a 30-
day preservation period. A comparative analysis was 
conducted against control samples preserved using the 
conventional 4 0% salt method. Finally, the preserved skins 
were processed into upper crust leather, and pollution loads 
during soaking —especially TDS and NaCl content —were 
assessed to gauge environmental impact. By introducing a 
Justicia gendarussa -based alternative to salt curing, the 
research contributes to cleaner effluent discharge and 
sustainable water management (SDG 6). Furthermore, the 
use of renewable, plant -derived materials promotes eco -
friendly and responsible production practices within the 
leather industry (SDG 12). 
 
MATERIALS AND METHODS 
 
Materials 
 
Industrial-grade sodium chloride (NaCl) and tanning 
chemicals were procured from suppliers in Savar, Dhaka, 
Bangladesh. All reagents utilized for analytical procedures 
were of analytical grade. Justicia gendarussa specimens were 
collected from the Dinajpur, thoroughly washed, sun -dried, 
pulverized, and subsequently stored under controlled 
conditions for experimental use. Fresh goatskins were 
sourced from Kaptan Bazar, Dhaka, and subjected to cleaning 
and preparat ion protocols at the Institute of Leather 
Engineering and Technology for subsequent preservation 
experiments. 
 
Methods 
 
Collection and preparation of Justicia Gendarussa leaf 
powder 
 
The leaves of Justicia gendarussa were collected from 
Dinajpur, Bangladesh and subsequently air -dried under 
sunlight for approximately 4 to 5 days. Following complete 
drying, the leaves were ground using a mechanical grinder to 
obtain a fine powder (Figure 1). This powdered material was 
then utilized for the preservation of skins in the present 
study.

Optimization and selection of the most effective 
combination 
 
The combinations of Justicia gendarussa plant leaf powder 
and 10% of NaCl solution as curing agents on the skins were 
assessed periodically for seven days (Table 2). Organoleptic 
properties like hair slip, odor, fungal growth, physical feel, etc. 
were assessed for the enhancement of this study and the best 
possible combination was chosen for carrying out the next 
analysis procedures. During these seven days, the 1 st,3rd 5th 
and 7 th days were to be decided to observe the organoleptic 
959 Environ Res Tec, Vol. 9, Issue 5, pp. 956-967, October 2026 
 
 
properties for the quality of the preserved skin. The 
observation is included in Table 2. 
Among the four tested formulations, S -3 demonstrated the 
most consistent and effective preservation performance, 
significantly outperforming the other combinations in terms 
of microbial inhibition, physical stability, and maintenance of 
hide quality. Throughout the 7 -day observation period, S -3 
showed no signs of hair slip, odor, fungal growth, or worm 
infestation, indicating excellent preservation of the skin’s 
structural and biochemical integrity. The absence of hair slip 
in S -3 suggests that proteolytic bacterial activity was 
effectively suppressed, preventing the enzymatic degradation 
of follicular and collagenous proteins [35]. In contrast, S -1 
exhibited early signs of degradation, with medium to high 
levels of hair slip observed by Day 7, along with medium to 
high fungal growth and slight worm infestation —symptoms 
indicative of microbial colonization and poor preservation 
efficacy. 
Similarly, S -2 showed moderate performance, delaying 
degradation until after the 5th day. However, by Day 7, slight 
signs of hair slip, odor, fungal presence, and worm activity 
became evident, suggesting a decline in preservation capacity 
over time. While S -2 initially maintained skin integrity, its 
limited long-term effectiveness dimi nishes its suitability for 
practical application. S-4, on the other hand, maintained good 
microbial resistance throughout the study, with no visible 
signs of microbial or parasitic contamination. However, it 
resulted in a progressive loss of skin softness, culminating in 
a medium -hard texture by Day 7. This indicates possible 
moisture loss or early structural stiffening, which could 
hinder flexibility and workability during downstream leather 
processing. 
In stark contrast, S -3 maintained a consistently soft and 
flexible texture across all days, indicating superior hydration 
retention and collagen stabilization [36]. This physical 
consistency, coupled with the complete absence of microbial 
deterioration, underscores the formulation’s dual 
functionality: antimicrobial protection and preservation of 
physical quality. These results suggest that the components of 
S-3 act synergistically to inhibit microbial growth while 
preventing dehydration and structural hardening of the hide. 
Therefore, in direct comparison, S -3 emerges as the most 
effective preservation formulation, combining microbial 
stability with superior physical performance. Its performance 
far exceeds that of S -1 and S -2, which showed progressive 
degradation, and even surpasses S-4, which, despite resisting 
microbial attack, failed to retain the skin's desired physical 
characteristics. This comprehensive efficacy makes S -3 the 
optimal choice for short -term goatskin preservation under 
ambient storage conditions.

into two equal halves along the backbone. Then another leaf

This was applied on the flesh side of one half and another half 
flesh side was applied with 40% NaCl for comparing the result

with organoleptic properties, moisture conte nt

Evaluation of the Preservation Method 
 
Moisture content determination 
 
About 5 g of samples were cut from both the preserved skin 
samples (Experimental and Control) and then weight was 
measured. The cut samples taken in a crucible were placed in 
an oven for about three hours at 105°C. After proper drying, 
the skins were taken out placed into desiccator for cooling 
down. Then weights were again measured until a constant 
weight is achieved. The whole determination procedure and 
method was conducted according to the given standard [37]. 
 
Shrinkage temperature determination 
 
ISO 3380:2015 specifying a method for the determination of 
the shrinkage temperature of leather up to 100° C was 
applied for determining shrinkage temperature of the 
preserved skins [38]. 
 
Total bacteria count 
 
The whole method was processed to conduct the bacterial 
count of the preserved skin samples, about 5 gm of the both 
skin samples were cut carefully and collected in two separate 
stomacher bags. These samples were carefully taken to the 
laminar airflow so that no contamination occurred in the lab 
during bacterial count test. Then nine times (of the weight of 
both samples) sterilized water was added to the stomacher 
bag containing skins. These samples containing bags were 
shaken at 230 rpm for 30 seconds. After that they were again 
taken to the laminar airflow. Nine test tubes were taken for 
each skin samples and filled with 9 ml of water for conducting 
serial dilution of the skins. Sterilization was performed by 
taking the test tubes into an autoclave machine and 
sterilization was done for about 150 minutes. These sterilized 
test tubes were taken out from autoclave machine and taken 
to laminar flow again after cooling down them. 
1ml of from each stomacher bag was taken with micropipette 
and poured into both the first test tubes accordingly (expt. 
and control) containing 9 ml of water in these which resulted 
in 10-2 dilution. These were shaken well after that. Then 1ml 
from the first test tubes were taken and transferred to the 
second test tubes respectively f orming 10 -3 dilution. This 
Environ Res Tec, Vol. 9, Issue 5, pp. 956-967, October 2026 960 
process was continued until 10 -9 dilution is achieved. Petri 
dishes were already prepared with agar media before which 
was incubated before and then kept to refrigerator. These 
were prepared with labelling the sample name (experimental 
and control) and dilution numbers. The petri dishes were 
transferred to the laminar flow and the lids were taken out. 
After that 0.1 ml of the respective solutions were taken out 
using micropipette from the test tubes and poured them 
carefully throu ghout into the petri dishes. Then these petri 
dishes were gently swirled over by plastic spreader until the 
solution is properly mixed with the agar without causing any 
damage to them. Then petri dishes were incubated at 37°C for 
48 hours. The next day was reserved for counting the number 
of colonies formed on the media [39]. 
 
Processing of leather 
 
After 30 days of preservation, conventional leather 
processing method was followed for both the experimental 
and control goat skins to be manufactured as shoe upper crust 
leathers. Before processing the leathers, both the skins were 
indicated with signifying marks so that they could be easily 
found out after completion of the process. All the pre-tanning, 
tanning and post –tanning processes were carried out with 
proper care and awareness following the conventional 
leather processing techniques [40]. After the conversion of 
the preserved skins into shoe upper crust leather, the 
physical properties like tensile strength, grain crack 
resistance and fib er structure (by scanning electron 
microscope (SEM)) was assessed appropriately. 
 
Pollution load emission evaluation during leather 
processing 
 
During leather processing, pollution load was estimated from 
soaking liquor emerged from the soaking operation from both 
the experimental and control preserved skin samples. The 
soaking liquor from both the samples were collected for the 
determination of Total Dissolved Solid (TDS), Electrical 
Conductivity (EC) and NaCl content followed by the standard 
methods [41-44]. TDS was determined gravimetrically, EC 
was measured using a calibrated conductivity meter, and 
NaCl concentration was quantified, reported consistently in 
g·L⁻¹ (with % w/v in parentheses). Liquor of both 
experimental and control preserved skin samples were 
filtered before analysis to remove suspended solids. 
 
Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) 
 
Samples of cured crust leather, approximately 2 mm × 2 mm 
in size and around 1.0 mm in thickness, were obtained from 
the standard sampling region of the leather. These samples, 
representing both experimentally and conventionally 
preserved hides, were analyzed using a Fourier Transform 
Infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Bruker ALPHA II, USA) [45]. 
 
Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) 
 
Thermal stability of the samples was evaluated through 
Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) using a BK-DSC300L 
model calorimeter (BIOBASE, China) [45]. 
 
Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) 
 
Leather specimens measuring approximately 2 mm × 2 mm 
with a thickness near 1.0 mm were obtained from both 
experimentally treated and conventionally processed crust 
leathers, following the standardized sampling protocol [30]. 
For cross-sectional examination, the samples were mounted 
on aluminum stubs and sputter -coated with a thin platinum 
layer using an automatic fine coater (JEC 3000FC, JEOL, 
Japan) to improve electrical conductivity. Imaging was 
performed with a field emission scanning electron 
microscope (FESEM) (JSM-7601F, JEOL, Japan) operated at an 
accelerating voltage of 5 kV and ambient temperature (25 °C), 
with images captured at various magnifications [45]. 
 
Analysis of mechanical attributes of leather 
 
Mechanical properties of the produced crust leathers, i.e, 
tensile strength, percentage of elongation at break, stitch tear 
strength, loa d at grain crack and distension tests were 
determined by adopting the standard methods IUP -06 norm 
with Tensile tester (STD 172), DIN 53331 with Tensile tester 
(STD 172), IUP -09 with Lastometer (STM 104) respectively. 
Before carrying out the determination, the leather samples 
were conditioned at 20 ±2°C temperature and 65 ±2% 
relative humidity over a period of 48 hours. All mechanical 
tests were performed on specimens and cut in both 
horizontal and vertical directions relative to the backbone to 
assess the le ather properties and each experiment were 
performed in triplicate. Then samples were taken out from 
the sampling locations [46]. 
 
RESULT AND DISCUSSION 
 
Organoleptic Properties Investigation of the 
Experimental and Control Skins During Preservation 
 
Raw hides and skins primarily consist of proteins, comprising 
approximately 30% of their total weight, which makes them 
particularly susceptible to microbial degradation [47]. The 
activity of bacteria generates proteolytic enzymes that 
degrade structural proteins within the skin, l eading to the 
formation of ammonia —a key indicator of the onset of 
putrefaction[47, 48]. One of the earliest manifestations of this 
deterioration is hair slip, as bacteria initially target the 
proteins within hair follicles. Preservation effectiveness is 
commonly evaluated based on observable parameters such as 
hair slip, unpleasant odor, the presence of worms, fungal 
infestation, and changes in the physical texture of the skin 
[47]. I t is quite necessary to observe the organoleptic 
properties to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed 
preservation method comparing with the conventional salt 
preservation method. Table 3 shows the organoleptic 
properties of both the experimental and control goat skin 
samples during the 30 days preservation of the final 
experiment. 
Bacteria cause the production of proteolytic enzymes which 
cause the putrefaction of skin. These can easily colonize the 
hair follicles initi alizing hair slip; the first indication of 
putrefaction on skin and proteins breakdown to amino acid 
then to ammonia also implies bad odor or smell [49]. Thus, 
ultimately causes severe effect on physical feel of skins. That’s 
why, it came out must to assess the efficacy of the proposed 
preservation method. The comparat ive investigations of the 
organoleptic properties during preservation are displayed in 
Table 3. 
From Table 3, it is clearly seen that both the preserved skins; 
experimental and control didn’t face any hair slip or bad odor 
during 30 days preservation. Both the experimental and 
control preserved skins exhibit the same degree of physical 
feel, softness and flexibility without hair slip and putrefaction 
odor. Both the preserved skins achieved flexibility from the 
9th to the 12th day of preservation.

through Different Parameters

Moisture content (%) is an important parameter through 
which the degree of skin degradation and effectiveness of a 
preservation method evaluation can be performed. Raw skins 
containing 60-70% moisture content easily prone to bacterial 
attack. During the 30 days preservation of the optimized 
(experimental- using 15% leaf powder+10% salt) and control 
(40% salt); both the preserved skins were assessed by 
determining the percentage of moi sture content on the 
1st,6th,9th,15th and 30 th days of the preservation period. The 
results achieved from determination were displayed in the 
Figure 4.

Shrinkage temperature 
 
Shrinkage Temperature (Hydrothermal Stability) is an 
important parameter which can investigate the degree of 
structural stabilization of collagen matrix. For understanding 
the eff icacy of the proposed method, an analysis was made 
using the collected data presented in the following (Figure 5). 
The variations on the shrinkage temperature on the 
experimental and conventional preservation method weren’t 
much noticeable. The shrinkage temperature values for hides 
treated with Justicia gendarussa leaf powder and 
conventional salt -based preservation showed fluctuations 
over the preservation period. Notably, the experimental 
treatment generally exhibited slightly lower values in the 
early days but converged toward the control values over time. 
However, it is important to recognize that shrinkage 
temperature measurements on fresh hides containing 
hair/wool can be influenced by these natural components 
and may not accurately reflect structural stability; this test is

these limitations, the data indicate that Justicia gendarussa 
leaf powder can maintain hydrothermal stability comparable

suitable for fresh hides are recommended to validate these 
observations. So it can be a indication that Justicia gendarussa

Total bacterial count 
 
Bacterial Count on the preserved goat skins (experimental 
and control) is one of those important parameters which can 
depict the performance of the preservation method by 
investigating whether the method is inhibiting bacterial 
growth or not. This bacterial count was conducted on both the 
skins preserved with experimental and control method. The 
results were shown at the Table 4. It has been visualized that 
both the experimental and control skins were found to be 
3.63×109 and 2.37×10 10 CFU/gm on bacterial count 
respectively. Bacterial count was decreasing for both the 
methods through the preservation period was passing out. 
Specially the decreasing tendency on bacterial count was 
highly noticed after 15 days on experimental preservation 
method. The slight increase in total bacterial count observed 
between days 9 and 15 in both experimental and control skins 
can be attributed to the na tural microbial growth dynamics 
during the early -mid phase of preservation. Microbial 
populations can fluctuate due to residual nutrients in the skin 
matrix, local moisture variations, or adaptation to the 
preservation environment. Following this transient increase, 
the bacterial load decreased as the preservative effect of the 
treatments stabilized over time, limiting further microbial 
growth. This pattern is consistent with previous reports on 
microbial dynamics in preserved skins, where temporary 
fluctuations can occur before a steady decline is achieved. 
Though both the methods showed efficacy in inhibiting 
bacterial growth on the 30th day comparing the very first day; 
experimental preservation method worked well in lowering 
bacterial count than control method. Therefore, this can be 
justified as the experimental preservation method (15% leaf 
powder+10% salt) has antimicrobial activity through which 
it was successful in inhibiting bacterial population [52, 53].

FTIR of leather 
 
The FTIR spectral analysis between conventionally preserved 
leather using common salt and the experimental sample 
treated with Justicia gendarussa leaf powder highlights key 
similarities and differences in chemical composition 
retention (Figure 6). Both samples exhibit broad peaks 
around 3281.29 cm⁻¹ (conventional) and 3303.69 cm⁻¹ (leaf 
powder), indicating the presence of O –H and N–H stretching 
vibrations, which confirm the preservation of hydrogen -
bonded amide and hydroxyl groups in collagen. Simi larly, 
prominent C –H stretching vibrations at 2926.98 cm⁻¹ 
(conventional) and 2924.26 cm⁻¹ with an additional peak at 
2858.91 cm⁻¹ (leaf powder) suggest that aliphatic 
components remain intact in both treatments, though the 
sharper appearance in the leaf -treated sample may imply 
better stabilization. The amide I region, essential for collagen 
integrity, appears at 1632.25 cm⁻¹ in the conventionally 
preserved sample and at 1635.72 cm⁻¹ in the experimental 
one, confirming effective preservation of the protein 
backbone in both cases. However, the leaf powder -treated 
sample shows more defined peaks in the amide II and III 
regions (1551.09 cm⁻¹, 1447.97 cm⁻¹, and 1236.55 cm⁻¹), 
compared to the broader and less intense signals at 1543.06 
cm⁻¹, 1446 cm⁻¹, and 1234.27 cm⁻¹ in the salt-treated leather. 
Moreover, the fingerprint region exhibits peaks at 1032.49 
cm⁻¹ (conventional) and 1034.43 cm⁻¹ (leaf powder), along 
with comparable bending vibrations near 664 –658 cm⁻¹, 
indicating the presence of collagen-associated polysaccharide 
or glycoprotein structures in both samples [45]. Overall, a 
closer examination r eveals that the Justicia gendarussa -
treated samples consistently show slightly sharper and more 
defined peaks in regions associated with amide I and II bands, 
which correspond to collagen backbone vibrations. This 
suggests a marginally improved preservatio n of collagen 
secondary structure compared to the salt -treated samples. 
Although the differences are not dramatic, they are consistent 
and reproducible across replicates, supporting the conclusion 
that Justicia gendarussa leaf powder can interact with and 
stabilize collagen, making it a promising environmentally 
sustainable alternative.

DSC 
 
The DSC thermogram compares the thermal behavior of 
leather samples processed using a conventional common salt 
method and an experimental approach employing Justicia 
gendarussa powder. Both samples exhibit a prominent 
endothermic peak between 90°C and 110°C, corresponding to 
moisture loss or the evaporation of bound water (Figure 7). 
This similarity indicates comparable water retention 
characteristics between the two tanning systems. In the 
intermediate temperature range (100°C –250°C), the heat 
flow remains relatively stable, with minor fluctuations, 
reflecting polymeric relaxation or molecular reorganization 
within the collagen matrix. Notably, a significant exothermic 
transition is observed between 250°C and 310°C in both 
samples, associated with the therm al degradation or 
denaturation of collagen fibers [45]. However, the 
experimental sample shows a broader exothermic response, 
suggesting a modified decomposition profile due to potential 
interactions between J. gendarussa phytochemicals and the 
collagen structure. Overall, the comparable thermal 
transitions indicate that J. gendarussa -treated leath er 
demonstrates similar, if not slightly improved, thermal 
stability relative to the conventionally tanned sample, 
supporting its viability as a sustainable tanning alternative.

FESEM analysis 
 
Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) was 
employed to examine the microstructural characteristics of 
crust leather samples derived from both the experimental 
preservation method and the conventional salt -curing 
process (Figure 9). This analys is aimed to assess the 
structural integrity of collagen fibers and their resistance to 
microbial degradation. The FESEM images of the 
experimental group revealed a compact and coherent fibrous 
network with well -preserved collagen bundles, indicating 
effective stabilization of the protein matrix and minimal 
microbial infiltration. The absence of fiber disintegration or 
structural collapse suggests that the applied preservation 
treatment successfully maintained the physical integrity of 
the leather at the microscopic level [45]. 
In comparison, the salt -cured control sample also exhibited 
dense and uniformly arranged fibers without noticeable signs 
of bacterial attack, thereby reaffirming the established 
efficacy of salt-curing as a microbial deterrent and structural 
preservative. The comparative evaluation of both samples 
indicates that the experiment al preservation technique is 
capable of offering similar, if not enhanced, protection against 
microbial degradation, while preserving the essential fiber 
architecture. These findings underscore the potential of the 
experimental method as a sustainable and effective 
alternative to traditional salt -curing, particularly in the 
context of reducing environmental salt load and promoting 
eco-friendly leather processing practices.

CONCLUSION 
 
The present study establishes that Justicia gendarussa leaf 
powder, when combined with a reduced amount of sodium 
chloride, can serve as an efficient and environmentally 
sustainable alternative to the conventional high -salt method 
for raw skin preservation. Among various formulations 
tested, the combination o f 15% Justicia gendarussa powder 
and 10% NaCl exhibited the most favorable preservation 
performance over a 30 -day storage period. This formulation 
maintained the natural appearance, softness, and flexibility of 
965 Environ Res Tec, Vol. 9, Issue 5, pp. 956-967, October 2026 
 
 
the skin without any indication of microbial growth, hair slip, 
or putrefaction. 
Biophysical and biochemical analyses confirmed the 
effectiveness of this preservation approach. The 
experimental skins retained moisture within acceptable 
limits, and the shrinkage temperature remained within the 
thermal stability range necessary for further leather 
processing. Additionally, total bacterial count was 
significantly lower in the experimental samples, reflecting the 
antimicrobial efficacy of phytochemicals present in Justicia 
gendarussa. FTIR spectra confirm ed the preservation of 
characteristic collagen peaks, indicating minimal alteration to 
the molecular structure of skin proteins. DSC analysis 
revealed consistent thermal degradation patterns between 
experimental and control samples, further verifying the 
integrity of the collagen matrix. 
Environmental evaluations demonstrated a substantial 
advantage of the experimental system. The soaking effluent 
from the Justicia gendarussa -salt mixture showed 
significantly reduced values of total dissolved solids (TDS), 
electrical conductivity (EC), and NaCl concentration 
compared to the conventional salt -only method. This 
suggests that using plant-based preservatives not only lowers 
salt usage but also contributes to reduced salinity in tannery 
wastewater—one of the critical challenges facing the leather 
industry today. Furthermore, the physical testing of finished 
leathers produced from the preserved skins showed that 
tensile strength, tear strength, grain crack resistance, and 
elongation at break met or exceeded standar d requirements 
for shoe upper leather, ensuring the functional viability of the 
process at an industrial scale. 
In conclusion, the use of Justicia gendarussa leaf powder as a 
natural preservative offers multiple advantages: effective 
microbial inhibition, collagen stabilization, improved 
environmental sustainability, and satisfactory mechanical 
performance of the final leather product. While the study 
primarily focused on preservation efficacy and 
environmental benefits, the economic feasibility of using 
Justicia gendarussa also appears promising. Being a plant 
already utilized in traditional medicine, its availability is 
relatively widespread, and the observations suggest that, 
when combined with reduced salt, the cost could be 
competitive with conventional high-salt curing thus can be 
implemented on detailed cost–benefit analyses for large-scale 
industrial application. Its required quantity for preservation 
is relatively low, and the plant is locally available in many 
tropical regions, making it a feasible option for small - and 
medium-scale tanneries. This low -salt, plant -based 
preservation strategy addresses critical environmental 
concerns associated with salt -intensive curing methods and 
aligns with the principles of green chemistry and sustainable 
development, particularly SDG 6 (Clean Water and 
Sanitation) and SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and 
Production). The study recommends further scale -up trials 
and cost –benefit assessments to evaluate the commercial 
applicability of this eco -friendly preservation method in the 
leather industry.
