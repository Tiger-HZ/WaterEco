Cave systems can be dif ﬁcult to recognise where karst landforms are subdued or overprinted with
other landscape processes. Subtle linear depressions on carbonate platforms are commonly
interpreted as ﬂuvial or tectonic landforms, but may also mark collapse above underlying cave
systems. We test this on the Nullarbor Plain, southern Australia, a stable >200,000 km² carbonate
platform exposed for >14 Myr. Integrating morphometrics, electrical resistivity tomography, passive
seismic proﬁling, cave records, cliff exposures and sedimentology, we show that kilometre-scale,
shallow, sediment-ﬁlled trenches are surface expressions of vertically propagated collapse and
sagging above deep cave conduits. These depressions lack integrated drainage, downstream
sediment transport and sedimentological evidence of channelised ﬂow; instead, they overlie vertically
extensive low-resistivity zones, align with mapped caves and collapse dolines, and project to cliff-face
exposed stratal deformation. The Nullarbor trenches provide diagnostic criteria for recognising
subdued cave-collapse systems on Earth and an analogue for interpreting linear depressions on other
rocky planetary surfaces.
Caverniferous landscapes most commonly develop through dissolution of
soluble rocks (commonly carbonates and evaporites); known as karst ter-
rains, these comprise ca. 15% of Earth ’s ice-free terrestrial surface 1,2.
Potentially analogous caverniferous landscapes have been identi ﬁed on
other planets, including thousands of candidate cave entrances3– 5.O nE a r t h ,
caves and cave deposits can persist as relict elements in the landscape, with
passage morphology and sedimentﬁlls recording hydrological regimes that
no longer exist at these sites2,6. The relative stability of cave environments
and the protection they provide from surface disturbance promote the
preservation of palaeoenvironmental archives, archaeological remains and
organic materials7– 10. This preservation potential is also relevant for plane-
tary caves, which may provide accessible subsurface environments where
organic matter or biosignatures, should they ever have existed, could be
protected from surface radiation, temperature extremes and weathering9,11.
Recognition of caves is therefore vital for reconstructing past climates,
environments and the history of life on Earth, as well as for assessing
astrobiological potential on other worlds.
Many low-gradient cave passages in karst landscapes develop under
phreatic or epiphreatic conditions at or below the watertable and are
generally hidden from view 12; even after they are drained they can be
difﬁcult to locate. Therefore, where such cave passages in ﬂuence the
ground surface above them, their surface expression provides an
important constraint on their spatial position. The most common surface
geomorphological features linked to caves form when the mechanically
unstable roof of an underground cavity collapses; this may propagate the
cavity upwards until the surface is breached 6,13,14. The clearest examples
are collapse dolines, which are closed and relatively circular
depressions15. Collapse of larger segments of cave passages can form
aligned dolines or linear valley-like depressions that can be readily linked
to subsurface cave systems2, e.g. Peruaçú Gorge in Minas Gerais, Brazil16,
Stockyard Gully in Western Australia 17, and a section of the Grand
Canyon in Arizona, USA 18. Additional surface expressions of karst sys-
tems are unroofed caves19– 21; these form by gradual chemical denudation
(surface lowering), preserving the original conduit morphology and often

On low-relief, tectonically stable carbonate platforms in arid climate
regions, the surface expression of cavecollapse is generally subdued, because
gradual surface lowering (due to slow dissolution rates), calcretisation and
aeolian inﬁll obscure collapse morphologiesas they develop. As a result, cave
collapses are difﬁcult to recognise, even though deep karst voids can persist
for tens of millions of years. Collapsed cave passages may be present only as
subtle linear depressions that are ea sily overlooked or misinterpreted as
ﬂuvial, tectonic or even aeolian-related landforms. In more tectonically and/
or hydrologically active settings, collapsed cave features may be equally
subtle and can be overlooked entirely due to the dominance of other, more
pronounced surface structures. As a result, cave collapse systems, potentially
globally widespread, are often undetected because their surface signature is
difﬁcult to identify.
Here we resolve this problem by demonstrating diagnostic criteria for
recognising low relief cave-collapse systems on the arid Nullarbor Plain of
southern Australia, which is the la rgest onshore exposure of Cenozoic
carbonate marine sediments anywhere in the world22. The Nullarbor Plain is
tectonically stable and compositionally homogeneous, and despite being
subaerially exposed for over 14 million years (Fig.1), represents an excep-
tional archive of subtle depositio nal, structural and environmental
structures23– 25. By integrating morphometrics, electrical resistivity tomo-
graphy, passive seismic proﬁling, stratigraphy and sediment analysis, we
demonstrate that shallow, sediment-ﬁlled linear depressions on the Nul-
larbor Plain represent long-term upward propagation of collapse from cave
systems to the surface, rather than shallow ﬂuvial valleys, which they
resemble in planform geometry.
The Nullarbor Plain surface depressions have evolved under prolonged
aridity, allowing collapse morphologies to persist without signiﬁcant ﬂuvial
reworking. Thus, they offer a template for recognising collapse trench for-
mation in dry settings on other planets. This may apply both to potential
karst settings, e.g. on Titan and Mars26,27, as well as to volcanic terrains, e.g.
on Mars, where elongate troughs have been interpreted as evidence of

Regional setting
The Nullarbor Plain is a low-relief carbonate platform covering more than
200,000 km² of the southern margin of Australia. It is composed of a gently
southward, seaward-dipping succession of Cenozoic limestones separated
by subtle disconformities: Mi ddle-Upper Eocene Wilson Bluff
Limestone30– 32, Upper Oligocene-Lower Miocene Abrakurrie
Limestone30– 32, and Middle Miocene Nullarbor Limestone30– 34. Since the sea
regressed in the Middle Miocene and exposed the platform to weathering,
the region has experienced long-term landscape stability with minimal
tectonic deformation, no glaciation and negligible deposition of sediment,
allowing geomorphic records of past environments to persist. These include
longitudinal dunes etched into the limestone25 and topographically elevated
primary depositional bioherms preserved in the Nullarbor Limestone23.T h e
present surface relief is extremely s ubdued, surface drainage is absent or
ephemeral, and surface karsti ﬁcation is weakly expressed. The southern
margin of the Nullarbor Plain is an extensive cliff-line, the Great Southern
Scarp, which is among the longest uninterrupted escarpments in the world35.
Sub-horizontal cave systems are a c haracteristic feature of the Nul-
larbor Plain36. Caves on the Nullarbor Plain a re typically categorised as
“shallow” or “deep”37– 39, extending <30 m and 50– 150 m below the surface,
respectively40. Shallow cave passages are con ﬁned to the Nullarbor Lime-
stone and range up to 200 m in length37. They are often characterised by sub-
horizontal passages with abundant deposits of dark brown calcite, as well as
halite and gypsum40. These shallow caves are associated with pocket valleys
in the Hampton and Wylie Scarps 41, and their genesis is associated with
mixing corrosion during the Late Miocene - Pliocene sea-level highstand42.
Deep caves occur predominantly within ~60 km of the southern coast.
Most of them are accessed through collapse dolines and contain passages
extending hundreds of metres and reaching tens of metres in width and
height40. Besides the signiﬁcant size of their passages and chambers, the deep
caves are characterised by extensive collapse, general absence of calcite
deposits, and continuation below the present water table. Halite and gypsum
speleothems may be present43.T h ef o r m a t i o no fd e e pc a v e si su n r e l a t e dt o
the formation of shallow caves. The se caves developed predominantly
within the older Wilson Bluff Limestone as extensive trunk conduits,
probably during periods of higher effective recharge in the Oligocene to
Early Miocene, when the sea withdrew from the carbonate platform and
allowed cave development40. The orientation of known cave passages sug-
gests the caves formed by coastward- ﬂowing groundwater, with conduit

regression and uplift in the Late Miocene– Early Pliocene, lowering of the
watertable drained the caves and removed buoyancy, initiating progressive
roof failure, so that the cave passage s are now characterised by extensive
collapse38,44.
Results
Along the eastern edge of the Nullarbor Plain, close to the Great Southern
Scarp, ﬁve trenches (elongated linear to gently curvilinear depressions) are
developed, oriented generally NNW-SSE, ranging from several kilometres
to more than 20 km in length and generally 100– 500 m wide, yet typically
less than 9 m deep (Table 1;F i g .1). These trenches have sediment-ﬁlled
ﬂoors and subdued margins. They are not integrated into any surface
drainage network and show no evidence of downstream sediment transport.
Sediment within the trenches becomes progressively ﬁner-grained with
depth. Sand dominates the shallow upper layer, whereas deeper layers show
progressively higher proportions of silt and clay. The sediment is composed
of calcite, quartz and clay (illite an d kaolinite) with traces of feldspars,
dolomite, aragonite, halite, anatase and gypsum (see the Supplementary
material for the complete analytical data).

Chronological framework of trench development
The ﬁve trenches in this study show a west – east transition from cave-
connected collapse dolines to continuous sediment-ﬁlled trenches (Fig.1),
corresponding closely to a decrease in the thickness of the carbonate stra-
tigraphy from ~250 m in the west near Abrakurrie Trench to ~110 m in the
vicinity of Yalata Trench (Fig. 4). The original cave passages probably
formed at or towards the base of the carbonate sequence. The eastern trench
systems near the basin margin appear to represent a more advanced stage of
development, where the cave collapses propagated upwards through a
smaller thickness of limestone in a shorter time. The discontinuous collapse
features in the west are interpreted to reﬂect a less advanced stage in which
collapse has not yet fully propagated to the surface through the thicker
limestone.
T h es y s t e m a t i cw e s t– east variation in trench morphology therefore
provides a chronological framework for the long-term evolution of cave-
collapse systems under stable cond itions, preserving a continuum from
readily identiﬁable collapse dolines to shallow, subdued and barely per-
ceptible surface footprints.
This developmental sequence on the Nullarbor Plain is not purely
relict, as shown by a mid-Pleistocene (~460 ka) U-Th date on a calcrete layer
3 m below the present-day surface calcrete at Clay Dam Cave in Koonalda
Trench. The presence of older calcrete layers beneath the dated layer
demonstrates that although present-daykarst processes operate at low rates,
they are suf ﬁcient to maintain a net rock-mass de ﬁcit, so collapse, sub-
sidence and inﬁlling have occurred repeatedly and probably continue today,
as indicated by fresh rock collapses from cave ceilings and collapse doline
walls50.
Recognition of collapsed cave systems
The Nullarbor Plain provides a global reference example for recognising
collapsed cave systems whose surface expression is visible but subdued, i.e.
low-relief, sediment-ﬁlled and lacking visible steep collapse walls or canyon-
like morphology that make many cave-collapse valleys readily identiﬁable.

records of former drainage systems, so laterally extensive, shallow trenches

larbor Plain provide an analogue for recognising hidden, laterally extensive
subsurface void systems from subtle surface depressions in arid, low-relief
terrains. In planetary settings, linear depressions and collapse alignments

subsurface passage systems. The transferable element of our study is
therefore diagnostic; collapse alignment, spatial clustering of morphologi-

Electrical resistivity tomography
Electrical resistivity tomography (ERT) is a relatively fast, low impact geo-
physical method to portray differences of electrical subsurface
properties57– 59. Physical-chemical sediment properties such as mineralogy,
texture, soil moisture levels, salinity or temperature all have some inﬂuence
on the electrical properties of subsurface material and thus respond to
electrical ﬁelds60. 2-D ERT measurement lines provide the opportunity to
interpret measured differences in a geomorphic-sedimentological way.
We used the multielectrode system ‘4pointlight hp’ from Lippmann
Geophysical Instruments61 with a maximum of 100 steel electrodes with
varying spacings between 2 and 5 m. A roll-along technique was used to
extend ERT lines where necessary. Early tests showed the Wenner array as
an effective electrode conﬁguration with a stable and good signal to noise
ratio. Wenner describes a 4-electrode array with active current electrodes on
the outside (electrode 1 and 4) and the two inner electrodes (electrode 2 and
3) measuring the voltage drop. The apparent resistivity (ρa), measured in
Ωm (denoting Ohm’s law), is calculated by Loke, Chambers62:

Pseudo-sections detailing the geometry of different electrode conﬁg-

and 100 mA. Each point was measured three times and the average was

ERT has been tested on the Nullarbor Plain before in search of open
cave passages, but with limited success due to dry and well insulated
ground63. However, we collected ﬁeld data in October 2022 and October

contact resistance ensuring robustﬁeld data.

models of the speciﬁc resistivity by using linear least-squares optimisation
inversion techniques. The chosen convergence limit of 3.0% was usually
reached afterﬁve to six iterations with absolute error between 2.5 and 15%
between measured and modelled apparent resistivities. Relative elevation
level of each electrode was extracted from the drone survey and applied to
the resistivity lines prior to the inversion.
Interpretation of ERT images was derived by separating resistivity
zones by their physical values and shapes. These ERT-shapes were suc-
cessfully identiﬁed in nearby outcrops and accessible cave systems corro-
borating the ERT-derived interpretation. Additionally, we also collected
representative samples (sediments and rocks) to measure their electric
properties under controlled conditions in the laboratory (see Supplemen-
tary material), which further consolidated our interpretation.
Passive seismic ﬁeld set-up
In this study, we use the passive HVSR method 65 to record subsurface
ambient noise caused by either human-induced forces such as noise from
road or train traf ﬁc, or by natural forces such as wind, waves or
earthquakes66,67. Passive seismic was mainly applied to depths below ERT’s
depth of exploration at >30– 40 m depth and to estimate depth to bedrock
where ERT was inconclusive. High-frequency noise in the signal (often
>25 Hz) is mostly attributed to windy conditions during the measurement.
Strong winds are frequent in the Nullarbor and were identiﬁed as an issue
during previous attempts using passive seismics63.
We used a Tromino ENGY 3G seismometer (MOHO, Venice Italy)
with a sampling rate of 128 Hz to collect HVSR data along three axes (Z
component up-down, Y component east-west, X component north-south).
We oriented the instrument to north and ensured tight coupling to the
ground. Measurements were taken for 20 min and windy days were avoided
to ensure better data quality and less noise.
Grilla software (MOHO) was used to analyse the ﬁeld data. Noisy
sections of the 20 min measurement window were removed for the sub-
sequent analysis which were restricted to 0.1– 64 Hz. We used the 0.1– 20 Hz

Drone photogrammetry
Drone photography was carried out using a DJI Mavic 2 Pro and a DJI
AIR2S drones. The aerial imagery was obtained in theﬁeld to provide higher
resolution data to complement regional satellite images, digital elevation
models and ERT results. This was done to determine the accurate digital
elevation model for ERT lines, to document morphology of collapse caves,
and to document the walls of the Nullarbor (Bunda) Cliffs. At each ERT site
several hundred photographs were taken at an angle of 90° from heights of
20– 30 m with the UAV’s factory-installed camera. Similarly, at each point
on the cliffs that was correlated to the closest point of the theoretical outﬂow
of the linear depressions, the cliffs were recorded 1 km in each lateral
direction. The recordings allowed us to produce images with a resolution up
to a few cm.
The acquired imagery was then processed in Agisoft Metashape Pro-
fessional. First, images of inadequate quality were removed, i.e. those with a
quality score of less than 0.5 as recommended by software developer. Then,
photogrammetric methods (photogrammetry is a technique of evaluating
forms, dimensions and positions of objects by analysing images of them68)
were used to produce a dense point cloud representing the surface. Further,
on the basis of the point cloud, a digital elevation model and orthorectiﬁed
image or orthophoto were produced (see also Peternel et al.69 and Cignetti
et al.70 for the details of the methodology). The point recordings and theﬁnal
products were prepared in the GDA2020 MGA projected coordinate
system.
Geological analyses
Sediment samples from trenches (Abrakurrie Trench 31°38 ’49.5” S,
128°29’11.6” E; Wombat Trench 31°32’41.7” S, 129°20’29.3” E), including
Abrakurrie Cave and Clay Dam Cave (cave locations can be obtained from
Australian Speleological Federation Inc. (ASF)), were collected to analyse
their grain size and mineralogy at the Faculty of Natural Sciences and
Engineering, University of Ljubljana. Permit reference numbers are “RA-
00001504; File A00031-24 ”, “File 00514-1999; Case 2302956 ”,a n d
“E27407-1”.
Sediment grain size analysis was performed using a FRITSCH Ana-
lysette 22-28 instrument combining a laser granulometer and dynamic
image analysis. The size range of the measurements with the aforemen-
tioned instrument ranges between 10 nm and 2 mm. Sediment samples
were ﬁrst quartered using a stainless steel sample splitter to obtain a suitable
amount for analysis (~1 g). Then, 3 ml of distilled water was added to the
sample in a PVC container and homogenized for 5 min at a frequency of
25 Hz with a MillMix 20 shaker. The obtained data was used to classify
sediments according to Blott and Pye71.
The mineralogy of samples was determined using X-ray diffrac-
tion (XRD) with an Empyrean III diffractometer (Malvern Panalytical)
equipped with a MultiOptics module (iCore and dCore) and a 1Der
detector on randomly oriented powder mounts. A spinning stage was
used to ensure uniform sample exposure during measurements. The
samples were ﬁnely ground to a particle size less than 30 μm to reduce
the inﬂuence of the preferred orientation and placed in a back-loading
sample station for optimal surface alignment. Diffraction data were
collected over a 2 θ range of 3° to 70° with a step size of 0.004° and a
counting time of 12 s per step. The instrument operated with a Cu
X-ray tube at 45 kV and 40 mA, using a ﬁxed divergence slit of 0.5°
and a 14 mm incident beam mask. Diffraction patterns were processed
using HighScore Plus version 5.3. Background determination, peak
position and intensity were performed, followed by phase identi ﬁca-
tion through comparison with patterns in the ICDD PDF-4 Minerals
2025 database. Quantitative phase analysis was conducted using the
Rietveld reﬁnement method, in which a polynomial function was ﬁtted
to the measured diffraction patterns. The re ﬁnement yielded weighted
proﬁle R-factors (Rw) ranging from 10.5 to 12.9 and goodness-of- ﬁt
(GOF) values between 1.2 and 2.9.
U/Th dating
Two calcreted sediments of the Clay Dam Cave of the Koonalda Trench
were dated by the uranium-thorium (U/Th) method at the University of
Queensland. Ages were corrected for non-radiogenic230Th incorporated at
the time of deposition. Full details of the method are provided in Hellstrom
(2003, 2006)72,73. Age errors are reported as 2σ uncertainties.
