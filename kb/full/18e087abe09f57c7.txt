Abstract 
Objectives. To examine whether diet-quality assessment represents the two-sided nature of 
nutrient–health relationships; to determine which reference value licenses an individual 
inference; to identify how intakes above a limit are reached; and to propose a two-sided 
component score for use within an existing index. 
Background. Risk rises at both ends of the intake range for many micronutrients, yet diet-
quality indices score components monotonically and exclude supplements by construction, 
while population reference values are used as individual targets despite guidance against this. 
Methods. Critical narrative review. Literature was identified through PubMed/MEDLINE, 
Scopus and the Cochrane Library to September 2026, with direct consultation of the 
reference-value documents of the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and 
Medicine, the relevant United Kingdom expert committees, and the European Food Safety 
Authority. Selection was purposive rather than systematic. 
Results. Seven vitamins have no upper limit because adverse-effect data are insufficient, not 
because safety is shown, yet effects of excess are documented for most micronutrients, 
differing in study design, not in whether harm occurs. Where limits exist, that gap is three- to 
seven-fold for vitamin A, zinc, iron and selenium. Supplements supply one to three orders of 
magnitude above requirement and drive most exceedance in adults; fortification does in 
children. No analysis reports aggregate exceedance across nutrients, and solubility does not 
predict safety. Of eight instruments in routine use, none applies a two-sided rule at nutrient

level or counts supplements; a smaller nutrient-based literature does more, but none does all. 
We set out a candidate module covering every micronutrient, anchored on the estimated 
average requirement and the upper limit, separating food, fortification and supplements, and 
weighting penalties across four tiers. 
Conclusion. Two-sided, supplement-aware assessment is constructible now. We set out the 
staged descriptive, biochemical and prognostic work that would establish whether it is worth 
adopting. 
1. INTRODUCTION 
Nutritional adequacy is usually discussed as though it had one direction. Public health 
messaging, dietary guidance and the indices built to operationalise them are organised around 
the idea of getting enough — enough fibre, enough calcium, enough vitamin D — and the 
scoring systems that quantify diet quality largely reflect that orientation. For a substantial 
number of nutrients, however, the relationship between habitual intake and risk is U-shaped 
or J-shaped: harm accumulates at low intakes, falls across a range of adequacy, and rises 
again as intake climbs. Iodine is the textbook case, with both deficiency and excess producing 
thyroid dysfunction.¹,² Selenium, iron, zinc, vitamin A, vitamin B6 and vitamin E all have 
well-described upper-end toxicities.³⁻⁷ The framework that defines nutrient requirements has 
acknowledged this since the tolerable upper intake level was formalised in the late 1990s.⁸ 
Yet the instruments most widely used to score diet quality are, with limited exceptions, 
monotone in their components. Higher scores are awarded for more of the things considered 
beneficial and fewer of the things considered harmful, and in the great majority of cases no 
scoring penalty attaches to an intake that has climbed past the point of benefit. This is not an 
oversight so much as an inheritance: the indices were built to discriminate between broadly 
inadequate and broadly adequate diets in populations where inadequacy was the dominant 
problem, and they perform that task well.⁹,¹⁰ The dietary-pattern evidence they encode is 
itself substantial, including randomised evidence for pattern-level change.¹¹ Whether they 
remain fit for purpose in populations where fortification, supplementation and abundant food 
supply have made the upper end a live concern is a different question, and one that has 
attracted less attention than it deserves. 
This review makes three linked arguments. The first is that bidirectional risk is real, is well 
characterised for a subset of nutrients, and is largely unrepresented in diet-quality scoring. 
The second is that the reference values used to judge whether an individual’s intake is 
adequate are frequently the wrong ones: the recommended dietary allowance and its 
international equivalents are population reference values, and the guidance that accompanies 
them says explicitly that they should not be used to assess an individual’s intake. Yet that is 
how they are most commonly used, in clinical practice, in consumer applications and in the 
construction of personal intake targets.¹²,¹³ The third is that intakes above an upper limit are 
reached by a specific and identifiable route, principally concentrated supplements and 
fortified staples, and only rarely through whole foods, and that no widely used assessment 
instrument counts either of them in a way that would allow the upper end of the distribution 
to be seen at all. Having set out these three, we propose what a two-sided component score 
would require. 
These three arguments share a structure. In each case a tool that is well suited to one 
inferential task (describing populations, ranking diets, screening for inadequacy in resource-
poor settings) has been carried across to a different task, that of characterising an individual, 
without the accompanying change in what may legitimately be concluded. Our aim is not to 
disparage the instruments, all of which do what they were designed to do, but to be precise

2. METHODS 
This is a critical narrative review. It was not conducted as a systematic review, was not 
registered, and does not claim exhaustive coverage of the literatures it draws on. It is reported 
in accordance with SANRA, the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles, 
which asks that a narrative review justify its importance, state its aims, describe how the 
literature was identified, reference appropriately, and present evidence and data so that the 
reader can judge the strength of each claim.¹⁴ 
Literature was identified through searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus and the Cochrane 
Library from database inception to September 2026, using combinations of the terms diet 
quality index, nutrient adequacy, dietary reference intakes, estimated average requirement, 
tolerable upper intake level, nutrient excess, dietary supplements, fortification, U-shaped, 
non-monotonic and nutrient toxicity. Reference lists of retrieved articles were hand-searched, 
and the primary reference-value documents of the National Academies of Sciences, 
Engineering, and Medicine, the UK Committee on Medical Aspects of Food Policy and its 
successor the Scientific Advisory Committee on Nutrition, the UK Expert Group on Vitamins 
and Minerals, and the European Food Safety Authority were consulted directly and not 
through secondary summaries. 
A targeted search was also made for prior instruments of the kind proposed in Section 8. 
Alongside the database searches described above, we examined the dietary guidance and 
reference-value documents of the United States Departments of Agriculture and of Health 
and Human Services, the European Food Safety Authority and the United Kingdom expert 
committees, together with the defining publication of each diet-quality instrument examined, 
for any scoring scheme applying a two-sided rule at the level of an individual nutrient or 
counting supplemental intake. Neither the national dietary guidance of the three jurisdictions 
nor any of those instruments does either; a smaller family of nutrient-based indices that does 
is described in Section 6. The search was purposive, not systematic, and included no 
controlled-vocabulary or patent search, so statements here about the absence of a prior 
instrument record what we located rather than what exists. 
Sources were selected purposively to support a critical argument and to represent competing 
positions where the evidence is contested, particularly on the upper limbs of intake–risk 
curves, where the literature is thinner and the trial evidence is mostly of supplementation, not 
of habitual dietary intake. Selection and appraisal were performed by the authors without 
duplicate independent screening. The same applies to the assignment of nutrients to evidence 
tiers in Section 8.4: it is a structured summary of the authors' reading of the located literature, 
not a formal evidence appraisal, and it has not been replicated by an independent reader. The 
review is therefore subject to selection bias, and readers should treat the coverage of each 
individual nutrient as illustrative and not as complete. Where we describe the properties of a 
published index, we cite the index’s own defining publication and have verified the scoring 
description against it. 
3. THE REFERENCE VALUES, AND WHAT EACH ONE LICENSES 
3.1 The guidance, and the practice 
The Dietary Reference Intakes comprise four values, and the report devoted to their 
application in dietary assessment is unambiguous about which of them supports which 
https://doi.org/10.33774/coe-2026-h1smp ORCID: https://orcid.org/0000-0003-1085-3157 Content not peer-reviewed by Cambridge University Press. License: All Rights Reserved
4 
inference.¹² The estimated average requirement (EAR) is the median requirement of a group: 
half of a comparable population requires more, half less. The recommended dietary 
allowance (RDA) is derived from the EAR and set roughly two standard deviations above it, 
so as to meet the requirements of approximately 97.5% of the population. The adequate 
intake (AI) is used where the evidence was insufficient to derive an EAR at all. The tolerable 
upper intake level (UL) marks the highest habitual intake expected to pose no risk of adverse 
effects. We refer to this value as the upper limit throughout, and use UL only where brevity 
requires it; its counterparts in the United Kingdom and European systems are named in 
Figure 1. 
The consequence, stated explicitly in the guidance and repeated in the subsequent 
applications literature, is that the RDA should not be used to assess the adequacy of an 
individual’s intake.¹²,¹³ An intake below the RDA cannot be called inadequate, because the 
RDA exceeds the requirement of all but a small fraction of people; a person consuming 
below it may comfortably exceed their own requirement. The EAR is the value against which 
an individual’s usual intake should be compared, and the comparison yields a probability 
rather than a verdict. Where only an AI exists, an intake above it suggests low probability of 
inadequacy, but an intake below it supports no quantitative conclusion whatever — a point 
that is regularly lost in translation. 
Practice diverges from this guidance in a specific and consequential way. The RDA is the 
value that appears on food labels, in consumer applications, in supplement marketing and in 
most personal target-setting tools, and it is routinely presented as a threshold that individuals 
should reach. The inferential error is not that the RDA is a bad number; it is that a value 
constructed to sit above almost everyone’s requirement is being used as though it were each 
person’s requirement. The result systematically overstates the prevalence of individual 
inadequacy and, where it drives supplementation, pushes intakes toward the upper limb of a 
curve whose position is often unknown. 
The distinction between group and individual assessment sharpens the point. For a group, the 
prevalence of inadequate intake can be estimated by the EAR cut-point method, in which the 
proportion of the group whose usual intake falls below the EAR approximates the proportion 
whose intake is inadequate, provided intakes and requirements are approximately 
independent and the requirement distribution is roughly symmetric.¹³,¹⁵ Where those 
conditions fail, as they do for iron in menstruating women because the requirement 
distribution is markedly skewed, a full probability approach is required instead. For an 
individual, neither method returns a verdict: the output is a probability that the person’s usual 
intake is below their own unobserved requirement, and that probability is bounded away from 
zero and one across most of the plausible intake range. 
What follows is a claim about the honesty of outputs; the arithmetic is not in dispute. An 
assessment tool that reports “you are not meeting your requirement for magnesium” has 
asserted something it cannot know. One that reports “your usual intake is below the median 
requirement, so there is a moderate probability that it is inadequate for you” has said what the 
data support. The second formulation is less satisfying and considerably more defensible, and 
the gap between the two is where a large amount of consumer nutrition advice currently 
operates.

3.3 Measurement error compounds the problem 
Even the correct comparison is fragile in practice, because the quantity being compared is 
measured badly. Biomarker-based validation studies have shown substantial and 
systematically biased under-reporting of energy and protein intake by both food-frequency 
questionnaires and 24-hour recalls, with the degree of misreporting related to body mass 
index and other participant characteristics.¹⁷,¹⁸ Because usual intake must be estimated from a 
small number of observation days, within-person variability further widens the uncertainty 
around any individual estimate.¹⁹ An individual-level adequacy statement therefore carries 
two distinct sources of error: the requirement is unknown, and the intake is imprecisely 
measured. Honest reporting would attach an interval to it and not a point estimate. 
4. THE TWO-SIDED INTAKE–RISK RELATIONSHIP 
4.1 The shape of the curve, and what the reference values mark on it 
The canonical representation of nutrient risk, formalised in the risk-assessment model the 
National Academies adopted for setting upper limits, plots probability of an adverse outcome 
against habitual intake and shows two rising limbs separated by a range of intakes at which 
neither deficiency nor excess is expected.⁸ Figure 2 reproduces this structure and marks the 
reference values on it. 
 
Figure 2. The two-sided intake–risk relationship and the reference values that mark it. Risk rises at both low 
and high habitual intake, separated by a range in which neither is expected. The lower limb carries two values 
derived from a requirement distribution (EAR or AR; RDA, RNI or PRI); the upper limb carries one derived by 
risk assessment (UL). The adequate intake (AI) is used where no requirement estimate could be derived. The 
distance between lower and upper values differs markedly by nutrient and is not to sc ale. 
Three features of that figure carry unusual weight. The reference values are not symmetric: 
the lower limb is populated by two values derived from requirement distributions, while the 
upper limb is marked by a single value derived from a no-observed-adverse-effect level and 
an uncertainty factor, and the two are not commensurable. The distance between them varies 
enormously by nutrient, from wide for vitamin C or thiamin to narrow for iodine, selenium 
and preformed vitamin A, so that the practical difficulty of staying between the cliffs is 
specific to the nutrient and does not generalise. And for a substantial number of nutrients no

upper level has been set at all, which reflects absence of evidence rather than evidence of 
safety. 
The deficiency limb nevertheless remains the more important of the two at global scale. 
Dietary risks were estimated to account for eleven million deaths worldwide in 2017, the 
largest attributable burden of any single risk factor assessed in that analysis, and the great 
majority of that burden reflects inadequate intake of protective foods, not excess of 
micronutrients.²⁰ Nothing in this review should be read as suggesting that the upper limb 
deserves equal weight in global nutrition policy. The argument is narrower: in populations 
where fortification and supplementation are widespread, the upper limb becomes relevant for 
a non-trivial minority of individuals, and the instruments used to assess those individuals 
cannot currently see it. 
4.2 Where the upper limb is well characterised 
For a minority of nutrients, the excess side is documented with a confidence approaching that 
of the deficiency side. Iodine intake shows a well-described U-shaped relationship with 
thyroid dysfunction, with both insufficiency and excess implicated in hypothyroidism and in 
autoimmune thyroiditis.¹,² Selenium has a comparably narrow window: status below 
approximately 100 µg/L is associated with adverse outcomes, while supplementation in 
already-replete populations has been associated with an increased incidence of type 2 
diabetes.³,⁴ Pyridoxine at high supplemental doses produces a sensory neuropathy that was 
described in detail four decades ago and continues to be reported.⁵ Iron overload, whether 
genetic or iatrogenic, produces well-characterised organ damage.⁶ Chronic high-dose zinc 
induces copper deficiency.⁷ 
Two trial results deserve particular emphasis because they illustrate that the upper limb can 
be steep enough to reverse the direction of a public-health recommendation. Beta-carotene 
supplementation, undertaken on the strength of consistent observational associations between 
carotenoid intake and lower lung-cancer risk, increased lung-cancer incidence in smokers in 
two independent trials.²¹,²² A meta-analysis of high-dose vitamin E supplementation trials 
reported a dose-dependent relationship with all-cause mortality.²³ Whatever the residual 
controversy about the pooled estimates, the episode establishes the principle: for these 
nutrients, the assumption that more is better was not merely unsupported but wrong. 
4.3 Two structural features of the limits themselves 
The first is coverage: for a substantial minority of nutrients no upper limit exists at all. In the 
North American system a numeric UL has been set for eight vitamins (vitamin A, vitamin C, 
vitamin D, vitamin E, niacin, vitamin B6, folate and choline) and for around fifteen minerals 
and trace elements, while seven vitamins are formally recorded as not determinable: vitamin 
K, thiamin, riboflavin, vitamin B12, pantothenic acid, biotin and the carotenoids.¹⁵ Those 
entries are frequently misread. They do not certify safety. The National Academies record 
them as not determinable "due to lack of data of adverse effects", and caution explicitly that 
this "does not mean that there is no potential for adverse effects resulting from high 
intakes".¹⁵ Absence of a limit is absence of evidence. 
The second is width. Where both a requirement and a limit exist, the arithmetic distance 
between them is narrower than the phrase "upper limit" tends to suggest. Taking the 
published adult values and dividing the limit by the recommended intake gives, for men: 
vitamin A roughly 3.3-fold (900 µg retinol activity equivalents against a limit of 3,000 µg of 
preformed retinol), zinc 3.6-fold (11 mg against 40 mg), iron 5.6-fold (8 mg against 45 mg), 
selenium 7.3-fold (55 µg against 400 µg), and iodine 7.3-fold (150 µg against 1,100 µg).²⁴

Vitamin C is more forgiving at roughly 22-fold (90 mg against 2,000 mg), and calcium

Magnesium is an instructive anomaly, exposing how easily these values are misread. Its 
recommended intake for men is 400–420 mg while its upper limit is 350 mg, so that the 
ceiling appears to sit beneath the floor. The resolution is that the two values do not refer to 
the same quantity: the magnesium limit "represent[s] intake from a pharmacological agent 
only and do[es] not include intake from food and water".²⁵ The same scope-dependence 
applies elsewhere. The folate limit applies to synthetic folic acid from fortified food and 
supplements but not to food folate; the vitamin A limit applies to preformed retinol only, not 
to provitamin A carotenoids; the niacin and vitamin E limits likewise apply to synthetic or 
supplemental forms.²⁴,²⁶ A ratio computed without regard to scope is meaningless, and any 
index that compares intake against a limit must know which intake the limit was written 
about. 
Upper limits are also less uniform in their evidentiary basis than their presentation suggests. 
They are derived by dividing a no-observed-adverse-effect or lowest-observed-adverse-effect 
level by a composite uncertainty factor that has ranged from 1 to 36 across nutrients.¹² 
Renwick’s appraisal of micronutrient toxicology is blunt about the consequence: limited data 
exist on the toxicity of most micronutrients, few studies meet the criteria applied to other 
food chemicals, and there is no clear scientific rationale for the reduced uncertainty factors 
used in this field.²⁷ Jurisdictions consequently disagree on the same nutrient. The selenium 
limit is 400 µg in the United States but 255 µg in the European Union, and the vitamin E 
limit 1,000 mg against 300 mg.²⁸,²⁹ A bidirectional index should therefore weight its upper-
side penalties by the strength of the evidence behind each limit instead of treating every limit 
as an equally firm edge.

The distinction drawn in the final column matters. A tolerable upper intake level is a risk-
assessment construct, derived by applying uncertainty factors to a no-observed-adverse-effect 
level, and its existence does not imply that adverse outcomes have been observed at intakes 
just above it in free-living populations.⁸ For several nutrients the limit rests on a single study 
in a small sample. Treating every limit as an equally firm cliff edge would overstate what is 
known, and a bidirectional index should weight its upper-side penalties according to the 
strength of the underlying evidence instead of applying a uniform rule. 
It is also worth correcting an impression that the short list above may create. Documented 
effects of excess are not confined to a handful of nutrients. Assembling the excess-side 
literature for every micronutrient carrying a reference value yields described adverse effects 
for the large majority of them; what differs between nutrients is not whether harm has been 
reported but the design of the studies reporting it. Section 8 grades that evidence nutrient by 
nutrient and uses the grading to set the depth of the scoring penalty. 
4.5 Where it is not — and why that matters for index design 
There is a further asymmetry in how the two sides of the curve have been studied, and it is 
visible in the structure of the literature and not in any single study. Deficiency has been 
aggregated across nutrients: one analysis of a national survey reports that 31% of the United 
States population is at risk of at least one vitamin deficiency or anaemia, with 6.3% at risk in 
two nutrients and 1.7% in three to five.³⁰ No equivalent analysis exists on the excess side. No 
published estimate exists, for any general population, of the proportion exceeding the upper 
limit for one or more nutrients considered together. The question has been asked 
systematically of deficiency and not of excess. 
The two sides also differ in the character of their evidence. Nutrient toxicities tend to be 
documented as recognisable clinical entities reported in case series, not in cohorts or trials: 
zinc-induced copper deficiency, with its combination of myeloneuropathy and cytopenias, is 
described almost entirely in this way.³¹ Such evidence establishes that a harm exists but 
generalises poorly to dose–response, which is precisely what is needed to place a threshold. 
Deficiency, by contrast, has accumulated a large secondary and interventional literature. The 
consequence is not that excess-side evidence is of poor quality but that it is differently 
shaped, and less suited to the quantitative task of drawing a curve. 
No bibliometric analysis quantifies this contrast, so we make the point qualitatively and not 
numerically. It is nonetheless recognised within the bodies that set the values. Reviews of 
upper-level methodology note the limited confidence attaching to individual limits and the 
difficulty of grading excess-risk evidence consistently across nutrients.³² 
This asymmetry has a direct implication for index construction, and it limits our own 
argument. An index cannot score bidirectionally for a nutrient whose upper limb has not been 
located. Any bidirectional scoring scheme will therefore be partial, applying two-sided rules

4.6 Two nutrients can interact across their limbs 
A further complication, and one that no current index represents, is that the upper limb of one 
nutrient can interact with the deficiency state of another. The most discussed instance 
concerns folic acid and vitamin B12. High folic acid intake can correct the anaemia of B12 
deficiency while the neurological damage progresses, and the concern that fortification might 
therefore mask B12 deficiency in older adults has shaped fortification policy debates for two 
decades.³³ The evidence remains contested — the interaction is better established as a 
biochemical phenomenon than as a population-level harm, and observational findings linking 
high folate with low B12 status to cognitive outcomes have been difficult to replicate 
consistently.³⁴ We cite it not as settled but as an illustration of a structural point: a scoring 
system that treats each nutrient as an independent component cannot represent a risk that 
arises from the joint configuration of two. 
4.7 Where the two-sided framing is genuinely contested 
For two of the most prominent candidate U-shapes, the shape itself is disputed. Sodium is the 
clearest case. Observational analyses using morning fasting urine estimates have reported a J-
shaped association with cardiovascular outcomes, with the lowest risk at intakes above those 
recommended by guidelines.³⁵ Critics have argued that the finding is an artefact of reverse 
causation and of systematic error in estimating usual sodium intake from spot samples, and 
the trial evidence on blood pressure continues to support reduction. The disagreement is 
methodological and not merely interpretive, and it has not been resolved. 
Vitamin D is the second. Non-linear Mendelian randomisation has been reported to support a 
causal contribution of low status to dementia risk,³⁶ while large supplementation trials have 
not shown the corresponding benefits for major clinical outcomes,³⁷ and reanalyses using 
revised non-linear methods have weakened the earlier causal claims. A bidirectional index 
would have to take a position on both nutrients, and neither position could currently be 
described as evidence-based in the strong sense. Alcohol offers a third instance, where an 
apparent protective effect at low intake has been challenged by burden-of-disease and 
Mendelian randomisation analyses concluding that the intake minimising health loss is zero.³⁸ 
Our proposal is deliberately restricted to nutrients where the direction of the upper limb is not 
in dispute; extending it to sodium, vitamin D or alcohol would import a controversy rather 
than resolve one. 
5. THE SUPPLEMENT CHANNEL 
Everything said so far concerns the shape of the intake–risk relationship. It leaves open a 
practical question that determines whether any of it matters: by what route does an ordinary 
person in a high-income country actually arrive at an intake near the upper limb? Analyses 
that decompose usual intake into naturally occurring food, enrichment and fortification, and 
supplements give a reasonably clear answer for adults, and a more complicated one for 
everyone else.³⁹⁻⁴¹ 
For most nutrients, and for the population aged two years and over, supplements are the 
largest incremental contributor to intakes above a limit. Adding them to food and fortification 
raises the proportion above the limit from essentially zero to around 2% for iron, 2.6% for 
calcium, and 0.5% for vitamin B6 and vitamin D.⁴² For three nutrients, however, fortification

alone already produces material exceedance before any supplement is taken: in the same 
population, 8.8% exceed the folic acid limit and 1.9% the niacin limit from enriched food 
alone, and 4.6% exceed the zinc limit from naturally occurring food by itself.⁴² 
In young children the ordering reverses. Among those aged two to eight, food and 
fortification alone place 41.7% above the folate limit, 39.9% above the zinc limit and 10.1% 
above the niacin limit; supplements raise these further, to 52.4%, 46.3% and 18.5%, but they

— the paediatric limits for zinc, vitamin A and folate are extrapolated from adult data with 
large uncertainty factors,⁴⁰ and several of the investigators who report these figures argue the 
limits themselves may be set too low.⁴³ 
Nor are whole diets uniformly self-limiting. For a small set of nutrient–food combinations an 
ordinary portion exceeds the limit outright: a single serving of liver supplies preformed 
vitamin A at several times the adult limit; five or six Brazil nuts approach or exceed the 
selenium limit; dried seaweed can deliver iodine at many multiples of it; and a modest 
serving of oysters can meet or exceed the zinc limit. Documented toxicity has followed each 
of these.⁴⁰ A heavy enriched-grain intake, the fifteen to twenty slices of bread that a large 
eater might consume, can likewise bring niacin toward its limit from food alone, which is 
consistent with fortification, not supplementation, being the operative route for that nutrient. 
One inference that might seem natural does not survive contact with the evidence. Since 
around 72% of United States adults are overweight or obese (crude prevalence 72.4%),⁴⁴ and 
micronutrient intake rises with energy intake, one might expect high-energy eaters to exceed 
limits from food alone. The literature points the other way. Energy-dense diets are 
characteristically micronutrient-dilute, and people with obesity show higher rates of 
micronutrient inadequacy and not of excess.⁴⁵⁻⁴⁷ No published analysis has stratified food-
only exceedance by energy intake and found it rising. Eating a great deal is not, on the 
current evidence, a route to the upper limb; it is more often a route to the lower one. 
5.1 The doses are not marginal 
The first thing to establish is the scale of the discrepancy between what physiology requires 
and what the market supplies, because it is larger than most discussions of nutrient excess 
assume. Vitamin B12 is the clearest case. The adult requirement is 2.4 µg per day. Retail 
preparations are sold at 500, 1,000 and 5,000 µg per tablet, between roughly 200 and 2,000 
times requirement. Biotin, with an adequate intake of 30 µg, is marketed at 5,000 to 20,000 
µg for hair and nail indications, some 170 to 670 times requirement, and is used in multiple 
sclerosis research at 300 mg, approximately ten thousand times. Figure 3 sets out the 
comparison across nutrients on a logarithmic scale, which is the only scale on which it can be

5.2 Chronic exposure is the relevant exposure 
The reassurance usually offered about water-soluble vitamins derives from single-dose 
pharmacokinetic studies. Those studies are sound, and their finding is real: absorption of 
several water-soluble vitamins saturates, and surplus appears in urine. Vitamin C is 
completely absorbed at a single 200 mg dose, with bioavailability declining above 500 mg 
and the absorbed excess excreted, from which the authors concluded that daily doses above 
400 mg have no evident value.⁴⁸,⁴⁹ Vitamin B12 absorption via intrinsic factor saturates at 
roughly 1.5–2 µg per meal, so that only about one per cent of a large oral dose crosses by 
passive diffusion.⁵⁰ 
But the exposure of interest is not a single dose. It is 500 µg taken every morning for three 
months, or three years. Chronic high intake is a different pharmacological situation, and there 
is mechanistic reason to expect adaptation, since indefinite unchanged handling is unlikely: 
high ascorbate concentrations down-regulate the intestinal transporter SVCT1, reducing 
uptake substantially in cell models, and the transporters for thiamin, riboflavin, biotin and 
pantothenate are likewise substrate-regulated.⁵¹⁻⁵³ Direct human data on transporter 
adaptation under sustained megadosing are lacking; this is a mechanistically supported 
expectation, not a demonstrated clinical phenomenon. The narrower and safer claim is simply 
that acute pharmacokinetics do not license confident inference about chronic exposure at two 
or three orders of magnitude above requirement, and that reassurance drawn from the former 
is being applied to the latter without warrant.

5.3 Who actually exceeds the limits 
Population survey data identify the route. Roughly 58% of United States adults report using a 
dietary supplement in the preceding thirty days, rising to about 80% of women aged sixty and 
over; industry surveys report a higher figure of around 74%, a difference attributable to non-
probability online panels and a broader definition of use, and not to any real disagreement 
about behaviour.⁵⁴,⁵⁵ Among adults, adding supplements to food raises the proportion 
exceeding an upper limit for calcium, iron, selenium, zinc, vitamin A, folic acid, vitamin B6, 
vitamin C and vitamin D. For most nutrients and most age groups the proportion affected 
remains below five per cent.⁵⁶ It is not uniformly small, however, and the exceptions matter: 
in an earlier survey cycle 10.3% of the population aged two and over exceeded the limit for 
niacin and 8.4% for zinc once fortification and supplements were counted,⁴¹ and 9.0% of 
adults aged 51 to 70 exceeded the calcium limit.⁵⁶ Among supplement users specifically, 
roughly one in ten exceeded the limits for iron and for zinc.⁵⁷ 
In children the figures are of a different order. Among supplement-using children aged two to 
eight years, 84% exceed the upper limit for zinc, against 45% of non-users, with 72% above 
the vitamin A limit and 71% above that for folic acid.⁴⁰ Canadian survey data show a similar 
pattern, with more than 80% of children exceeding the limits for vitamin A and niacin in 
some age groups.⁵⁸ One caveat: the paediatric zinc limit is extrapolated from adult data and 
its adverse-effect basis is contested, so these proportions indicate exposure above a stipulated 
threshold, not demonstrated harm. 
One limitation cuts against the reassurance the small per-nutrient percentages appear to offer. 
Every published analysis reports exceedance one nutrient at a time. We could find no 
estimate, for any general population, of the proportion of people exceeding the limit for at 
least one nutrient considered across the whole set, even though the corresponding aggregation 
has been performed on the deficiency side.³⁰ The distinction is not trivial. What can be said 
without further data is only that the aggregate is bounded below by the largest single-nutrient 
rate and above by the sum of the individual rates; independence would place it near the upper 
bound and clustering among the same multivitamin-using individuals pulls it toward the 
lower, and the interval between them is wide. We do not attempt to narrow it. The analysis 
that would is neither novel nor expensive: apply the usual-intake methods already used for 
single nutrients to the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey dietary recalls 
combined with its Dietary Supplement Database, and report the proportion of participants 
whose usual intake exceeds the upper limit for at least one nutrient in a prespecified set. Until 
that is done, the per-nutrient framing cannot be taken to bound population-level over-
exposure. 
Product composition compounds this. An analytical survey of adult multivitamin and mineral 
products found niacin labelled above its upper limit in 31% of products, with smaller 
proportions above the limit for magnesium, manganese, zinc, vitamin B6 and vitamin C.⁵⁹,⁶⁰ 
Measured content also departs from label: the same programme found most ingredients 
present in excess of the declared amount, by around a quarter for selenium and iodine. 
5.4 Dose multiples do not by themselves demonstrate harm 
It would be convenient to reason directly from these multiples to harm, but for several of the 
most extravagantly dosed nutrients the evidence of harm is thin or absent. Vitamin B12 has 
no upper limit precisely because no adverse effect has been demonstrated, and there is no 
controlled evidence that a daily intake two hundred times the requirement produces 
symptomatic harm in a healthy adult.¹⁵,⁵⁰ The one reproducible effect attributed to high-dose 
vitamin B12 alone is an acneiform eruption, described in case reports and a small case series 
https://doi.org/10.33774/coe-2026-h1smp ORCID: https://orcid.org/0000-0003-1085-3157 Content not peer-reviewed by Cambridge University Press. License: All Rights Reserved
14 
and for which a mechanism has been demonstrated experimentally, and it is uncommon.⁶¹⁻⁶³ 
Where trials of high-dose B vitamins have reported excess cancer or accelerated renal 
decline, the preparations combined folic acid with vitamin B12 and sometimes vitamin B6, 
and the signal is generally attributed to folic acid; these designs cannot isolate a single 
agent.⁶⁴⁻⁶⁶ Observational associations between high circulating vitamin B12 and cancer are, 
on the evidence, largely a matter of reverse causation, since hepatic disease and malignancy 
themselves raise measured concentrations.⁶⁷ 
The defensible claim is narrower than the dose multiples might invite, and we state it 
deliberately. It is not that a two-hundred-fold multiple of requirement is known to be harmful. 
It is that such an exposure, sustained for years, is one about which the long-term evidence is 
largely absent in either direction, and that the absence of an upper limit — a statement about 
what has been studied — has been widely read as a statement about what is safe. That 
inferential slide is the problem, and it is a problem an assessment instrument could at least 
make visible by recording the intake. 
5.5 Water solubility does not predict safety 
Underlying the whole pattern is a heuristic that deserves to be named and retired: that water-
soluble vitamins are inherently safe because surplus is excreted. It is memorable, it is taught, 
and it is wrong often enough to be dangerous. 
Vitamin B6 is the decisive case. It is freely water-soluble, and it causes a dose- and duration-
dependent sensory neuropathy, classically described at multi-gram intakes but reported at a 
few hundred milligrams daily and, in recent regulatory review, without a clearly identifiable 
threshold.⁵,⁶⁸ The European Food Safety Authority reduced its upper limit to 12 mg per day in 
2023.⁶⁸ Australia's Therapeutic Goods Administration has required a peripheral-neuropathy 
warning on products supplying more than 10 mg daily since 2022, and in November 2025 
rescheduled oral preparations supplying more than 50 mg but not more than 200 mg per 
recommended daily dose as Pharmacist Only medicines with effect from 1 June 2027; 
preparations above 200 mg daily were already prescription-only.⁶⁹,⁷⁰ Niacin, also water-
soluble and essentially completely absorbed even at gram doses, causes flushing at intakes 
barely above requirement and hepatotoxicity at pharmacological doses.⁵⁰ Chronic high-dose 
vitamin C produces dose-dependent hyperoxaluria and is associated with kidney stones in 
men.⁷¹ Folic acid at fortification-plus-supplement intakes leaves unmetabolised folic acid in 
circulation and can mask the haematological presentation of B12 deficiency, although its 
supposed acceleration of the accompanying neuropathy is contested.⁷²,⁷³ Biotin causes no 
toxicity in the conventional sense yet produces a clinically serious harm of a different kind, 
interfering with streptavidin-based immunoassays including cardiac troponin; the regulator 
has received one report of a death following a falsely low troponin result, although causation 
in that case was not established.⁷⁴ 
Set against this, the fat-soluble vitamin K has no upper limit because no adverse effect has 
been demonstrated for phylloquinone or the menaquinones — while the synthetic, water-
soluble vitamer menadione is hepatotoxic and has been withdrawn from human use.²⁴ The 
solubility rule does not merely fail at the margins; for vitamin K it inverts. The point is not 
new: these arguments were set out in a review published four decades ago.⁷⁵ They have not 
been absorbed into how nutrient intake is assessed.

There is a second and more consequential omission, which follows directly from the previous 
section. None of these instruments counts supplements. This is not an oversight but a design 
decision, stated explicitly for the Healthy Eating Index, which assesses diet in concordance 
with the dietary guidelines’ focus on meeting nutrient needs from foods and beverages rather

structural: because supplements are the route by which individuals reach intakes above upper 
limits, an instrument that excludes them cannot detect excess even in principle. It can 
measure dietary pattern quality and it can measure adequacy; it is blind to the tail of the 
distribution where the second cliff lies. Food-group diversity indicators share the same 
structural limitation for the same reason: a supplement is not a food group, so no count of 
groups can register it. 
What none of them does is apply a two-sided rule at the level of the nutrient. The moderation 
components operate on food groups and dietary constituents chosen for their association with 
chronic disease (sodium, added sugars, saturated fat, processed meat), not on micronutrients 
with defined upper limits. An intake of preformed vitamin A, selenium or iodine well above 
the UL is invisible to every instrument in the table, because none of them scores those 
nutrients at all, in either direction. The gap is therefore narrower than it first appears, and 
more specific: it lies at the micronutrient level, where upper limits exist and are not 
consulted.

should be read against a smaller nutrient-based literature; it does not fill an empty space. The

decline across a graded interval; where a lower threshold is stated explicitly it is a 
recommended intake rather than the median requirement, which carries forward the 
inferential problem set out in Section 3; supplements, where counted, are added into a single 
total, not separated from enrichment and fortification; and the depth of any penalty is not 
scaled to the strength of the evidence behind the limit. None of these indices has entered 
routine surveillance use. 
7. IMPLICATIONS FOR INDEX CONSTRUCTION AND FOR PRACTICE 
We propose four changes, ordered from least to most demanding. 
State the reference value and the inference together. Any instrument that judges an 
individual’s intake should name the reference value it uses and the conclusion that value 
supports. Where the EAR exists, it should be the comparator, and the output should be 
expressed as a probability of inadequacy and not as a pass or fail. Where only an AI exists, 
the instrument should say that intakes below it are uninformative. This costs nothing but 
candour. 
Count supplements. This is the single change that would do most, because it is the channel 
through which excess occurs. An instrument that omits supplements is not merely 
incomplete; it is systematically unable to observe the phenomenon a bidirectional score exists 
to detect. Total usual intake from food and supplements can be modelled with established 
survey methods, and any index intending to speak about the upper end must do so. 
Supplements must also be separated from enrichment and fortification instead of being folded 
into a single total, because several limits are defined for a particular chemical form or source 
and an undifferentiated total cannot be compared against them. 
Score bidirectionally where the evidence allows. For the subset of nutrients with a defined 
limit and a documented upper limb (iodine, selenium, preformed vitamin A, iron, zinc, 
vitamin B6 and a small number of others), a two-sided rule is constructible today, with 
penalties weighted by the evidentiary strength of the limit, not applied uniformly. 
Where no official limit exists, use an established derivation rather than an invented rule. It is 
tempting to fill the gap with a heuristic such as a fixed multiple of the requirement, and we 
considered doing so. It does not survive scrutiny. Absorptive and excretory capacity are 
governed by transporter kinetics and plasma concentration, which bear no necessary 
relationship to a nutrient’s dietary requirement, so a requirement-multiple is dimensionally

8. A PROPOSED TWO-SIDED COMPONENT SCORE 
The preceding sections identified three properties that a nutrient assessment would need if it 
were to represent the upper limb of the intake–risk relationship: it must compare intake 
against a reference value that licenses the inference being drawn, it must count the sources 
through which high intakes are actually reached, and it must be able to score an intake down 
for being too high. No instrument in routine use has any of the three, and the nutrient-based 
indices described in Section 6 have some but not all. This section specifies a component score 
that would combine them. It is a design proposal derived from the evidence reviewed above; 
it has not been constructed, applied to survey data or validated against any outcome, and 
nothing here constitutes a recommendation for use. 
Set against that literature, what is proposed here is a combination rather than a first. Its 
elements are a continuous function anchored on the estimated average requirement at the 
lower end and on the upper limit at the upper end, declining across a graded interval instead 
of switching at a threshold; application to every micronutrient carrying a reference value and 
not to a selected set; decomposition of intake into food, enrichment and fortification, and 
supplements; and a penalty depth scaled to the strength of the evidence behind each limit. We 
are not aware of a published index combining these, but for the reason given in Section 2 that 
records only what we located, not what exists. 
8.1 Which nutrients it covers 
The module applies to every micronutrient carrying a reference value, whether or not an 
upper limit has been set for it. In practice that is the set of vitamins, minerals and trace 
elements for which the National Academies, the United Kingdom expert committees or the 
European Food Safety Authority publish a requirement or an adequate intake. Table 4 lists 
twenty-six entries and not one per nutrient because thiamin, riboflavin and pantothenic acid 
share a row, the evidence position being identical for all three. Nutrients that carry a 
reference value but for which we located no excess-side outcome literature (molybdenum is 
one) do not appear in Table 4; under the module they would be scored on the deficiency side 
and recorded in Tier 4 on the upper side. Two considerations make the wider scope preferable 
to restricting it to nutrients with demonstrated harm. 
The first is that documented effects of excess are not confined to a few nutrients. Assembling 
the excess-side literature for each micronutrient yields described adverse effects for the large 
https://doi.org/10.33774/coe-2026-h1smp ORCID: https://orcid.org/0000-0003-1085-3157 Content not peer-reviewed by Cambridge University Press. License: All Rights Reserved
19 
majority, from randomised evidence at one end to single clinical reports at the other (Table 
4). Restricting the module to a shortlist would omit most of what is known. 
The second is that a nutrient without an upper limit is not thereby a nutrient known to be safe. 
A limit is recorded as not determinable when adverse-effect data are insufficient, a distinction 
the National Academies state explicitly.²⁷ Excluding such nutrients would convert an 
evidence gap into a safety finding — the inference this review has criticised elsewhere. It 
would also fail where it is most needed: as Section 4 showed, the nutrients without limits are 
among those marketed at the highest multiples of requirement, so a module that skipped them 
would be silent precisely where exposure is most extreme. 
The scope is therefore universal, and the strength of evidence is carried explicitly within the 
scoring, not used to decide what enters it. This does not contradict the earlier statement that 
any bidirectional scheme will be partial. Scoring is universal; penalties are not. Every 
micronutrient is scored and every intake recorded, but a penalty on the upper side is applied 
only where evidence supports one, and Tier 4 exists to carry the nutrients where it does not. 
8.2 Anchors 
The lower anchor is the estimated average requirement rather than the recommended dietary 
allowance, for the reason set out in Section 2: the recommended allowance is a population 
reference value and supports no inference about an individual, whereas comparison against 
the median requirement yields a probability that can be honestly reported. The upper anchor 
is the upper limit where one exists, and where none does, the Guidance Level derived by the 
United Kingdom expert group from the highest intake at which no adverse effect has been 
observed.⁹³,⁹⁴ Because jurisdictions disagree (the selenium limit is 400 µg in the United States 
against 255 µg in the European Union, and the vitamin E limit 1,000 mg against 300 mg), an 
implementation must declare which system it uses and apply it consistently across all 
nutrients instead of selecting the more convenient value for each. We would use the reference 
values of the jurisdiction in which the assessment is made, since those are the values against 
which its dietary guidance is written; where a nutrient carries a limit in one system and not in 
another, the available value should be used and the asymmetry reported. For nutrients with 
neither, the module records intake but applies no upper-side penalty. 
8.3 The scoring function 
The function is piecewise and asymmetric, mirroring the asymmetry of the evidence on the 
two sides (Figure 5a). The score rises from zero at the lower anchor to a maximum, holds 
across a plateau of intakes at which neither deficiency nor excess is expected, and declines as 
intake approaches and passes the upper anchor. 
Three parameters govern its shape, and each is a judgement rather than a datum: where the 
plateau begins, where it ends, and whether the penalty reaches zero at the upper anchor or 
continues to decline beyond it. We state them as choices requiring justification because the 
alternative, settling them silently, is the practice this review criticises. Different defensible 
choices will produce different scores for the same diet, and any application should report how 
sensitive its results are to them.

8.4 Four evidence tiers 
Upper limits are not equally well founded. They are derived by dividing an observed adverse-
effect level by an uncertainty factor that has ranged from 1 to 36 across nutrients, sometimes 
on the basis of a single study or of animal data, and jurisdictions differ by up to three-fold on 
the same nutrient.⁸,²⁷⁻²⁹ Applying an identical penalty above every limit would treat these as 
equivalent. We therefore propose that the depth of the upper-side penalty be scaled to the 
evidence behind the limit, in four tiers defined as follows. 
Tier 1. Harm demonstrated in humans by systematic review, meta-analysis or randomised 
trial. The penalty is applied in full: the component score falls to zero at the upper anchor. 
Tier 2. Harm documented in clinical series, cohort studies, or the derivation of the upper 
limit itself. The penalty is attenuated: the score falls to an intermediate value and not to zero. 
Tier 3. Harm described in case reports, animal studies or authoritative clinical summaries, 
without dose–response data. The penalty is shallow and functions as a flag rather than a 
sanction. 
Tier 4. No adverse effect identified at any studied intake. No penalty is applied. The intake is 
nonetheless recorded and reported as a multiple of requirement, accompanied by the 
statement that the upper end of the range has not been characterised. This is a weaker claim 
than a penalty and a stronger one than silence, and it is what the evidence supports. It should 
also be said plainly that this is where the module is weakest, and that its weakness falls 
exactly where Section 5 located the most extreme exposures. The nutrients marketed at the 
largest multiples of requirement — vitamin B12 and biotin above all — are among those for 
which no limit has been set, so no penalty can be justified and none is applied. Recording the 
intake and stating the gap is the most the evidence permits; it is less than the situation 
warrants. 
One distinction should be made explicit, because the tiers and the scope of the proposal 
answer different questions. A tier records how strong the evidence is that harm occurs 
at high intake; it is not a decision to include the nutrient in an operating instrument. 
Sodium and vitamin D are placed in Tier 1 because trial and meta-analytic evidence of 
upper-end harm exists for both, but for the reason given in Section 4.7 we would 
exclude both, and alcohol, from any implementation until the shape of their intake–risk 
relationships is less contested. Table 4 grades evidence; it does not settle scope.

8.5 Intake must be counted by source 
The module requires usual intake disaggregated into naturally occurring food, enrichment and 
fortification, and supplements. This is a requirement rather than a refinement, for two reasons 
established earlier. Several limits are defined for a particular chemical form or source: 
magnesium for supplemental intake only, folate for synthetic folic acid, vitamin A for 
preformed retinol, niacin and vitamin E for synthetic or supplemental forms. An 
undifferentiated total cannot be compared against them without error.²⁸,²⁹,³¹ And the sources

differ in which population they carry past a limit, supplements predominating among adults 
and fortification among young children.⁶¹ No existing diet-quality instrument disaggregates in 
this way, and none counts supplements at all. 
8.6 Two outputs, not one 
The module should report two quantities instead of collapsing them. The first is an adequacy 
statement referenced to the estimated average requirement and expressed as a probability. 
The second is a separate excess flag, referenced to the upper anchor and tiered as above. 
Combining them into a single number would reproduce in miniature the problem this review 
identifies: a person consuming twice the requirement of one nutrient and a tenth of another is 
not well described by an average of the two, and the two findings call for different responses. 
Reporting them separately also keeps visible the distinction between an inference about 
inadequacy, which the reference values support probabilistically, and an observation of intake 
above a stipulated threshold, which is a statement about exposure rather than about harm. 
One consequence of reporting an excess flag needs stating, because it bears directly on 
clinical use. High intakes are frequently deliberate and correct: iron for diagnosed deficiency, 
folic acid in pregnancy, vitamin B12 in pernicious anaemia, vitamin D under supervision for 
documented insufficiency. Each of these would raise the flag. The flag is a statement about 
exposure relative to a population threshold and carries no judgement of clinical 
appropriateness, and any instrument implementing the module should say so in its output 
instead of leaving the inference to the reader. An implementation intended for clinical 
settings would also need an exemption path in which a recorded indication suppresses the 
penalty while preserving the intake record, so that a therapeutic regimen is not scored as a 
dietary fault. Pregnancy is the sharpest instance. The requirement for folate rises from 400 to 
600 µg dietary folate equivalents and that for iron from 18 to 27 mg, while the corresponding 
upper limits, 1,000 µg of folic acid and 45 mg of iron, are unchanged,²⁴,⁵⁰ so the interval 
between the two anchors narrows precisely where guidance directs intake upward. 
Periconceptional folic acid and iron supplementation are among the most widely 
recommended interventions in nutrition, and a woman following that guidance would be 
flagged on both nutrients. In this population an exemption path is not a refinement but a 
precondition of the module being usable at all. 
8.7 What would establish whether this is worth building 
The proposal is testable in three stages of increasing cost. The first is descriptive and could be 
undertaken with existing national survey data: apply the module to intakes recorded with 
supplement use and determine whether it identifies a distinguishable group whom 
conventional scoring rates favourably. The second is biochemical: establish whether that 
group differs in relevant biomarkers, which would indicate that the flag corresponds to 
something in the body and not only in the arithmetic. The third is prognostic and requires 
cohort data with long follow-up: determine whether two-sided scoring carries information 
about incident outcomes beyond conventional scoring. Only the third would justify adoption, 
and we would not advocate use of any such instrument before it. 
9. LIMITATIONS 
This review is narrative and purposive, not systematic, and no formal risk-of-bias appraisal 
was applied to the sources. Coverage of individual nutrients is illustrative. A comprehensive 
account of upper-limb evidence for every nutrient with a defined UL was beyond its scope, 
and the nutrients we discuss were selected partly because their upper limbs are well 
documented, which risks conveying more certainty about the excess side than the literature 
https://doi.org/10.33774/coe-2026-h1smp ORCID: https://orcid.org/0000-0003-1085-3157 Content not peer-reviewed by Cambridge University Press. License: All Rights Reserved
24 
supports. Our characterisation of each index rests on its defining publication and not on how 
it is applied in practice, and applications may differ. The argument is confined to 
micronutrients; macronutrient distribution and energy raise related but distinct issues that we 
have not addressed. It is also developed for non-pregnant adults and for children: pregnancy 
and lactation raise distinct questions: requirements that shift while limits do not, 
supplemental intakes that are deliberately elevated and usually correct, and outcomes arising 
in a second individual. We have not reviewed these, and they would require separate 
treatment before the module were applied in those groups. The scoring module of Section 8 is 
a theoretical proposal: it has not been constructed, applied to data or validated, and its 
parameters are stipulated, not estimated. The evidence tiers of Table 4 reflect the strongest 
design we located for each nutrient and not a systematic appraisal of all excess-side evidence, 
and tier assignment should be revisited as upper limits are revised; several rest on a single 
study, and Tier 4 records an absence of investigation rather than a demonstration of safety. 
Because assignment was made by the authors without duplicate independent screening, and 
because the tier determines the depth of the upper-side penalty, both the tiers and the penalty 
depths derived from them should be treated as a proposal requiring independent replication 
and not as a settled grading. Finally, the review is written from a high-income-setting 
perspective, and its emphasis on excess should not be read as suggesting that the upper limb 
deserves comparable weight in settings where inadequacy remains the dominant nutritional 
problem. 
CONCLUSION 
Nutrient–health relationships have two sides, but the instruments used to assess diet quality 
mostly see one. The gap is narrower than it first appears — several established indices 
already penalise excess at the food-group level — and it is also more specific: at the level of 
individual micronutrients, where upper limits have been defined and where the distance 
between the two cliffs is sometimes small, no widely used index looks upward at all, and the 
nutrient-based indices that do have not entered routine use. Alongside this sits a persistent 
inferential error in which population reference values are read as personal targets. Neither is 
remedied by a better slogan; each requires an explicit statement of what is being measured 
and what may be concluded from it. The evidence base will support a partial bidirectional 
index today, and it will support a fuller one only if the upper limb receives some fraction of 
the attention that has been given to the lower.
