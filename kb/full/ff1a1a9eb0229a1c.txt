Groundwater is the principal source of drinking water for Sargodha Tehsil, Punjab, Pakistan, but its quality is 
increasingly influenced by urbanization, agricultural activities, and changing land
 -
use practices. This study 
aimed to provide a comprehensive spatial assessment of groundwater quality, evaluate its suitability for 
drinking, and identify areas vulnerable to potential health risks. The research employed an integrated 
methodology combining physicochemical analysis, statistical techniques, Geographic 
Information System (GIS)
 -
based spatial analysis, Groundwater Quality Index (GWQI), Human Health Risk Assessment (HHRA), and 
household survey information. 
A total of 200 groundwater samples were systematically collected across Sargodha Tehsil and analyzed for nine 
physicochemical parameters, including pH, TDS, EC, turbidity, total hardness, chloride, sulphate, fluoride, and 
nitrate. The analytical results were compared with World Health Organization (WHO) drinking
 -
water 
guidelines to assess groundwater suitability. Descriptive statistics, frequency distribution, box
 -
and
-
whisker 
plots, and Pearson correlation analysis were applied to determine the variability 
 and relationships among the 
investigated parameters. The results showed considerable variation in groundwater quality, with localized areas 
exhibiting elevated concentrations of several parameters, particularly TDS and nitrate. 
The georeferenced groundwater
 -
quality data were further analyzed in ArcGIS using Spherical Kriging 
interpolation to generate spatial distribution maps. The maps revealed pronounced spatial heterogeneity and 
identified localized groundwater
 -
quality hotspots rather than uniform contamination across the study area. A 
weighted arithmetic Groundwater Quality Index (GWQI) was subsequently calculated using WHO permissible 
limits to provide an integrated assessment of drinking
 -
water suitability. The resulting GWQI 
 classification 
identified areas with comparatively good groundwater quality as well as distinct zones where groundwater 
quality was poor and unsuitable for drinking. 
Human Health Risk Assessment was also conducted to evaluate potential non
 -
carcinogenic health risks 
associated with groundwater consumption through the ingestion pathway. Hazard Index (HI) values were 
estimated for both adults and children. The assessment indicated comparatively higher potential health risks for 
children, particularly in areas where contaminant concentrations were elevated. In addition, household survey 
data regarding drinking
 -
water sources, bore depths, and water
 -
treatment practices were i
 ncorporated to 
provide a broader understanding of groundwater use and community exposure. 
Overall, the study demonstrates significant spatial variability in groundwater quality across Sargodha Tehsil, 
with specific zones requiring greater attention and management. The integration of hydro chemical analysis, 
GIS
 -
based Spherical Kriging, GWQI, HHRA, and household assessment provides a robust framework for 
identifying vulnerable areas and supporting evidence
 -
based groundwater management. The study recommends 
targeted groundwater
 -
quality monitoring, zone
 -
specific water
 -
treatment measures, improvemen
 t of drinking
 -
water infrastructure, public awareness, and sustainable groundwater governance to reduce potential health 
risks and protect groundwater resources in Sargodha Tehsil.

Introduction 
Background of the Study 
Groundwater, the water that percolate beneath the Earth's surface and pack the cracks and pore in soil and rock, is one of 
humanity's most vital, yet often invisible, resources 
 ()
 . It is not just a secondary source; it makes up approximately 99% of all the 
liquid freshwater on our planet that isn't frozen in glaciers and ice caps. This hidden reserve is the backbone of global wat
 er 
security, sustaining human life, civilization, and the environment in ways that are often taken for granted 
 (Spiro, 2025)
 . 
The most direct and essential role of groundwater is providing potable water for billions of people. Globally, about fifty pe
 rcent of 
the Earth's population depends on groundwater, either entirely or in part, for their daily needs 
 (Yan, 2025)
 . In many rural areas 
and regions prone to drought or with limited surface water (like rivers and lakes), groundwater from wells and boreholes is t
 he 
only accessible source of safe water. This reliance is specifically pronounced in dry climates. The water stored underground 
 in 
geological formations called aquifers is often naturally filtered, making it less susceptible to pollution and contamination 
 from 
surface sources than river water 
 (Rizvi, 2025)
 . This relative cleanliness is essential for public health, helping to halt the spread of 
waterborne sickness and meeting basic human right to clean drinking water. Furthermore, many industrial processes, especially 
those requiring high
 -
quality water, also depend on this steady and reliable underground supply 
 (Dawoud, 2025)
 . 
South Asia is a global leader in groundwater use, withdrawing about 50% of the world's total groundwater. The majority of thi
 s 
water about 89% in some areas like India goes toward irrigated agriculture 
 (Wei, 2025)
 . This massive use, often enabled by 
private wells and subsidized electricity for pumps (like tube
 -
wells), has been key to the success of the Green Revolution and 
ensuring food security for billions of people. Without groundwater, food production would be highly vulnerable to the 
unpredictable nature of the South Asian monsoon. 
South Asia is dominated by two actively recharged aquifer systems: the vast Indus Basin (which includes Punjab) and the 
enormous Ganges
-
Brahmaputra
 -
Meghna (GBM) Basin 
 (Balasubramanya, 2025)
 . These two huge systems cover much of Pakistan, 
Northern India, Bangladesh, and Nepal, holding immense reserves of freshwater. These massive alluvial aquifers are constantly 
being replenished by annual monsoon rains, the extensive network of perennial rivers, and seepage from surface irrigation. 
Outside of these main basins, there are also numerous smaller, crucial regional aquifer systems in peninsular India and the 
Himalayan foothills 
 (Rani & Arzjani, 2025)
 . However, the Indus and GBM represent the region's primary, highest
 -
yielding, and 
most actively recharged groundwater resources. It is the life
 -
support system for over 750 million people. 
In Pakistan, the percolation method is a vital strategy for improving groundwater quality, particularly where reliance on 
boreholes is high and surface water sources (like rivers) are increasingly polluted 
 (Panda, Chidambaram, Ganesh, Biswal, & 
Alsabti, 2025)
 . Much like in Punjab, the method involves constructing basins or bunds (e.g., check dams) to slow down fast
 -
moving surface water during monsoon rains. By holding the water in place for an extended period, the method allows for 
maximum seepage into the ground. This process is crucial because it helps address the widespread issue of bacterial and sewag
 e 
contamination, especially in peri
 -
urban areas where wastewater management is often inadequate 
 (Basharat, 2025)
 . 
The groundwater resource in Punjab, often celebrated as India's agricultural heartland and food basket, is currently facing a
 n 
unprecedented environmental crisis that forms the central focus of this research 
 (Kanwar, Khan, & Shoaib, 2025)
 . The problem is 
rooted in the success of the Green Revolution era, where the state rapidly shifted its agricultural strategy. Initially, this 
involved 
the expansion of canal networks, but this quickly gave way to a heavy reliance on groundwater extraction via tube wells. Toda
 y, 
agriculture consumes a disproportionate share of the total water budget approximately 97% making the sustainability of the 
aquifer directly synonymous with the sustainability of the state’s economy 
 (Rizvi, 2025)
 . 
Result of this intensive extraction is a rapid and alarming rate of groundwater depletion. Numerous studies confirm that the 
annual withdrawal of water from the aquifers significantly surpasses the natural rate of recharge from precipitation and cana
 ls 
(Olusanya, 2025)
 . This unsustainable water mining has led to an official designation of nearly 79% of the state's administrative 
blocks as "Over
 -
Exploited" by government bodies. The decline is not uniform; the central districts of Punjab, where rice 
cultivation is most concentrated, are the worst affected, with the water table falling at an average rate of close to half a 
 meter 
every year. This continuous drop forces farmers to bore deeper and deeper wells, which exponentially increases the energy 
required for pumping and places an enormous financial burden on the agricultural community, particularly small and marginal 
landholders 
 (Gebreslassie, 2025)
 .

Beyond the sheer quantity of water, the crisis is compounded by significant issues of water quality and contamination. As the 
water table descends, farmers are forced to draw water from deeper strata, which may contain naturally elevated concentration
 s 
of harmful elements like arsenic and fluoride 
 (Bjornlund, 2025)
 . Simultaneously, the shallow aquifers, which are essential for 
supplementary recharge, are susceptible to contamination from surface
 -
level activities. 
Groundwater challenge in Punjab transcends mere hydrological metrics to become a complex socio
 -
economic and policy 
challenge. The crisis is structurally reinforced by flawed incentive systems, most notably the provision of subsidized or fre
 e 
electricity for agricultural use and the guaranteed procurement of water
 -
intensive crops at a Minimum Support Price (MSP) 
(Kamruzzaman et al., 2025)
 . These policies have created a cycle where farmers are financially incentivized to pump groundwater 
without considering its economic or ecological cost, delaying the urgent adoption of water
 -
saving technologies. The widening gap 
in access to water where large landholders can afford the capital to drill deep submersible wells while poorer farmers face w
 ell 
failure raises critical questions of equity and social justice 
 (Kishore, Birthal, & Srivastava, 2025)
 . 
The quality issue is exacerbated by the problem of groundwater depletion. Punjab, a heavily agrarian region, has an extremely 
high rate of groundwater extraction, primarily for irrigation 
 (Raina, 2025)
 . This rate often far exceeds the natural rate of 
recharge from rainfall, leading to an 'over
 -
exploited' status in most blocks. As the water table drops, deeper and potentially 
poorer quality water is accessed. This depletion also concentrates existing contaminants and, in some cases, mobilizes geogen
 ic 
contaminants like arsenic and fluoride by changing the water chemistry. This significant drop in water level, observed across 
most of the state, creates an urgent challenge for long
 -
term sustainability. 
A percolation tank or pond is essentially a reservoir built across a natural stream or water channel. When it rains, this str
 ucture 
captures surface runoff that would otherwise be lost. Instead of flowing away, the water slowly infiltrates through the soil 
 and 
rock layers the process of percolation to recharge the underlying aquifer. This recharge action directly raises the water tab
 le, 
making water more accessible. Critically, as the water moves through the soil layers, it undergoes natural filtration, which 
 helps 
to reduce the concentration of certain contaminants like nitrates and pesticides before they reach the main groundwater suppl
 y 
(Rizvi, 2025)
 . 
This method also indirectly combats salinity and fluoride issues by diluting the existing, highly concentrated groundwater wi
 th 
fresh, filtered rainwater. The large
 -
scale implementation of percolation tanks can create decentralized points for groundwater 
replenishment, reducing the stress on deep tube wells and improving the overall balance of the local water cycle 
 (Acharya, 2025)
 . 
Groundwater crisis in Punjab is characterized by three main types of contamination: Arsenic, Salinity, and Industrial/Bacteri

pollution. Central Punjab (Lahore, Faisalabad, Sheikhupura, Gujranwala). These cities face heavy industrial pollution. In Lah
 ore, 
the water table is falling by nearly 2.5 to 3 feet annually, leading to the intrusion of low
 -
quality water. In Faisalabad and 
Sheikhupura, industrial waste (effluents) has introduced heavy metals like chromium and lead into the deep aquifers. 
Southern Punjab (Multan, Bahawalpur, Rahim Yar Khan, Muzaffargarh), this region is a "hotspot" for Arsenic contamination, 
which occurs naturally but is worsened by over
 -
pumping. In Muzaffargarh, studies show high levels of arsenic poisoning in 
children. Furthermore, 60
 -
70% of groundwater in the south is naturally saline (brackish), making it unfit for both drinking and 
agriculture. 
Specific Clusters (Kasur & Sargodha), Kasur is notorious for high fluoride and chromium levels due to its leather tanneries. 
 In 
Sargodha, recent reports highlight high Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) and nitrates, largely due to agricultural runoff and poo
 r

Study Area 
The study area for this research is Sargodha City, located in the Sargodha District of Punjab, Pakistan (figure 1). The city 
 is 
situated in the Chaj Doab, the fertile plain between the Jhelum and Chenab rivers 
 (Masood, 2024)
 . Sargodha is a significant urban 
and administrative center, with a population of over 975,000 (2023), and it serves as the hub for a largely agricultural regi
 on 
known for its high
 -
quality citrus production. 
The city's geography is characterized by its largely flat, alluvial plains at an average elevation of 190 meters above sea le
 vel 
(MALIK, 1958)
 . This topography makes the groundwater aquifer a primary source of water for both domestic and agricultural 
use. The area experiences a semi
 -
arid climate with hot summers and moderate winters. The rapid urbanization and industrial 
growth of Sargodha have placed significant pressure on its groundwater resources, making them susceptible to contamination 
from both natural and anthropogenic activities. 
Boundaries of Sargodha City's municipal corporation will serve as the geographical scope. The research will involve collectin
 g 
groundwater samples from various locations within these boundaries, including residential, commercial, and industrial zones, 
 to 
capture the spatial variation of water quality. This approach will allow for a comprehensive GIS
 -
based analysis to identify areas 
where groundwater is most vulnerable to pollution 
 (Arooj, 2025)

The third objective of the research was to evaluate the suitability of the groundwater in Sargodha City for human consumption
 . 
This objective was highly dependent on the laboratory analysis of the groundwater samples, as the concentration of every key 
parameter determines compliance with health standards. For this evaluation, a Ground Water Quality Index (GWQI) was 
calculated. The GWQI served as the primary model for this objective, converting the complex array of chemical results e.g., T
 DS, 
Nitrates, Chloride into a single, standardized numerical score. This calculation involved assigning a relative weight to each 
parameter based on its perceived impact on health and then normalizing the concentration against established drinking water 
standards e.g., WHO or local Pakistani standards. Following the calculation of the GWQI for each sampling point, GIS techniqu
 es 
were reapplied. Using the spatial analysis tools, a continuous GWQI suitability map was interpolated, visually classifying ar

consumption hinged entirely on accurate measurements from these samples. Each key parameter's concentration played a critical 
role in assessing whether the water met health standards. The introduction of the Ground Water Quality Index (GWQI) provided 
a robust framework, acting as the primary model to simplify the interpretation of multiple chemical parameters, such as TDS, 
Nitrates, and Chloride, by transforming them into one comprehensive score. After computing GWQI per point, the reapplication

Summary of Key Findings 
This study evaluated the spatial distribution and chemical quality of groundwater in the study area to assess its suitability 
 for 
consumption. By analyzing key chemical parameters and employing Kriging (Spherical) interpolation for spatial mapping, severa

In contrast, Fluoride levels averaged 1.45 mg/L, with maximum concentrations reaching 1.62 mg/L. Because these values 
approach and occasionally exceed the WHO limit of 1.50 mg/L, there is a localized threat of dental and skeletal fluorosis for 
the

Main Conclusions (Synthesizing the spatial

 -

The ultimate objective of this research was to move beyond isolated data points and understand how the physical movement of 
water, chemical contaminants, and human lives intersect across the landscape of Sargodha City. By weaving together laboratory 
results, advanced geographic modeling, and household surveys, this study confirms that groundwater quality is deeply 
intertwined with both the local environment and public health. 
The Interconnected Nexus 
The core conclusion of this thesis is that the groundwater in Sargodha City cannot be viewed as a natural resource, but must 
 be 
understood as a dynamic system where geology and human activity directly impact human biology. The laboratory analysis of the 
nine core parameters specifically variables like Total Dissolved Solids (TDS), Electrical Conductivity (EC), nitrates, and fl
 uorides 
revealed that a significant portion of the urban aquifer exceeds safe consumption thresholds. The true meaning of these chemi
 cal 
numbers only becomes clear when viewed through a spatial lens. The Kriging interpolation models proved that these 
contaminants are not scattered randomly, they form distinct geographic hazard zones, heavily influenced by local soil 
characteristics, dropping water tables across the 200 sampling points, and poor urban drainage systems.

Policy Recommendations for Urban Water Management in Sargodha 
The empirical findings of this study provide a critical roadmap for local regulatory bodies, including the Sargodha Developme
 nt 
Authority (SDA), the Water and Sanitation Agency (WASA), and public health departments. To mitigate the growing challenges of 
groundwater degradation and protect the local population, the following structural and strategic interventions are strongly 
recommended. 
Implementation of Zone
 -
Based Pumping and Regulatory Frameworks 
The spatial maps generated via Kriging interpolation clearly demonstrate that groundwater degradation is not uniform across 
Sargodha City rather, 
 it is concentrated in distinct geographic hotspots. Local authorities must enforce strict zoning laws 
regarding water extraction. In sectors where the water table is drastically dropping or where chemical contaminants like nitr
 ate 
and fluoride exceed safe limits, the installation of new, shallow domestic tube wells should be legally restricted. The munic
 ipal 
government should regulate deep
 -
aquifer extraction and monitor industrial or agricultural water usage in the periphery of the 
city to prevent further depletion and lateral movement of contaminated plumes. 
Deployment of Targeted, Grid
 -
Specific Water Treatment Infrastructure 
Different areas of Sargodha exhibit varying hydro geochemical characteristics, water treatment strategy is highly inefficient
 . 
Policy planners should utilize the GIS risk maps produced in this research to deploy targeted water purification infrastructu
 re. 
Community grids showing critically high levels of Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) and Fluoride, the government should prioritize 
the 
installation of localized, low
 -
cost Reverse Osmosis (RO) plants or specialized activated alumina filters. In areas marked by high 
turbidity and microbiological risks, community
 -
level chlorination and rapid sand filtration units should be established to ensure 
the immediate supply 
 of pathogen
-
free drinking water. 
Upgradation of Urban Infrastructure and Source Protection 
The presence of elevated nitrate levels in specific urban clusters strongly points toward the leaching of domestic sewerage a
 nd 
poor solid waste management into the shallow unconfined aquifer. There is an urgent need to rehabilitate Sargodha’s aging 
wastewater infrastructure. Municipal bodies must ensure the complete physical separation of open sewage channels from 
municipal drinking water pipelines to eliminate cross
 -
contamination. Strict environmental regulations must be enforced on local 
small
-
scale industries and workshops, mandating the proper treatment of their chemical effluents before disposal, thereby cutting 
off the source of hazardous groundwater recharge. 
Integration of Public Surveys into Community Health and Awareness Programs 
The socio
 -
environmental data collected through the 20
 -
variable public perception survey indicates a significant gap between 
water quality awareness and actual household health burdens. The Health Department, in collaboration with local educational 
institutions, should launch targeted public awareness campaigns in identified high
 -
risk zones. Residents must be educated on 
simple, cost
 -
effective household water treatment methods, such as boiling and basic filtration. A transparent, public
 -
facing 
groundwater quality monitoring dashboard should be established by the city management, allowing citizens to check the safety 
status of the water in their respective union councils, fostering a community
