for phthalate ester metabolites in bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops

Abstract
Phthalates (PAEs) are high production volume plasticizing chemicals linked to endocrine disruption in mammals. Phtha-
lates are released from the products to which they are added, leading to environmental exposure for humans and wildlife. 
Recent studies of bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops truncatus) urine have demonstrated prevalent exposure to phthalates. This 
study developed a protocol for phthalate ester metabolite (mPAE) detection in bottlenose dolphin blubber (~ 200 mg), a 
matrix that can be sampled remotely, and has been used to assess contaminant exposure (e.g., persistent organic pollutants 
and trace metals) in cetaceans in the past. Sonication in ammonium acetate, solid-phase extraction, and high-performance 
liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry were used to extract and quantify mPAEs in blubber, and the finalized 
method was applied to samples (n = 27) collected during dolphin health assessments conducted in Sarasota Bay, FL, USA 
in 2016, 2017, 2022, and 2023. Detectable concentrations of three mPAEs (mono-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (MEHP; n = 6; 
censored mean: 3.0 ng/g wet weight; 5.2–12.4 ng/g ww), monoethyl phthalate (MEP; n = 2; ng/g ww; 39.7–50.1 ng/g ww), 
monoisodecyl phthalate (MIDP; n = 3; 26.0–27.4 ng/g ww)) were observed across the dataset, with at least one mPAE 
detected in 37% of sampled dolphins. Blubber provides a remote sampling option to investigate PAE exposure, particularly 
high-molecular-weight compounds, in populations where health assessments are not feasible. Blubber samples of the size 
analyzed in this study can be obtained via remote biopsy dart sampling, a method used globally to monitor contaminants in 
marine mammals. This approach facilitates exposure assessments in at-risk or remote populations, increases the efficiency 
of field sampling efforts, and reduces stress for animals.

Introduction
Introduction to phthalates and their sources
Phthalates (PAEs) are a class of manufactured chemicals 
derived from phthalic acid (benzene-1,2-dicarboxylic acid) 
that are colorless with almost no odor (ATSDR 2008). Phtha-
lates are commonly added to plastics to make them flexible 
(ATSDR 2008), but they are also added to personal care prod-
ucts (e.g., soap, shampoo, and conditioners), medications, 
pesticides, and cleaning products to enhance fragrance and 
durability (ATSDR 2008; Centers for Disease Control and Pre-
vention 2017). Global phthalate production was estimated at 8 
million metric tons per year in 2011 and is estimated to reach 
10.5 million tons in 2026 (Ceresana 2019; Net et al. 2015). 
Today, frequently used phthalate compounds include diethyl 
phthalate (DEP), di-n-butyl phthalate (DBP), butyl benzyl 
phthalate (BBP), di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), diisode-
cyl phthalate (DIDP), and diisononyl phthalate (DINP), which 
are common components of personal care products, nail pol-
ish, adhesives and sealants, polyvinyl chloride (PVC) plastics, 
rubber products, and automotive fuel products, respectively 
(ATSDR 2008; Koniecki et al. 2011; Staples et al. 1997; U.S. 
Environmental Protection Agency 2026).
Due to their widespread use, phthalates are present in every 
environmental compartment. Both phthalates and their metab-
olites (mPAEs) have been detected in seawater around the 
world (reviewed in Hidalgo-Serrano et al. 2022). Additionally, 
it has been reported that concentrations of these compounds 
vary seasonally, with higher concentrations in dry seasons 
compared to wet seasons, due to seawater dilution caused by 
increased river flow during rainy periods (Heo et al. 2020; 
Wang et al. 2021). Phthalates have been detected in sediments 
worldwide, with low-molecular-weight compounds most often 
found in coastal areas because of their use in personal care 
products (reviewed in Hidalgo-Serrano et al. 2022 and Palu-
selli and Kim 2020). Generally, PAE concentrations are higher 
in sediments than in surrounding seawater due to the lipophilic 
properties of PAEs, especially high-molecular-weight com-
pounds, which enable them to absorb carbon-rich particulate 
matter (Wang et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2020). Once phthalates 
enter the marine environment, they are distributed in the food 
web (Net et al. 2015) and taken up by and potentially concen-
trated in large fauna such as cetaceans (Baini et al. 2017; Fossi 
et al. 2014; Hart et al. 2018Rian al. 2020).
Phthalate metabolism and preferred sampling 
matrix
Exposure to phthalates may occur via ingestion, inhalation, 
or dermal absorption (Centers for Disease Control and Pre-
vention 2017; Net et al. 2015 ). Different phthalates have 
varying exposure routes, owing to their diverse applica-
tions in numerous products. In humans, dermal exposure 
is considered the major route of exposure to diethyl phtha-
late (DEP), which is the compound often found in personal 
care products such as soap, shampoo, and hair conditioners 
(Koniecki et al. 2011). In contrast, the main route of expo-
sure to plasticizing phthalates such as DEHP is through inha-
lation or oral ingestion (Net et al. 2015). After uptake, phtha-
lates are metabolized in a two-step process that includes 
hydrolysis and conjugation. In studies of rats, the majority 
of doses of both DEP (82–88%) and DEHP (42–96%) were 
eliminated within 24 h, no matter the administration method 
(Kluwe 1982). Hydrolysis of diester parent compounds to 
monoester metabolites occurs in the liver and small intes-
tines (Kluwe 1982), and while this step detoxifies most 
xenobiotics, the monoester of DEHP, MEHP, is actually 
more bioactive than its parent compound (Ventrice et al. 
2013). Conjugation occurs in several organs including the 
liver, lungs, small intestine, and kidney, forming hydrophilic 
glucuronide conjugates that are easily mobilized for excre-
tion in urine (Silva et al. 2004; Ventrice et al. 2013).
To date, urine has been considered the most reliable 
matrix for phthalate exposure assessment in humans (Silva 
et al. 2005). This is because phthalates are made more 
hydrophilic and therefore more soluble in urine as they are 
metabolized (Frederiksen et al. 2007; Silva et al. 2004). In 
addition to urine, exposure assessments in humans have also 
used non-urine matrices such as breast milk, saliva, serum, 
and amniotic fluid (Bräuner et al. 2022; Frederiksen et al. 
2007; Silva et al. 2005). Several phthalate metabolites have 
been detected at high proportions in breast milk samples 
tested in multiple cohort studies (Frederiksen et al. 2007).
Phthalate exposure in marine fauna and study 
rationale
Marine mammals, particularly bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops 
truncatus), are widely considered ideal sentinels of marine 
ecosystem health. As long-lived, coastal, top-level preda-
tors with mammalian physiology similar to humans, they 
are vulnerable to many of the same environmental threats 
(Bossart  2011). Their protected status under the Marine 
Mammal Protection Act, coupled with their high public visi-
bility as coastal species, has made them an important species 
for health monitoring and conservation efforts. The Sarasota 
Bay, FL, dolphin population has been studied continuously 
for over 50 years, making it one of the best-characterized 
marine mammal populations in the world (Wells et al. 2005). 
This extensive baseline allows for detailed interpretations 
of health trends and contaminant exposure over time, both 
within the population and in comparison to human health 
risks. An additional advantage to using marine mammals

as ecosystem sentinels is their unique fat storage systems. 
Blubber is found in a variety of species including whales, 
seals, walruses, sea lions, and polar bears, and can act as a 
reservoir for anthropogenic contaminants (Bossart 2011). 
Blubber is a highly organized tissue composed of 80–90% 
lipids, and plays key roles in buoyancy, thermal insulation, 
and energy storage (Yu et al. 2022).
Its lipid-rich composition makes it particularly relevant 
for studying the accumulation of lipophilic environmental 
pollutants. The metabolic activity of blubber can vary by 
layer, age, body region, and environmental conditions (Mon-
tie et al. 2008; Yu et al. 2022), allowing researchers to poten-
tially gain insight into both short- and long-term exposure 
depending on sampling depth and context.
Previous studies in Sarasota Bay have shown that approxi-
mately 75% of free-ranging dolphins tested had detectable 
levels of multiple phthalate compounds, as measured in 
urine (Dziobak et al. 2021; Hart et al. 2018). However, urine 
sampling requires catheterization during catch-and-release 
assessments, which is logistically demanding, expensive, 
and may be stressful to the animals. In contrast, blubber 
samples can be collected quickly and remotely from free-
swimming dolphins using dart biopsy methods (Noren and 
Mocklin 2012), which are less invasive and have been used 
extensively across marine mammal contaminant studies 
(e.g., Baini et al. 2017; Fair et al. 2006; Wells et al. 2005).
Although studies have used blubber to conduct phtha-
late exposure assessment in marine mammals, most have 
relied on small sample sizes or measured diester parent 
compounds, which are prone to background contamina-
tion (Fossi et al. 2014, n = 5 fin whales; Routti et al. 2021 
n = 7, 6, 5 for blue whales, fin whales, and bowhead whales, 
respectively). Measuring monoester phthalate metabolites 
(mPAEs) instead, particularly those with higher molecular 
weights, may provide a more accurate indication of expo -
sure, as these compounds are less likely to be artifacts from 
sampling or sample processing, and some may preferentially 
accumulate in lipid-rich tissues. Evidence from studies of 
human breast milk, which has both aqueous and lipid char -
acteristics, supports the potential of lipid-rich matrices to 
carry and retain mPAEs (Frederiksen et al. 2007). Addition-
ally, blubber may capture metabolites too hydrophobic to 
be efficiently metabolized for excretion in urine, potentially 
extending the window of detectable exposure.
The objectives of this study were two-fold: (1) develop 
a method for mPAE isolation from blubber to build upon 
previous methods that have quantified mPAEs in solid tissue 
matrices (Rian et al. 2020; Valton et al. 2014) and (2) apply 
the new protocol to measure phthalate metabolite concentra-
tions in blubber samples collected from free-ranging bot-
tlenose dolphins in Sarasota Bay, FL. Ultimately, this new 
method focusing on a new tissue matrix will allow for expo-
sure assessment that can complement standard protocols that 
utilize urine, and potentially expand surveillance to areas 
where dolphins are particularly at risk of phthalate exposure 
and corresponding adverse health events (e.g., urban estu-
aries, developed coastlines) and where acquisition of urine 
samples may be impractical.

Protocol development
Methods were adapted from previous studies using solid 
tissues (liver from harbor porpoise, Phocoena phocoena,

Rian et al. 2020; muscle and liver from roach, Rutilus ruti-
lus, Valton et al. 2014; urine from bottlenose dolphin, Hart 
et al. 2018). Our protocol was developed and validated using 
a single 50-g full-depth blubber sample collected during 
necropsy via scalpel biopsy from a freshly dead bottlenose 
dolphin stranded near Charleston, SC (animal ID: SC1704, 
collected and frozen for storage on Jan 19, 2017).
Twelve mPAEs were selected for screening, including 
both low- and high-molecular-weight mPAEs (Table  1). 
Spike samples contained a known amount of mPAEs (SI 
Table 1). Three reagent spikes and 16 matrix spikes of vary-
ing mass were prepared and analyzed during the protocol 
development process to determine adequate blubber and 
mPAE masses required for data assessment. The utilization 
of reagent blanks in concert with reagent spikes allowed 
for the determination of instrumental response, recovery 
of analytes and identification of potential process-related 
contamination respectively. Subsequently, the use of matrix 
spikes allowed for the quantification of the influence the 
blubber had on the instrumental protocols set by the preced-
ing sample types.
Quality assurance/quality control (QA/QC) samples were 
processed along with blubber samples, and calibration stand-
ards included reagent blanks (HPLC water), reagent spikes 
(HPLC water + spike solution (analytes ranged in mass from 
9.5 to 605.4 ng per 100 µL aliquot depending on analyte)), 
and matrix spikes (blubber (0.2 g) + spike solution (9.5 to 
605.4 ng per 100 µL aliquot depending on analyte)) were 
prepared as described below.
Phthalate metabolite extraction and quantification 
protocol
Approximately 200 mg of blubber was placed into methanol-
rinsed amber glass vials (Fig.  1B), submerged in 1 mL of 
1 M ammonium acetate, and spiked with 100 µL isotopically 
labeled mPAE internal standards and 4-methylumbelliferone 
(SI Table 1). This mixture was sonicated twice for 20 min 
(Bransonic 2510 ultrasonic bath, VWR; Fig. 1C); after each 
round of sonication, 1 mL of the ammonium acetate mix-
ture was pipetted out of the blubber vial and into a solvent-
rinsed test tube. An additional 1 mL of ammonium acetate 
was added before the second round of sonication. After both 
rounds of sonication the extracts were combined. Twenty-
five µL of 4-methylumbelliferone β-D glucuronide was then 
added to each combined sample and then vortexed. To com-
plete deglucuronidation 250 µL aliquots of 1 M ammonium 
acetate and 5 µL of β-glucuronidase (Escherichia coli K12; 
200 units/mL) were added to the samples and incubated at 
37 °C for 90 min (Fig.  1D). The samples were then pro-
cessed via solid-phase extraction using Bond Elut NEXUS 
cartridges (60 mg, 3 mL, Agilent) that were conditioned 
with a column volume each of acetonitrile and 0.14 M phos-
phate buffer (Fig. 1E) prior to sample loading. After loading 
the total combined volume of sample extract (approximately 
2.5 mL), the cartridges were rinsed with 2 mL of 0.1 M for-
mic acid and with 2 mL of HPLC water. Analytes were then 
eluted with one column volume of ethyl acetate followed 
by one column volume of acetonitrile. Following elution, 
samples were evaporated to dryness under nitrogen at 50 °C 
in a Biotage TurboVap LV Evaporator (Uppsala, Sweden) at 
15 psi. Samples were then resuspended in 500 μL of HPLC 
water (water was the initial mobile phase for the LC-MS 
method). Finally, 10 µL of the isotopically labeled recovery 
standard was added, and samples were stored at −20 °C until 
analysis.
Isolation and quantification of each mPAE was conducted 
using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; 
Agilent 1100; Phenomenex Gemini HPLC Column (3 um; 
50 mm × 2 mm) analytical column) coupled to a triple quad-
rupole mass spectrometer (MS; Applied Biosystems Sciex 
API 4000) with electrospray ionization (ESI negative) inter-
face (Fig.  1F). Separation was achieved over 11 min at a 
flow rate of 200–300 μL/min with a gradient mobile phase

Method application to blubber samples 
from Sarasota Bay, FL, Bottlenose Dolphins
The Sarasota Bay dolphin community includes approximately 
170 individuals with multi-decadal, multi-generational, year-
round site fidelity to Sarasota Bay, FL (Fair et al. 2006; Wells 
et al. 2005). Long-term monitoring has included photo-iden-
tification studies, behavioral focal follow work, and periodic 
health assessments. Catch-and-release health assessments 
of inshore dolphins have been utilized to better understand 
endemic disease, establish baseline physiological measures, 
and evaluate exposure to, and potential effects of, chemical, 
biological, and physical stressors (Barratclough et al. 2019). 
During health assessments, free-ranging dolphins are encircled 
with a seine net (500 m by 4 m), temporarily restrained, and 
brought onboard a specialized platform for a veterinary exami-
nation (Barratclough et al. 2019; Fair et al. 2006). During this 
time, various biological samples (e.g., urine, blubber, gastric

Methods for blubber collection during health assessments 
have been reported previously by Wells et al. (2005). Briefly, 
wedge-shaped, full-thickness blubber samples (approxi-
mately 5 × 2.5 × 2 cm deep) were obtained by veterinarians 
with a scalpel and surgical forceps from a site below the cau-
dal insertion of the dorsal fin (Fig.  1A). Before biopsy, the 
site was rinsed with water and cleaned, and lidocaine hydro-
chloride was administered for local anesthesia (Wells et al. 
2005). Surgical instruments, foil, and sample receptacles 
were hexane-rinsed, acid-washed and autoclaved to mini -
mize contamination. If samples were larger than 200 mg, 
they were subsampled to reach that mass in the lab while 
retaining full-thickness (Fig.  1B). Field blanks, prepared 
with DI water from NIST, were used for quality assurance/
quality control (QA/QC) analysis, along with lidocaine and 
seawater from the capture site. These blanks, treated identi-
cally to samples, were stored at −80 °C in Teflon jars, and 
their values, if above limit of detection (LOD, “detectable 
concentrations”), were subtracted from detection concentra-
tion values to account for any mPAE contamination resulting 
from laboratory processes.
Reagent blank values, if detected, were also subtracted 
from detection concentration values to account for any 
mPAE contamination resulting from laboratory processes. 
Since extracted sample mass varied slightly across animals, 
the LOD differed for each mPAE in each sampled animal 
but was determined based on the lowest point of the calibra-
tion curve that could be detected on the instrument divided 
by the mass of blubber extracted (SI Table 6). In addition, 
continuing calibration verification standards were run with 
each batch of blubber samples to ensure the integrity of the 
calibration curve. Reagent spikes and matrix spikes used 
during method application had the same composition as 
during protocol development (SI Table 1).
Statistical analysis
Descriptive statistics were used to summarize mPAE con-
centrations and the proportion of Sarasota Bay dolphins with 
concentrations above the LOD. For mPAE with greater than 
20% of observations above LOD, means and standard devia-
tions were calculated using Regression on Order Statistics 
(ROS) and Kaplan-Meier (K-M) methods (Helsel 2006 ). 
Comparisons by demography were not conducted due to 
limited sample sizes. For dolphins sampled more than once, 
the sample most recently obtained was used for analysis.

used to assess the repeatability of the method and separation,

Method validation
mPAEs were quantified in blubber from 27 dolphins opportun-
istically sampled in Sarasota Bay and the QA/QC samples from 
Sarasota Bay batches were compared to the QA/QC samples 
from the protocol development phase in Table 2. Matrix spikes 
for both protocol development samples and samples from Sara-
sota dolphins had higher percent recovery and lower RSD when 
averaged across all analytes. Solid-phase extraction (SPE) 
mean percent recoveries for method validation matrix spikes 
were > 90% for all analytes. All samples except for one (Matrix 
Validation 5; SI Table 2) fell within two z-scores of the mean. 
The mean percent recovery for QA/QC samples associated with 
unknown field samples fell within acceptable ranges for all 12 
analytes (Table 2). In matrix spikes, 89.7% (n = 58) of analytes, 
and in reagent spikes, 82% (n = 53) of analytes fell within the 
acceptable recovery range (80–120%; Hennion 1999; Lipps, 
Braun-Howland, Baxter, eds 2023; U.S. Environmental Protec-
tion Agency 1995; see SI Table 2). However, it should be noted 
that percent recovery was low (60.6–90%), but consistent for 
MEHP for both matrix and reagent spikes. MIDP had a lower 
mean percent recovery across reagent spikes. Percent recover-
ies and RSDs for each analyte in the unknown samples were 
similar to their counterparts from method validation. Average 
LODs ranged from 2.2 ng/g ww (MEHP) to 27.2 ng/g ww 
(MCNP) (SI Table 6). The average LOD for MEP and MIDP 
were 18.5 ng/g ww and 2.5 ng/g ww, respectively (SI Table 6).
Method application to Sarasota Bay bottlenose 
dolphin blubber
Blubber was collected from 27 dolphins sampled during catch-
and-release health assessments conducted in 2016 (n = 9), 
2017 (n = 8), 2022 (n = 4), and 2023 ( n = 6). Samples were 
obtained from 13 males and 14 females, ranging in age from 
2 to 34 years, and 67% of sampled dolphins were classified 
as sexually immature (based on sex hormone concentrations; 
Table 3).
Thirty-seven percent (n = 10) of SSB blubber samples had 
detectable concentrations (“detect”) of at least one mPAE 
(Table 4). Detectable concentrations were observed for three 
mPAEs: MEHP (n = 6); MEP (n = 2); and MIDP (n = 3). 
Detectable concentrations ranged from 5.2 to 12.4 ng/g ww for 
MEHP, 39.7 to 50.1 ng/g ww for MEP, and 26.0 to 27.4 ng/g 
ww for MIDP (Table 4). Only one dolphin (F295), a 2-year-
old female, had detectable concentrations of more than one 
mPAE (MEHP and MIDP; Table 4). There were two detects in 
2016, one detect in 2017, three detects in 2022, and five detects 
in 2023. The oldest dolphin (FB33) sampled, a 34-year-old 
female, had the highest concentration of any analyte (MEP; 
50.1 ng/g, Table 4).
Discussion
Phthalate metabolite detection in bottlenose 
dolphin blubber
This method was based on, and expanded upon methods 
that extracted mPAEs from roach (fish) muscle tissue 
and harbor porpoise liver (Rian et al. 2020; Valton et al. 
2014). We utilized a smaller sample size (~ 200 mg) than 
the muscle method (2 g), but larger than the liver method 
(~ 100 mg). Our method utilized two rounds of sonication 
in ammonium acetate to extract mPAEs from the solid 
matrix of blubber as in Rian et al. (2020). Like all of the 
previous methods, we utilized β-glucuronidase to ensure 
that only deconjugated compounds were quantified. Soni-
cation was followed by solid-phase extraction as in the 
urine and liver studies. Muscle samples were extracted 
via liquid/liquid extraction and then samples were cleaned 
with solid-phase extraction (Valton et al. 2014). All stud-
ies concentrated extracts under a nitrogen stream before 
analysis on LC-MS/MS. This study detected and quantified 
12 mPAEs. The muscle method quantified concentrations 
of seven monoesters, and the liver study quantified 17.
The low, but consistent, mean percent recoveries 
for MEHP could be attributed to non-ideal chemical or 
physical behaviors of analytical systems that can be dif-
ficult to detect until after experimentation has concluded. 
Analytes can be lost from the analytical process before, 
during, and after extraction from a matrix (Kumar et al. 
2022). Prior to extraction, a portion of the MEHP may 
have been irreversibly bound to components of the matrix 
(proteins, salt complexation; Kumar et al. 2022). During 
extraction, MEHP may have experienced incomplete elu-
tion from the SPE cartridge, as documented in previous 
studies of mPAEs (Kumar et al. 2022). Post-extraction, 
some analytes may experience ion suppression, where 
interfering compounds may co-elute with analytes of inter -
est (Hart et al. 2018; Millipore Sigma 2021). Additionally, 
as MEHP is a metabolite of one of the most widely used 
diester phthalates, DEHP, contamination with DEHP in 
laboratory or field sampling materials and sample blanks 
has been documented, leading to a lower percent recovery 
due to the subtraction of blank contamination concentra-
tions after samples were extracted (Valton et al. 2014).
While the percent recoveries for MEHP spikes were 
lower than other analytes in this work, they are simi-
lar to prior studies that were foundational to this study. 
The percent recovery for MEHP in spikes was similar to

MEHP recovery in a study performed with roach liver, 
plasma, and bile (65%), and to recoveries for MBP, and 
MiNP in a study performed with bottlenose dolphin urine 
(68% ± 20% and 64% ± 16%, respectively) and recoveries 
in this study were higher for all other analytes that were 
also examined in previous research (Hart et al. 2018; 
Valton et al. 2014). While the percent RSD was high for 
MEHP during the method development phase, further 
sample processing showed consistent MEHP recover -
ies (~ 70%; % RSD ~ 25%) and these data meet generally 
accepted verification and validation criteria. The United 
States Environmental Protection Agency typically vali-
dates extraction methods with recoveries in the range 
of 70 to 130% (Hennion 1999; U.S. Environmental Pro-
tection Agency 1995), and the American Public Health 
Association recommends percent recoveries of 70–130% 
for volatile organics (Lipps, Braun-Howland, Baxter, eds 
2023). This was also the percent recovery range utilized 
for previous work using dolphin urine (Dziobak et al. 
2021).

Phthalate metabolites in Sarasota Bay dolphins
Three mPAEs of the 12 scanned were detected in the analyzed 
samples: MEP, MEHP, and MIDP. Detectable concentrations 
of MEHP and MEP were also observed in urine samples from 
Sarasota Bay dolphins (Dziobak et al. 2021; Hart et al. 2018). 
MIDP was newly included in our analytical suite for dolphin 
blubber samples, highlighting the value of expanding metabo-
lite screening. Because previous urine-based studies (Dziobak 
et al. 2021; Hart et al. 2018) did not include MIDP, its detec-
tion here cannot be interpreted as a novel exposure. This result 
further supports the use of blubber for assessing phthalate 
exposure, especially with a larger analyte suite. Additionally, 
a broad range of detected concentrations was found in the stud-
ies shown below (Table 5). While some metabolites have been 
detected in excess of 1000 ng/g ww, most metabolites were 
detected in the range of 1 to 100 ng/g ww, similar to the range 
detected in this study. This may provide further evidence that 
the method utilized here may be appropriate for analysis of 
fatty tissues of species other than T. truncatus in the future.
The detection range for MEHP in this study was similar 
to concentrations found in Sanjuan et al. (2023) and Valton 
et al. (2014), but lower than other studies of this metabolite 
(Table 5). This study detected three mPAEs, and a wide range 
of phthalate metabolites have been detected in marine mam-
mals in several tissue matrices (Fossi et al. 2014; Rian et al. 
2020; Routti et al. 2021; Xie et al. 2023). Other metabolites 
beyond the 12 assessed in this study have also been detected 
in marine animals but they are not included in Table 5. Other 
solid tissue matrices such as liver (Rian et al. 2020; Sanjuan 
et al. 2023; Xie et al. 2023) and muscle (Fossi et al. 2014; 
Fourgous et al. 2016) tend to show a larger number of phtha-
lates than found in blubber in this study or skin in others, but 
these tissues cannot be obtained from live dolphins. Studies 
that utilized skin biopsies for phthalate metabolite assessment 
only found two of the metabolites surveyed for in this study. 
These results demonstrate that blubber biopsy has promise for 
detecting phthalate exposure in marine mammals.
Exposure to MEHP could be attributed to the widespread 
use of the parent compound, DEHP, in rigid plastics includ-
ing food packaging, medical devices, and polyvinyl chloride 
(PVC) products. Globally, approximately 97% of DEHP is 
used in the production of PVC and is added to PVC in a pro-
portion up to 30% (Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease 
Registry 2008). Plastic pipes make up about 54% of the pipes 
installed worldwide, and production for PVC makes up 62% 
of the demand for DEHP (Heim and Dietrich 2007). MEP is a 
metabolite of DEP, a phthalate added to a variety of personal 
care products (Koniecki et al. 2011). DEP is used as a sol-
vent vehicle for fragrance and cosmetic ingredients, as well as 
alcohol denaturant. It is the most frequently used phthalate in 
personal care products and cosmetics (Koniecki et al. 2011).

Comparisons between phthalate metabolite 
detection in blubber and urine
The proportion of dolphins with detectable concentrations 
(37%) was lower than previous studies that used urine as 
an assessment matrix (~ 75%; Dziobak et al. 2021). MMP, 
MBP, MiBP, MBzP, MNOP, MEOHP, MiNP, MEHHP, 
and MCNP were not detected in any of the blubber sam-
ples analyzed. This is not entirely surprising, given previous 
work. Other studies utilizing bottlenose dolphin urine as a 
detection matrix analyzed a smaller suite of eight mPAEs 
(Dziobak et al. 2021; Hart et al. 2018). MBP, MiNP, MiBP, 
MEHHP, and MBzP were not detected in any dolphin sam-
ples in the 2018 study, although the nondetection of MBP 
and MiNP may have been due to method limitations (Hart 
et al. 2018). In the 2021 study, MiBP was not detected, 
and the detection percentages of MMP, MEOHP, MEHHP, 
MBzP, MBP, and MiBP were much lower (1.96–7.84%) 
compared to MEP and MEHP (29.41–54.90%; Dziobak 
et al. 2021).
The lower detection frequency of phthalate metabolites 
in blubber (37%) compared to urine (75%), and the smaller 
number of metabolites detected in this study compared to past 
work may be due to differences in the chemical properties of 
the matrices (blubber and urine) and between low- and high-
molecular-weight phthalates. Low-molecular-weight phtha-
lates (with ester side chains of one to four carbons), such as 
DMP, DEP, DBP, and DIBP, are more hydrophilic and tend 
to partition into urine, muscle, and liver rather than lipid-rich 
tissues like blubber (Rian et al. 2020; Routti et al. 2021). In 
contrast, high-molecular-weight phthalates (with ester side 
chains of five or more carbons), including DEHP, DOP, and 
DINP, are more lipophilic. The octanol–water partition coef-
ficient (KOW) estimates a chemical’s lipophilicity and is 
widely used to predict bioaccumulation in lipid-rich tissues 
(Gossett et al. 1983). Short-branched, low-molecular-weight 
phthalates like MEP and MMP have lower log KOW values 
(1.86 and 1.37, respectively) than higher-molecular-weight 
compounds such as mBzP, MEHP, and MIDP (3.07, 4.73, and 
5.71, respectively; US Environmental Protection Agency Esti-
mation Programs Interface Suite™ for Microsoft® Windows, 
v 4.11; Table 6; U.S. Environmental Protection Agency 2017).
This pattern could explain metabolic differences and 
subsequent differential partitioning of the two groups of 
phthalates. Low-molecular-weight phthalates are primarily 
excreted in urine as monoesters following initial hydrolysis 
(Silva et al. 2003). Parent compounds like DEHP, DiNP, 
and DiDP, which have higher log KOW values, may cause 
them to partition into blubber before metabolism. Hence, 
they have been previously detected in cetacean blubber 
(Andvik et al. 2024; Routti et al. 2021). Detection of high-
molecular-weight monoester metabolites (e.g., MEHP, 
MIDP) in blubber may suggest some degree of partition-
ing into lipid-rich tissues after metabolism. In humans, 
phthalate metabolites found in breast milk are primarily 
unconjugated monoesters (Calafat et al. 2004; Main et al. 
2006). Lower detection of phthalates in blubber may there-
fore result from the preferential excretion of low-molecular-
weight metabolites in urine and other hydrophilic matrices, 
while only a small fraction partitions into blubber (Silva 
et al. 2003). It is unclear whether high-molecular-weight 
phthalates are substantially metabolized for urinary excre-
tion, as previous dolphin studies did not include MNOP, 
MINP, MIDP, or MCNP (Dziobak et al. 2021; Hart et al. 
2020). Furthermore, human exposure estimates based on 
urinary MEHP and MiNP levels may be underestimated, 
as these are not the primary excreted metabolites of DEHP 
and DiNP (Frederiksen et al. 2007).
Finally, the mPAEs detected in previous work may have 
not been detected here because of the variable and relatively 
high limits of detection in this study compared to studies 
that used urine as a sampling matrix. In previous studies 
that utilized urine, the LOD for MEHP ranged from 0.24 to 
0.6 ng/mL for MEHP and 1 to 3.43 ng/mL (Dziobak et al. 
2021; Hart et al. 2018). The LODs in this study were the 
same or greater than detected concentrations in previous 
work (Dziobak et al. 2021; Hart et al. 2018). The average 
LOD for MEHP and MIDP were 2.2 and 2.5 ng/g blubber, 
respectively. These were some of the lowest LODs across all 
mPAEs in this study, enabling detection of smaller amounts 
of these metabolites in analyzed samples. However, this 
trend does not hold true for MEP, for which the average 
LOD was 18.5 ng/g. It should be noted that MEP had the 
fewest detects (n = 2) of the three detected mPAEs and might 
have been more prevalent in this population had the aver -
age LOD been lower. The LODs were high in this study 
because a small mass of blubber (approximately 200 mg) 
was used for extraction. Sample sizes larger than 200 mg 
during method development tended to have excessive co-
extracted material (e.g., lipids) that clogged SPE cartridges 
and made evaporation and concentration difficult. Reducing 
sample mass compared to prior work allowed for greater 
extraction capability, even if it did increase LODs.

Limitations and strengths
This study faced several limitations related to both blubber 
protocol development and its subsequent application. First, 
protocol development relied on a single blubber sample 
from an adult coastal bottlenose dolphin from Charleston, 
SC. This individual was not from the Sarasota Bay popu-
lation, and species, age, and sample location can impact 
blubber composition and the concentrations of contami-
nants within the tissue matrix (Montie et al. 2008; Ellisor 
et al. 2013) and may subsequently influence the extrac-
tion and recovery of analytes. Despite this, the developed 
method was successfully applied to blubber from Sarasota 
Bay bottlenose dolphins of varying age classes, demon -
strating its broader utility. The relatively small sample 
mass (200 mg) used in the extraction protocol led to higher 
limits of detection (LOD) for some analytes, which may 
have impacted detection rates. Future work may want to 
investigate methods to mitigate lipid interference associ-
ated with larger sample masses. Additionally, the lim -
ited understanding of phthalate metabolism in blubber 
poses challenges in interpreting residue concentrations as 
measures of overall exposure. While studies in humans 
suggest age-related differences in phthalate metabolism 
(Domínguez-Romero and Scheringer 2019; Wittassek 
and Angerer 2008), it is unknown whether similar pat-
terns exist in dolphins, highlighting a key area for future 
research. Finally, our metabolite panel was selected to 
enable comparisons with previous urine-based studies 
(Dziobak et al. 2021; Hart et al. 2018), which largely 
focused on low-molecular-weight phthalates. Future work 
should expand the analytical suite to include more high-
molecular-weight mPAEs, which are more likely to parti-
tion into lipophilic tissues like blubber.
Despite the limitations, this study presents several 
important strengths that support the reliability and rel-
evance of our findings. Most notably, our adaptation of 
existing methods to extract and quantify mPAEs from 
bottlenose dolphin blubber fills a critical gap in marine 
mammal health surveillance. To our knowledge, this is 
the first study to analyze phthalate metabolite concen-
trations in blubber samples collected from live, healthy 
dolphins. The success of this work was made possible 
by the long-term catch-and-release health assessments 
conducted in Sarasota Bay, which provided access to a 
diverse set of blubber samples. Building on established 
laboratory and analytical techniques (Dziobak et al. 2021; 
Hart et al. 2018; Rian et al. 2020; Valton et al. 2014), 
our method proved effective, with most analytes showing 
acceptable recovery values (70–130%; U.S. Environmen-
tal Protection Agency 1995). While MEHP recovery in 
reagent spikes was slightly below this range, its consistent 
performance in matrix spikes aligned with results from 
prior studies. Although the samples used in this study

Conclusions
This study adapted methods used to extract mPAEs from 
tissues of aquatic and marine fauna for use in bottle -
nose dolphin blubber. mPAEs have been well-studied in 
humans (Bräuner et al. 2022; Silva et al. 2007, 2005, 
2004) and bottlenose dolphin urine (Dziobak et al. 2021; 
Hart et al. 2018). Phthalates have also been analyzed in 
marine mammal blubber, but with extremely small sam-
ple sizes or with samples exclusively taken from stranded 
animals. This advancement not only expands the reper -
toire of monitoring techniques but also offers a poten-
tially less-invasive approach for ongoing surveillance 
beyond traditional health assessments. Furthermore, 
the rigorous precautions taken to mitigate contamina-
tion risks in the lab underscore the integrity of our data, 
bolstering confidence in the validity of our results. By 
standardizing methods for mPAE detection in bottlenose 
dolphin blubber, our study supports application of the 
approach of remote biopsy dart sampling, and facilitates 
the expansion of exposure surveillance to vulnerable 
areas, contributing to the understanding of associated 
adverse health effects and advancing marine conserva-
tion efforts.
This study also demonstrated that blubber is a viable 
matrix for assessing phthalate exposure in live dolphins. The 
successful adaptation of techniques from other tissues and 
species supports the potential for using remotely collected 
blubber biopsies in future research. These findings provide a 
foundation for further work into mPAE exposure and effects 
in marine mammals and emphasize the importance of con-
tinued method development and environmental monitoring 
in support of marine conservation.
Blubber is less effective than urine for detecting low-
molecular-weight compounds, which are commonly found 
in personal care products, whereas high-molecular-weight 
compounds are primarily used in hard plastics (Hart et al. 
2020). If future research aims to examine the trophic transfer 
of mPAEs from microplastics to dolphin prey and ultimately 
to dolphins (Merrill et al. 2023; Dool and Bosker 2022), 
focusing on high-molecular-weight compounds may be 
more informative. While blubber is a less reliable indicator 
of overall phthalate exposure compared to urine, it can still 
complement urine analysis, particularly for high-molecular-
weight phthalate metabolites.
