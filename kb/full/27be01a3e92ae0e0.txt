Abstract
This study investigated the seasonal impact on microplastic (MP) characteristics 
and concentration in the sludge streams (primary, secondary (waste activated) and 
digested) at a municipal wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) in Ontario. MPs were 
recovered after digestion and density separation of primary, secondary and digested 
sludge samples and were quantified and characterized (shape, colour, polymer type, 
and chemical contaminants) using microscopy and FTIR. The primary sludge had the 
highest number of MP particles among the three sludges, regardless of the season. 
The results show that sludge samples collected during the spring season had the 
highest MP concentrations (1.5–2.5 times higher), along with increased levels of tire 
and road wear particles (TRWPs), attributed to snowmelt and stormwater. Black and 
blue were the frequently recorded MP colours, with no discernible seasonal trends 
among different sludge types. Fibres remained the predominant MP type irrespective 
of the season and the sludge type. FTIR analysis of the 20–30% subset of MPs also 
revealed that PET was the most common polymer, with significant amounts of PA, 
PE, and PP recorded. There were no significant seasonal changes in the polymer 
type of the MPs. The spectral analysis further revealed the presence of a range of 
pharmaceutical drugs, industrial chemicals, and emerging contaminants on MPs, 
often obscuring the polymer identification while highlighting the role of MPs as 
carriers of environmental contaminants. Seasonal dynamics play a crucial role in 
assessing the emission of MPs into aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems. Understanding 
these fluctuations enables the development of more targeted and effective 
intervention strategies that are responsive to periods of heightened risk and that 
mitigate their environmental impacts.

 1 Introduction
Wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) are an important pathway for MP movement 
through the environment, acting both as a point of accumulation and emission [ 1]. MPs 
in WWTPs originate from diverse sources depending on the receiving sewer network, 
such as household, industrial and stormwater runoff [ 2–4]. Personal care products and 
microfibres (MFs) are released during laundry, forming a major contributor to the MP 
load of the WWTPs [ 5, 6]. The concentration, size, shape and type of MPs in WWTPs 
depend on several factors, such as demography, urbanization, and treatment techniques 
adopted [7–9].
Although WWTPs are not designed to remove MPs, the literature indicates that 
a removal efficiency of over 95% can be achieved for capturing MPs from the influent 
stream [10]. It is essential to acknowledge that MP removal through wastewater treat -
ment processes results in their accumulation in the sludge streams, rather than their 
complete removal. The MPs are mainly captured in sedimentation tanks, and as a result, 
they are present in biosolids that may be later land-applied for beneficial use, poten -
tially impacting soil ecology and the broader environment [ 11–13]. Additionally, the 
remaining 5% of MPs that escape treatment are mainly MFs and smaller MPs, which are 
discharged into surface waters [ 14, 15]. In short, MPs that pass through WWTPs can 
potentially contaminate both aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems [16, 17].
MP concentrations and removal are influenced by a wide range of factors, and the 
MP removal performance differs among treatment plants. This is largely attributed to 
the differences in treatment processes and the sewershed characteristics in WWTPs. 
However, studies have also shown that for the same treatment processes, the removal 
efficiency varies among treatment plants [ 18]. While some of these variations can be 
attributed to the design and operation of the WWTP [ 19], they are also likely influenced 
by seasonal changes. Several studies have explored the impact of seasons on MP con -
centrations and removal efficiencies in WWTPs, reporting considerable discrepancies 
in the results obtained. To elaborate, Conley et al. [ 20] reported no seasonal trends in 
MP concentration in a treatment plant in the United States, as opposed to a higher MP 
influx in winter in a mid-eastern WWTP reported by Ben-David et al. [ 21]. The lowest 
MPs were found during the winter season, and the highest in summer, in another study 
conducted in central urban Asia [ 22]. These contrasting findings highlight the complex -
ity of seasonal influences on MP dynamics and underscore the need for further research 
to clarify these patterns across different regions, climates, and treatment systems.
Previous studies have primarily focused on MP levels in wastewater streams and their 
impact on the aquatic environment [22–25], whereas sludge streams remain understud -
ied in comparison. Seasonal fluctuations of MFs are well-documented in final effluents, 
with fibres dominating regardless of the season [ 21, 26–28]. However, there are knowl -
edge gaps in whether sludge streams follow similar trends or if the dominant MP shape, 
size, and polymer type shift with seasonal changes.
MPs have diverse characteristics that impact their removal through treatment pro -
cesses due to differences in their size, shape, density and polymer structure. Polyethylene 
terephthalate (PET), polyamide (PA), polypropylene (PP), polyvinyl chloride (PVC) and 
polyethylene (PE) are the most frequently detected MP polymers in wastewater [ 29–31]. 
Wang et al. [ 32] established seasonal patterns in MP morphology and polymer profile 
in road dust, suggesting that stormwater, which is an important contributor to MP load

in WWTP , could potentially introduce seasonal variation of these MP parameters in 
sludge.
MPs also play a significant role in the environmental transport of emerging contami -
nants through adsorption. Studies show that a variety of pollutants are effectively sorbed 
by MP particles, such as heavy metals [ 33], antibiotics [ 34] and organic and inorganic 
compounds [ 35, 36], thereby contributing to secondary pollution. MPs in sludge have 
been found to exhibit enhanced adsorption capacity, likely due to changes in physico -
chemical properties post-wastewater treatment [ 33]. These insights underscore the 
need to better understand the interactions between MPs and emerging contaminants in 
wastewater treatment sludges to inform effective environmental management strategies 
and the beneficial use of biosolids.
This study investigated the seasonal dynamics of MP concentrations and polymer pro -
files using sludge samples from a municipal WWTP in Ontario, a region characterized 
by significant temperature fluctuations between winter and summer (-35  °C to 35  °C) 
and distinct seasons. The objectives of the study were: (1) To assess the seasonal varia -
tion in MP concentration and characteristics in primary, secondary, and digested sludge 
streams; (2) To determine the predominant types, colours, and polymer compositions of 
MPs present in different sludge streams across seasons; (3) To evaluate the presence and 
variety of emerging contaminants adsorbed onto MPs in wastewater sludge; (4) To inves-
tigate the influence of seasonal factors, such as snowmelt and stormwater, on the abun -
dance and types of MPs; and (5) To provide insights into the role of seasonal dynamics in 
the occurrence, removal and discharge of MPs from sludge treatment streams at waste -
water treatment plants.
2 Materials and methods
2.1 WWTP sampling and sewershed
Samples were obtained from a WWTP in Ontario, Canada that serves a population of 
one million people. The facility employs an activated sludge system with nitrification for 
biological treatment and chemical precipitation for phosphorus removal. The city has a 
combined sewer system with a daily average capacity of 550 million litres per day (MLD) 
and an average flow of 450 MLD. The sewershed spans over 2,800 km 2 with approxi -
mately 2,850 km of sanitary sewers and 100 km of combined sewers in the older parts of 
the city. Thus, approximately 3.5% of the sewer system is combined, with likely more MP 
pollution entering with stormwater.
For the quantification of MPs in the sludge streams, primary sludge, secondary sludge 
(thickened waste activated sludge), digested sludge, centrate and dewatered biosol -
ids were collected. For the seasonal variation study, two grab samples (1–3  L each) of 
primary, secondary (thickened) and digested sludge were collected from the treatment 
plant on different days within each season – Winter (February), Spring (April), Summer 
(June) and Fall (September) in 2023. Samples were stored at 4 °C in glass containers until 
the analysis was done. Digested sludge was obtained from the outlet of the anaerobic 
digester in the plant.
2.2 Extraction of MPs from sludge samples
MP analysis of sludges was done in duplicates with 20–50 mL subsamples. Due to the 
low solid content in the centrate samples, larger sample volumes of approximately 2 L

Choran et al. Discover Water (2026) 6:111 
were processed. MP extraction was performed using a modified version of the method 
described by Rodrigues et al. [ 37], as outlined below. The samples were initially treated 
with Fenton’s reagent (a 1:1 ratio of 35% H 2O2 and 0.05 M FeSO 4). The digestion agent 
was added in small batches while the sample was continuously mixed. The temperature 
was maintained at 60 °C throughout digestion to minimize damage to the MPs. After 2 h 
of digestion, the samples were left for an additional 24 h after the addition of an excess of 
Fenton’s reagent to ensure complete digestion. After 24 h, the supernatant decanted and 
was vacuum filtered (Filter 1) through a 10 μm nylon filter (47 mm diameter, Millipore 
Sigma, Darmstadt, Germany). A few drops of soap solution were added to the top layer 
to displace the particles towards the outlet. The soap solution was tested for MP content. 
The filter was washed with 35% ethanol to remove any unreacted peroxide and prevent 
damage to the filter itself. To the fraction after removing the supernatant, zinc chloride 
(ZnCl2) solution of density 1.6–1.7 g/L was added and mixed using a magnetic stirrer for 
30 min. The volume of ZnCl 2 solution added was sample-dependent to achieve a com -
puted final solution density of approximately 1.6 g/L. This solution was left for density 
separation for an additional 24 h, and the supernatant was carefully filtered through a 10 
μm pore size nylon filter (Filter 2). The MP sample analysis is illustrated in Fig. 1.
2.3 Quantification and physical characterisation of MPs
After extraction, suspected MPs were enumerated using microscopy with a Leica M165 
C (Leica Microsystems, Germany) at magnifications of 7.3× to 120×. The detection 
limit in this study is approximately 20 μm, constrained by both the capabilities of visual 
examination for MPs and the sensitivity of FTIR instrumentation. For systematic quan -
tification, a custom-designed circular grid with alphanumeric coordinate markings was 
printed in high resolution and positioned beneath the transparent nylon filter, 47 mm in 
diameter. Grids provided a more precise framework, enabling tracking of segments that 
were counted and that remained unexamined. Given the prolonged period of counting

required for thorough analysis, this methodology was particularly important in mini -

Although the extraction process involves both organic matter digestion and density 
separation, the isolated particles may still include particles other than MPs. Achieving 
complete elimination of organic matter is challenging, often requiring longer process -
ing times and/or harsher protocols that can damage the integrity of MP particles while 
still leaving non-MP residues. Similarly, density separation is not entirely selective and 
can retain inorganic materials, such as straw or other low-density organic substances, 
alongside MPs. To address these limitations, visual sorting criteria were adapted from 
Hidalgo-Ruz et al. [ 38] and supplemented with additional guidelines based on prelimi -
nary observations. Key features included the absence of discernible organic structure, 
consistent thickness in fibres and the exclusion of any particles that broke down into 
powder or resulted in smudging on contact with the needle probe. Visual assessment 
alone is unreliable for separating synthetic fibres from natural (cellulose) ones, as (ste -
reo)microscopic examination does not exhibit any distinguishable features, requiring an 
additional step for polymer verification with analytical instruments.
Suspected MP particles were sorted visually based on colour and shape. Primarily, two 
shape categories, fibres and fragments, were identified. Particles having cylindrical form 
with a high length to width ratio were characterized as fibres, while fragments were flat 
with irregular edges [ 39, 40]. Less common shapes were grouped under an “others” cat -
egory, which comprised pellets, microbeads and foams as described by Hartmann et al. 
[41]. For colour classification, particles were categorized into broad colours (red, blue, 
black, green) with both light and darker shades grouped within the same category, along 
with transparent particles. Dominant colours were identified and maintained as distinct 
categories, whereas those observed in smaller proportions were collectively grouped 
under “others” in colours.
Confirmed MPs were expressed as the number of particles per gram of total solids 
(#MPs/gTS), where TS was determined by drying a well-mixed sample to a constant 
weight at 103–105 °C and calculating the ratio of dried residue to initial wet weight of 
the sample. The MP count was then adjusted based on the TS of the sample, which is 
synonymous with MPs per g dry weight used in other studies for comparison purposes.
2.4 Polymer identification and quantifying confirmed MPs
Polymer verification of a 30% subset, stratified by shape (proportional to the observed 
ratio of different MP types), was performed using FTIR spectroscopy (Nicolet iN10; 
Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The minimum match quality threshold was set at 60%, 
and spectra rounding to 60% were considered acceptable. MPs were carefully transferred 
to a sequentially numbered glass slide using tweezers. Very fine particles that could not 
be handled with tweezers were scraped onto the slide using a fine needle. The confirmed 
MPs were expressed as a percentage for each shape class and applied to individual filters 
to obtain the confirmed MP count. The OMNIC polymer spectra library (Thermo Fisher 
Scientific, USA) was used; if no match was found, the open-source database Open Specy 
[42] was consulted to identify any additional matches. Particles showing no spectral 
matches in both databases were excluded from the dataset to avoid false positives. When 
FTIR analysis yielded only compound or chemical matches, the analysis was repeated 
at various points on the same sample to attempt precise material identification. In cases

particle, as some did not withstand the ATR pressure, were lost after the first reading, or 
broke into fragments too small for further FTIR analysis.
2.5 Statistical analyses
Statistical analysis was conducted to assess the difference in MP characteristics (colour, 
type and polymer composition) across different sludge samples and seasons. Statistical 
difference in recovery efficiency was ascertained using one-way ANOVA. Two indepen -
dent samples of each sludge type were analyzed in duplicate per season. All statistical 
analyses were performed at 5% significance level with MS Excel® (version 2412) using the 
Real Statistics Resource Pack software (Release 8.9.1) [43]. The normality of the data was 
verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Two-way ANOVA was performed to investigate the 
significant differences in MP characteristics among sludge types and seasons. Where 
applicable, Tukey’s post hoc test was applied to identify which specific groups differed.
2.6 MP recovery efficiency
A recovery experiment was conducted to determine the effectiveness of the modi -
fied protocol for MP analysis. Secondary sludge was selected for the recovery experi -
ment because it represents a more challenging matrix for MP extraction, particularly 
due to the presence of flocs and its higher total solids content compared to primary and 
digested sludge samples. MP fragments and fibres were spiked into 100 mL secondary 
sludge samples. The fragments (LNS Technologies, USA) were divided into three dif -
ferent size ranges (1.5–2.5 mm, 0.85–1.5 mm and 0.2–0.85 mm). They were imaged 
using Leica M165 C (Leica Microsystems, Germany) to distinguish the spiked particles 
from background MPs based on their documented size and morphology. Spiked fibres 
(sourced from everyday textile materials) measured in the 200–950 μm range with an 
average thickness of 25–30 μm. Table 1 shows the polymer type, densities (average val -
ues from the literature as reported by Ruggero et al. [ 44] and the number of MPs added. 
The tests were done in triplicate. Each filter required approximately 72 to 80 h for com -
plete analysis, including the extraction of MPs, a drying period to ensure sample stabil -
ity, and subsequent polymer verification to accurately identify particle composition.

ing digestion and density separation protocols. On average, microscopic analysis of 
each filter took up to 8  h, and each sample consisted of two filters. Background MP

3 Results and discussion
3.1 Method efficiency
The recovery experiments yielded 75–100% recovery of spiked MPs presented in Table 2, 
comparable to ranges reported by others [45, 46]. In the higher size range (1.5–2.5 mm), 
100% of the MPs were recovered from both polymer types tested; however, efficiency 
dropped by over 10% with the lowest size class (0.2–0.85 mm). The reduced recovery 
efficiency was statistically significant for the PP polymer at the lowest size range ( p < 
0.05), whereas the HDPE polymer did not exhibit a significant reduction. Fragments had 
a slightly higher recovery rate compared to fibres, but the difference was not statisti -
cally significant ( p = 0.14). There were no significant differences in the recovery of MPs 
among different polymer types ( p = 0.2). The particles mostly remained intact after the 
extraction procedure, although some of their colours appeared lighter post-process. PET 
fibres had the lowest recovery at 75.5%, likely due to their white colour that blended with 
the nylon filter, rather than due to differences in density. Therefore, it is likely that there 
was a colour bias in the results, underestimating the number of white and transparent 
MP particles.
3.2 MP distribution in sludge streams
On average, MP concentration in sludges varied from 63 to 430 particles/gTS. The pri -
mary sludge had 221 ± 25 MPs/gTS, significantly higher ( p < 0.05) than the other sludge 
samples (Fig. 2A), with an average of 2.6 times more MPs than secondary sludge (98 ± 
23 MPs/gTS). Similar results are reported in other studies, where MP removal through 
primary treatment was found to be between 78% and 98% in different treatment plants 
[3, 47, 48]. Digested sludge and centrate recorded 165 ± 42 MPs/gTS and 118 ± 19 MPs/
gTS, respectively. Dewatered biosolids contained 91 ± 24 MPs/g TS, significantly lower 
than the 228 to 1353 MPs/g TS reported by Sivarajah et al. [ 49], but higher than the 
8–14 MPs/g TS reported by Crossman et al. [ 50] in Canadian biosolids. This discrep -
ancy is likely due to differences in analytical approaches, particularly the adoption of the 
polymer confirmation step. Although all three studies used similar extraction protocols 
for organic matter removal, Sivarajah et al. [ 49] relied on visual assessment and physical

In addition to methodological differences, infrastructural and population size can also 
be influential factors. Biosolids analysed by Crossman et al. [ 50] were from a sewershed 
with a separate stormwater system and serving a smaller population. On the other hand, 
the sewer system in this study features a combined system in some sections of the city, 
serving a larger population, which likely results in higher MP influxes from both storm -
water and urban sources.
Of the MP types assessed, fibres were dominantly present, followed by MP fragments 
and others (Fig. 2A), with the exception of biosolids, where the quantities of fibres and 
fragments were equivalent. The category “others” included granules, microbeads, and 
pellets, which were found in lower amounts. The predominant presence of fibres in all 
three sludge types is consistent with other studies [ 51–54]. MFs have been identified as 
a key contributor to MP pollution in domestic wastewater. Domestic laundering is the 
primary source of fibres in WWTPs, with an estimated MF release of 7–69 mg per kg 
of laundry [ 55]. Another significant source of MFs is atmospheric deposition, owing to 
their higher mobility [56–58], which eventually enters the sewers and WWTPs through 
stormwater runoff. MFs have been recorded as the dominant MP type in several atmo -
spheric deposition studies resulting from the shedding of textiles due to various mecha -
nisms such as mechanical abrasion and UV irradiation that damage the fabric [ 59–61]. 
In biosolids, no significant difference between fibres and fragments was observed ( p > 
0.05). This divergence from the fibre-dominant trend was likely due to the removal of 
lighter fibres during sludge dewatering at the plant, decreasing the fibre concentration in 
biosolids. This was further supported by the findings of Nakao et al. [ 62], where 75% of 
MFs were reported to escape into the centrate stream upon dewatering. Re-entering the 
wastewater stream, these MFs break down into fragments, altering the MP shape profile 
in dewatered sludge.

The distribution of MPs varied in the different filters (Fig. 2B). Filter 1 captures com -
paratively lighter particles with densities near water from the post-digestion superna -
tant, whereas Filter 2 isolates higher-density MPs following the ZnCl2 density separation 
(Sect. 2.2). All three sludge samples had 50–80% of the MP fraction retained in Filter 1, 
corresponding to a significant share of comparatively lighter particles. The trend, how -
ever, reversed in the biosolids, where most particles were captured in Filter 2, as the cen-
trate removed lighter particles. Although larger and denser MPs are known to settle in 
primary clarifiers, studies have shown evidence of lower-density polymers retained at 
this stage [63]. The MP retention in treatment processes is more complex than the sim -
ple density-based separation used during extraction, involving other processes such as 
coagulation, flocculation, and flotation.
In secondary clarifiers, low-density MPs may settle due to their potential entrap -
ment in sludge solids through extracellular polymer substances (EPS) secreted by the 
microbes, increasing the density of the resulting flocs [ 64–66]. Although the digestion 
process during the sample extraction can release a good amount of these MP particles, it 
does not ensure the complete removal of organic material. This could imply an increased 
density of MPs due to partial attachment to the sludge particles, resulting in a higher 
number of particles in Filter 2. These findings reflect the influence of sludge treatment 
processes, such as dewatering, on the type and density of MPs retained in the biosol -
ids. Additionally, density separation is crucial for effectively isolating MPs from complex 
matrices, such as biosolids, further emphasizing the importance of extraction protocols 
and treatment design in MP assessment. The diversity of MP fragments and fibres iso -
lated in this study from wastewater sludges is illustrated in Fig. 3. The figure highlights 
the range of colours, shapes, and polymer types present, emphasizing the heterogeneous 
nature of MP contamination in the waste stream.

Fibres remained the dominant MP shape in all the sludge samples throughout all sea -

The spring season had the highest MP concentration in all three sludge samples ( p < 
0.05). There was an average of a 1.5 to 2.5-fold increase relative to MP concentrations 
in other seasons. Although MP levels in primary and digested samples in summer were 
higher than in fall and winter, the increase was insignificant ( p > 0.05). Winter and fall 
samples showed similar MP distribution in all the samples analyzed ( p > 0.05). Similar 
results were observed in the WWTP study in Lithuania, with the highest MP numbers 
occurring in spring due to strong winds, melting ice, and heavy rainfall [ 69]. Further-
more, seasonal patterns are reported in atmospheric MPs, with pronounced levels in 
the spring season [ 70]. In contrast, the seasonal study by Ben-David et al. [ 21] reported 
increased MP levels in winter, likely driven by increased laundering and surface runoff 
typical in Middle Eastern countries. Regional climate differences may be a significant 
factor that can explain these discrepancies. Subzero winter temperatures are typical for 
Lithuania and Ontario, Canada, while temperatures in Karmiel (Israel) are consistently 
above 0 °C throughout the winter [71].
3.3.2 Colour analysis
Colour classification of MPs is often recommended for several reasons. Although colour 
cannot provide definitive source information, it can be an ancillary parameter for source 
tracing [72]. It is also a vital parameter in aquatic toxicology studies, where the colour of 
the MPs influences the ingestion preference for aquatic life [ 73]. The colour can also be 
used to assess the background contamination during sample handling. In this study, MPs 
were classified into broad colour categories. Black, blue, green, red and transparent were 
recorded colours, with a small number of other colours (Fig. 5). Black and blue colours 
accounted for more than 50% of the MPs recorded every season, along with a substan -
tial number of transparent particles. The black colour was observed in comparable

3.3.3 Seasonal polymer composition and source attribution
The percent match to the polymers in the library varied from around 65–90%. Figure 6 
shows the polymer distribution in the sludge streams in all four seasons. PET emerged 
as the most detected polymer type across all seasons, with other polymers, such as PA, 
PP , and PE (including LDPE and HDPE), also present in substantial amounts. PET con -
centrations fluctuated across different sludge types and seasons. The primary sludge had 
the highest fraction of PET (39.5%, combining all the seasons), with seasonal variations 
ranging from 32.5–46.4%. Secondary sludge exhibited lower amounts of PET, with an 
average of 31.5% (27.4–34.8%), while anaerobically digested sludge contained 27.9% PET. 
A statistically insignificant deviation was observed in the digested sludge samples in 
summer, where PET and PE were present in similar fractions but at higher concentra -
tions than PA. Similar to this study, the abundance of PET polymer was also reported 
among isolated MPs in WWTPs in different countries [ 19, 30, 82, 83]. An atmospheric 
deposition study in Southern Ontario by Welsh et al. [ 84] further complements the cur-
rent trend in polymer concentrations, revealing significant levels of PET and PA. While 
both polymers are known to be important components in the textile and apparel indus -
try [85–87], PET has more versatile applications in packaging, including plastic bottles 
[88]. Research also shows that, on average, polyester/PET-infused clothing sheds over 
five times more than nylon apparel. PET shedding increased tenfold in the fleece-type 
fabrics [ 89] that are increasingly used in colder urban climates. These factors likely 
explain the prevalence of PET in wastewater sludges.

due to the presence of snow and ice in winter, can explain the high contribution of MPs 
in the spring as the snow and ice melt.
The dominance of fibres and PET polymer across all the seasons along with the con -
sistent prevalence of black and blue colored MPs, indicates that seasonal variation has 
limited influence on MP characteristics. These patterns further suggest that source 
contributions remained relatively stable throughout the study period. In contrast, sea -
sonal differences were more prominent in MP abundance, likely reflecting the impact 
of hydrological conditions and runoff dynamics into the wastewater streams. Therefore, 
seasonality appears to primarily affect MP levels than their composition.

4 Limitations of the study and future research
Although ongoing efforts are improving the standardization of MP detection and quan -
tification, methodological differences remain important when reporting MP concen -
trations and comparing results among studies in different matrices. The present study 
provides a detailed assessment of seasonal MP abundance and characteristics across 
sludge streams from a municipal WWTP , while also identifying methodological consid -
erations and limitations that can guide future investigations.
The analytical detection limit of approximately 20  μm focused the analysis on par -
ticles reliably detectable by the microscopy and FTIR analysis. Consequently, smaller 
MPs and nanoplastics were not evaluated. Future studies could extend the size range 
using higher-resolution techniques, such as laser direct infrared (LDIR) spectroscopy. 
The reported MP concentrations in this study were not corrected for recovery efficiency, 
as the recovery experiment was conducted to provide a general estimate of method per -
formance and showed variation with particle size, polymer type, and shape; therefore, 
applying a single correction factor was not considered appropriate. Instead, the recovery 
results provide context for interpreting the reported concentrations. It is recommended 
that future studies develop and adopt standardized recovery-test protocols to enable 
more consistent evaluation of method performance and improve comparability across 
MP studies.
Additionally, this study used FTIR data to indicate variation in the types and amounts 
of contaminants potentially adsorbed onto MPs. More targeted, high-resolution chemi -
cal analysis would help confirm and quantify these contaminants using advanced tech -
niques such as GC-MS/MS and Py-GC-MS. Future research should extend seasonal 
monitoring to different contributing sources including stormwater systems to better 
characterize MP pathways and their contribution to sludge. Studies across WWTPs with 
different treatment configurations, sewer systems and characteristics would further test 
the applicability of the seasonal patterns observed here. Higher-frequency sampling dur-
ing rainfall and snowmelt events could improve source attribution, particularly for tire 
and road-wear particles.
5 Conclusion
This study explored the influence of seasons on the type and concentration of MPs in 
primary, secondary and digested sludge samples. Primary sludge had the highest MP 
levels across all seasons. Peak MP levels occurred in the spring, along with higher levels 
of tire and rubber-related particles. The higher abundance of MPs was likely due to run -
off from snowmelt during spring, following prolonged subzero temperature conditions 
during winter. The most prevalent polymer type was PET in both primary and secondary 
sludge, while the fibres were the most dominant MP type. PA, PP , and PE (both high- 
and low-density) were also found in substantial amounts in various sludge samples. 
This remained consistent across all seasons and different sludge samples. MP colour

distribution revealed that blue and black were more commonly found colours, and their 
distribution did not vary seasonally. These results point out that seasonal variation influ-
enced MP concentrations rather than their characteristics such as shape, polymer type 
and color distribution. Several other contaminants, including fertilizers and pharmaceu -
tical drugs, were found adsorbed onto suspected MPs, often obscuring their underly -
ing material properties and potentially leading to an underestimation of MP abundance 
in sludge samples. This finding also supports the role of MPs as carriers of emerging 
contaminants, such as PFAS. The results further demonstrate that MP levels in sludge 
fluctuate seasonally, which can directly influence their concentrations in soils receiving 
biosolid applications and in surface waters after wastewater effluent discharges, particu -
larly in the spring. Understanding these fluctuations enables the development of more 
targeted intervention strategies that are responsive to periods of heightened risk, thereby 
helping to mitigate the environmental impacts of MPs.
