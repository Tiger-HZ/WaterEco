Enhanced weathering accelerates carbon dioxide removal from the 
atmosphere through the rapid dissolution of land amendments of crushed, 
cation-rich rocks. The export of enhanced weathering dissolution products 
to drainage networks has important implications for carbon dioxide 
removal durability and measurement yet has received little attention. Here 
we observe rapid and pronounced streamwater chemistry responses within 
weeks of a crushed basalt application to a temperate headwater catchment: 
alkalinity exceeded counterfactual estimates by as much as 557 μeq l−1, 
accompanied by changes in silica, lithium and major-ion chemistry 
consistent with basalt weathering. Difference-in-differences analysis 
identified repeated treatment effects with enhanced weathering accounting 
for 7–17% of streamwater alkalinity in successive summers and autumns, but 
less in winter and spring. This seasonal response reflects a combination of 
temperature controls on basalt dissolution kinetics and hydrologic controls 
on weathering-product export. Over 2 years, 9.5–11% of theoretical carbon 
dioxide removal potential was exported as alkalinity (34.7 t CO2 km−2 yr−1).
Anthropogenic CO2 emissions are the primary driver of climate change 
and ocean acidification, affecting both terrestrial and marine climates 
and ecosystems1,2. T o achieve the Paris Agreement goal of limiting 
global warming to less than 2 °C, large-scale carbon dioxide removal 
(CDR) will be necessary3. T errestrial enhanced weathering (EW) is a CDR 
approach that involves spreading powdered silicate rocks (for exam-
ple, basalt) over agricultural and other lands to accelerate chemical 
weathering reactions that convert atmospheric CO2 to alkalinity (pri-
marily HCO3
−) and release base cations4,5. These weathering products 
may be retained within the watershed or exported with soil waters and 
groundwaters to streams and rivers that drain into the ocean, where 
storage lifetimes may exceed tens of millennia 6,7. Recent theoretical 
assessments indicate that EW implemented on global croplands could 
sequester 0.5 to 2 Gt CO2 yr−1 at costs comparable to other CDR strate-
gies, which become more competitive when agriculture co-benefits 
from improved soil health are considered 8–10. Despite the growing 
interest in EW, uncertainty in its practical potential remains and is 
underpinned by a scarcity of both observations and demonstrations 
of approaches suitable for quantifying applied-silicate weathering 
at the field scale.

Here we present multiple lines of evidence demonstrating rapid, 
seasonal watershed responses to EW, using high-resolution streamwa-
ter chemistry data from a 59-ha hay- and pasture-dominated headwater 
catchment (W-2) within the Sleepers River Research Watershed 23–25, 
Vermont, USA (Fig. 1). Following a 1-year baseline monitoring period, 
we applied basalt powder (hereafter ‘basalt’) in June 2023 over 8.9 ha of 
the southern (downstream) portion of the catchment (15% of the total 
catchment area) at a rate of 20 t ha−1. We isolated the basalt-weathering 
signal from background water chemistry by analysing streamwater 
concentration–discharge (C–q) relations before and after basalt appli-
cation and by comparing streamwater observations to a no-basalt coun-
terfactual estimated from weighted regressions on time, discharge 
and season (WRTDS)26,27. We then used a generalized additive model 
coupled with a difference-in-differences (GAM-DiD) approach28 that 
leveraged observations from a nearby reference watershed (W-9) to 
quantify seasonal dynamics in the streamwater export of basalt-derived 
alkalinity, base cations and associated CDR rates. In addition, we 
applied an endmember mixing analysis to evaluate the effects of basalt 
treatment on seasonal shifts in weathering-product concentrations 
within quick-flow and slow-flow pathways that transmit solutes to the 
stream. This watershed-scale EW study describes how streamwater 
chemistry responds to the deployment of silicate feedstock and intro-
duces a transferable framework for evaluating the seasonal dynamics 
of EW-attributable CO2 removal.
Results and discussion
Rapid and pronounced streamwater chemistry responses to 
watershed-scale EW
Streamwater alkalinity and base cation concentrations in the W-2 
stream responded within 1 month of the basalt application and 
remained elevated relative to counterfactual WRTDS estimates from 
Most EW estimates rely on silicate-feedstock dissolution rates deter-
mined in laboratories or in near-surface soils from plot-scale or farm-field 
experiments11–13, thereby neglecting hydrologic and geochemical pro-
cesses beneath shallow soil horizons that may modify CDR as EW prod-
ucts are transported to surface waters. For example, ion exchange may 
temporarily retain base cations derived from feedstock dissolution and 
release protons that neutralize bicarbonate, thus delaying alkalinity 
generation and its export to surface water until those cations are later 
desorbed14. Secondary-clay formation and carbonate precipitation 
in groundwaters beneath application areas may sequester feedstock 
weathering products, effectively reducing net CDR7,15–17. Moreover, the 
routing of rainfall along watershed flow paths will introduce time-varying 
hydrologic lags between feedstock dissolution in near-surface soils 
and the expression of alkalinity in streamwater, potentially affect -
ing downstream CDR quantification and underscoring the need for 
watershed-scale monitoring that captures these transport dynamics.
The streamwater chemical response to EW is likely to be dynamic, 
shaped by factors that vary at seasonal and sub-seasonal timescales. 
Seasonal changes in temperature within EW watersheds can affect silicate 
weathering through abiotic and biotic mechanisms18–20. Weathering rates 
increase with temperature, a process often modelled following Arrhenius 
kinetics. T emperature-induced increases in soil pCO2 (via soil respiration) 
and organic acids (via root and microbial exudation) will further acceler-
ate dissolution20,21. Beyond temperature, variations in precipitation will 
modulate watershed wetness and flow-path connectivity that, in turn, 
govern catchment-water residence times and hence the extent of water–
feedstock interactions22. An understanding of how temperature–precipi-
tation interactions influence temporal variation in EW contributions to 
streamwater alkalinity is essential to informing EW monitoring schemes; 
however, the ways these seasonal processes regulate alkalinity generation 
and export at the watershed scale are largely unknown.

The post-application enrichments in trace metal and silica concen-
trations provide further evidence for the rapid transport of basalt weath-
ering products to the stream (Supplementary Fig. 2). Concentration 
increases of silica and lithium, which are both much more abundant in 
basalt than carbonates30,31, mirrored the increases in alkalinity and base 
cations. Moreover, δ7Li values declined from an average of 19.0 ± 1.0‰ 
during the pre-treatment period to 17.5 ± 1.0‰ within 3 months after 
treatment, and the pre- and post-treatment distributions were signifi-
cantly different (P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 4), which is consistent 
with increased contributions of isotopically depleted Li derived from 
basalt-feedstock weathering (basalt δ7Li value, 2.34 ± 0.65‰). Further-
more, concentrations of rubidium, which substitutes for potassium in 
feldspars and micas present in the basalt32, were similarly greater for 
equivalent stream discharges under post- relative to pre-treatment 
conditions. While iron, magnesium and strontium are released during 
dissolution of both basalt and carbonates33, their concomitant increases 
with silica, lithium and rubidium suggest freshly applied silicates as 
the most likely source of their increases in concentration immediately 
following the basalt application (Supplementary Fig. 2).
Preferential streamwater export of Ca relative to Na and Mg 
suggests either non-stoichiometric basalt dissolution and/or faster 
transport of Ca in response to the basalt treatment (Fig. 2 and 
Supplementary Fig. 2). The estimated increase in Ca concentration 
driven by EW is more than 20-fold greater than the corresponding 
increases in Na and Mg. Transport-related fractionation caused by 
cation-exchange reactions potentially contributes to the preferential 
Ca export. Competitive sorption34 of cations on the Ca-dominated soil 
exchange complex at W-2 (mean Ca saturation ~85%)35 can lower the 
selectivity of the exchange sites for basalt-derived Ca and lead to the 
displacement of Ca by other basalt-derived base cations, particularly 
Mg, resulting in the earlier breakthrough of Ca relative to Mg. In addi-
tion, some Mg released from basalt may be retained in Mg-rich clays36;

Seasonality of the EW treatment effect
The percentage of the observed streamwater concentrations attribut-
able to EW (%cEW ) exhibited recurring seasonal patterns during the 2 
years following basalt application, with stronger responses in summer 
and autumn and weaker responses in winter and spring (Fig. 3a–c). 
Results of the GAM-DiD modelling showed that the basalt treatment 
caused statistically significant changes in streamwater chemistry dur-
ing July–September 2023, with EW accounting for 14% to 18% of alkalin-
ity, Ca, Mg, Na and Si (%cEW; Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Fig. 5a). This 
strong treatment effect, coinciding with high discharge during summer 
storms (Fig. 3g,h), drove elevated EW-sourced export (that is, solute 
load per unit treated watershed area) in summer, which then declined 
as streamflow decreased later in the season (Fig. 3d–f). The EW treat-
ment effect on concentration remained significant but weakened dur-
ing October–December 2023 (Fig. 3a–c), and stream discharge also 
trended downwards during this period (Fig. 3g), leading to more than 
a 50% reduction in the export of alkalinity, base cations and Si (Fig. 3d–
f and Table 1). During the wintertime ( January–March 2024) when 
streamflow and temperature were at their lowest (Fig. 3g,i), EW treat-
ment effects on solute concentrations were the weakest and statistically 
indistinguishable from zero, yielding small (in 2025, slightly negative) 
export estimates that reflect model uncertainty rather than a physical 
sink. EW-sourced alkalinity export sharply decreased from its sum-
mertime high of 102.55 t CO 2 km−2 yr−1 to less than 3 t CO 2 km−2 yr−1 
(alkalinity expressed as removed CO2, assuming 1 mol of CO2 per 1 mol 
of HCO 3
−), and base cations showed approximately proportional 
decreases (Fig. 3d–f and Table 1). The treatment effect on streamwater 
concentration increased again in April–June 2024 as temperatures rose 
and streamwater export was controlled by snowmelt-driven discharge 
(Fig. 3a–f). Comparable seasonal patterns in the EW-attributable con-
centration and export percentages were observed during the second 
year ( July 2024–April 2025), though with slightly lower intensities 
(Fig. 3a–f and Table 1).
This recurring seasonal cycle of the EW treatment effect suggests 
that interactions among rainfall-runoff processes, watershed wetness 
and temperature shaped the seasonal patterns in EW-derived solute 
concentrations and export. Comparison of C–q relations in the sum-
mer–autumn periods before and after basalt application reveals a 
weakening of the streamwater dilution pattern, which is consistent with 
a lowering of the difference in solute concentrations within slow-flow 
and quick-flow pathways that deliver water and solutes to the stream 
(Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 6). By contrast, the C–q relations for 
winter–spring periods exhibited greater slopes and were similar before 
and after basalt application (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 6), which 
suggests comparatively large differences in solute concentrations 
between quick- and slow-flow pathways in the baseline period were 
preserved after the basalt treatment. Seasonal C–q relations fitted with 
a sigmoidal inverse power-law model (equation (8 ) in the Methods, 
with quick-flow/slow-flow endmembers) show that solute concentra-
tions associated with slow-flow pathways remained stable year-round 
(alkalinity, 4,186 ± 228 μeq l−1; Ca, 4,256 ± 455 μeq l−1). However, con-
centrations of solutes transmitted via quick-flow pathways increased 
markedly during the summer–autumn seasons of the post-application 
period with alkalinity and Ca increasing by up to 1,200 μeq l−1 (Fig. 4b 
and Supplementary Fig. 6).
In temperate catchments, such as W-2, quick flow comprises both 
new water (recent rainfall or snowmelt) that is delivered to the stream 
via overland and near-surface flow and resident (pre-event) soil water 
and shallow groundwater that is displaced primarily during hydrologic 
events and in diminishing quantities during recession to baseflow23,40. 
EW-derived solutes in quick flow are likely associated with shallow 
pre-event water that has interacted extensively with the feedstock 
between storm periods. Lowland areas proximal to the stream with a 
shallow water table and high soil moisture are the probable sources of 
such solute-rich quick flow. By contrast, slow flow showed no detectable 
EW signals, indicating its upslope origin towards the watershed divide 
and correspondingly long residence times.
The summer–autumn increase in EW solute concentrations within 
quick-flow pathways implicates temperature as an important factor in 
regulating basalt-dissolution rates. Following the Arrhenius framework 
for silicate weathering applied by Brantley et al.20 (equation (9), Meth-
ods), we find that the logarithm of the discharge-normalized EW-derived 
alkalinity export rate (Enorm; equation (5), Methods) exhibits a linear 
relationship with the reciprocal of temperature, yielding an apparent 
activation energy, Eaapp, of 58.5 ± 15.5 kJ mol−1 (R2 = 0.47, P = 0.002; Fig. 5), 
which represents the temperature sensitivity of basalt dissolution. This 
Eaapp value is within the expected range for basalt weathering (40–
90 kJ mol−1) (ref. 20) and reveals that dissolution of the basalt feedstock 
responds strongly to the seasonal temperature variation observed at 
W-2. This Eaapp value exceeds laboratory dissolution activation energies 
for Ca-bearing plagioclases41, the primary alkalinity-producing minerals 
in basalt, indicating enhanced temperature sensitivity at the watershed 
scale. This is consistent with the pattern identified by Brantley et al.20, in

Export rates of basalt weathering products and 
associated CDR
On an annual basis, EW-derived alkalinity export averaged 9.47 t C km−2 yr−1, 
equivalent to a maximum CDR export rate of 34.72 t CO 2 km−2 yr−1 
(assuming 1 mol of CO2 per 1 mol of HCO3
− and attributing all of it to 
silicate weathering; Table 1), which is among the highest rates reported 
in studies that quantify CDR via changes in alkalinity or cation fluxes in 
streams, leachate waters or pore waters36,42–46. Previous watershed-scale 
studies reported of 2.5–13 t CO2 km−2 over 15 years after wollastonite 
addition (3.44 t ha −1) in a temperate forest 42, while another study 
reported an average of 10 t CO2 km−2 across three small watersheds 
(<0.02 km2) within a tropical oil palm plantation after three annual 
applications of crushed andesite (50 t ha−1) (ref. 43). In addition, the 
annual CDR rate in our field trial is approximately equal to the median 
CO2 consumption rate (33 t CO2 km−2 yr−1) estimated from bicarbonate 
measurements made in rivers draining 12 basaltic watersheds distrib-
uted across volcanic arcs, oceanic islands and continental flood basalt 
provinces47. Lysimeter and mesocosm studies usually yield lower rates 
(1–26 t CO2 km−2 yr−1), even though many used higher rock application 
rates of up to 200 t ha−1 (refs. 11,44,45,48,49). The lack of leachate EW 
signals in some of these experimental studies likely represents a combi-
nation of factors, including short experimental durations, the absence 
of natural structure and associated transport pathways in repacked soils, 
and, likely foremost, the use of acidic soils that will at least temporarily 
scavenge base cations and neutralize alkalinity. Recent field-based stud-
ies that estimated CDR via soil-based techniques report higher removal 
(10–370 t CO2 km−2 yr−1) (refs. 50–53); however, these studies used higher 
feedstock application rates and do not account for processes, such 
as ion exchange and carbonate precipitation, that may reversibly or 
irreversibly reduce CDR efficiency.
Over 22 months, the EW-derived alkalinity export was 63.8 t CO2 km−2 
(5.7 t over the 8.9 ha treated area; Fig. 3d), representing approximately 
11% of the maximum CDR potential of the applied basalt (582 t CO2 km−2, 
51.8 t CO2 over the 8.9 ha treated area; equation (1) in the Methods). While 
the applied basalt contained minor inorganic carbon (0.14 ± 0.02%, or 
0.22 t in total), its maximum dissolution would account for 28% of the 
exported alkalinity, of which half (14%) derives from the carbonate min-
eral itself, and the other half (14%) derives from CO2 consumed during 
carbonate weathering. This indicates that most of the alkalinity export 
was generated through silicate weathering. In total, this corresponds 
conservatively to 9.5% of the basalt’s CDR potential, with 8% from 
silicate weathering and 1.5% from CO2 consumed during the potential 
carbonate weathering. This value approaches the upper limit of realiz-
able export as predicted by soil-column, reactive-transport models that 
account for cation sorption and secondary mineral formation. Under 
such constraints, alkalinity export is generally estimated to be <10% 
after 2 years in most areas of the United States54. One reason for the early 
emerging signals of weathering in this study, despite the high pH and 
cold climate, is the high initial base saturation of soils in W-2 (85% Ca), 
indicating that the potential maximum loss to cation sorption may be 
lower compared with US averages; suitable hydrologic conditions may 
have also contributed to the early emerging signals of weathering. Col-
lectively, these findings demonstrate that weathering products can be 
exported efficiently from headwater catchments where transport to 
streams and rivers occurs over seasonal-to-annual timescales.

year. This temporal consistency indicates that comparable hydrologic 
and environmental conditions in 2023 and 2024 (Fig. 3g–i) exerted a 
stronger control on the weathering signal than processes intrinsic to 
the weathering material itself, such as the depletion of reactive surface 
area or secondary phase formation, at the timescale of about 2 years55,56.
The EW signal in W-2 streamwater was detected within weeks of 
basalt application, consistent with rapid delivery of basalt weathering 
products along quick-flow paths originating close to the stream, yet 
only 9.5–11% of the potential maximum CDR was exported as alkalin-

ment was 1.3 years 57. Our water-isotope analysis (Methods) showed 
that 23 ± 2% of streamflow comprised water younger than 2.3 ± 0.8 
months, implying that most water resides in longer-term catchment 
storage. Thus, it is likely that EW products transported along slow-flow 
pathways originating upslope and farther from the stream had yet to 
emerge and may be difficult to resolve in the future because of dilution. 
These results imply that silicate deployments that preferentially target 
near-stream corridors may be most responsive to streamwater-based 
monitoring, reporting and verification, while also suggesting that 
extended monitoring windows will likely be necessary to observe 
weathering-signal tails and avoid underestimating CDR.
Near-stream corridors often have perennially moist soils that 
favour rapid weathering and may be ideal locations for repeated sili-
cate applications if potential reductions in CDR efficiency associated 
with high-silicate loadings are well understood. At W-2, for example, 
the calcite saturation index of streamwater exceeds 0 at an alkalin -
ity of ~2,000 μeq l−1 and increases by approximately 0.1–0.2 units per 
500 μeq l−1 once alkalinity exceeds ~2,000 μeq l−1 (Supplementary Fig. 8). 
Repeated annual applications could therefore push W-2 waters towards 
seasonal (that is, summertime) calcite precipitation, reducing net alka-
linity export and effective CDR. Catchments characterized by conditions 
conducive to carbonate precipitation or secondary-mineral formation 
may require less frequent applications or reduced doses. However, 
the presence of soil calcite within the W-2 catchment makes it a unique 
environment relative to many targets for EW.
Our results demonstrate temperature sensitivity of alkalinity 
export and hence CDR. These findings indicate that CDR efficiency will 
be comparatively low in temperate watersheds, even where year-round 
precipitation promotes runoff and weathering-product transport. 
Accordingly, monitoring, reporting and verification frameworks that 
rely on streamwater chemistry should account for seasonal variability 
to avoid misestimation of EW-derived alkalinity export. While this work 
begins to clarify how interactions between temperature and hydrologic 
variation affect EW solute deliveries to surface waters and associated 
CDR, additional watershed-scale trials across diverse hydroclimatic 
settings are needed. Integrating such observations with an emerging 
set of watershed-scale reactive transport models that accurately and 
consistently represent watershed-runoff processes and geochemical 
reactions would be an important next step in advancing a framework 
for informing decisions on optimal locales for EW deployments.
Methods
Site description
The study site, the W-2 watershed, is a 59-ha headwater catchment 
situated within the Sleepers River Research Watershed in Danville, Ver-
mont, USA. It is a low-elevation (285 m to 377 m) agricultural catchment 
(average slope ~7.3°), with 27% forest and 73% hayfield and pasture23. 
The area experiences a cold, humid continental climate, with mean 
annual precipitation of 1,050 mm yr−1 and mean annual air tempera -
ture of 5.7 °C. The mean daily discharge from the W-2 stream averages 
0.76 ± 0.19 mm d−1, with approximately two-thirds of annual discharge 
occurring as baseflow29. While discharge peaks in spring during snow-
melt, heavy storms during summer and autumn may drive discharge 
to levels more than an order of magnitude above baseflow. The stream 
weir location for discharge measurement, where water samples were 
also collected, is Lat: 44.45958 Lon: −72.0920.
The watershed is underlain by fine-grained, silty, calcareous till 
deposited during the Wisconsinan glaciation58. Beneath the till is the 
Late Silurian to Early Devonian Waits River Formation, which consists 
of quartz-mica schist with beds of quartz-rich limestone 59. Till thick-
ness ranges from less than 2 m near the hilltops to over 10 m downslope 
towards the stream. The soil in the upland hayfield is a sandy loam, 
whereas the downslope pasture soil is a loam with approximately 
equal sand and silt contents, with pH ranging from 4.9 to 7.7 (median 
pH 6.1) (ref. 35).

Streamwater chemistry and meteorological observations
Streamwater samples were collected biweekly or monthly before basalt 
application from May 2022 through June 2023, and weekly or twice 
per week after basalt application from July 2023 through April 2025, 
with more intensive sampling during storms (hourly). The collected 
streamwater samples were either filtered on-site immediately or fil -
tered in the laboratory within 24 h through 0.22 μm syringe filters, and 
divided into subsamples in acid-washed, filtered-streamwater-rinsed, 
high-density polyethylene bottles for major cations (calcium (Ca 2+), 
magnesium (Mg2+), sodium (Na+), potassium (K+)), alkalinity and trace 
metal analysis. The samples for trace metal analysis were acidified 
by adding 50% nitric acid. All the samples were stored at 4 °C until 
analysis. All measurements were performed at the Yale Analytical and 
Stable Isotope Center (YASIC) following the methods described in 
ref. 29. Aliquots from selected samples were further processed for Li 
separation and measurements at the Yale Geochemistry Center. The 
samples were first digested with aqua regia, evaporated and redissolved 
in 1 ml of 0.2 N HCl, and then Li was separated following the methods 
described in ref. 63. The Li isotopic composition measurements were 
performed on a Thermo Finnigan Neptune Plus ICP-MS and the Li 
isotopic compositions are reported relative to the L-SVEC-1 lithium 
carbonate standard, NIST 8545. A typical standard error of a single 
measurement was 0.07‰ (1s.d.) and the external precision was better 
than 0.08‰ (1 s.d.).
In addition to our own observations, we used published measure-
ments of stream chemistry made from March 1992 through May 2017 
at the W-2 watershed and measurements of stream discharge and 
chemistry from January 2019 through April 2025 at the W-9 watershed 
published in a U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) data release64. Watershed 
W-9 is a 41-ha forested headwater catchment located 6 km from W-2 in 
the Sleepers River Research Watershed. Daily estimates of air tempera-
ture and precipitation for the W-2 and W-9 watersheds were extracted 
from the Parameter-elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes 
Model (PRISM) database65.

Policy information about availability of computer code
Data collection No software was used for data collection. 
Data analysis We applied WRTDS to estimate streamwater chemistry at the W-2 and W-9 watersheds, implemented using the EGRET R package. To 
quantify basalt-treatment effects on streamwater chemistry, we implemented a Generalized Additive Model coupled with a Difference-in-
Differences (GAM-DiD) approach that leverages stream chemistry observations from a reference watershed. The GAM-DiD model was 
implemented using the mgcv package in R. The code is available at https://github.com/sfcmarie/GAM-DiD. R version 4.3.3 was used for all the 
analysis. 
The endmember mixing analysis was performed in MATLAB R2023b, using the fmincon function for pre-treatment nonlinear parameter 
estimation and lsqcurvefit function for post-treatment endmember estimation. 
The apparent activation energy of basalt weathering was estimated using linear regression with R version 4.3.3.

Ecological, evolutionary & environmental sciences study design

Study description We conducted a watershed-scale enhanced rock weathering (ERW) experiment at W-2 watershed, a headwater catchment located in 
the Sleepers River Research Watershed, VT. Following a one-year baseline monitoring period (May 2022 to Jun 2023), we applied 
Pioneer Valley Basalt powder (hereafter “basalt”) in June 2023 over 8.9 ha of the southern portion of the catchment (15% of the total 
catchment area) at a rate of 20 t ha-1. We isolated the basalt-weathering signal from background water chemistry by analyzing 
streamwater concentration–discharge (C–Q) relations before and after basalt application and by comparing streamwater 
observations to a no-basalt counterfactual estimated from WRTDS (Weighted Regressions on Time, Discharge, and Season). We then 
employed a Generalized Additive Model coupled with a Difference-in-Differences (GAM-DiD) approach that leveraged observations 
from a nearby reference watershed (W-9) to quantify seasonal dynamics in the streamwater export of basalt-derived alkalinity, base 
cations and associated CDR rates. Additionally, we applied a novel endmember mixing analysis to evaluate the effects of basalt

treatment on seasonal shifts in weathering-product concentrations within quick-flow and slow-flow pathways that transmit solutes to 
the stream.
Research sample Streamwater samples were collected to monitor the effects of enhanced rock weathering (ERW) in the watershed. Water samples 
were analyzed for major cations [calcium (Ca2+), magnesium (Mg2+), sodium (Na+), potassium (K+)], alkalinity, and trace metal 
concentrations. 
Sampling strategy The collected streamwater samples were either filtered onsite immediately or filtered in the lab within 24 h through 0.22 μm syringe 
filters, and divided into subsamples in acid-washed, filtered-streamwater-rinsed, high-density polyethylene (HDPE) bottles for major 
cations [calcium (Ca2+), magnesium (Mg2+), sodium (Na+), potassium (K+)], alkalinity, and trace metal analysis. The samples for trace 
metal analysis were acidified by adding 50% nitric acid. All the samples were stored at 4 °C until analysis. Sample sizes per 3-month 
lag ranged from 53–89 for pre-treatment groups (using WRTDS estimated data) and 9–25 for post-treatment groups (using 
measurement data). Despite smaller post-treatment counts, bootstrap resampling of the GAM-DiD model demonstrated stable 
treatment effect estimates with narrow 95% confidence intervals, confirming that replication was sufficient for robust statistical 
inference.
Data collection All measurements were performed at the Yale Analytical and Stable Isotope Center (YASIC). The data was recorded by Fengchao Sun. 
Timing and spatial scale Streamwater samples were collected biweekly or monthly before basalt application from May 2022 to June 2023, because the 
streamwater chemistry was relatively stable. In order to monitor the streamwater chemistry changes after basalt application, 
streamwater samples were collected once to twice per week after basalt application from July 2023 to April 2025, with more 
intensive sampling during storms and snowmelt (hourly). There was a sampling gap between December 2022 to April 2023 because 
of the snowy and frozen conditions in the winter in VT. The stream weir location for discharge measurement, where water samples 
were also collected, is Lat: 44.45958 Lon: -72.0920.
Data exclusions No data were excluded. 
Reproducibility Analytical measurement errors for duplicate water samples were generally within 3%. All data processing and statistical analyses are 
reproducible. Model scripts and data sources are fully described in the Methods section and/or provided in the Supplementary 
Materials.
Randomization Randomization was not applied. Treatment was implemented at the watershed scale, and effects were evaluated using a quasi-
experimental difference-in-differences framework based on predefined pre- and post-treatment periods.
Blinding Blinding was not implemented during data analysis because the study design relied on objectively defined pre- and post-treatment 
periods and predefined control and treatment groups, minimizing subjective decision-making.

Field conditions W-2 watershed is a low-elevation (285 m to 377 m) agricultural catchment, with 27% forest and 73% hayfield and pasture. The area 
experiences a cold, humid continental climate, with mean annual precipitation of 1,050 mm yr-1 and mean annual air temperature of 
5.7 degree Celsius. The mean daily discharge from the W-2 stream averages 0.76 ± 0.19 mm d-1.
Location The study site, the W-2 watershed, is a 59-ha headwater catchment situated within the Sleepers River Research Watershed in 
Danville, Vermont, USA. The stream weir location for discharge measurement, where water samples were also collected, is Lat: 
44.45958 Lon: -72.0920.
Access & import/export The landowner, farmer Dave Langmaid, granted us permission to access the W-2 watershed.
