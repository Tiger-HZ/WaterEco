 Abstract Groundwater targeting in semi-arid basaltic terrains is uncertain because surface indicators of recharge, storage and transmissivity do not necessarily represent the same hydrogeological process. This study develops a field-constrained workflow for delineating groundwater potential zones and recharge-prone zones in the Moha-Karewadi area of the Deccan Traps, Beed district, Maharashtra, India. Seven surface predictors - drainage density, lineament density, slope, land use/land cover, lithology, soil and geomorphology - were prepared in a GIS environment and weighted by the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) to generate the Groundwater Potential Zone (GWPZ) map. A second AHP model incorporated a measured infiltration-rate layer derived from Moha-Karewadi site-level field records to generate the Groundwater Recharge Zone (GWRZ) map; external records from other sites were retained only for data audit and were not used as validation of the final map. Subsurface constraints were provided by eight Schlumberger Vertical Electrical Soundings (VES), interpreted as one-dimensional layered-earth models and assembled into pseudo-sections and geoelectric sections. Very high groundwater potential is spatially restricted, covering about 2.07% of the area, whereas very high recharge suitability covers about 8.86% and occurs mainly where favourable geomorphology, low drainage density, high lineament density and higher infiltration capacity coincide. VES interpretation indicates shallow weathered and fractured basalt near the tanks, commonly within about 7–10 m below ground level, underlain locally by compact basalt that restricts deeper percolation. The main contribution is the separation of groundwater potential and recharge suitability as related but non-equivalent decisions, followed by reconciliation with infiltration and resistivity evidence. The workflow provides a practical screening approach for managed aquifer recharge and groundwater exploration in hard-rock watersheds.

 Geospatial Techniques for Groundwater Potential Assessment 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 1 Introduction Groundwater in hard-rock and basaltic terrains is commonly stored and transmitted through secondary porosity, including weathered mantle, joints, fractures, vesicles and interflow zones. In such aquifers, productive zones are spatially discontinuous, and borewell success depends on the coincidence of recharge, storage and transmissive pathways rather than on lithology alone [ 26 , 28 , 29 , 40 ]. These controls are particularly important in semi-arid regions where monsoon rainfall is concentrated in a short season and groundwater abstraction supports domestic water supply and agriculture. Remote sensing and GIS-based multicriteria methods, especially AHP and related MCDA approaches, are widely used for groundwater potential mapping because they can combine geomorphology, drainage, lineament, slope, soil, geology and land-use information over large or data-scarce areas [ 18 , 23 , 33 ]. Applications from crystalline, deltaic, volcanic and semi-arid terrains demonstrate the utility of such approaches, including geophysical-topographic integration, fuzzy-AHP, watershed-scale groundwater-potential zoning and recharge-priority mapping [ 1 , 2 , 4 , 8 , 14 , 16 , 20 , 22 , 35 ]. However, these studies also highlight an important limitation: surface-derived suitability does not automatically confirm the presence of a productive or rechargeable aquifer at depth [ 10 , 21 ]. This limitation is critical in the Deccan Traps, where weathered basalt may favour shallow storage, compact basalt may impede vertical percolation, and fractured or vesicular units may provide localised pathways. A location may therefore appear favourable in a surface-based groundwater potential map but still be unsuitable for artificial recharge if near-surface infiltration is low or if compact basalt restricts downward movement. Comparable groundwater quality, vulnerability, managed-aquifer-recharge and prediction studies show that mapped indicators must be tested against hydrological or aquifer evidence before management decisions are made [ 3 , 5 , 19 , 34 , 36 ]. This distinction explains why groundwater-potential zoning and recharge zoning should be treated as related but different decisions. Previous studies have combined geospatial methods with geophysical surveys or hydrogeological indices to improve groundwater targeting [ 6 , 21 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 37 , 38 ]. Nevertheless, many local applications still present AHP-derived maps descriptively, with limited explanation of why layers were selected, how weights were validated, how infiltration modifies recharge suitability, and how resistivity sections support or constrain the final interpretation. These gaps reduce the reproducibility and field value of the maps. The present study addresses this gap through a field-constrained assessment of the Moha-Karewadi watershed, where percolation tanks and recharge interventions require spatial prioritisation. The working hypothesis is that a single surface-derived suitability map cannot adequately represent both groundwater occurrence and recharge acceptance in basaltic terrain. Accordingly, the study integrates (i) a seven-layer AHP model for Groundwater Potential Zones, (ii) a separate recharge model that includes measured infiltration rates, and (iii) VES-based subsurface validation. The objectives are to delineate groundwater potential zones, identify recharge-prone zones, test the hydrogeological consistency of both outputs against field evidence, and develop a transparent workflow for managed aquifer recharge planning in comparable hard-rock watersheds. 2 Study area The study area covers the adjoining villages of Moha and Karewadi in Manjalgaon taluka, Beed district, Maharashtra, India. The mapped area is approximately 32 km² and lies within the semi-arid Deccan basalt terrain of the Marathwada region. The work focused on the surroundings of percolation tanks, including a tank desilted during 2016–2017, because these structures are locally important for recharge and water security. The area lies near 18°52’16.104” N, 76°17’56.004” E to 18°52’56.3988” N, 76°18’59.1264” E. The region experiences hot and dry pre-monsoon conditions, with peak summer temperatures approaching 42 °C, and receives most rainfall during the June-September monsoon. Average monsoon rainfall is about 737 mm. The terrain includes dissected structural lower plateau, pediment-pediplain complexes, water bodies and black cotton soils. These features make the area suitable for testing whether surface-based groundwater and recharge maps can be strengthened by infiltration and VES evidence. The location and elevation setting of the study area are shown in Fig. 1 . 2.1 Geological and hydrogeological setting The Moha-Karewadi area forms part of the Deccan Volcanic Province and is underlain by Late Cretaceous-Palaeocene basalt flows. The lithological map used in this study distinguishes six basalt map units (codes 0832–0837); at the scale of this investigation, however, groundwater behaviour depends more directly on flow facies, weathering, joints and fracture continuity than on the map-unit label alone. Regionally, Deccan basalt successions comprise alternating massive or compact flow interiors and vesicular-amygdaloidal, brecciated or weathered flow-top zones. Interflow contacts and weathered or fractured horizons can provide secondary porosity and transmissivity, whereas unfractured massive basalt and clay-rich interflow material may restrict vertical percolation [ 15 , 27 ]. Within the study area, highly dissected structural lower plateaus dominate the surface setting, with local pediment-pediplain surfaces, ponds and water bodies. Black cotton soils overlie the basalt; their clay-rich character can favour near-surface storage but may suppress intake where compaction, swelling or waterlogging occurs. The working hydrogeological model is therefore a discontinuous shallow weathered/fractured basalt aquifer, with local deeper fracture or vesicular pathways and comparatively resistive compact-basalt intervals that constrain downward movement. This geological framework provides the basis for including geomorphology and lineament density in the AHP, using infiltration tests to assess near-surface acceptance, and applying VES to distinguish likely weathered/fractured zones from compact basalt [ 7 , 39 ]. The model is evaluated against the field evidence presented in Sect. 4.9 . Drainage density acts as an inverse indicator of infiltration opportunity and residence time; lineament density represents fracture-controlled permeability; slope controls runoff versus infiltration; land use/land cover controls surface roughness, water retention and imperviousness; lithology represents the hydrostratigraphic framework; soil affects near-surface storage and infiltration; and geomorphology indicates erosion, depositional surfaces and zones of possible weathered mantle development. These layers were used for the GWPZ model. The infiltration-rate layer was not included in the GWPZ model because recharge suitability and groundwater potential are not identical; it was added only in the GWRZ model to capture measured near-surface acceptance. 3.3 Analytical hierarchy process and weighted overlay AHP was used to convert expert hydrogeological judgement into reproducible weights following Saaty’s pairwise-comparison approach [ 24 , 25 ]. Only the Moha-Karewadi site-level records located directly within the mapped study area were used to support the recharge interpretation; additional Beed and Nanded records in the raw sheets were retained only for data audit and were not used as independent validation of the Moha-Karewadi maps. At each included site, the inner and outer rings were maintained at an equal water head of about 10–15 cm to reduce lateral flow from the inner ring. Measurements continued for 2–4 h, and the near-steady infiltration rate was used for classification. The audited Moha-Karewadi near-steady values span approximately 8.41–168.88 mm/h across tank-bed and downstream/peripheral settings, reflecting soil texture, clay content, silt accumulation, geomorphic position and surface condition. The interpolation used for the recharge map was classified into five infiltration-rate classes from 4.01 to 178.77 mm/h. The field arrangement and representative decline of infiltration rate toward a steady state are shown in Fig. 3 . The interpretation is consistent with infiltration and near-surface hydrologic studies showing that soil structure, antecedent condition, drainage and surface disturbance strongly affect percolation behaviour [ 9 , 12 , 13 , 17 , 41 ]. Current was injected through electrodes A and B, and potential difference was measured between M and N. The apparent resistivity was calculated using Eq. ( 5 ): $$\:{\rho\:}_{a}=K\left(\frac{\varDelta\:V}{I}\right),\:K=\frac{\pi\:\left[{\left(\text{AB}/2\right)}^{2}-{\left(\text{MN}/2\right)}^{2}\right]}{\text{MN}}$$ 
 (5)
 where ρa is apparent resistivity, K is the geometric factor, ΔV is potential difference and I is current. A pseudo-section is a graphical arrangement of apparent resistivity values against electrode spacing and traverse position. It is not a direct geological cross-section; rather, it helps visualise vertical and lateral resistivity variation before or alongside inversion. IPI2DWin was used for 1D layered-earth interpretation by forward modelling the apparent-resistivity curve and iteratively adjusting layer resistivity and thickness until the calculated curve matched the measured curve within an acceptable misfit. Lithological interpretation was made by comparing resistivity magnitude, curve shape, depth continuity and field geological context. Low to moderate resistivity at shallow depths was interpreted as weathered or fractured basalt where supported by setting and curve behaviour; relatively high and laterally persistent resistivity was interpreted as compact or massive basalt. The interpretation was therefore hydrogeological rather than purely colour-based. Recent Deccan basalt resistivity studies also show that conductive or moderate-resistivity anomalies should be interpreted together with geological setting and field evidence rather than treated as direct proof of aquifer productivity [ 6 ]. 3.6 Statistical and validation analysis Quantitative evaluation used four complementary checks: class-wise area statistics for the GWPZ and GWRZ maps, hydrogeological consistency between mapped classes and VES-derived lithological interpretation, an audit of the raw infiltrometer data sheets, and an ANOVA-ready model for future independent grouped testing. The raw infiltrometer sheets contain repeated time-step readings within individual tests. These readings describe the decline of infiltration rate toward a near-steady condition and were therefore not treated as independent spatial replicates. For site-level screening, the near-steady infiltration metric was defined as the mean of the final three valid rate readings for a test, where at least three readings were available. One-way ANOVA is appropriate only when these independent site-level values are grouped by a predefined soil, geomorphic or recharge class, as indicated in Eq. ( 6 ). $$\:{H}_{0}:{\mu\:}_{1}={\mu\:}_{2}=\cdots\:={\mu\:}_{k};\:F=\frac{{\text{MS}}_{\text{between}}}{{\text{MS}}_{\text{within}}}$$ 
 (6)
 The available records support descriptive site-level screening, but they do not provide a complete georeferenced table in which every independent infiltration record is assigned to the final soil, geomorphic and recharge-map classes. Inferential p values were therefore not used as evidence for the final zoning, because doing so would either rely on pseudo-replication of time-step readings or on incomplete class membership. The analysis instead reports reproducible class-area statistics, VES-based hydrogeological validation and the ANOVA-ready model. The data-screening outcome is provided in Supplementary File S1. This treatment follows the physical-hydrology requirement that infiltration must be interpreted from independent field tests and site context, not from repeated time-step observations alone [ 9 ]. It also keeps the statistical interpretation conservative: the map is validated by physically independent evidence from VES and field interpretation, while the ANOVA framework is supplied for future datasets with replicated site-wise class assignments. 4 Results and discussion 4.1 Drainage density Drainage density is inversely related to infiltration opportunity in this setting. Areas of very low to low drainage density favour longer residence time and greater percolation, whereas high drainage density indicates greater surface runoff and limited infiltration. The drainage-density classes were derived from a 30 m DEM using sink filling, flow direction and flow accumulation routines. The 0–0.5 km/km² class was ranked most favourable, and areas above about 2.39 km/km² were ranked least favourable. The mapped drainage-density distribution is shown in Fig. 4 . 4.2 Lineament density Lineaments represent potential fractures, joints and structural pathways that can enhance groundwater movement in basaltic terrain. Higher lineament density was therefore assigned higher rank in the GWPZ model. In the study area, lineament densities of about 2.1–6.9 km/km² were treated as favourable, while 0–1.3 km/km² was treated as low-favourability. The discussion clarifies that lineament density is a proxy and must be interpreted with VES and geomorphology rather than as direct proof of aquifer productivity. The lineament-density map is shown in Fig. 5 . Lower slopes promote ponding, longer residence time and greater recharge opportunity, whereas steep dissected slopes promote rapid runoff. The 0–4-degree class was therefore ranked highest, while slopes above 16 degrees were ranked lowest. The slope classification is shown in Fig. 6 . 4.4 Geomorphology Geomorphology provides an integrated expression of erosion, deposition, topographic position and likely weathered-zone development. Highly dissected structural lower plateau occupies about 2530.42 ha and was ranked low because it promotes runoff and discontinuous storage. Pediment-pediplain complexes, ponds and water bodies were ranked higher because they favour water retention, sediment accumulation and recharge opportunity. The spatial distribution of geomorphic classes is shown in Fig. 7 . Clayey soils can store water but may reduce infiltration when swelling, compaction or silt accumulation is high. Therefore, soil was not interpreted alone. In pediment-pediplain settings, calcareous clayey soil may support moderate infiltration where slope is low and the vadose zone is not clogged by fine sediment. In steeper dissected areas, the same soil texture is less favourable because runoff dominates. The soil distribution is shown in Fig. 8 . 4.6 Lithology Lithology controls the hydrostratigraphic framework, but the groundwater significance of individual basalt units depends on weathering, fractures, vesicularity and topographic position. Low-lying basalt units associated with pediment-pediplain areas were assigned higher ranks because they allow greater residence time and possible weathered-zone development. High-elevation compact basalt units were ranked lower where they coincide with steep slopes and reduced infiltration opportunity. The lithological distribution used for ranking is shown in Fig. 9 . The very high class covers about 2.07% of the area, and the high class covers about 8.28%. These favourable zones are mainly associated with low-lying geomorphic positions, lower drainage density, higher lineament density and suitable lithology. The limited spatial extent of the very high class is hydrogeologically reasonable for a dissected basaltic terrain, where productive zones are controlled by localised weathering and fracture networks. The final GWPZ map is shown in Fig.  17 . 4.11 Groundwater recharge zone map The GWRZ output includes the infiltration-rate layer and therefore answers a different question: where can water enter the subsurface most effectively? The very high recharge-suitability class covers about 8.86% of the area. Low and very low recharge classes together dominate the landscape, reflecting steep or dissected terrain, low infiltration or unfavourable near-surface conditions. The contrast between the GWPZ and GWRZ maps confirms that groundwater occurrence and recharge suitability should not be treated as identical processes in basaltic terrain. Fig. 17 Full size image Groundwater Potential Zone map derived from seven AHP-weighted thematic layers Fig. 18 Full size image Groundwater Recharge Zone map generated after adding the infiltration-rate layer 4.12 Integrated validation and hydrogeological implications The validation discussion compares the two maps with VES evidence. In the Karewadi downstream sector, VES results show shallow weathered/fractured basalt and a deeper fractured interval at selected locations, supporting moderate to high GWPZ classes where lineaments and favourable geomorphology coincide. In Moha, high infiltration zones near tank-associated surfaces indicate recharge opportunity, but VES sections also show compact basalt below shallow weathered material; therefore, recharge interventions should be designed to enhance shallow storage and avoid assuming unlimited deep percolation. The practical implication is that artificial recharge should not be placed only on the basis of surface AHP suitability. Priority should be given to locations where (i) infiltration rate is moderate to high, (ii) slope and drainage density favour residence time, (iii) lineaments or weathered zones provide storage/transmissivity and (iv) compact basalt barriers do not isolate the recharge zone from the usable aquifer. Low-infiltration areas may still be improved through desiltation, surface treatment, farm ponds or check structures, but they require site-specific design rather than blanket classification as favourable.  1. 
 
 The study demonstrates that groundwater potential and recharge suitability should be mapped as related but separate hydrogeological decisions in semi-arid basaltic terrain. 
 
 
 
 2. 
 
 The GWPZ map identifies a spatially restricted very high potential class (about 2.07%) and a high class (about 8.28%), mainly where favourable geomorphology, low drainage density, higher lineament density and suitable lithology coincide. 
 
 
 
 3. 
 
 The GWRZ map, which adds measured infiltration rate, identifies about 8.86% of the area as very high recharge suitability. This difference confirms that surface potential and actual recharge acceptance are not equivalent. 
 
 
 
 4. 
 
 VES interpretation supports the mapped outputs by showing shallow weathered/fractured basalt zones near the tanks, commonly within about 7–10 m bgl, and compact basalt at greater depths in several sections. 
 
 
 
 5. 
 
 For recharge planning, priority should be assigned to locations where favourable map classes, measured infiltration and VES-derived weathered/fractured zones overlap. Low-infiltration areas require desiltation or engineered surface treatment before recharge structures are implemented. 
 
 
 
 6. 
 
 The workflow is suitable as a first-stage screening tool for data-limited hard-rock watersheds, but final site selection should include site-wise infiltration data, well-yield observations and repeated water-level monitoring where available.
