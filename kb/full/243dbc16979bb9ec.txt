Abstract
Purpose This study aims to investigate the physicochemical and ecotoxicological properties of postcoagulation water treat-
ment sludge (PCWTS) in the context of its use for anthropogenic land reclamation. It also attempts to promote the beneficial 
reuse of post-coagulation sludge as a valuable resource, with the intention of contributing to future research in this field.
Materials and methods In the course of the research, PCWTS after the water treatment process by a prehydrolysed coagu -
lant, polyaluminium chloride (PAC, [Al₂(OH)ₙCl6−n]ₘ), and soil from three anthropogenically altered areas were subjected 
to in-depth physicochemical (UV‒VIS, F-AAS, WDXRF, CHNS, and SEM‒EDS) and ecotoxicological analyses and assess-
ments. The potential ecological risk index (PERI), potential ecological risk factor (ER i), and geoaccumulation index (I geo) 
were calculated and interpreted. Statistical analyses (ANOV A and Tukey’s HSD test) were also used to further clarify the 
ecotoxicological results.
Results and discussion The ecological risk assessment of heavy metal pollution indicated low-to-moderate risk levels 
(ERi<80 and PERI = 206), according to the background of the soils of the Upper Silesia region. The calculated values of ERi 
were in the order of Ni > Cu> Cd > Cr> Pb > Zn, whereas Igeo was Ni > Cu> Cr > Zn> Cd > Pb. The results of the ecotoxico -
logical tests demonstrated the potential suitability of the PCWTS as an amendment for anthropogenically degraded soils. 
The use of PCWTS as a soil-improving agent does not increase the risk of heavy metal contamination in soils classified as 
suitable for use in both agricultural and green areas, and contributes to improved soil fertility by providing essential nutrients 
(N 9.7 g/kg, P 2.3 g/kg, K 22.9 g/kg).
Conclusion Despite the presence of minor heavy metals, PCWTS applied to degraded soils does not pose a high risk of 
HMs, resulting in nontoxicity and beneficial effects on plant growth. The use of PCWTS for land reclamation in industri -
ally degraded areas is an example of sustainable sludge management in water treatment plants and aptly corresponds with 
the ideas of circular economy and the principles of sustainable development. While this study provides valuable short-term 
insights based on a 14-day evaluation, the absence of a long-term assessment remains a limitation that warrants further 
investigation.

1 Introduction
The use of advanced surface water treatment processes is 
associated with the production of varying amounts of waste 
in the form of sediments, which may contain various pol -
lutants. For this reason, they should be stored in landfills 
or disposed of in accordance with all the relevant environ -
mental regulations. However, the sludge originating from 
water treatment processes can also be treated or beneficially 
reused in line with the principles of green chemistry, circu -
lar economy and sustainable development.
Water treatment requires the application of several key 
unit processes. One of the stages is coagulation, in which 
organic and inorganic compounds are removed in sus -
pended, colloidal and dissolved forms. The most commonly 
used coagulants in the water treatment process (WTP) are 
nonhydrolysed aluminium salts. These include aluminium 
sulfate (VI) (Al 2(SO4)3, alum) and aluminium chloride 
(AlCl3) (Pająk 2023; Łukasiewicz 2016; Tan et al. 2024). 
In many cases, aluminium sulfate (VI) is the first-choice 
coagulant because it is inexpensive, available, safe to han -
dle, and easy to use (Bérubé and Dorea 2008; Matilainen 
et al. 2005). Nevertheless, the effective removal of con -
taminants, production of high-quality water, and increased 
need to optimize the cost-to-price ratio require the use of 
prehydrolysed coagulants (e.g., polyaluminium chlorides, 
PAC, [Al₂(OH)ₙCl (6−n)]ₘ, and aluminium chlorohydrate, 
ACH, Al nCl3n−m(OH)m), which display many advantages 
over conventional (nonhydrolysed) coagulants (Jiang and 
Graham 1998; Łukasiewicz and Rząsa 2017; Nowacka et 
al. 2014).
There are several practical methods for recovering and 
utilizing the reuse of postcoagulation water treatment sludge 
(PCWTS). Some of them include binding of hydrogen sul -
fide (H2S) in sewage treatment plants, the removal of phos-
phorus during wastewater treatment, the immobilization of 
phosphorus in eutrophic lakes (Ahmad et al. 2016), as well 
as its external reuse for the manufacturing of construction 
materials such as cement and bricks (Mohamed et al. 2022). 
PCWTS can be used for road surfaces, for instance in the 
case of soil-sludge mixtures with the addition of cement 
in paving structures as sub-bases, soil-sludge mixtures 
with the addition of lime as sub-bases, and to strengthen 
the ground (Takao at el. 2024). Additionally, PCWTS can 
be used to improve soil properties during land reclamation, 
thereby protecting the surface of a closed landfill from water 
and wind erosion (Balkaya 2016).
Compared with wastewater treatment sludge (WWTS), 
the application of PCWTS for land reclamation has not 
been widely reported in the subject literature (Boudjabi and 
Chenchouni 2021; Lue-Hing 2023; Tamanini et al. 2008). 
However, it seems to be a promising strategy, especially in 
areas degraded by mining activity. The physicochemical 
characteristics of PCWTS allow it to be utilized as a soil 
substitute because of its content of essential nutrients such 
as nitrogen (N), phosphorous (P), potassium (K), calcium 
(Ca) and magnesium (Mg) (Ashworth et al. 2020; McCau-
ley et al. 2011). However, the total concentrations of plant 
nutrients in aluminium-based residuals do not necessarily 
reflect plant availability. According to the Polish Waste Act 
(Parliament of the Republic of Poland 2012), sludge and 
backwash water from WTPs should be treated as hazardous 
waste because of their contents of toxic metals, inorganic 
and organic contaminants and harmful microorganisms. 
Surface water, especially river water, contains many con -
taminants; thus, the use of sediments could lead to recon -
tamination of the environment (Ahmad et al. 2016). The 
composition and properties of PCWTS depend largely on 
its source, including the characteristics of the raw water and 
the applied treatment process. Changes in drinking water 
quality have been the subject of many studies for many 
years (Piedgoń 2024; Mensah-Akutteh et al. 2022; Ouyang 
et al. 2006). The quality of raw water may vary seasonally 
and reflect contaminant inputs resulting from activities car-
ried out within the catchment area. Therefore, the composi-
tion of PCWTS produced even within one water treatment 
plant may change (Fiore et al. 2022). The properties of 
the PCWTS are also influenced by the type and dosage of 
coagulant used (aluminium- and iron-based), as well as the 
particular stages of the WTP, including technological pro -
cesses such as thickening, dewatering or storage (Ahmad 
et al. 2016; Fiore et al. 2022; Płonka et al. 2026; Takao et 
al. 2024). For these reasons, the results obtained for sludge 
from a single water treatment plant should not be directly 
extrapolated to residuals generated at other facilities. Con -
sidering the potential environmental risks and variability 
associated with the WTP, the assessment proposed in this 
study takes into account multiple criteria, including the fol-
lowing three aspects.
Firstly, it needs to be noted that in the case of PCWTS, 
there is a risk of environmental pollution due to the presence 
of heavy metals (HMs), such as arsenic (As), barium (Ba), 
cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), copper (Cr), nickel (Ni),

lead (Pb) and zinc (Zn) (Bartoszewski 1996; Leszczyńska 
2009). The contamination of soils by HMs due to their tox-
icity, long-term persistence and bioaccumulation capacity 
may lead to environmental problems. However, the envi -
ronmental risk depends not only on total concentrations 
but also on HMs fractionation and speciation, soil pH, and 
redox changes, which affect their mobility and potential 
uptake by plants.
In addition, the high aluminium content of PCWTS 
deposited in soil requires particular attention. The solubil -
ity, speciation and bioavailability of aluminium are strongly 
dependent on the soil pH. Under acidic conditions (pH < 5.5), 
the amount of exchangeable and potentially toxic form of 
aluminium (Al3+) in the soil solution increases and becomes 
more available for plants. (Nguyen et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 
2018). Moreover, Al-based water treatment residuals have 
a high phosphorus sorption capacity, which may reduce its 
bioavailability and potentially affect plant growth (Zhao et 
al. 2018). Aluminium speciation may also be influenced 
by interactions with dissolved organic matter (Weng et al. 
2022). Additionally, when assessing the environmental 
safety of PCWTS application to soil, potential long-term 
changes associated with its ageing (Agyin-Birikorang and 
O’Connor 2009) or weathering over time should also be 
considered. Because of this, it is necessary to carry out 
physicochemical and ecotoxicological studies to determine 
the composition and properties of PCWTS, and to identify 
its potential adverse effects on the environment, even when 
it is applied in degraded areas.
Secondly, phytotoxicity tests are crucial for environmen-
tal monitoring and risk assessment, especially in cases of 
waste disposal in contaminated soils (EMPPO 2007). Stan-
dard parameters analysed during these studies, typically 
including root and shoot length, germination percentage, 
and biomass production for selected species of test organ -
isms, should be determined.
Third, to assess the environmental risk associated with 
the potential introduction of heavy metals, the potential eco-
logical risk factor (ERi), the potential ecological risk index 
(PERI) and the geoaccumulation index (Igeo), should be cal-
culated (Hakanson 1980; Muller 1969). Considering these 
circumstances and the lack of comprehensive and sufficient 
scientific work in this field, the objective of this study was 
to assess the short-term suitability of PCWTS containing 
aluminium compounds as an amendment for three anthro -
pogenically degraded soils. The investigations carried out 
to assess the potential use of the PCWTS for land reclama -
tion included: (i) physicochemical characterization of the 
PCWTS and degraded soils, (ii) environmental risk assess -
ment calculations based on HMs content, (iii) short-term 
ecotoxicological testing, and (iv) short-term laboratory-
scale pot tests. The specific novel contribution of this study 
are the integrated assessment of PCWTS, which combines 
physicochemical characterization, ecological risk assess -
ment, and short-term plant response in different industri -
ally degraded soils. Although the land application of water 
treatment residuals has been investigated (Dayton and Basta 
2001; Alvarenga et al. 2018), their use for the reclamation of 
anthropogenically degraded and contaminated soils, includ-
ing mine spoil areas, remains relatively poorly documented. 
The subject of the research carried out aligns with the con -
cept of circular economy (European Commission 2019) and 
the idea of sustainable development, providing an approach 
to the use of byproducts, minimizing, and even improving 
the condition of the environment.
2 Materials and methods
2.1 Postcoagulation water treatment sludge and 
anthropogenic sites
PCWTS samples were collected repeatedly over a three-
month period at several-day intervals (24 individual sam -
ples in total) from a water treatment plant located in the 
Silesian V oivodeship. The samples were collected after 
implementing the water treatment process via a prehy -
drolysed coagulant, polyaluminium chlorides, PAC, and 
[Al₂(OH)ₙCl6−n]ₘ (pH 4.4 ± 0.1, d 1.125 ± 0.113 g/mL, Al 
11.5 ± 0.1%, chloride 6.5 ± 0.1%, alkalinity 80.1 ± 0.1%). 
The test samples were obtained by mixing and accurately 
averaging individual samples. The samples from three 
anthropogenically transformed sites (A1, A2 and A3) were 
collected from areas degraded by industrial activity from 
the surface in the most industrialized region of Poland, the 
Upper Silesian Coal Basin. Two sites are located in the 
central part of the basin, and one is on the border of Sile -
sia and Malopolska V oivodships. Samples A2 and A3 were 
collected from coal mine spoil heaps, and sample A1 was 
from an industrial area heavily contaminated with heavy 
metals. The primary samples from the anthropogenic sites 
were collected from depths of 0–20 cm at ten sampling 
points in accordance with ISO 18400-104. The collected 
samples were placed in plastic bags and thoroughly mixed 
to obtain laboratory samples. An average of 25 kg of 
laboratory samples of PCWTS and A1, A2 and A3 were 
transported to the laboratory and stored in a refrigerator at 
4 ± 1 °C. Before physicochemical analysis, all the samples 
were air-dried at ambient temperature (14 days, 20 ± 1 °C, 
relative humidity, 55 ± 5%), crushed in a ball mill and 
sifted through a 2 mm sieve (Retsch GmbH, Germany). 
The sludge and soil samples were analysed as described 
in the analytical procedures section. The locations of the 
sampling sites are presented in Fig. 1.

2.2 Physicochemical analysis of solid samples
The moisture content and dry matter content were deter -
mined by drying the sample at 105 ± 1 °C to a constant 
weight according to PN-EN 12880:2004. Ash and organic 
matter contents were determined by burning the sample at 
550 °C according to PN-EN 12879:2004. The total phos -
phorus (Total P) content was determined spectrophotomet -
rically (Nanocolor® UV‒VIS, Macherey-Nagel GmbH, 
Germany) according to PN-Z-15011-3, whereas the total 
nitrogen (Total N) amount was determined according to 
the Kjeldahl method described in PN-EN 13342:2002. The 
concentrations of metals (Cd, Cr, Cu, K, Ni, Pb, and Zn) in 
the PCWTS were determined via flame atomic absorption 
spectrometry (FAAS) according to PN-ISO 8288:2002 
(SolaarS4, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) after prior 
mineralization of the samples in aqua regia (HCl+HNO 3 
3:1) under reflux cooling. The contents of Al, P, Cd, Co, 
Cr, Cu, K, Mn, Mo, Ni, Pb, and Zn in the soil samples 
were measured via wavelength-dispersive X-ray fluo -
rescence spectrometry (WDXRF) (Rigaku ZSX Primus, 
Rigaku Holdings Corp., USA). The infrared spectroscopy 
method (ELTRA CHNS, Eltra GmbH, Germany) was used 
to determine the content of total sulfur (Total S). The con -
centrations of total dissolved solids (TDS) and salinity, 
as well as measurements of pH and electrical conductiv -
ity (EC) in water leachate (water extracts, soil: deionized 
water, 1:5), were determined via the analytical procedures 
described in detail in Sect. 2.3. SEM/EDS analysis was 
performed via a high-resolution scanning electron micro -
scope (Apreo 2 S LoVac; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). 
The studied sample was placed on double-sided adhesive 
carbon tape (SPI Supplies, West Chester, USA) and then 
sputtered with Au (2.5 nm) under vacuum in an Ar atmo -
sphere via a Safematic CCU-010 HV high vacuum com -
pact coating unit (Safematic GmbH, Zizers, Switzerland). 
For EDS analysis, an EDS UltraDry detector was used 
(Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). T1 (backscattered elec -
trons) and ETD (Everhart–Thornley) (secondary electrons) 
were used for imaging. All the experiments were performed 
in triplicate.
2.3 Physicochemical analysis of water leachates
The water leachate (water extract) was prepared accord -
ing to PN-EN 12457-2:2006 (dry matter: deionized water 
1 kg:10 L), an overhead shaker (Rotax 6.8, Velp Scien -
tific, Italy), 24 h, 20 rpm, 21 ± 1 °C). After shaking, the 
water extract was separated via a vacuum filter (0.45 μm, 
Whatman, UK). The pH and electrical conductivity (EC) 
were measured electrometrically (CPC-401, Elmetron ®, 
Poland) according to PN-EN ISO 10523:2012 and PN-EN 
27888:1999, respectively. Total suspended solids (TSS) and 
sulfate (VI) were determined gravimetrically via methods 
described in PN-EN 872:2007 and PN-ISO 9280:2002, 
respectively. Chloride was measured via the Mohr method 
according to PN-ISO 9297:1994. Chemical oxygen demand 
(COD), total and dissolved organic carbon (TOC, DOC), 
total phosphorus (Total P), total nitrogen (Total N) and the 
concentrations of Ca 2+, Mg 2+, SO 4
2–, and F – were deter -
mined spectrophotometrically (Nanocolor ® UV‒VIS, 
Macherey-Nagel GmbH, Germany) via test kits (Macherey-
Nagel, Germany). The concentrations of trace metals (Cd, 
Cr, Cu, Ni, Pb, and Zn) were quantified via FAAS (SolaarS4 
AA Series Spectrometer®, Thermo Fisher Scientific®, USA) 
prior to digestion with aqua regia according to PN-ISO 
8288:2002. The calibration curves for each metal were pre-
pared via five standard solutions (Certipur ®, Merck®, Ger-
many). The R2 values were no less than 0.9993 (Ni), 0.9999 
(Cu), 0.9991 (Pb), 0.9999 (Cr), 0.9985 (Cd), and 0.9993 
(K). A segmented calibration curve fit with a minimum cur-
vature of -4% and a maximum curvature of 7% was used 
for Zn determination. All the experiments were performed 
in triplicate.

2.4 Microbiotests
The ecotoxicity assessment was carried out via second-gen-
eration microbiotests with test organisms (MicrobioTests 
Inc., Belgium), both for the solid PCWTS and their water 
leachates. The extracts were prepared from a sample of 
PCWTS (dry matter: deionized water 1 kg:10 L, overhead 
shaker (Rotax 6.8, Velp Scientific, Italy), 24 h, 20 rpm, 
21 ± 1 °C). The solid particles were removed from the 
extracts via centrifugation (4000 rpm, 30 min, DM0 424, 
Chemland, Poland). The laboratory conditions of the micro-
biotests are presented in Table 1. All tests were performed 
in triplicate.
2.5 Pot tests
This test aimed to determine the effect of PCWTS amend -
ment on short-term plant growth in samples collected from 
anthropogenic sites. For the plant test, a mixture of univer -
sal grass seeds consisting of 30% Lolium perenne-Aut, 10% 
Festuca rubra-Dipper, 10% Festuca rubra-Aido, 5% Fes-
tuca rubra-Capricio, 15% Poa pratensis-Lincolnshire, and 
15% Festuca arundinacea-Starlett (Rolimpex SA, Iława, 
Poland) was used. The study was conducted in pots measur-
ing 6⋅6⋅6 cm with drainage holes. Before analysis, the sam-
ples from the anthropogenic sites and sludge were air-dried, 
crushed and sieved through a 2 mm sieve. PCWTS was 
added to A1, A2, and A3 at weights of 10, 15, and 20 wt% 
and blended. A weight of 0.5 g of grass seeds was subse -
quently placed at a depth of 0.5 cm. Pots were watered with 
20 mL of distilled water per day, covered in the plant propa-
gator and stored under laboratory conditions (22 ± 1 °C) 
with exposure to daylight for 12 h daily. After 14 days, the 
sprouted grass biomass was harvested, and the plants were 
subsequently washed under running water to remove soil 
particles and weighed. All the experiments were performed 
in triplicate.

3 Results
3.1 Analysis of the physicochemical parameters of 
the PCWTS and anthropogenic site samples
The physicochemical parameters of the PCWTS, which 
define its suitability for application in anthropogenic soils, and 
samples collected from anthropogenic sites (A1–A3) are pre-
sented in Table 2. Analysis of the sludge composition revealed 
dry matter and organic matter at the levels of 22.1% and 
30.3%, respectively, with a total carbon content of 10.22%. 
The measured concentration of total nitrogen was 9.7 g/kg, 
while the total sulfur concentration was 3.5 g/kg.
The results of the physicochemical analysis of the A1–A3 
samples revealed that the organic matter content ranged from 
very low (4.6%) to high (25.7%). The measured parameters 
responsible for the salinity at the anthropogenic sites revealed 
that sample A2, which was collected from a coal mine spoil 
heap, presented the highest values of EC (2980 µS/cm) and 
TDS (1493 mg/L) in comparison with samples A1 (108 µS/cm 
and 53.9 mg/L, respectively) and A3 (685 µS/cm 342 mg/L), 
respectively. The pH value of sample A2 was strongly acidic 
(pH 2.8), which corresponded to high salinity (1580 mg 
NaCl/L). In comparison, samples A1 and A3 were moderately 
acidic (pH 5.25) and alkaline (pH 8.35), while the PCWTS 
sample was nearly neutral (pH 6.5).
Analysis of the PCWTS sample at the soil-to-water ratio 
(L: S = 5:1) revealed that the concentrations of TDS and 
salinity were 126 and 122 mg/L, respectively. The results 
revealed that the EC value of the sludge was 251 µS/cm, 
whereas the corresponding pH values after 2 and 24 h 
reached 6.50 and 6.55, respectively.
The concentrations of metals in the tested samples and their 
limit values for soils classified into Groups II–IV according 
to Polish regulations (Minister of the Environment, 2016) 
are presented in Table 3. The total P content in the PCWTS 
(2.3 g/kg) was much higher than that in samples collected

relatively low content in soil is beneficial for limiting the 
occurrence of eutrophication in water bodies (Muisa et al. 
2020).
One of the major components in the residual from the 
coagulation process in the WTP is aluminium. Prehydro -
lysed forms of coagulants such as polyaluminium chloride 
generate lower concentrations of residual aluminium in 
treated water than nonhydrolysed salts do. The content of 
Al2O3 in the polyaluminium chloride coagulant was 22.6%, 
and its measured pH was 4.2 (Łukasiewicz and Rząsa 
2017). Our study indicated that the Al content in PCWTS 
expressed in Al2O3 was 10.7%, which was relatively lower 
than that in typical sludge characterized in the literature. In 
comparison, the Al2O3 content in the most widely used alum 
sludge (Al2(SO4)3∙18H2O) ranges between 12.1 and 51.1% 
(Agyin-Birikorang and O’Connor 2009; Hovsepyan and 
Bonzongo 2009; Pająk 2023).
Data from the literature confirm that prehydrolysed 
coagulants are considered more effective in the water treat -
ment process and require lower dosages than nonhydro -
lysed coagulants do (Kłos and Gumińska 2010). The lower 
Al concentration in sludge is beneficial for its use in land 
reclamation and makes PCWTS a suitable amendment 
for soil. In accordance with Polish regulations concerning 
ground surface pollution (Minister of Environment 2016), 
Al is not listed as a toxic element in soil. However, some 
coagulants have shown that an increase in aluminium com -
pounds in soil solutions is harmful to trees and agricultural 
plants (Kluczka et al. 2017). The toxic effects of Al on 
plants are associated with Al 3+ cations, whose concentra -
tion increases when the soil pH is less than 5.5 (Zhao et al. 
2018). In acidic soils, the concentration of Al 3+ is greater 
than that in slightly alkaline soils. Insoluble aluminium 
compounds such as aluminium oxide (i.e., Al 2O3) are con-
siderably less toxic than soluble forms such as aluminium 
chloride (AlCl3), aluminium nitrate (Al(NO3)3), aluminium 
acetate ((CH3COO)3Al), and aluminium sulfate (Al2(SO4)3) 
(US EPA 2003). Furthermore, some studies have reported 
that using sludge containing aluminium compounds as a 
soil ameliorant has no adverse effect on plant growth (Das -
sanayake et al. 2015). Kim et al. ( 2002) confirmed that the 
application of alum sludge to acidic soil with a pH of 4 in 
a greenhouse increased the buffer capacity, hydraulic con -
ductivity, water-holding capacity, and phosphate adsorption 
of the soil, leading to increases in biomass and root elon -
gation. However, other studies reported different findings. 
For example, Dayton and Basta ( 2001) reported that the 
use of more air-dried sludge with Al 2(SO4)3 in farmlands 
may decrease crop yield. A laboratory study conducted by 
Agyin-Birikorang and O’Connor (2009) has confirmed that 
land application of freshly generated aluminium sludge has 
no harmful effect on plants or grazing animals but should be 
avoided because of the low availability of Al 3+. Neverthe-
less, in some cases, the high Al content in sludge may also 
cause adverse effects. The land application of aluminium 
sludge can affect the adsorption of phosphates and other 
plant nutrients in soil (Ahmad et al. 2016). The surface 
water quality criterion (Minister of Maritime Economy and 
Inland Navigation 2019) indicates that aluminium concen -
trations below 3 mg/L are generally considered not harm -
ful to the aquatic environment or soil. However, at low pH 
values, aluminium occurs in the form of bioavailable and 
soluble Al3+ ions or hydroxocomplexes, posing a potentially 
lethal threat to aquatic organisms (Mortula et al. 2009). This 
is important in the context of soil remediation and waste 
management. The present research has revealed that the 
concentration of Al in the water extracts from PCWTS was 
0.01 mg/L, indicating that there was no environmental risk 
to surface water or soil after disposal. In addition, prehy -
drolysed forms of coagulants, such as polyaluminium chlo-
ride, generate lower concentrations of residual aluminium 
in treated water than nonhydrolysed salts do, and the use 
of prehydrolysed coagulants results in a better coagulation 
process at a lower dosage (Kłos and Gumińska 2010).
Several researchers have demonstrated a significant cor -
relation between soil pH and the phytotoxicity of Al spe -
cies. The concentrations and toxicity of Al species in the soil 
environment depend on the pH level and chemical composi-
tion of the soil solution. Several soluble Al species, such as 
Al3+, Al(OH)2+, Al(OH)2+, and Al(OH)4−, have been shown 
to occur when the soil pH is less than 5. Al dissolution 
occurs as a result of soil acidification, which leads to the 
release of phytotoxic Al3+. At a pH < 4.3, trivalent Al3+, the 
most abundant and toxic species, has the greatest impact on 
plant growth (Rahman et al. 2018; Kisnieriené and Lapei -
kaité 2015; Singh et al. 2017).
Although aluminium mobilization under strongly acidic 
conditions may represent a potential environmental risk, 
our results show (Table 2) that the Al concentration in the 
PCWTS (56.4 g/kg) is lower than that observed in two of 
the degraded soils tested in this study, i.e., A2 (84.7 g/kg) 
and A3 (85.2 g/kg). The concentration of Al in PCWTS 
is also lower than that in other sludge produced in other 
water treatment plants, which was 93.3 g/kg (Takao et al. 
2024). Particular attention should be given to soil A2, which 
originates from a mining waste heap and is characterized 
1 3
Page 15 of 22 358 
Journal of Soils and Sediments (2026) 26:358 
by strongly acidic conditions (pH = 2.8). In this case, add -
ing PCWTS is unlikely to increase the environmental risk 
of Al in the degraded area. In contrast, compared with the 
PCWTS, soil A1 (pH = 5.2) is characterized by a lower Al 
concentration (15.4 g/kg). Therefore, under these condi -
tions, the application of PCWTS should be considered more 
carefully. Owing to the variable composition of PCWTS 
resulting from many factors, its use for the reclamation of 
anthropogenically degraded soils must be preceded by a 
detailed analysis.
4.2 Ecotoxicological responses of PCWTS leachate 
in the context of literature data
In the literature, studies addressing the ecotoxicological 
assessment of postcoagulation sludge derived from the 
water treatment process are lacking. However, several stud-
ies have investigated the ecotoxicity of sewage sludge or 
leachates from municipal waste, and microbiological tests 
have been used to assess their potential effects on soil or 
aquatic microorganisms (Table 7). Sotero-Santos et al. 
(2005) analysed the toxicity of alum sludge to Daph-
nia similis, and no toxic effect on the mortality of the test 
organism was observed. Yuan et al. ( 2020) reported that 
dewatered water treatment residue has no toxic effect on 
the survival, growth, reproduction, body morphology, or 
antioxidant enzyme activity of Daphnia magna. These find-
ings are consistent with the results obtained in the present 
study. An acute immobilization test of PCWTS, which uses 
Daphnia magna, indicated 30% toxicity after 24 and 48 h of 
exposure, which corresponds to moderate toxicity, accord -
ing to the Guidelines for the Testing of Chemicals (OCDE 
2004).
Moreover, the results of the remaining toxicity tests on 
the water extract from the PCWTS revealed in this study 
were also negative. These findings indicate that the percent-
age immobilization (I 15,30<50%) of Aliivibrio fischeri and 
the percentage inhibition of root growth of Sinapis alba and 
Lepidium sativum (I < 50%) were less toxic. In comparison 
with a study carried out by other authors, at the same ratio 
of 1:10 kg/L, the growth inhibition of test plants in a sample 
of sludge from pulp waste stimulated the growth of L. sati-
vum (86.63%) and inhibited the growth of S. alba (62.92%) 
(Novak 2023). The toxicity of two types of sewage sludge 
from a wastewater treatment plant (dewatered and anaerobi-
cally stabilized) in relation to Sinapis alba was studied by 
Adamcová et al. ( 2016a, 2016b). The results revealed that 
both sludges at a concentration of 10% were toxic, and the 
average growth inhibition factors were 96.9% and 77.1%, 
respectively. Aquatic toxicity tests were carried out by Kočí 
et al. ( 2010) using eluates (1:10 kg/L) from water-treated 
sludge. The calculated value of inhibition for Sinapis alba 
root elongation was 18%, which is comparable to our results 
(27.2%). Compared with wastewater treatment sludge, the 
data in Table 9 show adverse effects (more than 50% inhibi-
tion) on the growth of Sinapis alba and Lepidium sativum in 
sewage sludge (Adamcová et al. 2016a) or industrial waste-
water (Novak 2023). One of the reasons for the toxic effects 
on plant growth and development is the salinity of the soil, 
expressed as the electrical conductivity of its water leachate. 
According to the general classification (Miller and Dona -
hue 1995), slight soil salinity may be observed when the EC 
exceeds 2 mS/cm, and high salinity may occur when the EC 
is greater than 8 mS/cm. Under these conditions, sprouting 
and biomass growth may be restricted. In general, the main 
ions associated with soil salinity are K+, Na+, SO4
2– and Cl–. 
Our research confirmed that the measured concentration of 
dissolved ions in water leachate from PCWTS is signifi -
cantly low (77 mg NaCl/L for TDS, 20 mg/L for SO4
2– and 
42 mg/L for Cl –), and the measured value of EC is 0.154 
mS/cm.
The results of the pot test in which a mixture of grass 
was added to anthropogenic soils suggest that there was no 
acute phytotoxic effect at PCWTS amendment rates rang -
ing from 10 to 20%. A positive impact on biomass growth 
was observed across all the tested soil samples (A1–A3) 
collected from areas degraded by industrial activity. These 
findings indicate that PCWTS may improve the quality of 
contaminated soils. However, as the pot test was conducted 
under short-term conditions only, further investigations are 
still required. To fully evaluate the environmental safety and 
suitability of PCWTS for soil application, long-term stud -
ies that track the accumulation of potentially toxic metals in 
plant tissues and changes in their concentrations in the soil 
over time would be beneficial.
4.3 Ecological risk of PCWTS and regulatory 
thresholds of heavy metals
Another reason is the high concentration of toxic sub -
stances, including HMs. Water-treatedsludge contains sub -
stances that may originate from both the raw water and the 
reagents used in the treatment process (Piedgoń 2024; Fiore 
et al. 2022). Studies indicate that the majority of postcoagu-
lation sludge does not contain excessive amounts of toxic 
substances that would classify them as hazardous. However, 
when sludge contains high concentrations of heavy metals, 
it may pose a threat not only to soils but also to groundwater 
and even surface water.

ment sludge to soils are not available. Therefore, the deter -
mined HM concentrations in the tested PCWTS were 
compared with the maximum contents of toxic elements

described as hazardous for soil (Minister of Environment, 
2016), ranged from 20.4 to 624 mg/kg for Cu, 6.7 to 38.5 mg/
kg for Cr, 0.12 to 1.8 mg/kg for Cd, 18.2 to 43.58 mg/kg for 
Ni, 2.7 to 22 mg/kg for Pb, and 28.9 to 98 mg/kg for Zn. 
The concentrations of the same HMs in this study were rela-
tively high. However, the total concentration of HMs in the 
tested PCWTS was below the permissible levels in terms of 
sewage sludge application for land reclamation in nonagri -
cultural areas. In accordance with the Polish requirements 
(Minister of Environment 2015) and the European Council 
Directive 86/278/EEC (European Commission 1986), which 
establish the criteria for the use of municipal sewage sludge 
which may be applied to land, the limit values of toxic ele -
ments are as follows (in mg/kg): 25 for Cd, 1200 for Cu, 
1000 for Cr, 400 for Ni, 1000 for Pb, and 3500 for Zn. An 
ecological risk assessment revealed that the use of PCWTS 
as a soil-improving agent may not pose a high risk of HMs 
contamination in areas degraded by industrial activity as 
evaluated against the background concentrations of soils 
in the Upper Silesia region. The calculated risk level for 
green areas, excluding agricultural soils, located in Poland 
was considerable, with a total PERI value of less than 600. 
However, considering the background values for soil in the 
Upper Silesia region, the risk was moderate, and the PERI 
value did not exceed 300. A comprehensive review of sub -
ject literature revealed a lack of data concerning ecological 
risk assessments for water treatment sludge. Ashong et al. 
(2024) demonstrated that the presence of Mn, Pb and Zn in 
post-treatment sludge from the WTP was very low and did 
not have a toxic effect on either the environment or human 
health. For comparison purposes, Table 8 presents the val -
ues of the ecological risk assessment indicators for sludge 
from water and wastewater treatment. The highest risk 
level, with a PERI greater than 1000, was indicated for sew-
age sludge. The analysis revealed that cadmium (Cd) was 
the most harmful element. These results are consistent with 
our analysis of green areas in Poland, where the calculated 
value of ERi for Cd was significant (287). However, the aim 
of the present research was to assess the feasibility of using 
PCWTS as a material for land reclamation in anthropogeni-
cally degraded soil in the case of which the Cd concentra -
tion may exceed pre-existing levels before the addition of 
PCWTS. Compared with the sewage sludge sample, the 
PCWTS sample was characterized by a higher content of Ni 
(74 mg/kg), but its ER i was moderate. Although the SEM/
EDS results provided complementary information on the 
elemental composition of the PCWTS sample surface, this 
method does not allow the detection of potentially toxic 
elements at trace concentrations, which may pose an envi -
ronmental risk. However, the high contents of aluminium, 
silicon, and iron observed seem to suggest that the PCWTS 
sample possesses considerable mineral purity and consists 
mainly of inorganic residue. Our results align with those 
of Saleman et al. ( 2023), who reported that the weight% 
of sludge from a water treatment plant ranged mainly from 
7.25 to 26.85% carbon, 29.04–40.93% oxygen, 6.83–7.59% 
aluminium, and 7.33–8.70% silicon.
To assess the mobility of toxic metals in soil, additional 
studies are still required. The use of sequential metal extrac-
tion procedures (Rauret et al. 2001) in sludge and its mix -
tures with anthropogenic soils may constitute a crucial 
aspect in this case. Such analyses could provide deeper 
insight into the specifications of metals and their potential 
mobility in response to long-term environmental processes, 
including weathering, changes in pH, and redox conditions. 
An additional limitation of this study consists in the fact that 
the leaching tests were carried out separately for samples of 
degraded soils (A1–A3) and for PCWTS, whereas no analy-
sis was conducted for soil with a 10–20% PCWTS amend -
ment. Therefore, assessing the mobility and bioavailability 
of HMs and Al in soil mixtures, particularly under strongly 
acidic conditions, requires additional research.
5 Conclusion
PCWTS is a semisolid byproduct generated during the treat-
ment of surface water to obtain high-quality drinking water. 
It is a mixture of water, organic matter, inorganic particles, 
and treatment chemicals such as coagulants and flocculants. 
PCWTS mainly results from coagulation, flocculation, and 
sedimentation processes and typically contains aluminium or 
iron hydroxides, depending on the applied coagulant. HMs 
and micropollutants may limit reuse, and improper disposal 
may contaminate soil, as well as ground and surface water. 
For this reason, in many countries, regulations often dictate 
disposal and reuse strategies. The composition of PCWTS 
together with its physicochemical and ecotoxicological 
properties are of key importance in the process of assessing 
its environmental impact and delimitating its practical use. 
However, the results of this short-term study indicate the 
potential suitability of PCWTS as an amendment for land 
reclamation in low-quality and industrially degraded soils.

be drawn from the results of ecotoxicological studies using 
selected test organisms (Aliivibrio fischeri, Daphnia magna, 
Lepidium sativum, and Sinapis alba). The results revealed the 
nontoxicity of PCWTS and its water extracts in the case of all 
the microbiotests. However, the PCWTS results from other 
water treatment plants should be further investigated. One 
of the reasons is that the HMs content in PCWTS strongly 
depends on the source water, the nature of the catchment area 
and the coagulant used. In turn, the results of the short-term 
pot tests using a grass mixture demonstrate that the effect of 
PCWTS amendment on increasing biomass is proportional 
to the dose used. Hence, despite the presence of minor heavy 
metals, PCWTS can be safely reused in soil, resulting in non-
toxicity and beneficial effects on plant growth and soil qual-
ity. Therefore, in terms of the presence of heavy metals, the 
application of sludge is more justified at anthropogenic sites 
in Upper Silesia than in Polish soils in general. One limita -
tion of this study is the lack of long-term field experiments, 
along with assessing the accumulation of toxic metals in plant 
tissues and recultivated soils. To confirm the use of PCWTS 
for land reclamation at anthropogenic sites characterized by 
strongly acidic properties, such as mining waste heaps, further 
research under low-pH conditions is crucial.
The use of PCWTS aligns harmoniously with the con -
cepts of sustainable development and circular economy. 
However, pollution control and the potential development 
of technologies that can safely return these materials to the 
environment as recultivated soil are of vital importance. 
This work can constitute an important step in the discussion, 
design, planning, testing, and evaluation of the beneficial 
use of PCWTS support for more sustainable management 
practices and the implementation of circular economy 
principles. These findings highlight that the application of 
PCWTS in anthropogenic soils may contribute to sustain -
able land management, particularly in regions affected by 
long-term industrial degradation.
