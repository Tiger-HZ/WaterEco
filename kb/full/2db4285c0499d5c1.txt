Combined Reuse of Paint Industry and Cattle 
Slaughterhouse Effluents for the Fertigation of Mombaça 
Grass
Warlyton Silva Martins · Lidiane Brito Souza · Thainara Lima Araújo · Rodrigo Ribeiro Fidelis · 
Anna Karla dos Santos Pereira · Grasiele Soares Cavallini
Received: 18 October 2025 / Accepted: 1 September 2026 / Published online: 12 September 2026 
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract This study aimed to evaluate the effects of 
the combined application of effluents from the paint 
industry and cattle slaughterhouses on the fertigation 
of Mombaça grass (Panicum maximum cv. Mom-
baça). The experiment was conducted in pots with 
12 plants using a completely randomized design in a 
5 × 2 factorial arrangement, with five sampling times 
(0, 2, 4, 6, and 8  months) and evaluated under two 
conditions: with and without application of the com-
bined effluent at a 1:1 ratio. Fertigation promoted a 
significant increase in Mombaça grass productiv -
ity, reaching an average of 18.28 t/ha of dry matter. 
Greater extraction of nitrogen, calcium, phospho-
rus, potassium, and magnesium was observed over 
12  months, even with continuous plant extraction. 
An increase in potential cation exchange capacity 
was also observed, indicating improved soil fertility. 
In contrast, there was a decreasing trend in pH. The 
effective cation exchange capacity (ECCe) remained 
stable, revealing a dynamic balance between nutri-
ent input and absorption. The data demonstrate the 
agronomic potential of reusing these effluents as a 
sustainable alternative for fertigation yet highlight 
the importance of long-term studies to assess poten-
tial environmental risks and contaminant accumu-
lation. This study contributes to the integration of 
liquid-waste management and sustainable agricultural 
practices.
Keywords Fertigation · Industrial effluent · Soil 
fertility · Panicum maximum
1 Introduction
The intensification of agriculture in the face of the 
growing demand for food, fiber, and energy has 
imposed significant challenges on the sustainability 
of production systems, especially in the face of water 
scarcity and the increasing generation of industrial 
waste (FAO, 2022). Notably, the agricultural reuse of 
effluents emerges as a strategic alternative, associat-
ing the use of wastewater with the supply of nutri-
ents to the soil, promoting the conservation of natural 
resources and complementary fertilization (Kiziloğlu 
et al., 2020; Santos et al., 2023).
Paint manufacturing and cattle slaughtering indus-
tries consume large quantities of water and, con-
sequently, generate significant volumes of effluent 
(Martins et al., 2025; Nair et al., 2021), making them

subjects of great interest for agricultural reuse. How -
ever, these effluents present concerning environmen-
tal limitations: paint industry effluent contains toxic 
metals and compounds derived from organic solvents 
(Nair et al., 2021), while cattle slaughterhouse efflu-
ent has high concentrations of sodium and pathogenic 
microorganisms (Freitas et  al., 2026). Although fer -
tigation is an economically viable reuse method, its 
application to the soil requires caution, particularly 
due to potential risks associated with nutrient leach-
ing, salinization, contaminant mobilization, impacts 
on groundwater quality, and surface runoff into water 
bodies.
Effluent from the cattle slaughter industry is widely 
used in fertigation, especially in pastures, because of 
its high content of organic matter, nitrogen and phos-
phorus (Pereira AKS et al., 2024), in addition to other 
macro- and micronutrients that can significantly influ-
ence the chemical attributes of the soil, such as pH, 
potential acidity (H⁺ + Al3⁺), cation exchange capac-
ity (CEC), and base saturation, thereby impacting 
fertility, nutrient availability and soil’s ionic balance 
(Lima et  al., 2023; Nascimento et  al., 2022; Yang 
et al., 2021).
Effluents from the paint industry are rarely used 
in fertigation systems because they are highly toxic. 
However, ecotoxicological studies on planarians 
(Girardia tigrina) have shown that raw effluent from 
the paint industry was not toxic to this organism at 
a contact time of 96 h (Carlos et al., 2025), whereas 
a solution containing 26.8% cattle slaughter effluent 
resulted in the death of 50% (LC50) of planarians at 
the same exposure time. It is also worth noting that 
the evaluated slaughter effluent had been previously 
treated through screening, fat separation boxes, 
and facultative lagoons (Pereira MAB et  al., 2024). 
Costa et al. (2024) evaluated the ecotoxicity of cattle 
slaughter and paint industry effluents combined (1:1) 
and reported that the LC50 occurred with a solution 
containing 60.76% of the mixture; in other words, the 
addition of paint effluent resulted in a reduction in the 
toxicity of the final mixture compared with the iso-
lated use of cattle slaughter effluent.
From this perspective, this study evaluated the 
effects of the combined application of these effluents 
on forage cultivation systems, as the paint indus-
try effluent is composed of macro- and micronutri-
ents that can be used for plant production, mainly 
carbonates (Carlos et  al., 2024). Furthermore, 
Goldstein-Plesser et  al. (2026) describe grasses as 
effective bioindicators of contamination, as they 
exhibit various stress responses when exposed to 
environmental stressors such as heavy metals. The 
primary site of plant-metal interaction is the root api-
cal meristem, where the metal inhibits growth and 
disrupts typical root formation (Angulo-Bejarano 
et  al., 2021). Thus, the development of grasses can 
also serve as a bioindicator of soil toxicity.
Mombaça grass (Panicum maximum cv. Mom-
baça) was used in the present study. This species 
exhibits high productivity, is sensitive to variations in 
soil fertility, and has a high nutrient extraction capac-
ity, making it an efficient bioindicator plant (Mesquita 
et al., 2022). This grass responds significantly to the 
availability of macro-and micronutrients, and is influ-
enced by conditions such as soil acidity and salinity, 
which are frequently altered by the application of 
effluents (Melo et al., 2021).
Thus, understanding how soil chemical attributes 
change as a result of the application of combined 
effluents from the paint and cattle slaughter industries 
is essential to validate reuse as a sustainable and safe 
practice. Furthermore, reports on studies involving 
the combined application of effluents are scarce, and 
there is a need for information grounded in scientific 
rigor to encourage this reuse alternative, aiming for 
contaminant dilution and nutrient complementarity. 
This study aimed to evaluate the impact of this appli-
cation on the fertility of soil cultivated with mombaça 
grass, with an emphasis on the nutrient content, acid-
ity, pH, and cation exchange capacity, as well as pos-
sible effects of the combined effluent on plant growth.

The experiment was conducted in a greenhouse at 
the Instituto Tocantinense Presidente Antonio Car -

as Red-Yellow Latosol, was stored in 5 L pots, and 
its physical and chemical characteristics are presented 
in Table  1. No soil correction was carried out, since

2.2 Effluent From the Paint and Cattle Slaughter 
Industry Used for Fertigation
Effluents were collected from regional industries, 
with the cattle slaughter effluent coming from a 
slaughterhouse with an average slaughter of 1,200 
heads of cattle per month and a small paint indus-
try that manufactures water-based paints, oil-based 
paints, and varnishes. In this paint industry, lead- and 
chromium-based inputs are not used in the production 
process, as the paints are intended for use in civil con-
struction. Only the cattle slaughter effluent underwent 
prior treatment, consisting of a grating, a fat separa-
tion box, and three optional lagoons. After collection, 
the samples were characterized using the parameters 
listed in Table  2. For elemental analysis, a 10  ml 
aliquot of the samples was separated, acidified with 
5% suprapur nitric acid, and then filtered through a 
0.45 µm membrane. After reading the multi-element 
standard curve, aliquots (acidified and filtered) were 
analyzed using Plasma Atomic Emission (ICP-AES) 
equipment, with a limit of quantitation (LOQ) of 
0.01 mg/L.
2.3 Preparation of Mombaça grass samples
2.3.1 Grass Cultivation
Mombaça grass was selected as the primary plant for 
this study because of its greater need for nutrients, 
demand for deep soil, good drainage systems, and 
adaptation to the local climate, which is character -
ized by high temperatures and > 1,000 mm of rainfall 
annually. Mombaça grass was planted with approxi-
mately 30 seeds per pot. After 18 d of sowing, thin-
ning was performed, leaving 12 plants in each pot. 
The pots were organized in rows with a spacing of 
1  m between them. The distance between the pots 
within each row was 0.5 m.
The experiment was implemented in a completely 
randomized experimental design in a 5 × 2 double 
factorial scheme, consisting of different collection 
times (T1 = 0 months, T2 = 3 months, T3 = 6 months, 
T4 = 9 months, and T5 = 12 months) with and without 
the application of the combined effluent (1:1), with 
five replicates.
The crop water requirements in all pots were cal-
culated using the daily reference evapotranspira-
tion. The crop water demand in all plots was calcu-
lated using daily reference evapotranspiration (ET0) 
and the Penman–Monteith method: ET0 × Kc = ETc 
(Allen, 2006). Crop evapotranspiration (ETc) was cal-
culated using the daily values of the crop coefficient 
(Kc). Accumulated water depths were applied daily to 
replenish ETc.
2.3.2 Fertigation
Once the combined effluent water depths were estab-
lished for application to the experimental plots, 60 kg/
ha of nitrogen was used as the reference concentration 
to calculate these depths. Water depth and fertigation 
were determined by weighing the pots, and the differ-
ence in weights corresponded to the volume applied 
to increase the field capacity. The combined effluent 
was applied using manual watering to promote con-
tact with grass leaves, simulating application through 
sprinkler systems. The first dose of the combined 
effluent was applied 20 d after planting the grass, and 
subsequent applications were performed once a week.

The data on soil chemical attributes obtained in

3 Results and Discussion
Regarding the effluent characteristics, it is observed 
that the electrical conductivity (EC) and sodium 
adsorption ratio (SAR) meet the irrigation require-
ments described by Ayers and Westcot (1985). How -
ever, regarding soil salinization, the infiltration rate, 
which relates both EC and SAR, should also be con-
sidered. In this case, the degree of restriction on efflu-
ent use for irrigation was classified as mild to mod-
erate. Salinity increases the infiltration rate; however, 
a high SAR value (sodium concentration relative to 
calcium and magnesium concentration) decreases the 
infiltration rate. Consequently, a low infiltration rate 
promotes the proliferation of vectors (mosquitoes), 
while a high infiltration rate allows for greater leach-
ing, which may lead to groundwater contamination 
or soil accumulation. With high salinity and high 
SAR, both problems can occur simultaneously (Ayers 
& Westcot, 1985). Based on the SAR calculation, it 
is also possible to establish that the use of effluent 
in irrigation does not promote soil toxicity due to 
sodium.
Pereira MAB et  al., (2024) conducted studies 
with bovine slaughter effluent, and according to the 
recorded concentrations of sodium, calcium, and 
magnesium, the SAR of the effluent was 4.38 and 
the EC was 914 µS/cm. Based on data from Estime 
et al., (2026) and Freitas et al., (2026), the SAR val-
ues obtained for bovine slaughter effluent were 7.2 
and 11.6, and EC values were 1503 and 1884 µS/cm. 
Consequently, the combination of cattle slaughter 
effluent with paint industry effluent (1:1) resulted in 
a RAS value < 3.0 and EC < 700 µS/cm, that is, below 
the maximum limit recommended for irrigation water 
(Ayers & Westcost, 1985).
In addition, the combination of effluents promoted 
the reduction of E. coli, meeting the value recom-
mended by ISO 16075–2/2020, but was not sufficient 
to reach the level required by ANZG (2024). In the 
study by Estime et  al., (2026) the presence of this 
microorganism in cattle slaughter effluent ranged 
from 2.10 4–3.105  CFU/100  mL, therefore, taking 
these values as a reference, the combination of efflu-
ents represented a decay of 1.3 to 2.5 logs.
Quantification of BOD demonstrated that even 
after combining the effluents, the biodegradable 
organic matter content was high, according to the 
limits recommended by ISO 16075–2/2020; however, 
a reduction in the organic load was observed after 
mixing. Freitas et al. (2026) recorded BOD values of 
142 mg/L for cattle slaughter effluent; consequently, 
the combination with paint effluent promoted a dilu-
tion of the organic load to 74 mg/L. The TSS (Total 
suspended solids) parameter also exceeded the rec-
ommended value (< 50 mg/L) for use in drip irriga-
tion systems (Ayers & Westcot, 1985). Carlos et  al. 
(2025) obtained a TSS value of 73  mg/L for paint 
industry effluent, while Estime et al. (2026) reported 
values between 57.5 and 123 mg/L for cattle slaugh-
ter effluent. BOD and TSS parameters can contrib-
ute to the clogging of irrigation systems through the 
formation of biofilms and deposition of particles. In 
addition, the transport of this effluent by surface run-
off or percolation into the soil can pose risks to water 
bodies.
The oligonutrients evaluated in the physicochemi-
cal characterization did not exceed the values estab-
lished by ISO 16025–2/2020 and ANZG (2024) for 
irrigation water.

(p < 0.01) of the combined application of effluents 
from the paint industry and cattle slaughter via fer -
tigation on forage productivity. The differences

The data presented in Table  3 show that an 
increase exists in the productivity of Mombaça grass 
(Panicum maximum cv. Mombaça) in the treatments

industry and cattle slaughter combined for 12 months 
compared to the control treatment (without effluent 
application). The average productivity of the ferti-
gated treatments was 18.28 t/ha of dry matter, signifi-
cantly outperforming the control group. Martins et al. 
(2025), using only cattle slaughter effluent, reached 
15.20 t/ha. This productive performance reinforces 
the potential of the effluent as a source of essential 
nutrients for forage growth, particularly nitrogen (N), 
phosphorus (P), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), and 
magnesium (Mg), which are continuously extracted 
by plants throughout cutting cycles.
Oliveira et al. (2019) reported that the application 
of slaughterhouse effluent rich in organic matter and 
nitrogen compounds significantly favors the accu-
mulation of biomass in tropical grasses, especially 
in cultivars with high nutritional requirements, such 
as Mombaça grass. Similarly, Souza et  al. (2020) 
observed that fertigation with effluents of animal 
origin and effluents rich in carbonates increased the 
dry mass production as well as the nutrient concen-
trations in plant tissues, evidencing greater efficiency 
in the absorption and use of the elements available in 
the soil.
The dynamics of nutrient extraction by mombaça 
grass over 12  months were directly associated with 
the vigorous growth and high regrowth rate charac-
teristics of the species. In environments with a reg-
ular nutrient supply, such as fertigation treatments 
with effluent, plants have a greater capacity to absorb 
and redistribute essential elements, especially N and 
K, which are in high demand for protein synthesis, 
chlorophyll formation, and osmotic regulation (San-
tos et al., 2021). In addition, a continuous supply of 
phosphorus contributes to the development of the 
root system, increasing the efficiency of water and 
nutrient capture, particularly in soil columns with a 
good physical structure.
In experiments conducted in Colombia and at the 
international level, Ramírez et al. (2020) reported that 
the regular application of effluents from cattle slaugh-
terhouses in forage production systems can increase 
productivity by up to 120% compared with areas 
managed only with mineral fertilizers, provided that 
there is control over the organic load and soil salinity 
levels.
3.2 Nutrient Extraction By Mombaça Grass

potassium (K), sodium (Na), calcium (Ca), and mag-
nesium (Mg) were calculated based on the produc-
tivity and concentration of nutrients in the plant dry 
matter (Table 4).
In plants irrigated only with water (control), a gen-

was observed (Table 3), which resulted in the absence 
of statistically significant differences in the removal 
of N, P, Ca, and Mg between collection months 
(Table 4). This behavior may be associated with the 
partial depletion of nutrients available in the soil, or 
with the physiological adaptation of the plant in the 
advanced stages of the cultivation cycle, which tends 
to prioritize the accumulation of structural biomass to 
the detriment of the intensive absorption of macronu-
trients. Studies by Oliveira et al. (2019) and Ramírez 
et al. (2020) also identified stabilization or a decrease

The greater accumulation of N, Ca, and Mg over 
12 months can be attributed to the continuous avail-
ability of these elements in the soil promoted by fer -
tigation with effluent, which contained high levels 
of nitrogen compounds, carbonates, and minerals 
from organic waste, and metabolites from animal and 
industrial processing. Studies by Oliveira et al. (2019) 
and Souza et al. (2020) indicate that cattle slaughter -
house effluent is a rich and efficient source of essen-
tial nutrients, favoring the growth of the aerial part 
and the efficiency in the absorption of divalent cati-
ons such as Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺, essential for the stability 
of cell membranes, enzymatic activity, and cell divi-
sion in tropical grasses. Furthermore, the character -
istics of the combined effluent indicated a slight to 
moderate restriction regarding changes in the infiltra-
tion rate (Ayers & Westcot, 1985), which may have 
contributed to the accumulation of these nutrients in 
the soil.
The marked nitrogen extraction, especially in the 
initial treatments (up to 3 months), is directly related 
to the role of this nutrient in the formation of struc-
tural proteins and in stimulating sprouting and tiller -
ing, which are characteristics that define the initial 
vigor of Mombaça grass. The greater accumulation of 
Ca and Mg throughout the 12-month cycle was asso-
ciated with the continuous need for these elements to 
maintain cellular integrity, nutritional balance, and 
pH regulation in the root apoplast, as reported by 
Santos et  al. (2021) in long-term experiments with 
grasses under different fertigation regimes.
In a study conducted by Ramírez et  al. (2020), 
similar behavior was observed in the response of 
Panicum maximum subjected to the continuous appli-
cation of cattle slaughterhouse effluent, with a sig-
nificant increase in N, Ca, and Mg concentrations in 
plant tissues compared to conventional mineral ferti-
lizers. These authors also highlighted that, in addition 
to nutritional supply, the rational use of effluent con-
tributes to improving the physicochemical properties 
of the soil, favoring the efficiency of root absorption.

The decrease in soil pH over time may be associ-
ated with the mineralization of organic matter present 
in effluents, the oxidation of nitrogenous compounds 
such as ammonia, and the release of organic acids, 
phenols, and other industrial chemical residues, espe-
cially in the case of paint industry effluents. Based 
on Huang et  al. (2023), the application of industrial 
effluents can promote soil acidification, especially 
when the material contains organic compounds with 
a high oxidizable charge, affecting the acid–base bal-
ance of the system.
From an agronomic perspective, the reduction in 
base saturation is concerning, as it implies a decrease 
in the availability of essential cations (Ca2⁺, Mg2⁺, K⁺, 
and Na⁺) in the cation exchange capacity (CEC) of 
the soil. This can compromise fertility and negatively 
affect crop development. Silva et  al. (2022a, b) also 
reported a V% reduction in soils treated with organic 
effluents of animal origin, revealing that base leach-
ing and replacement by H⁺ and Al3⁺ ions may occur 
with the intensification of applications in continuous 
systems.
This behavior may be related to the high nutri-
tional requirements of forage, especially in inten-
sive production systems. Mombaça grass is known 
for its high production potential, accumulating, per 
cycle, approximately 300 to 400 kg/ha of nitrogen, 
40 to 50  kg/ha of phosphorus, and up to 450  kg/
ha of potassium, in addition to significant amounts 
of calcium and magnesium, estimated at 250 and 
90  kg/ha, respectively, depending on management 
and soil and climate conditions (Euclides et  al., 
2021; Werner et al., 2022).
Given this high nutrient demand, the contribu-
tion of effluent application, especially in the first 
years of cultivation, may not be sufficient to sig-
nificantly alter soil nutrient availability, particularly 
when the application rate is moderate. However, 
some elements tend to gradually accumulate with 
continued use, which can generate more significant 
effects in the medium-to long-term, especially when 
combined with appropriate fertilizer management 
and efficient nutrient cycling.
However, some authors have reported that the 
pH and base saturation values remain unchanged 
or even increase in soils treated with agro-industrial 
effluents, especially when the residues have high 
concentrations of carbonates or metal oxides. Rod-
rigues et al. (2021) observed an increase in the pH 
of sandy soils treated with meat-packed plant efflu-
ent, owing to the presence of calcium residues and 
the alkalinity of the effluent. This divergence high-
lights the importance of chemically characterizing 
effluents before their application and monitoring 
their long-term effects.
The acidifying behavior observed in this study 
reveals the need for corrective management meas-
ures, such as the periodic application of agricultural 
amendments (limestone) to neutralize the generated 
acidity and adjustments to the frequency and dose of

Figure 2 reports the average values for the evalua-
tion of phosphorus (P), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), 
and magnesium (Mg) contents over 1  year of culti-
vation with Mombaça grass (Panicum maximum cv. 
Mombaça) under different management conditions, 
highlighting the marked differences between the 
treatments with and without the application of com-
bined effluents.
Figures  2A–D show that while the control group 
maintained relatively constant values with small 
decreasing fluctuations over the months of evaluation, 
treatments with the combined application of effluents 
from the paint industry and cattle slaughter showed 
significant increases in the levels of these nutrients, 
with subsequent stabilization over time.
Soil available phosphorus levels increased con-
siderably in the effluent treatments in the first few 
months and remained stable in subsequent months 
(Fig. 2A). In contrast, in the control group, phospho-
rus levels decreased slightly, reflecting the effects of 
crop extraction and the lack of replenishment. These 
results reveal that effluent application is effective in 
providing bioavailable phosphorus to the soil, which 
is particularly relevant given that this nutrient has 
low mobility and high adsorption in tropical soils, 
especially in acidic soils, where it is more readily 
absorbed by iron and aluminum oxides (Moreira; 
Siqueira, 2021).
The stability of phosphorus levels over time in 
soils with effluent may also be related to the gradual 
mineralization of organic phosphorus present in cattle 
effluent, which, based on Lima et al. (2023), consti-
tutes an important source of slow- and continuous-
release phosphorus, helping meet plant demands even 
under intense vegetative growth. Notably, Mombaça 
grass has a high rate of phosphorus accumulation, 
especially in the initial stages of regrowth, which 
reinforces the importance of the continuous replen-
ishment of this nutrient.
Potassium, an essential nutrient for osmotic regu-
lation, cell growth, and enzyme activation, exhibited 
a similar behavior to phosphorus: stability in the 
control group, with a slight downward trend and a 
substantial increase, followed by stabilization in the

highly soluble and mobile in soil, its dynamics are 
closely related to leaching and plant uptake.
According to Silva et al. (2022a, b), slaughterhouse 
and meat-processing plant effluents generally contain 
high levels of soluble K+, making it readily available 
to plants. This effect was confirmed by the analyzed 
data, indicating that the effluent fertigation system 
contributed to an adequate supply of this macronutri-
ent. However, the maintenance of these levels over 
time also indicates that the applied doses were com-
patible with crop demand and that the soil retention 
system was effective in preventing leaching losses.
Figures  2C and D show gradual increases in soil 
calcium and magnesium levels after effluent appli-
cation, whereas the control group showed a slight 
reduction in these cations over time. Both nutrients 
play crucial roles in the cellular structure, membrane 
integrity, and enzyme function. Mombaça grass has 
a significant capacity for extracting Ca and Mg, as 
already pointed out by Euclides et  al. (2021), espe-
cially in intensive management systems and under 
high growth rates.
The origin of base cations in the treated soil can 
be explained by the nature of the applied effluents. 
Based on Zhao et  al. (2021), combined industrial 
effluents, particularly those containing organic waste 
and neutralizing agents, often have a high load of 
exchangeable bases, which explains the increase in 
these elements. Furthermore, the balance between 
 Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ is crucial to avoid antagonisms in root 
uptake, which appears to have been maintained in the 
treatments, given the similar behavior between the 
two nutrients.
Figures  3A–C show the evolution of potential 
cation exchange capacity (CECp), effective cation 
exchange capacity (CECe), and potential acidity 
(H⁺ + Al3⁺) over 1 year of cultivation with Mombaça 
grass.
A significant linear increase in the CECp and 
potential acidity was observed throughout the grow -
ing season. Although the CECe also showed an 
increasing trend, it did not show a statistically signifi-
cant difference between the evaluated periods.
The increase in CECp over time can be attributed 
primarily to the increase in organic matter content 
and the input of basic cations from the applied efflu-
ents. Organic matter, especially in the form of organic 
waste of bovine origin, has a large number of active 
sites with cation adsorption and exchange capacities,

et  al., 2023). Furthermore, the input of complex 
organic compounds present in industrial effluents, 
such as polyphosphates and synthetic humic acids, 
from the treatment of metal surfaces in the paint 
industry can promote improvements in soil colloidal 
activity, as discussed by Zhang et al. (2022), resulting 
in a higher CECp.
Despite increasing over time, the CECe did not 
show statistically significant variations. This can 
be explained by the dynamic equilibrium between 
exchangeable cations in the solid phase and ions in 
solution, particularly considering the nutrient extrac-
tion regime imposed by the Mombaça grass crop. As 
previously discussed, the high nutritional require-
ments of this forage grass imply a high absorption 
rate of K⁺, Ca2⁺, and Mg2⁺, which may mitigate the 
positive effects of cation addition via effluent on the 
CECe (Euclides et al., 2021). The maintenance of sta-
ble CECe levels despite high absorption indicates a 
good balance between nutritional supply and demand 
in the system.
Potential acidity (H⁺ + Al3⁺) also showed a linear 
increase over time in the effluent treatments, indicat-
ing that, although there is a significant base input, 
there is also a simultaneous accumulation of protons 
and exchangeable aluminum in the sorption complex. 
This may be related to the mineralization of organic 
compounds present in the effluents, especially those 
from the paint industry, which can release short-chain 
organic acids during decomposition (Zhou et  al., 
2021). Furthermore, residues of dyes, pigments, and 
inorganic fillers that have not been fully degraded can 
interfere with acid–base balance of the soil, promot-
ing, in some cases, an increase in latent acidity.
This behavior is consistent with the observed trend 
of a slight reduction in pH and base saturation in the 
final months of the experiment, revealing a possible 
slow and cumulative acidification of the soil, possibly 
because of the oxidation of organic compounds and 
the release of H⁺ ions during the nitrification and deg-
radation processes of organic matter. Based on Lima 
et al. (2023), the prolonged use of organic and indus-
trial effluents can induce contrasting effects. Although 
promoting nutrient supply and increasing CEC, it can 
also acidify the soil in the long term, requiring cor -
rective management.
In the agronomic context, an increase in CECp is a 
positive indicator of improved soil nutrient retention 
capacity, which benefits intensive systems, such as 
forage production. However, the increase in poten-
tial acidity associated with a stable CECe indicates 
the need for constant monitoring and the possible 
application of acidity correctives (such as dolomitic 
limestone) to prevent aluminum saturation and reduce 
nutrient availability, especially phosphorus, whose 
adsorption intensifies in acidic environments.
4 Conclusion
Fertigation with combined effluent significantly 
increased the productivity of Mombaça grass, 
reaching an average of 18.28 t/ha of dry matter, 
indicating that the combination of the effluent did 
not result in phytotoxicity to the plant. Greater 
extraction of nitrogen, calcium, and magnesium was 
observed over 12 months, reflecting the nutritional 
efficiency of the treatments.
The agricultural reuse of effluents from the paint 
industry and cattle slaughter promoted an increase 
and stabilization of phosphorus, K, Ca, and Mg 
levels in the soil, even with intense extraction by 
Mombaça grass.
There was an increase in CECp, indicating an 
improvement in the nutrient retention capacity. 
However, an increase in potential acidity and slight 
reductions in pH and base saturation were also 
observed over time.
These results reveal the potential of effluent reuse 
as a sustainable alternative but reinforce the need for 
continuous monitoring and managing soil acidity.
The nutritional complementarity between efflu-
ents from cattle slaughter and the paint industry 
explains the observed agronomic efficiency. These 
results show the potential of this mixture as a sus-
tainable source of nutrients for fertigation.
Future studies should evaluate the long-term 
effects on different soils and crops, in addition to 
investigating the behavior of potential contami-
nants, to ensure agronomic and environmental 
safety, primarily related to the accumulation of 
toxic metals in the soil and the transport of contam-
inants to water bodies.
