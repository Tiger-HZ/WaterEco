Abstract: This study assessed the distribution of trace metals, rare earth elements (REEs), thorium (Th), and uranium (U) 
in sediments and surface waters of the Tshikapa and Kasai Rivers in Tshikapa, Democratic Republic of the Congo, and 
evaluated the associated contamination and potential ecological risks. Sediment physicochemical properties, grain -size 
distribution, trace elements, and REEs were determined using analytical techniques. Sediment pollution and contamination 
were assessed using the geoaccumulation index (Igeo), enrichment factor (EF), contamination factor (CF), degree of 
contamination (CD), ecological risk factor (Eri), and potential ecological risk index (R I), with reference to international 
sediment quality guidelines. Sediment metal concentrations generally followed the order RT > RKAV > RKAM. Cr, Cu, Zn, 
Cd, Pb, and Hg exceeded the corresponding Canadian Council of Ministers of the Environment (CCME) Interim Sediment 
Quality Guidelines at the investigated stations, while several concentrations also exceeded the probable effect levels, 
particularly at RT. In surface waters, Cd reached 4.2 µg L −1 at station RTL, exceeding the World Health Organization 
guideline value for drinking water. Total REE concentrations in sediments ranged from 111.1 to 298.4 mg kg −1, with the 
highest values at RT, whereas dissolved REEs, Th, and U generally remained low in surface waters. Sediment Th and U 
concentrations ranged from 8.3–19.0 and 0.7 –5.5 mg kg−1, respectively. The degree of contamination exceeded 32 at all 
stations, while RI values ranged from 630.4 to 2335.3, indicating high to very high potential ecological risk, with the highest 
risk at RT and RKAV. Correlation patt erns suggested that sediment organic matter and water content may influence the 
retention and distribution of several metals. The findings indicate significant metal contamination of river sediments and 
potential ecological concern, likely resulting from a combination of geogenic and anthropogenic inputs. Continued 
monitoring, source-apportionment studies, and appropriate measures to reduce contaminant inputs are recommended. 
 
Keywords: Tshikapa River; Kasai River; Trace Metals; Sediment Contamination; Ecological Risk.

I. INTRODUCTION 
 
The degradation of aquatic ecosystems by inorganic 
micropollutants, particularly trace metal elements (TMEs), 
constitutes a major global environmental concern. This 
concern is exacerbated by their intrinsic toxicity, non -
biodegradable nature, and propensity to bioaccumulate within 
food webs (Norvivor et al., 2024; Hao et al., 2022). Although 
natural mineral weathering processes contribute to the 
geochemical background levels (Yan et al., 2022; Sigue et al., 
2025), anthropogenic inputs—including untreated industrial 
and mining effluents, hospital discharges, urban wastewater, 
and agricultural runoff—represent the predominant pathways 
of contamination (Hoang et al., 2021; Paithankar et al., 2021). 
In these riverine systems, sediments play a critical bimodal 
role: they initially act as sequestration sinks before potentially 
becoming secondary sources, thereby regulating the release 
and bioavailability of pollutants to benthic biota ( Akonga et 
al., 2025; Cui et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2025; Jha et al., 2024). 
 
Despite the existence of environmental legislative 
frameworks in developing countries, the enforcement of 
standards governing effluent discharges remains hindered by 
structural deficiencies in technical expertise and analytical 
monitoring infrastructure. This operational gap exposes local 
populations to chronic health risks associated with the 
ingestion of heavy metals (HMs) through the food chain (Yan 
et al., 2022; Sharma et al., 2024; Tarigan et al., 2025). 
 
Although the scientific literature has documented 
various cases of contamination in Africa and the Democratic 
Republic of the Congo (DRC) ( Kipelo et al., 2025 ), data on 
the Kasaï River, a major tributary of the Congo River, remain 
fragmented, with the notable exception of the recent study by 
Manza et al. (2026). Nevertheless, the Kasaï River is of vital 
socioeconomic and ecological importance, supporting 
artisanal fishing, subsistence agriculture, and river 
transportation in southwestern DRC (Lesani et al., 2024). 
 
The vulnerability of this ecosyst em was tragically 
highlighted by the environmental crisis of July 2021. Massive 
mining discharges from Angola caused large -scale 
transboundary contamination, resulting in severe fish 
mortality and affecting nearly two million people. This 
pollution plume t raveled several hundred kilometers 
downstream to Kwilu Province, directly threatening the food 
security of the 150,000 inhabitants of Bandundu City and the 
7 million residents of the province, whose main source of 
animal protein depends on local fishery re sources 
(CRREBaC, 2021). 
 
In this context, the present study aims to (i) quantify the 
concentrations of trace elements (Hg, Pb, Cr, As, Cd, Cu, Ni, 
and Zn) in the sediment and water compartments of the Kasaï 
and Tshikapa Rivers; and (ii) assess the degree of 
contamination by comparing these concentrations with 
international quality standards for the protection of the 
biological integrity of aquatic environments. 
 
II. MATERIALS AND METHODS 
 
 Description of the Study Area 
The present study was conducted in the city of Tshikapa 
(Kasaï Province, Democratic Republic of the Congo), located 
along the Kasaï and Tshikapa Rivers (6°25′48″ S; 20°47′12″ 
E). The choice of these two watercourses was related to the 
transboundary polluti on event that occurred in July 2021, 
following the contamination of a tributary of the Kasaï River 
originating in Angola (CRREBaC, 2021) (Fig. 1). This event 
raised major environmental and public health concerns due to 
the potential risks to aquatic ecosys tems and riparian 
populations that depend on these resources for domestic, 
fishing, and commercial activities.

 Sampling Strategy and Sample Collection 
Sampling campaigns were conducted between March 
and April 2025. Sampling involved surface sediments and 
river water collected from three stations distributed along the 
Tshikapa and Kasaï Rivers, in the Kele, Sapide, and Abattoir 
neighborhoods. One station was located on the Tshikapa 
River and two on the Kasaï River. 
The sampling stations were coded as follows: RTs for 
the station lo cated on the Tshikapa River, and RKAVs and 
RKAMs for the stations located on the Kasaï River ( Fig. 2). 
Composite sediment samples were coded RT, RKA, and 
RKAM, respectively, whereas water samples were designated 
RTL, RKAVL, RKAML, and CTK.

 Sediment Sampling 
Surface sediment samples were collected manually at a 
depth ranging from 0 to 8 cm, approximately 5 m from the 
riverbank and at a water depth of less than 2 m. Each 
individual subsample consisted of approximately 50 g of 
sediment. 
 
To ensure adequate spati al representativeness of the 
investigated stations, five subsamples were collected at each 
site using a centered-square sampling design, with an average 
spacing of approximately 10 m between sampling points. The 
subsamples were subsequently homogenized to obtain one 
representative composite sample for each station. 
 
 Water Sampling 
Water sampling, preservation, and subsequent analyses 
were performed in accordance with the standard methods of 
the American Public Health Association ( APHA, 2022). At 
each station, water samples were collected in 500 -mL glass 
bottles that had previously been washed with detergent, 
soaked in a 10% nitric acid solution for 24 h, rinsed with 
double-distilled water, and dried at a temperature ranging 
from 90 to 95 °C (Boadi et al., 2020 ; APHA, 2022). 
 
Prior to sampling, the bottles were rinsed with water 
from the corresponding site to minimize the risk of cross -
contamination. Surface water samples were collected from 
the pelagic zone of each station, stored in a cooler containing 
ice, and immediately transported to the laboratory, where they 
were stored at 4 °C until analysis. 
 
Samples intended for dissolved metal analysis were 
filtered in situ through a 0.45 -µm membrane, whereas 
samples intended for the determination of total recovera ble 
metals were not filtered. All samples were subsequently 
acidified with nitric acid (HNO₃) to achieve a pH below 2, in 
accordance with the recommended analytical protocols.

 Physicochemical Characteristics of Sediments 
After sediment homogenization, representative aliquots 
were isolated for physical and chemical analyses. Particle -
size distribution, including clay, silt, and sand fractions, was 
determined by laser granulometry using a Coulter® LS -100 
diffractometer (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA), 
following the protocol established by Mabidi et al. (2024) . 
According to the nomenclature of Blott and Pye (2001), the 
textural classification of the samples was based on mean 
particle diameter: particles smaller than 2 µm were classified 
as clay, whereas the silt fraction ranged from 2 to 63 µm. 
Particles with diameters ranging from 63 µm to 2 mm were 
classified as sand. 
 
Water content (WC) was quantified gravimetrically by 
measuring the mass loss of aliquots of fresh sediment after 
drying at 105 °C for approximately 24 h, until constant mass 
was reached. In parallel, the organic matter (OM) fraction 
was determined using the loss -on-ignition (LOI) method. 
Aliquots of previously dehydrated sediment were subjected 
to combustion in a muffle furnace (Salvis AG, Luzern, 
Switzerland) at 550 °C for 1 h (Poté et al., 2008; Deko et al., 
2026). This approach provides an estimate of the total organic 
matter content through the thermal oxidation of carbonaceous 
compounds. 
 
 Pretreatment of Sediment and Water Samples 
Sediment samples were freeze -dried, ground, 
homogenized, and then sieved through a 63 -µm mesh. Acid 
digestion was performed according to the protocols described 
by Thevenon et al. (2013). 
 
Approximately 10 –15 mg of sediment powder was 
completely digested i n Teflon vessels placed on a glass -
ceramic hotplate using ultrapure acids. The procedure 
consisted of three successive steps: (i) addition of 1 mL of 
HNO₃ (Suprapur®, 65%); (ii) addition of a mixture consisting 
of 0.5 mL of HClO₄ (Suprapur®, 70%) and 0.5 m L of HF 
(Suprapur®, 40%); and (iii) final treatment with 0.5 mL of 
HNO₃ (Suprapur®, 65%). 
 
Between each step, the solvents were evaporated to near 
dryness. The resulting residues were then redissolved in 10 
mL of a 1% HNO₃ solution prior to chemical analys is. 
Analyses were performed within 24 h after dilution. 
 
For water samples, 10 -mL aliquots of acidified water 
were filtered using 0.45 -µm membranes (Millex® -LCR, 
Millipore). Appropriate dilutions were subsequently prepared 
using a 1% HNO₃ solution prior to instrumental analysis. 
 
 Analysis of Trace Metals and Rare Earth Elements in 
Sediments and Water 
The concentrations of trace metals and rare earth 
elements (REEs) in sediments and water were determined by 
inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; 
Agilent 7700x series), which is suitable for the analysis of 
complex environmental matrices. 
 
To minimize spectral interferences that could affect the 
quality of the analytical results, a helium -mode 
collision/reaction cell and interference -correction equations 
were used. Instrument calibration was performed using 
multielement standard solutions prepared at different 
concentrations (0, 0.02, 1, 5, 20, 100, and 200 µg L⁻¹), 
according to the protocols described by Poté et al. (2008) and 
Thevenon et al. (2013). 
 
Analytical quality and measurement reliability were 
verified using the certified reference materials TMDA-70 and 
LKSD4 for water and sediment analyses, respectively. 
Relative standard deviations obtained from three replicate 
measurements remain ed below 10%, whereas analytical 
blanks accounted for less than 2% of the sample signal. 
 
The concentrations measured in water and sediment 
samples are expressed in µg L⁻¹ and mg kg⁻¹ dry weight, 
respectively. 
 
 Analysis of Total Mercury in Sediments 
Total mercury (Hg) concentrations in sediments were 
determined using a direct mercury analyzer (DMA -80 III, 
MWS GmbH) operating according to the principle of cold 
vapor atomic absorption spectrometry (CV-AAS). 
 
The analytical procedure involved sample combustion, 
amalgamation of mercury onto a gold trap, and subsequent 
quantification of gaseous mercury by atomic absorption. The 
method recovery rate was greater than 99%, with a relative 
uncertainty of approximately 4%. The detection limit, 
calculated as the mean b lank value plus three standard 
deviations, was 0.003 µg g⁻¹. Analytical reproducibility was 
high, with variations among replicates remaining below 5%. 
 
Analytical quality control was ensured using the 
certified reference material SRM 2702 (National Institute of 
Standards and Technology, USA), which was analyzed 
simultaneously with the samples according to the Feststoffe 
Schnell protocol (without a drying step; combustion 
temperature set at 750 °C). The results showed an Hg 
recovery rate of 99% relative to the certified value. Total 
mercury concentrations are expressed as mg kg⁻¹ dry weight 
of sediment. 
 
 Statistical Analyses 
All analyses were performed in t riplicate. Statistical 
data processing, including Spearman correlation analyses, 
was performed using XLSTAT software (Addinsoft, 2020, 
New York, USA). 
 
 Assessment of the Nature and Degree of Sediment 
Pollution 
The nature and degree of sediment contamination were 
assessed using the enrichment factor (EF) and 
geoaccumulation index (Igeo), following the approach 
described by Maanan et al. (2004). 
 
 Enrichment Factor (EF) 
The enrichment factor was calculated to discr iminate 
the anthropogenic origin of trace metals using the following 
equation: EF = (Metal/Sc)Sample / (Metal/Sc)Background.

Where “Metal” represents the concentration of the 
metal of interest in the sample or in the geochemical 
background, and “Sc” repre sents the concentration of 
scandium used as the normalizing element. Upper 
Continental Crust (UCC) values proposed by McLennan 
(2001) were used as geochemical background values. 
 
 Geoaccumulation Index (IGEO) 
The degree of sediment pollution was assessed using the 
geoaccumulation index according to the following equation: 
Igeo = Log₂ [Cn / 1.5(Bn)] 
 
Where “Cn” corresponds to the concentration of metal 
n in the sediment and “Bn” to its geochemical background 
concentration. The factor 1.5 was introduced to account for 
natural variations in the lithogenic background (Akonga et al., 
2026). 
 
 Ecological Risk Assessment 
The ecological risks associated with trace metals in 
sediments were assessed using four indicators: the 
contamination factor (CF), degree of contamination (CD), 
individual ecological risk index (Eri), and potential ecological 
risk index (RI) (Deko et al. 2026). 
 
 Contamination Factor (CF) 
The contamination factor was calculated using the 
following equation: 
 
CF = Cn / Bn 
 
Where “Cn” represents the concentration of the metal in 
the sample and “Bn” its concentration in the geochemical 
background (Rubio et al., 2000; Förstner et al., 1989). 
 
 Degree of Contamination (CD) 
The degree of polymetallic contamination was 
determined from the sum of the contamination factors: 
 
CD = ΣCFi 
 
Where “CFi” represents the contamination factor of 
metal i. Eight trace metals were considered in the calculation 
of CD. 
 
 Individual Ecological Risk Index (Eri) 
The individual ecological risk index was calculated 
using the following equation: 
 
Eri = Tri × CFi 
 
Where “Tri” represents the toxic-response factor of the 
metal under consideration and “CFi” its contamination factor. 
The Tri values used were 40, 30, 10, 5, 5, 5, 2, and 1 for Hg, 
Cd, As, Co, Cu, Pb, Cr, an d Zn, respectively ( Håkanson, 
1980; Islam et al., 2014). 
 
 Potential Ecological Risk Index (RI) 
The potential ecological risk index was determined by 
summing the individual ecological risk indices: 
 
RI = ∑Eri 
 
Where “Eri” corresponds to the ecological risk index 
associated with each metal. This index simultaneously 
considers the level of contamination, metal toxicity, and the 
ecological sensitivity of exposed organisms ( Singh et al., 
2002). 
 
Finally, the ecotoxicological significance of the metal 
concentrations measured in sediments was assessed by 
comparing them with the threshold values of the Sediment 
Quality Guidelines (SQGs) and Probable Effect Levels 
(PELs) established by the Canadian Council of Ministers of 
the Environment (CCME, 1999). These guidelines allow the 
identification of concentrations that may cause adverse 
biological effects on aquatic organisms.

Sediment concentrations were compared with the 
Canadian Council of Ministers of the Environment (CCME) 
Interim Sediment Quality Guidelines (ISQGs) and Probable 
Effect Levels (PELs) for the protection of freshwater aquatic 
life. The CCME values used in this study are expressed on a 
dry-weight basis. For example, the freshwater ISQG and PEL 
values are 37.3 and 90 mg kg⁻¹ for Cr, 35.7 and 197 mg kg⁻¹ 
for Cu, 123 and 315 mg kg⁻¹ for Zn, and 0.6 and 3.5 mg kg⁻¹ 
for Cd. 
 
Several trace metals, particularly Cr, Cu, Zn, Cd, Pb, 
and Hg, exceeded their corresponding CCME ISQG values in 
the sediment samples. Cr concentrations ranged from 46.8 mg 
kg⁻¹ at RKAM to 187.9 mg kg⁻¹ at RT, exceeding the 
freshwater ISQG of 37.3 mg kg⁻¹. Similar exceedances were 
observed for Cu (164.6 –322.4 mg kg⁻¹; ISQG = 35.7 mg 
kg⁻¹), Zn (235.3 –473.1 mg kg⁻¹; ISQG = 123 mg kg⁻¹), Cd 
(1.1–2.2 mg kg⁻¹; ISQG = 0.6 mg kg⁻¹), Pb (61.0 –129.1 mg 
kg⁻¹; ISQG = 35 mg kg⁻¹), and Hg (0.3–2.1 mg kg⁻¹; ISQG = 
0.17 mg kg⁻¹). Fe concentrations were also relatively high 
(24,894.8–52,480.5 mg kg⁻¹) , consistent with the ferrallitic 
and ferruginous geological context of the Kasai Basin. 
 
Some concentrations also exceeded the corresponding 
PELs, indicating a greater likelihood of adverse biological 
effects according to the CCME framework. The highest 
values were recorded at RT for Cr (187.9 mg kg⁻¹), Cu (322.4 
mg kg⁻¹), Zn (473.1 mg kg⁻¹), and Pb (129.1 mg kg⁻¹), while 
Hg exceeded the PEL at RKAV (1.4 mg kg⁻¹) and RT (2.1 mg 
kg⁻¹). These exceedances indicate that benthic organisms may 
be exposed to pote ntially adverse levels of several 
contaminants, particularly at RT.

Sedimentary metal concentrations followed the 
decreasing order RT > RKAV > RKAM, suggesting a greater 
degree of metal accumulation at RT. This spatial pattern may 
reflect the combined influence of natural geochemical 
characteristics and anthropogenic inputs. Sediments act as 
long-term repositories for contaminants and can therefore 
provide an integrated indication of metal accumulation over 
time (Thevenon et al., 2011). The observed spatial variability 
is also consistent with differences in sediment 
physicochemical properties, particularly organic matter 
content and particle -size distribution. Organic matter can 
enhance metal retention through adsorption and 
complexation involving humic substances ( Jokinen et al., 
2020; Yan et al., 2022), while fine particles provide abundant 
reactive surfaces for contaminant sorption. Similar 
mechanisms have been reported in other aquatic systems, 
where organic matter and mineral phases contribute to metal 
stabilization through adsorption, complexation, and 
precipitation processes (Bastianini et al., 2024). 
 
The enrichment observed at RT, particularly for Cr, Cu, 
Zn, Pb, and Hg, may therefore reflect a combination of 
sediment geochemical properties and lo cal anthropogenic 
inputs. However, the available data do not allow a definitive 
separation of geogenic and anthropogenic sources. The 
relatively low concentrations of some elements at individual 
stations may reflect differences in lithology, hydrodynamics, 
particle sorting, and contaminant inputs. Additional source -
apportionment approaches would be required to identify the 
relative contribution of individual anthropogenic sources. 
 
In surface waters, trace -metal concentrations showed 
marked spatial variability, following the order CTK > RTL > 
RKAVL > RKAML (Table 2). 
 
Fe was the dominant element, with concentrations 
ranging from 32.2 µg L⁻¹ at RKAML to 257.2 µg L⁻¹ at CTK. 
This predominance is consistent with the ferrallitic and 
ferruginous geological contex t of the Kasai Basin. Cu 
concentrations ranged from 20.8 to 50.3 µg L⁻¹, Zn from 13.4 
to 37.4 µg L⁻¹, and Ba from 14.5 to 27.0 µg L⁻¹. 
 
Cr (0.1–0.5 µg L⁻¹), Ni (0.3 –1.0 µg L⁻¹), and Pb (3.7 –
6.6 µg L⁻¹) occurred at relatively low concentrations. In 
contrast, Cd represented the most concerning element, with 
concentrations ranging from 1.2 µg L⁻¹ at RKAML to 4.2 µg 
L⁻¹ at RTL. The Cd concentration at RTL exceeded the WHO 
guideline value reported for drinking water, indicating that 
untreated river water from th is location may not be suitable 
for direct human consumption. The relatively high 
concentrations of Fe, Cu, Cd, and Pb at RTL may reflect local 
inputs associated with domestic, artisanal, or mining 
activities and/or remobilization from contaminated 
sediments. 
 
Comparable trends have been reported in other tropical 
river systems. Norvivor et al. (2024), for example, reported a 
predominance of Fe among dissolved metals in the Nubui 
River. Their Pb concentrations ranged from 0.001 to 0.022 
mg L⁻¹, Cd from 0.020 to 0.035 mg L⁻¹, and Zn from 0.042 to 
0.067 mg L⁻¹. The Pb concentrations measured in the 
Tshikapa and Kasai Rivers (0.0037 –0.0066 mg L⁻¹) were 
lower than the upper values reported for the Nubui River, 
while Zn concentrations (0.0134 –0.0374 mg L⁻¹) were also 
generally lower. Cd at RTL, however, reached a 
comparatively concerning level, emphasizing the importance 
of monitoring this element in anthropogenically influenced 
tropical river systems. 
 
Overall, the predominance of Fe in the dissolved phase 
appears compatible with the regional geological and 
pedological setting, whereas the localized enrichment of Cd, 
Pb, and Zn may indicate additional anthropogenic 
contributions. Nevertheless, these interpretations should be 
considered indicative rather than defin itive because the 
available dataset does not allow rigorous source 
apportionment.

upper continental crust reference reported by McLennan

The observed variability may reflect differences in 
lithological input s, mineralogical composition, sediment 
sorting, and grain size. In particular, the predominance of 
sandy, silica -rich fractions at some locations may result in 
dilution of REE concentrations relative to finer, more reactive 
sediment fractions. Such effects have been reported in other 
sedimentary systems (Sager et al., 2024).

to 19.0 mg kg⁻¹ and from 0.7 to 5.5 mg kg⁻¹, respectively. 
These values exceeded the reported global soil averages of 
approximately 5 mg kg⁻¹ for Th and 1 mg kg⁻¹ for U (Zlobina 
et al., 2022), suggesting relative enrichment of these naturally 
occurring radionuclides. 
 
In surface waters, REEs, Th, and U occurred at very low 
concentrations, generally below 0.5 µg L⁻¹ ( Table 3 ). The 
dissolved REE pattern was dominated by LREEs, particularly 
La, Ce, and Nd, whereas most HREEs were below the 
analytical detection limits. Such a pattern is consistent with 
the strong association of REEs with dissolved organic matter, 
colloids, and particulate phases in natural surface waters. 
 
The low dissolved concentrations contrast strongly with 
the relatively high concentrations measured in sediments, 
indicating limited dissolved mobility. Th and U were also 
detected only at trace levels, generally ≤0.1 µg L ⁻¹. In 
particular, dissolved U concentrations were far below the 
WHO guideline value of 30 µg L⁻¹ for drinking water. These 
results suggest that most of the REEs and naturally occurring 
radionuclides are preferentially associated with the 
particulate/sedimentary phase rather than remaining in 
solution. 
 
The contrast between sedimentary enrichment and low 
dissolved concentrations suggests strong partitioning of these 
elements between solid and aqueous phases. Adsorption onto 
Fe and Mn oxyhydroxides, associat ion with particulate 
organic matter, and incorporation into mineral phases may 
contribute to their retention in sediments. Consequently, 
suspended particles and sediment transport may constitute 
important pathways for downstream transfer of these 
elements in the river system.

The Igeo values showed substantial spatial variability. 
At RT, V, Mn, Fe, As, Se, Ag, and Th generally fell within 
the practically unpolluted to moderately polluted categories, 
whereas Cr and Co indicated moderate pollution. Zn and Pb 
indicated moderate to strong pollution, while Ni, Cu, and Cd

Igeo value corresponding to extreme enrichment.

contamination, while Zn and Pb indicated moderate pollution, 
Cu moderate to strong pollution, Cd strong pollution, Hg 
strong to extreme pollution, and Ti extreme pollution. RKAM 
displayed relatively lower Igeo values for most elements, 
although Cu and Cd remained moderately to strongly polluted 
and Ti remained extremely enriched. 
 
The EF values provided further evidence of differential 
enrichment among the elements. Several elements, including 
V, Mn, Fe, As, Se, Ag, and Th, showed little or no 
enrichment, whereas Cr, Ni, Cu, Zn, Cd, Pb, and Hg 
displayed moderate to very high enrichment at one or more 
stations. 
 
However, the extremely high EF values obtained for Ti 
require cautious interpretation. Ti is commonly associated 
with resistant mineral phases and may be strongly influenced 
by local lithology and sediment mineralogy. Therefore, the Ti 
enrichment observed in this study should not be interpreted 
exclusively as evidence of anthropogenic contamination 
without additional mineralogical or source -apportionment 
evidence. 
 
For Cu, Zn, Cd, Pb, and Hg, the combination of elevated 
concentrations, Igeo values, and EF values is consistent with 
an additional anthropogenic contribution. Nevertheless, the 
possibility of geogenic control cannot be completely 
excluded, particularly in the absence of a detailed local 
geochemical background.

The correlation matrix showed several strong positive 
associations among the analyzed variables. Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, 
Co, Ni, Cu, Th, and Hg displayed very high correlations with 
one another and with OM, whereas Zn, As, and Pb showed 
strong relationships with water content and with one another. 
Ag displayed an intermediate pattern, with relatively high 
correlations with several metals and with the sediment 
physicochemical parameters. 
 
The observed association between several metals and 
OM suggests that organic matter may contribute to metal 
retention through adsorption and complexation processes. 
Similarly, the positive relationships between water content 
and several metals may reflect the influence of sediment 
moisture, pore -water conditions, and fine -particle 
accumulation on contaminant distribution. 
 
However, these correlations must be interpreted with 
considerable caution because only three sediment samples 
were available for the correlation analysis. With such a very 
small sample size, Spearman coefficients are strongly 
constrained and may attain extreme values. Consequently, the 
observed correla tions should be regarded as exploratory 
patterns of co -variation rather than statistically robust 
evidence of common contamination sources or causal 
geochemical relationships. 
 
The strong associations among several metals may 
nevertheless indicate similar geochemical behavior or 
common controlling factors. These observations are broadly 
consistent with Manza et al. (2026) , who reported strong 
associations between organic matter and several trace metals 
in sediments from the Kasai Basin, including Cd, Pb, Hg, As, 
and Co. Organic matter can promote metal retention through 
complexation with organic ligands and adsorption onto 
organic-rich particles. 
 
Manza et al. (2026) also identified carbonate phases as 
potential secondary regulators of Cd, Zn, Pb, and Cu through 
adsorption and co -precipitation mechanisms. Although 
carbonate contents were not quantified in the present study, 
the observed relationships with OM suggest that organic 
matter may represent one of the important factors controlling 
metal retention in these sediments. 
 
The correlation analysis suggests that sediment 
physicochemical properties, particularly organic matter and 
water content, may influence the spatial distribution and 
retention of trace metals. Nevertheless, additional samples are 
required to statistically validate these relationships and to 
distinguish between common sources, shared geochemical 
controls, and coincidental correlations. 
 
IV. CONCLUSION 
 
This study showed significant spatial variability in the 
physicochemical characteristics and contamination levels of 
sediments and surface waters from the Kasai and Tshikapa 
Rivers. Sediments were mainly composed of sand and silt, 
with clay contents below 1.5% and organic matter ranging 
from 2.20 to 7.72%. Several potentially toxic metals 
exceeded the CCME sediment quality guidelines, particularly 
Cr (46.8 –187.9 mg kg⁻¹), Cu (164.6 –322.4 mg kg⁻¹), Zn 
(235.3–473.1 mg kg⁻¹), Cd (1.1–2.2 mg kg⁻¹), Pb (61.0–129.1 
mg kg⁻¹), and Hg (0.3 –2.1 mg kg⁻¹). The highest 
concentrations were generally recorded at RT, indicating 
greater metal accumulation at this station. 
 
The pollution indices confirmed substantial sediment 
contamination, particularly by Cu, Zn, Cd, Pb, and Hg. The 
potential ecological risk index (RI) reached 2335.3 at RT, 
1638.1 at RKAV, and 630.4 at RKAM, corresponding to very 
high risk at RT and RKAV and high risk at RKAM. REEs 
were also relatively enriched in sediments, with ΣREE 
concentrations ranging from 111.1 to 298.4 mg kg⁻¹ and the 
highest value at RT. In contrast, dissolved REEs, Th, and U 
were generally below 0.5 µg L⁻¹, indicating strong 
partitioning toward the sedimentary and particulate phases. In 
surface waters, Cd was of particular concern, reaching 4.2 µg 
L⁻¹ at RTL and exceeding the WHO guideline for drinking 
water. 
 
The results indicate that the sediments of the studied 
river system constitute important reservoirs of potentially 
toxic metals and REEs, with RT being the most affected 
station. The observed enrichment of Cu, Zn, Cd, Pb, and Hg 
may reflect a combination of natural geochemical 
characteristics and anthropogenic inputs, although definitive 
source attribution requires further investigation. Continued 
monitoring of sediments, water, and aquatic organisms is 
therefore recommended, together with additional sampling, 
mineralogical characterization, local background 
determination, and source -apportionment studies to better 
distinguish geogenic from anthropogenic contributions and 
assess the associated environmental and human-health risks.
