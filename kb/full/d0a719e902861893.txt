Abstract
Purpose It is vitally important to comprehensively identify and address cancer survivors’ unmet needs. The new national 
standards for cancer survivorship care may improve survivorship care by defining quality care. The purpose of this convergent 
mixed-methods study was to describe how survivorship care at one cancer center aligns with the health system processes 
outlined in the national standards.
Methods Survey participants (N = 150) were survivors purposively sampled by treatment status, cancer stage, and geographic 
residence. Survey data evaluated how well providers assessed, listened to, and managed participants’ concerns across key 
domains and were primarily analyzed descriptively. Survey data were complemented by semi-structured interview data from 
n = 17 survivors, which were analyzed using directed content analysis.
Results Greater than 70% of participants reported that their providers regularly assessed and listened to physical and emo-
tional concerns. Fewer than 30% reported regular assessment of financial and insurance concerns. Once identified, most 
concerns were adequately managed by care teams, the exception being practical concerns. Interviewees highlighted quality 
survivorship care in general with some internal and external barriers. Participants recommended increasing awareness of 
available services and improving care coordination and navigation related to survivorship care.
Conclusions Results suggest that while survivorship care may align with the national standards related to physical and 
emotional concerns, there is critical need to improve assessment and management of financial concerns.
Implications for Cancer Survivors Cancer centers should conduct comprehensive screening that includes financial and other 
practical concerns and implement processes for connecting survivors with needed services.
Keywords Cancer · Cancer survivorship · Quality improvement · Standard of care
Introduction
In 2025, 18.6 million people living in the United States (US) 
are survivors of cancer [1]. By 2040, this number may reach 
26 million [2 ]. Roughly two million cancer diagnoses are 
made in the US annually [3 , 4] with overall 5- and 10-year 
survival rates at 70% and 48%, respectively [2 ]. Conse-
quently, there is a rapidly increasing need in cancer care to 
attend to the physical, psychological, social, and functional 
concerns of survivors [5] while addressing financial barriers 
to healthcare utilization [6].
Following the 2006 Institute of Medicine report, From 
Cancer Patient to Cancer Survivor: Lost in Transition 
[7], there have been quality improvement efforts around

While important, implementation of evidence-based 
SC as part of routine cancer care faces barriers. Nation-
wide studies have found that standalone services for men-
tal health, nutrition, and/or physical activity are frequently 
available to survivors [13]. However, specialized SC clinics 
are uncommon with only 31% of CoC accredited facilities 
and 41% of NCI Community Oncology Research Program 
institutions reporting dedicated SC clinics [13, 14]. Within 
cancer centers, SC is often delivered by extant cancer treat-
ment teams as opposed to dedicated, multidisciplinary teams 
or well-established, integrated networks of specialty pro-
viders. This is due to few providers with survivorship spe-
cialization, as well as limited awareness of, or referrals to, 
survivorship programs [14]. Gaps also exist in understanding 
best practices for implementing systematic surveillance and 
care navigation for cancer’s physical and psychosocial late 
effects and common issues like financial toxicity [15].
To improve SC implementation, the 2024 NCI National 
Standards for Cancer SC define standards and actionable 
recommendations for health systems [5 ]. These standards 
outline components of health system policy, processes, and 
evaluation/assessment central to quality SC [5 ]. As these 
new standards renew a focus on SC, this study relies on 
survivor-reported experiences to 1) describe the degree to 
which SC at one NCI Designated Comprehensive Cancer 
Center aligns with standards pertinent to health system 
processes [5 ] and 2) identify barriers to and facilitators of 
accessing SC.
Methods
Study design
This convergent mixed-methods study employs standard-
ized surveys and semi-structured interviews. The setting is 
a university-based NCI Designated Comprehensive Cancer 
Center in the southeast US.
Participants and procedures
Survivors were recruited from four outpatient clinics for 
solid tumors. Inclusion criteria were: 1) at least 18 years 
old, 2) at least three months into active treatment or being 
followed post-treatment, and 3) able to understand English 
well enough to comprehend and answer eligibility and other 
study questions.
After researchers pre-screened for eligibility using the 
electronic health record (EHR), potentially eligible survi-
vors were approached in clinic, informed about the study, 
screened for eligibility, and asked to provide verbal consent 
to participate. Recruitment went clinic-by-clinic, with atten-
tion to balanced representation of survivors with respect to 
treatment phase (active vs. post-treatment), disease stage 
(early vs. late), and residence (rural vs. nonrural). Partic-
ipants were asked to complete a 30-min survey either on 
paper or online. If participants did not finish in the clinic, 
they could do so at home. Participants (N = 150) received 
a $50 gift card for survey completion, with surveys done 
between February and August 2025. Additionally, some 
participants were purposively selected based on criterion 
and invited to complete a semi-structured interview, the 
goal being to amass representative perspective with in-depth 
exploration. Select participants were invited after complet-
ing their surveys in clinic to gain a roughly even distribution 
of cancer sites, active vs. post-treatment, rural vs. nonru-
ral residence, and genders in the interview subsample and 
recruitment stopped once each perspective was adequately 
represented. Eighty participants were invited and 17 com-
pleted interviews. Interviews (mean [SD] minutes 24.2 [7.6]) 
were audio recorded using HIPAA-compliant Zoom software 
and completed between April and August 2025. Participants 
received a $50 gift card or check for interview completion. 
Recordings were transcribed verbatim and cleaned using 
Rev software. The university’s Institutional Review Board 
approved all procedures (IRB #98,262).
Measures
EHR data
Clinical information about cancer site, stage, and treatment 
was extracted from the EHR as was county of residence, 
which determined rural versus nonrural status (2023 USDA 
Rural–Urban Continuum Codes 4–9 = rural) [16].
Survey
Sociodemographic items collected data on age, race and 
ethnicity, gender, relationship status, education, house -
hold income, and employment status using questions and 
response options similar to those in US population surveys 
[17]. To assess how the national standards were addressed 
across key domains (physical, emotional, practical, social, 
financial, and insurance concerns), we followed an approach 
similar to the Patient-Centered SC Index regarding clini-
cians’ assessment of and listening to concerns [18] (see 
Journal of Cancer Survivorship 
Supplemental File 1 for the survey). For each domain, par -
ticipants were asked to report how often their cancer treat-
ment team assessed the domain using a 5-point Likert scale 
from “has asked me at every visit” to “has not asked me”. 
Like an item from the Patient-Centered SC Index that asked, 
“Did your clinicians listen carefully to concerns related 
to…” participants were asked how often their treatment team 
“listened carefully” when they discussed concerns using a 
4-point Likert scale from “not at all” to “completely,” with 
“don’t know” as another option. For any domain-specific 
concern experienced, participants were asked whether the 
treatment team “advised or helped manage” the concern 
(Yes/No) or referred them elsewhere (Yes/No). Finally, par-
ticipants reported being asked about lifestyle behaviors (e.g., 
tobacco use, Yes/No) and whether they had received related 
advice or management.
Interview
Informed by the national standards, a semi-structured inter-
view guide with a core question set and follow-up probes 
was utilized (see Supplemental File 2). Questions centered 
around participants’ experience with care delivered at the 
cancer center (e.g., “If you had concerns, what recommenda-
tions or referrals were you given?”).
Data analysis
EHR and survey data
Descriptive statistics characterized participants’ sociodemo-
graphic and clinical characteristics. The standards state survi-
vors should be “assessed” for various concerns (e.g., practi-
cal and social) and “provided with” or referred for applicable 
resources, support, and services to manage any concerns [5]. 
This study examined alignment with the standards by measur-
ing three aspects of care delivery across domains/concerns. 
First, survivors were considered to have been asked about a 
domain if they reported being asked at “most/every” visits to 
reflect the importance of regular assessment. Additionally, this 
approach reflects the standards’ reference to survivors “being 
assessed at multiple points” for the physical and emotional/
psychological effects of cancer, an expectation we extended 
to all concerns [5]. Second, concerns were listened to if par-
ticipants reported their treatment team listened “mostly/com-
pletely” to them. Finally, concerns were managed if partici-
pants answered “yes” to receiving advice/help or referral about 
a concern they experienced. The proportion of participants 
who reported being asked, listened to, and having concerns 
managed was examined by domain. Chi-square tests or Fish-
er’s exact tests (cell counts < 5) evaluated group differences 
(i.e., treatment phase, disease stage, and residence) related 
to participants’ concerns and lifestyle behaviors. Pairwise 
deletion was used to manage missing data for each individual 
test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were 
conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 31.
Interview data
Interview data were analyzed using directed content analy -
sis [19]. One coder (SBH) developed an initial codebook 
with four parent codes (SC, facilitators and barriers, sug-
gestions and recommendations, and stories) plus child and 
grandchild codes, all with operational definitions and illus-
trative quotes. This codebook was reviewed (JLB, MAW) 
and subsequently revised. Next, SBH and MAW completed 
one round of consensus coding for three transcripts, with 
94.8% agreement across codes, after which three additional 
transcripts were coded to ensure consensus was maintained. 
Coders then worked independently to code all remaining 
transcripts using NVivo version 15, with questions resolved 
via consensus meetings. Afterwards, MAW and JLB manu-
ally applied thematic analysis to code-specific reports from 
NVivo. At this step, coders worked independently and then 
met to compare themes and reach consensus about both the 
themes and illustrative quotes.
Results
Sample characteristics
In total, 206 eligible survivors were approached about the 
study, 179 survivors consented, and 150 survivors completed 
the survey. Table 1 details participants’ sociodemographic 
and clinical characteristics (N = 150). Ages ranged from 22 
to 83 years (mean [SD] age 59.03 [13.13]). Participants were 
predominantly White (90.67%) and non-Hispanic (98.67%). 
Most participants were female (62.00%). Roughly one-half 
lived in rural counties (53.33%). Educational backgrounds 
varied, such that while 21.33% only had a high school edu-
cation, 42.67% earned a college degree. Annual household 
income was widely distributed, with 23.33% reporting 
incomes below $25,000 and 20.00% reporting incomes 
above $100,000. Regarding employment, half were either 
working (31.33%) or disabled (19.33%). The most common 
cancer sites were breast (28.00%), lung (16.00%), head/neck 
(12.00%), and gastrointestinal (19.33%). Forty-one percent 
of participants had early-stage disease and 50.67% were in 
active treatment.

Interview findings
Table 2 shows the codes applied to the interview data, with 
illustrative quotes. Below is a summary of themes identified.
Participants often commented on the generic nature of 
assessment wherein they were frequently asked questions 
such as “Is there anything you need?”. Structured, focused 
assessment beyond routine check-in questions about pain, 
depression, tobacco use, and medical history changes were 
less common (see Table 2, A). Participants noted that symp-
tom, function, and wellbeing screenings were often con-
ducted ahead of appointments via the patient-facing portion 
of their EHR or in clinic by rooming staff or nurses, with 
inconsistent follow-up assessment by oncologists (Table  2, 
B). When concerns or problems were identified, though, par-
ticipants felt that providers—often described as functioning 
as a “team”—adequately addressed them. Referrals to social 
workers, nutritionists, genetic counselors, and physical 
therapists were common, with other referrals like dentistry 
recommended only for certain disease sites (e.g., head/neck). 
Participants rarely recalled referrals for services to palliative 
care and fertility specialists. Notably, participants expressed 
low awareness of the cancer center having established “SC 
Visits”, with few interview participants reporting an offer to 
meet with a dedicated SC nurse after treatment completion 
(Table 2, C-E). Finally, participants described the experience 
of shared decision-making and involvement in care planning, 
and believed their preferences and priorities were respected.
Internal barriers to and facilitators for care were defined 
as factors within the health system that either undermined or 
contributed to delivery of SC, respectively. Common inter -
nal barriers included suboptimal care coordination among 
multidisciplinary teams, difficulty accessing supportive care 
services, logistical challenges in navigating the physical hos-
pital/clinic environment, and poor provider communication 
or insensitivity (Table  2, F–H). Common internal facilita-
tors included effective communication with providers who 
were approachable and accessible, providers who encour -
aged shared decision-making, providers who meaningfully 
engaged in informed consent to treatment and follow-up,

External barriers and facilitators to care were defined as

insurance coverage limitations or absence, limited financial

Finally, participants offered suggestions to improve SC. 
Themes centered around the cancer center providing more 
information about treatment and available services, more 
direct offers of assistance and referral so survivors have less

the perception that they underwent fully informed consent 
with questions answered and concerns addressed. Addition-

not a number. They actually care…” (SID 402).
Discussion
This mixed-methods study assessed the quality of SC expe-
rienced by survivors at an NCI Designated Comprehensive 
Cancer Center using the National Standards for Cancer SC 
as a guide. This is one of the first studies to directly examine 
clinical practice alignment with the national standards from 
the perspective of survivors. Results suggest that to meet the 
national standards, health systems will likely need to adopt 
systematic assessment and comprehensive management of 
financial concerns, including cost of care and health insur -
ance concerns, as well as practical concerns (e.g., returning 
to work). Health systems will also need to attend closely 
to the needs of vulnerable populations who may shoulder 
disproportionate burden of these concerns and experience 
unique barriers addressing them, such as rural survivors.
The national standards state that survivors should be 
assessed at “multiple points” in their follow-up care for the 
physical and emotional effects of cancer, as well as assessed 
for practical effects, lifestyle behaviors, and financial 
hardship, and subsequently provided with appropriate treat-
ment, resources, or referrals [20]. The present study demon-
strates that physical effects were the most assessed, listened 
to, and managed domain, followed closely by emotional 
concerns. No differences in these processes were observed 
between survivors in active vs. post-treatment and with early 
vs. late-stage cancers. This positive finding is unsurprising 
given the prominence of physical symptom management in 
cancer care and the prioritization of screening and treatment 
for psychological concerns in the National Comprehensive 
Cancer Network Guidelines for Distress Management [21]. 
Still, survivors in this study emphasized the importance of 
effective, compassionate communication from providers 
and care coordination as facilitators of quality SC. Recom-
mendations for improving care included increasing direct, 
proactive offers of assistance and referral to reduce the 
need for self-advocacy and offering additional help for sur -
vivors to manage the stress of treatment through improve-
ments in communication and increased material support for 
caregivers.
In contrast to physical and emotional concerns, survey 
data on survivor reports of assessment for financial concerns 
suggested a major practice gap: less than one-third reported 
being assessed for financial or health insurance concerns. 
Interviews with survivors complement this survey finding, 
as they demonstrated the salience of practical barriers to SC 
related to cost. Among participants who reported experienc-
ing a financial or insurance concern and were able to com-
municate the concern to their care team, 100% reported that 
their concern was managed. While this finding is encourag-
ing, demonstrating the capability to assist survivors when 
their needs are identified effectively, it could be an artifact 
of a small sample size and there is yet room for improve-
ment in assessment. That said, the low assessment rate found 
in this sample deviates from that in some other studies. In 
national studies of NCORP and NCCN sites, 72–78% of 
sites reported having a financial screening process in place 
for patients [22– 24]. At the survivor level, however, the 
occurrence of financial screening may not be implemented 
optimally, with 58% of breast cancer survivors in one sample 
reporting that they were not asked about financial stressors 
by their care team [25]. Thus, it is likely that even when 
there exists a policy that mandates screening for financial 
hardship and insurance concerns, and a process in place for 
population-level screening, there is still room for improve-
ment regarding the reach of that screening/assessment.
Inclusion of financial and health insurance concern 
assessment and management in the standards recognizes 
the public health crisis surrounding cost of care and finan-
cial toxicity in cancer. Financial toxicity, or hardship due 
to medical care costs [26], affects 28–48% of survivors 
[27], with evidence that worse financial toxicity is related 
to active treatment [28], late-stage diagnosis [28], and time

since cancer diagnosis [29]. While differences in assessment 
of financial concerns by cancer treatment status were not 
observed here, differences by rural vs. non-rural residence 
were observed, indicating that financial toxicity may be 
more likely to go unnoticed among survivors living in rural 
areas. It’s unclear why, but this may be due to care teams 
assuming that rural survivors do not want to discuss financial 
and other personal matters publicly due to cultural norms, 
an assumption that may inadvertently lead to inadequate 
assessment of financial (and practical) concerns. Financial 
toxicity is closely tied to health insurance concerns, wherein 
inadequate coverage, high treatment costs, and impacts to 
employment exacerbate toxicity [30–32]. The field has been 
grappling with how to address financial toxicity in cancer 
care for years, particularly in light of therapeutic advances 
conferring longer survival and treatment courses for likely 
incurable cancers [2]. While optimal methods for screening 
and managing financial toxicity remain to be determined, 
multi-level solutions are needed, including improved rates 
of proactive screening, access to financial navigation, and 
empathetic provider communication about financial hardship 
[22, 33–35]. Cancer centers need to implement proactive, 
standardized assessment of financial/insurance concerns 
while recognizing that assessment alone is insufficient and 
the burden of communication and management should not 
fall on survivors. Financial toxicity is an area of growing 
attention given projected costs of cancer care and potential 
for increased premiums and increased rates of under and 
uninsured survivors [36], so focused attention to this com-
ponent of the standards is critical.
Overall, findings emphasize the importance of popu-
lation-level, systematic, prospective, and comprehensive 
assessment of the needs – and lifestyle behaviors – of both 
active treatment and post-treatment survivors. In this study, 
which focuses on survivors’ reports of cancer care teams’ 
practices, it was found that cancer treatment teams effec-
tively managed survivors’ concerns once they were made 
aware, but social and financial/insurance concerns (and to a 
lesser extent practical concerns) often required survivors to 
seek assistance. Given that social determinants and practi-
cal matters like insurance coverage, travel distances, and 
financial hardship can delay or otherwise disrupt cancer 
care delivery and negatively influence cancer outcomes, it 
is imperative that the onus not be on survivors to voice con-
cerns in these domains, but instead for preventive measures 
to be adopted at the system-level. Cancer centers should 
ensure that assessment processes are standardized for each 
of the health system processes identified in the SC stand-
ards and that providers are knowledgeable about resources or 
referral options to manage each concern at the point of care 
to reduce possible disruptions in treatment. Moreover, peri-
odic audits of assessment processes can be a valuable tool 
to ensure that screening tools are working as planned and 
that survivors’ needs are being met. Gathering information 
directly from survivors’ can provide valuable insight in iden-
tifying gaps between policies and processes in action and 
inform efforts to improve on-the-ground screening practices.
Toward that end, the national standards provide a critical 
first step in a roadmap for improving SC quality in the US. 
Assessing direct alignment with the national standards was 
difficult in the present study as each standard provides a 
general description of important processes but little direction 
for measurement. Cancer care delivery researchers and lead-
ers can move implementation forward by operationalizing 
guidelines to include clearly defined steps for management 
of concerns and benchmarks for concordance, as well as 
developing a uniform process for cancer centers to inde-
pendently assess/rate themselves. Prior research using EHR 
review or de-identified patient data to assess overall can-
cer care quality [37] and adherence to Institute of Medicine 
practice guidelines [7] for SC plans [38–41] offer a blueprint 
for evaluating SC delivery.
The foremost study limitation is that this study did not 
measure every process indicator included in the standards 
comprehensively. For example, we measured care of survi-
vors’ overall “physical” concerns as opposed to separating 
out late-effects and chronic conditions. Second, the survey/
interview data were self-reported, meaning the results may 
be impacted by recall and/or social desirability bias. Relat-
edly, this study’s assessment of alignment with the stand-
ards relies on survivor report not EHR or claims data, or 
other objective indicators, which have value. Third, while 
it met the study aims, the survey used face valid items as 
opposed to psychometrically validated instruments. Fourth, 
as data were collected from one cancer center, the results 
likely do not generalize—without qualification—to cancer 
centers nationwide. Finally, the subset of participants who 
were interviewed was small, so qualitative data should not 
be viewed unequivocally as representative of the population, 
but as a means of expanding upon survey results. Even with 
these limitations, this study provides a robust, novel clini-
cal practice assessment of SC quality, guided by the new 
national standards. Future implementation research should 
help refine the national standards to ensure they are both 
practical and impactful, particularly regarding processes 
and evaluation and cancer centers should employ empiri-
cally supported quality improvement methods like Plan-Do-
Study-Act [42] to implement the national standards (possibly 
with special focus on financial matters) with the important 
goal of improving access to quality care for all survivors.
