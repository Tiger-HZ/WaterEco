Abstract 24 
Airborne particulate samples were collected on quartz filters to determine the concentrat ions, 25 
sources and health risks of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) in air in Kumasi, Ghana. A 26 
total of thirty two air samples were collected in Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and 27 
Technology campus (KNUST) (pristine site) and city centre (CC). Samples were extracted with 28 
1:2 v/v acetone: hexane mixture prior to GC –MS analyses. The sum of concentrations of 17 29 
PAHs in air ranged from 0.51–16 (KNUST) and 19–38 ng/m3 (CC). The concentration of 30 
Benzo[a]Pyrene, BaP, ranged from below detection limit to 0.08 ng/m3 (KNUST) and 1.6 to 5.6 31 
ng/m3 (CC). Chemical mass balance model showed that PAHs in air in Kumasi were mainly 32 
from fuel combustion. The total BaP toxic equivalent concentration (BaPeq) in CC was 18 times 33 
higher compared to KNUST; based on the European Legislation, Swedish and United Kingdom 34 
Standards for BaP in air, CC could be classified as highly polluted. Estimated carcinogenicity of 35 
PAHs in terms of BaPeq indicated that BaP was the principal PAHs contributor in CC (70%). 36 
Health risk of adults and children associated to PAHs inhalation was assessed by taking into 37 
account the lifetime average daily dose and corresponding incremental lifetime cancer risk 38 
(ILCR). The ILCR was within the acceptable range (10 –6 to 10–4) indicating low health risk to 39 
residents. 40

Introduction 48 
Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are compounds whose structure consist of two or 49 
more fused benzene rings in linear, angul ar or cluster arrangements . PAHs are ubiquitous 50 
environmental pollutants affecting the air, soil, stream, sediment, water and food. They are found 51 
naturally in coal, crude oil and in emissions from forest fires and volcanoes. Nevertheless, m ost 52 
PAHs entering the environment are formed unintentionally during burning of fossil fuel, biomass, 53 
wood etc. (Manoli et al. 2000). 54 
 Exposure to PAHs takes pl ace mainly by in halation of contaminated air or ingestion of soil, 55 
food and drinking contaminated water (Barranco et al. 2004; Dissanayake et al. 2004). PAH 56 
exposure to particular work or areas has been explored. These studies have provided many 57 
valuable insights on the potential threat of PAHs to human health. Cases include on –duty traffic 58 
policemen (Ruchirawat et al. 2002; Liu et al. 2007), incense smoke in vehicle (Kuo et al . 2003), 59 
fixed site with heavy traffic (Ho and Lee 2002), urban site, vegetation area, forest area 60 
(Vasconcellos et al. 2003), bus station and traffic tunnel (Pereira et al . 2002), outdoor air 61 
(Velasco et al. 2004), roadside air ( Chetwittayachan et al. 2002; Marr et al. 2004), and ambient 62 
traffic site (Lodovici et al. 2003). 63 
 Due to their ubiquitous occurrence, persistence, suspected carcinogenicity and mutagenicity, 64 
PAHs are included in the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), Environmental 65 
Monitoring Assessment and the European Union priority lists of pollutants. The US EPA fixed 66 
16 parent PAHs as priority pollutants (Mastral and Callén 2000; Magi et al. 2002; Szolar et al. 67 
2002; Schubert et al. 2003). 68 
4 
 
 As one of the most industrialized and economically significant cities in Ghana, Kumasi has 69 
been subject ed to heavy anthropogenic impacts due to rapid economic development and 70 
urbanization. The population and number of vehicles has drastically increased, during the past 71 
decade and many fuel filling stations are located in this region leading to greater fuel combustion 72 
rate. All of these have led to fuel leakages, smoke production from exhaust of automobiles and as 73 
a result high levels of PAHs are released into the environment. Other pollution sources in the 74 
Kumasi metropolis, Ghana, are the garbage, wood, and paper burning (Bortey–Sam et al. 2014). 75 
According to available literature, no studies on PAHs have been made before in this city. 76 
 The objectives of this study were: i) to determine the concentrations of 21 PAHs in particulate 77 
matter (PM10) in the Kumasi metropolis; ii) to identify the possible sources of these PAHs; and 78 
iii) to determine the toxic potential and possible health risk implications associated with them. 79

Materials and methods 81 
Study area and sampling 82 
Two samp ling sites were selected for this study . They were loc ated at Kwame Nkrumah 83 
University of Science and Technology campus, KNUST , (pristine site) and city centre, CC, 84 
(which is densely populated and host a number of vehicles, fuel stations and small scale 85 
industries). Airborne particulate samples (PM 10) were collected on quartz filters using a Sibata 86 
Low Volume Pump air sampler (SL–30; Shibata; Saitama, Japan) . The sampling sites were 87 
selected using Global Positioning System (GPS). The GPS coordinates at KNUST site were 88 
N06o40'24.7'', W001o34'04.8'' and that of CC were N06 o41'51.6'', W001o37’23.6’’. A total of 20 89 
samples were collected in KNUST (5 samples were collected each month) whilst 12 samples 90 
were collected in CC (3 samples were collected each month). 91 
 Air sampling was carried out for 8 h each day at a flow rate of 30 L/min. Thirty two air 92 
samples were collected in total from June to September, 2011. The air inlet was located 1.5 m 93 
above ground level to simulate the breathing zone. Samples/filters were wrapped in aluminum 94 
foil, double packed in zip–lock bags and stored at –20 0C in the Department of Chemistry, 95 
KNUST, Ghana. Finally, samples were transported to the Laboratory of Toxicology, Hokkaido 96 
University, Japan where they stored at –30 0C prior to analysis. 97 
 From June to September, the northern and central regions of Ghana receive 150–250 mm of 98 
rain per month; this period is the coldest and wettest in the year (McSweeney et al. 2013). 99 
Variations in temperature both annually and daily are quite small and t he minimum temperature 100 
is around 23 0C (73 0F). 101

Sample extraction and clean–up 104 
Extraction of PAHs in air samples (quartz filters) was done by soxhlet extractor (Soxtherm 105 
Gerhardt Variostat; Soxtherm V7.5, Germany) using 170 mL acetone/hexane (1:2; v/v) for 6 h 106 
after spiking with 25 µL of 1 mg/L PAH internal standard (acenaphthene_d10, phenanthrene_d10, 107 
chrysene_d12, and perylene_ d12). The extracts were dehydrated by filtering through anhydrous 108 
sodium sulfate (Kanto Chemical Co., Inc, Tokyo, Japan). The dehydrated extracts were collected 109 
into a round bottom flask. It was then concentrated to approximately 1.5 mL on a rotary 110 
evaporator and transferred into a test tube . The dehydrated extract in the test tube was further 111 
concentrated to 0.3 mL under a gentle N2 stream. 112 
 The extracts were cleaned –up to remove other contaminants that may have been extracted 113 
with PAHs. Clean –up was done using column packed with 5% water content silica gel (Kanto 114 
Chemical Co., Inc, Tokyo, Japan). It was then eluted with 100 mL of diethyl ether/hexane 115 
mixture in the ratio 1:4, v/v. The eluate was further concentrated to 2 mL using a rotary 116 
evaporator. 300 µL of n–decane was added to the extract and was further concentrated to 0.3 mL 117 
using a gentle N 2 stream. All solvents used (Kanto Chemical Co., Inc, Tokyo, Japan) were of 118 
analytical grade. 119

PAHs analysis 121 
PAHs analyses were carried out using Auto Sampler 3000 Gas Chromatograph (Focus GC) 122 
coupled with a Thermo scientific Mass Selective Detector (DSQ II MS, Kanagawa, Japan ) 123 
operating in the electron impact mode (GC–MS). The selective ion monitoring mode was used 124 
for quantification. A FactorFour capillary column, VF–Xms, 30 m, 0.25 mm inner diameter, 0.25 125 
7 
 
µm film thickness (Varian Inc., Lake Forest, CA, USA) was used for separation. Helium gas was 126 
used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.2 mL /min. Injector and mass transfer line 127 
temperatures were 260 oC and 280 oC, respectively. Temperature programming for the column 128 
were as follows, initial temperature of 90 oC held for 1 min, ramped to 280 oC at 10 oC/min and 129 
finally to 320 oC at a ramp rate of 5 oC/min and held for 10 min. 1 µL sample was injected in the 130 
splitless mode for analysis. 131 
 Concentrations of 21 individual PAHs (AccuStandard, New Haven, USA) including 16 132 
USEPA priority pollutants (naphthalene (Nap), acenaphthylene (Acl) acenaphthene (Ace), 133 
fluorene (Fle), phenanthrene (Phe), anthracene (Ant), fluoranthene (Flu), pyrene (Pyr), 134 
benz[a]anthracene (BaA), chrysene (Chr), benzo[b]fluoranthene (BbF), benzo[k]fluoranthene 135 
(BkF), benzo[a]pyrene (BaP), indeno[1,2,3–cd]pyrene (IDP), dibenz[a,h]anthracene (DBahA), 136 
benzo[g,h,i]perylene (BghiP), perylene (Peryl), benzo[e]pyrene (BeP), methylene phenanthrene 137 
(Methy–Phe), 1–methyl phenanthrene (Me–Phe) and retene (Ret)) were measured in each sample. 138 
Difficulties were often associated with the GC separation of BbF and BkF and s ince 139 
benzo[j]fluoranthene (BjF) co–eluted with BbF (Pietrogrande et al. 2014), the sum of these 140 
isomers was used as an abbreviation, BbF + BjF + BkF. All results were expressed in ng/m3. 141

Health Risk Characterization 162 
Health risk estimation can be calcu lated using PAH exposure through one of the following 163 
exposure pathways: ingestion, inhalation, or dermal exposure (USEPA 2005). In this study, 164 
inhalation of air particles contaminated with PAHs was considered. Health risk assessment of 165 
carcinogenic PAHs cannot be related to the sole total concentration, r ather each PAH has a 166 
different carcinogenic potential. For this purpose, toxicity equivalency factors (TEFs) are used, 167 
which allow to quantify the carcinogenic potential of other PAHs, relative to BaP, and estimate 168 
BaP–equivalent concentration (BaPeq) (Nadal et al . 2004). Nonetheless, usually the health risk 169 
associated with inhalatory PAH uptake is estimated on the basis of the sole BaP, which was the 170

principal contributor in every studied site (Halek et a l. 2008). In our study, the list of TEFs 171 
compiled by Tsai et al . (2004) was adopted (see Table 3), and the total PAH –associated 172

Data analysis 194 
Data were analysed using SPSS version 20.0 for windows (IBM SPSS Statistics., 2011) . The 195 
PAHs concentrations in KNUST and CC were tested for normality and distribution was 196 
considered statistically significant if p value was less than 0.05. Statistical difference of PAHs 197 
between the tw o sample sites was tested using ANOVA. Results were considered significant 198 
with p values less than 0.05. Principal component analysis (PCA) , was done to determine the 199 
distribution pattern of PAHs in air (except for Nap ; because concentrations were not detected in 200 
most samples from both sites ), and was performed using JMP statistical so ftware v. 10 (SAS 201 
Institute). The principal components were extracted with eigenvalues > 1 through varimax 202 
rotation. 203 
 Chemical mass balance version 8 (CMB8) was applied for source identification from both 204 
sample sites a nd source profiles/input were classified as either coal related (power plant, 205 
residential or coke oven) or traffic related (gasoline engine, diesel engine or traffic tunnel) (Li et 206 
al. 2003). Study by Bortey–Sam et al. (2014) indicated that coke ovens and traffic were the two 207 
major sources of PAHs in the area and these have also been considered to be the two most 208 
important source categories in many metropolitan areas in the 20th century (Li et al. 2003). 209 
 The basic idea of the CMB model is that the measured chemical pollutants in a sample are the 210 
sums of the contributions from several sources (Li et al. 2001; Wang et al. 2010). However, only 211 
relative compound concentrations in a source or a sample profile (e.g., normalized with BeP, or 212 
any other PAH compound) are important (Christensen et al. 1999). The inputs required by the 213 
EPA CMB8 include the ambient data and source profiles. In this study, the “ambient” data are 214 
11 
 
the experimentally measured concentrations, in ng/m3, of PAHs in PM10. The key to a successful 215 
CMB application is to obtain a set of source fingerprints which is consistent with the 216 
measurements at the receptor location. For the purpose of this work, 11 and 17 published PAH 217 
source profiles were collected fr om literature for coal and traffic related sources respectively (Li 218 
et al. 2003). 219 
 Nap was not included in the modeling, because of the high uncertainties in its source 220 
fingerprints and possible evaporative losses during c hemical analysis of the samples. Due to the 221 
lack of sufficient data for DBahA in many source profiles, this compound was not selected as 222 
fitting species in most model runs (Li et al. 2003). Similarly, Methy–Phe, Me –Phe, Ret, and 223 
Peryl were not included. The CMB8 model results were evaluated by using several fit indices 224 
such as coefficient of determination, R2 (≥ 0.85), chi–square distribution, χ² (≤ 3.0), percent mass 225 
accounted for (72–95%), T-statistics (≥ 3.0), and the residuals and uncertainty ratios (–1.8 to 4.2). 226 
The values in parentheses were the statistical parameters obtained from the modelling results. 227

Results and discussion 231 
PAHs in ambient air in KNUST and CC 232 
From the 21 PAHs analyzed , the more volatile compounds (Nap, Acl, Ace and Fle ) were not 233 
discussed at each sampling location because these lower ring molecules are present in the gas 234 
phase, especially at high ambient temperatures (20 –38 0C), which was observed throughout the 235 
sampling period. Moreover, their (Nap, Acl, Ace and Fle) c oncentrations were either undetected 236 
or low, which was also observed in studies by Oanh et al. (2000) and Salam et al. (2011). 237 
 The sum of concentrations of 17 PAHs (Σ17 PAHs) in air samples (PM 10) in KNUST ranged 238 
from 0.51–16 ng/m3 with mean and median values of 3.3 ± 3.3 and 2. 6 ng/m3 respectively. 239 
Meanwhile, Σ17 PAHs in air samples in CC ranged from 19 –38 ng/m3 with mean and median 240 
concentrations of 30 ± 4.9 and 30 ng/m3 respectively. The mean concentration of Σ17 PAHs in CC 241 
was 9 times greater than in KNUST. This may be attributed to the fact that the sampling in 242 
Kejetia was performed in a traffic area suffering high exhaust emission and oil seep from 243 
vehicles, and hosting handicraft and small industrial activities (e.g., corn milling). 244 
 PM10–bound PAH concentrations were compared with rec ent measurements in other urban 245 
areas around the world, including Turkey, Spain and the comparative information is illustrated in 246 
Table 1. The concentrati ons observed in CC was comparable with that of cities in Thailand 247 
(Wiriya et al. 2013) (Table 1), but they were lower than those detected in Zonguldak, Turkey 248 
(Akyuz and Cabuk 2009 ) (Table 1). However, Callen et al. ( 2008) and Pengchai et al. ( 2009) 249 
reported low PAHs concentrations in air (PM10) in Zaragoza (8.8 ± 7.9 ng/m 3) and Thailand 250 
(0.05–22 ng/m3), respectively (Table 1). 251

Most abundant PAHs in air in KNUST and CC 254 
The most abundant PAHs in air (PM 10) in KNUST were Phe (21%), BghiP (16%), Ret (15%), 255 
and Pyr (11%) (Table 2). Chr was the least abundant with concentration below detection limit in 256 
almost all samples (Table 2). Ret (one of the most abundant PAHs in air in KNUST) is a 257 
molecular marker of wood combustion (Shen et al. 2012). PAHs profile developed by Harrison 258 
et al. (1996) and Ho et al. (2002b) on the types of emission sources suggested that Phe and Pyr in 259 
KNUST were typical diesel vehicle markers whereas BghiP, the 2 nd most abundant, was 260 
indicative of gasoline vehicle emission. 261 
 As shown in Table 2, air samples in KNUST was dominated by 3–(44%), 6–(24%), 4–(21%), 262 
and 5–ring PAHs (11 %). Because only PAHs in the particulate phase were collected, it is 263 
expected that some of these 3 –ring PAHs will also be in the gas phase and concentrations could 264 
be underestimated, especially in KNUST, where 3 –ring PAHs showed high contribution. During 265 
combustion at low temperatures, the low molecular weight (LMW) PAH compounds (˂ 4 rings) 266 
are abundant (Lake et al. 1979). Whilst at high temperature combustion the high molecular 267 
weight (HMW) PAH compounds (≥ 4 rings) are dominant (Laflamme and Hites 1978) . 268 
According to that, ~55 % of PAHs in KNUST were released by high temperature combustion 269 
sources. 270 
 The most abundant PAH s in air in CC were BghiP, IDP, BeP and BaP (HMW PAHs) (Table 271 
2, p < 0.001 ). Their abundances were 29%, 19%, 14%, 12 % respectively (Table 2). Me–Phe 272 
were the least abundant PAHs there (0.01 ± 0.04 ng/m 3). This pathway has been reported as 273 
indicative of vehicle emission (Harrison et al. 1996; Oanh et al. 2000; Jamhari et al. 2014). 274 
 Moreover, in CC the PAHs profile was dominated by 6–(48%) (p < 0.001) , 5–(42 %) (p < 275 
0.001), 3–(5%) (p > 0.001), and 4–ring PAHs (4.5%) (p < 0.001) (Table 2). The high percentage 276 
14 
 
of HMW PAHs (95%) confirmed high te mperature processes, such as combustion of fuels in 277 
engines, as prevailing sources (Mostert et al. 2010; Tobiszewski and Namiesnik 2012). 278 
 PCA was applied to PAHs profiles in KNUST and CC. The PCA revealed that the first 279 
principal component (component 1) accounted for 47% of the variation while component 2 280 
accounted for 19% (Fig. 1). As observed from the score plot (Fig. 1a ), there was a clear 281 
separation between the two sampling sites (K and C). That could depend on the high levels of 282 
PAHs in CC compared to KNUST. From the loading plot (Fig. 1b), most of the PAHs (both 283 
LMW and HMW) wer e highly associated/clustered on one side of the component. Another 284 
interesting feature observed along the component (Fig. 1) was the clear separation between 285 
LMW and HMW PAHs, (Fig. 1b) with the HMW PAHs highly distributed in the CC of Kumasi 286 
(Fig. 1). 287

Levels of BaP in air in KNUST and CC 289 
Concentrations of BaP in air in KNUST ranged from below detection (from most sampling 290 
dates) to 0.08 ng/m 3. The mean and median concent rations of BaP in KNUST were 0.02 ± 0.03 291 
and 0.01 ng/m 3 respectively (Table 3). Since no environmental standard is available for Ghana, 292 
the United Kingdom (UK), Swedish Standards and European Legislation were adopted to assess 293 
the quality of air . The UK air quality s tandards, Swedish guideline and European Legislation, 294 
values for BaP are equal 0.25 , 0.1 and 1 ng/m 3 (for the total content in the PM 10 fraction 295 
averaged over a calendar year) , respectively (Dimashki et al. 2001; Bostrom et al. 2002; 296 
Directive 2004/107/EC). 297 
15 
 
 The mean concentration of BaP in air in KNUST (0.02 ± 0.03 ng/m3) was 5, 12, and 50 times 298 
below the BaP standard set by the Swedish , UK, and European Legislation respectively. 299 
Similarly, individual concentrations of BaP in air in KNUST were all below the recommended 300 
guideline values . Low concentrations of BaP and Σ 17 PAHs were recorded in air in KNUST 301 
because the sampling site was located within an area with low vehicular movement, low 302 
industrial and human activities and therefore PAHs from point sources were negligible. 303 
 BaP was t he fourth most abundant PAH in air in CC (p < 0.001) and concentration ranged 304 
from 1.6–5.6 ng/m3. The mean concentration of BaP in CC (3.7 ± 1.1 ng/m3) was 187 times 305 
higher than in KNUST (0.02 ± 0.03 ng/m 3) and exceeded 5 times that found in Taichung 306 
Industrial Park, TIP , Taiwan (particles–bound) (0.7 ng/m3; Guor–Cheng et al . 2004). Similarly, 307 
the mean concentrations of BaP in CC was higher than Zaragoza, Spain (Callen et al. 2013 ) and 308 
in Amritsar, India (Kaur et al. 2013). On the other hand, the BaP values were comparable with 309 
those of Naples, Italy (2.9 ng/m3) and Seoul, Korea (2.6 ± 3.3 ng/m3) (Caricchia et al. 1999; Park 310 
et al. 2002). 311 
 Anyway, the BaP concentrations in CC were 37, 15, and 4 times higher, respectively, than the 312 
air quality standards of Sweden , UK, and European Legislation. Therefore, the CC air was 313 
classified as heavily polluted. 314

Air toxicity assessment (CC) 327 
In Kumasi (CC) , PAHs including human carcinogenic compounds (BaA, BbF, BkF, BaP, Chr, 328 
DBahA, and IDP) (IARC, 2006) were at high concentrations (Tables 2 and 3). The mean 329 
concentration of these compounds were IDP (5.8 ± 1.2), BaP (3.7 ± 1.1), BbF + BjF + BkF (3.2 330 
± 0.98), DBahA (0.5 ±0.16) BaA (0.29 ± 0.08) and Chr (0.15 ± 0.06) ng/m3 (Table 3). 331 
 WHO (2000) stated that the BaP equivalent concentration producing an excess lifetime cancer 332 
risk of 1/10,000 is approximately 1.2 ng/m3. The mean concentrations of BaP alone in CC (3.7 ± 333 
1.1 ng/m3) exceeded this threshold . The toxic equivalent c oncentration (BaPeq) in air of 334 
carcinogenic PAHs was 0.3 ngBaPeq/m 3 in KNUST and 5.2 ngBaPeq/m3 in CC (Table 3). Thus 335 
BaPeqs in CC were, on the average, 18 times higher than in KNUST. 336 
 According to BaPeqs of individual PAHs, BaP was the predominant contributor to toxicity in 337 
CC. In comparison of the BaPeq ’s of PAHs in air with other conducted studies in PM 10, the 338 
BaPeq values of 16 USEPA PAHs in CC (5.3 ng/m3; Table 3) was found to be higher than 339 
similar work done in Christchurch, New Zealand (1.4 ng/m3; city) (Brown et al. 2005; assigned 340 
same TEF values as this study except for DBahA, which was assigned a value of 5); Dunedin, 341 
New Zealand (0.006 ng/m 3; city) (Brown et al. 2005) ; Taiwan (3.7 ng/m3; urban), (Chang et al. 342 
2006); Chiang Mai, Thailand (sub–urban, 0.18–3.7) (Wiriya et al. 2013). The BaPeq s from this 343

study were however lower than similar study from tr affic site (particle phase; 13 ng/m3) in Japan 344

 To assess the health risk for humans, adults and children were assumed to be exposed to 346 
airborne PAHs through inhaling PM 10. The estimates were made by considering LADD and the 347

 The LADD PAH values ranged from 2 .5E–9 to 2.1E–6 for adults (average 4.4E–7) and 6.9E–349 
8 to 5.8E –6 for children (average 1.3E –6). According to that, ILCR associated to carcinogenic 350 
PAHs (IARC 2006) ranged from 2.2E –10 to 5.4E –6 (adults) and 6.1E –10 to 1.5E –5 (children). 351 
The mean ILCR for adults (1.2E –6) and children (3.3E –6) were within the acceptable limit of 352 
10–6 to 10–4 as a cknowledged by regulatory agencies (USEPA 2005). The acceptable risk 353 
distribution is expressed as a constraints –based percentile and must be ≤ 10–6 to 10–4 using 354 
upper–bound factors, which represents the increase of cancer by one in the group of million of 355 
people (USEPA 2005). 356

BaP concentrations in air samples (PM10) in KNUST varied from below detection to 0.08 ng/m 3 359 
and 1.69 to 5.66 ng/m3 in CC. The mean concentrations of BaP in air was 0.02 ± 0.03 (KNUST) 360 
and 3.7 ± 1.1 ng/m3 (CC) respectively. By the Swedish, UK air quality s tandards and European 361 
Legislation value for BaP in air, CC is polluted with BaP. CMB model showed that PAHs in air 362 
in KNUST and CC were emissions from diesel and gasoline engines respectively. 78% of PAHs 363 
in CC originated from gasoline combustion. The estimated carcinogenicity of PAHs in terms of 364 
BaPeq confirmed that BaP was t he dominant PAHs contributor (70 %). Estimated probabilistic 365
