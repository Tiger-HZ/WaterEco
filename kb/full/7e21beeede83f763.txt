Microalgae and cyanobacteria reconfigure pigment–protein complexes as environments change, coupling light 
harvesting, photochemistry, and redox homeostasis. This review synthesizes the mechanisms driving shifts in 
chlorophylls, carotenoids, and phycobiliproteins in response to abiotic and anthropogenic stressors, including 
irradiance, temperature, salinity, nutrient limitation, and pollutants. Pigment measurements are most informative 
when interpreted as profiles of individual pigments, absolute abundances, and diagnostically relevant ratios, and when 
analyzed together with photosystem performance and independent oxidative-damage markers. Key strategies include 
chlorophyll antenna remodeling to rebalance light capture and protection; carotenoid-mediated nonphotochemical 
quenching, excitation quenching, reactive oxygen species scavenging, membrane stabilization, and, in some taxa, 
secondary carotenoid accumulation during prolonged adaptation. In cyanobacteria, phycobiliprotein plasticity enables 
redistribution of excitation energy and supports antenna-level energy dissipation, while extracellular UV-screening 
pigments such as scytonemin provide an additional protective layer.

Introduction
Photopigments of phototrophic microorganisms, 
including microalgae and cyanobacteria, are classically 
described as structural and regulatory components of 
the photosynthetic apparatus that enable spectral photon 
absorption and transfer of excitation energy to reaction 
centers. This interpretation is sufficient to describe primary

photochemical processes; however, it does not explain 
the persistent variability in pigment phenotypes observed 
in natural populations and in laboratory experiments under 
stress. Recent experimental and systems-level studies 
demonstrate that pigment systems function as a regulated 
component of cellular organization (Srivastava et al. 
2022), embedded in redox-dependent regulatory circuits 
that coordinate environmental inputs with photosynthetic 
electron transport. In this framework, the pigment 
apparatus functions not only as an absorption module 
but also as a mechanism that maintains the operational 
stability of photosynthetic complexes under unstable 
environmental conditions.
In most cases, the link between stress and pigment-
apparatus remodeling is mediated by control of excitation 
pressure and redox balance. When energy absorption 
exceeds the capacity for photochemical utilization and 
the available electron sinks, the probability of chlorophyll 
triplet-state formation and the generation of reactive oxygen 
species, including singlet oxygen, increases, leading 
to photooxidative damage of membranes and protein 
complexes. Therefore, quantitative and qualitative pigment 
changes should be interpreted as part of a coordinated 
regulatory response that redistributes excitation, limits 
reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation, and preserves 
photosystem stability under conditions in which redox 
homeostasis becomes vulnerable. In this context, 
photooxidative load, redox imbalance, and oxidative stress 
should be regarded as related but nonequivalent processes. 
Excess excitation energy increases photooxidative load, 
which may disturb cellular redox homeostasis and, when 
protective mechanisms become insufficient, culminate in 
oxidative stress and cellular damage (Shapiro et al. 2015, 
Allakhverdiev et al. 2024).
Regulation of pigment phenotypes is multilevel, 
reflecting coordinated changes in biosynthesis, complex 
assembly, and membrane architecture. Together, these 
processes generate the observed shifts in pigment 
composition and pigment ratios. At the biochemical 
level, changes in the balance between synthesis and 
degradation of tetrapyrroles and carotenoids determine 
the size of pigment pools. At the level of supramolecular 
organization, antenna size and the stoichiometry of 
pigment–protein complexes also vary, leading to changes 
in the distribution of excitation energy between light-
harvesting complexes and reaction centers. In green algae 
and plants, light-harvesting complexes of photosystem II 
(LHCIIs) function as essential antenna systems involved 
in light harvesting, energy transfer, and photoprotection 
(Li et al. 2023). In diatoms, fucoxanthin chlorophyll (Chl) 
a/c-binding proteins (FCPs) perform these functions, 
whereas cyanobacteria possess large phycobilisome 
megacomplexes (6–16 MDa) (Zheng et al. 2021, Nagao 
et al. 2022). At the membrane level, thylakoid organization 
and antenna connectivity are remodeled, which affects 
energy-transfer kinetics and the local redox state in 
the vicinity of photosystems. Because these rearrangements 
proceed together with the induction of enzymatic and 
nonenzymatic antioxidant mechanisms, the pigment profile 
reflects an integral indicator of cellular physiological 
state, rather than a proxy for biomass level or potential 
photosynthetic capacity (Nagarajan et al. 2022, Sun et al. 
2024).
The physiological consequences of pigment rearrange-
ments are also determined by cell-level optical factors. 
The effective absorption spectrum and the fraction of 
energy that is converted into photochemistry depend on 
pigment packaging, cell geometry, intracellular scattering, 
and self-shading. During acclimation and under stressors, 
these parameters change and shift the balance between 
photochemical energy use and its safe dissipation. 
Therefore, pigment sets that are similar in composition 
may yield different photobiochemical outcomes due 
to differences in optical path length, intracellular light 
gradients, and pigment–protein organization (Aizpuru 
and González-Sánchez 2024). This optical complexity 
is compounded in natural environments, where multiple 
simultaneous stressors alter both molecular pigment 
composition and cell-level optical properties through 
independent mechanisms.
Combined stresses provide the most informative 
context for analyzing pigment systems because, in natural 
environments, light regime, temperature, salinity, and 
pollution often vary jointly. Warming, salinization of 
inland waters, intensification of the ultraviolet component, 
and increasing metal pollution and xenobiotics create 
conditions under which the photosynthetic apparatus 
experiences simultaneous light-load pressure and 
constraints on electron transport (Allakhverdiev et al. 
2024). In such situations, the increasing need for 
redox buffering is closely linked to the capacity of 
antioxidant and photoprotective systems to maintain 
cellular homeostasis (Shapiro et al. 2015). As a result, 
resilience is determined by how rapidly the cell increases 
the share of nonphotochemical dissipation, maintains 
photosystem repair, and stabilizes redox potential 
within a range compatible with operation of the electron 
transport chain (Sorathia et al. 2025). An illustrative 
example is the interaction of high irradiance and nitrogen 
starvation in Haematococcus pluvialis and Dunaliella 
salina. Under high light, lumen acidification activates 
the violaxanthin (V)–antheraxanthin (A)–zeaxanthin (Z) 
(V AZ) cycle, promoting the conversion of violaxanthin 
to zeaxanthin and contributing to rapid nonphotochemical 
quenching (NPQ) on a timescale of minutes to hours. 
Nitrogen starvation, acting over days, upregulates 
MEP-pathway genes and carotenogenic enzymes, 
including ketolases such as BKT1, thereby redirecting 
carbon flux toward secondary carotenoid biosynthesis 
and driving astaxanthin hyperaccumulation in H. pluvialis 
and β-carotene hyperaccumulation in D. salina 
(Zhao et al. 2020, Zarekarizi et al. 2023). Critically, 
the combination of both stressors elicits a stronger 
photoprotective and antioxidant response than either 
alone: elevated oxidative load under co-stress accelerates 
astaxanthin deposition, and analogous synergistic 
carotenoid enhancement under combined high light 
and nutrient deprivation has been independently confirmed 
in the chlorophyte Coelastrella sp., indicating that 
this two-phase, temporally layered response represents

a conserved strategy rather than a species-specific 
phenomenon (Liu et al. 2026). 
The roles of individual classes of photopigments in 
this response differ, but they all serve the common purpose 
of coordinating light harvesting with photoprotection 
and antioxidant chemistry. Chlorophylls support primary 
photochemistry and at the same time define the risk of 
photodamage through the probability of triplet-state 
formation under excess excitation (Xue et al. 2020). 
Carotenoids combine light harvesting with chlorophyll-
triplet and ROS quenching, while xanthophyll cycles 
provide rapid and reversible thermal dissipation under 
excess light (Jahns et al. 2009, Ramanna et al. 2017, 
Ma et al. 2019). Phycobiliproteins expand spectral 
coverage in cyanobacteria and some algae and enable 
plastic redistribution of excitation when the light regime 
changes and under chemical stress (Srivastava et al. 
2022). Scytonemin belongs to specialized protective 
pigments and reduces ultraviolet radiation flux at the level 
of the cell envelope, supporting resilience under intense 
irradiation and under limited activity of energy-dependent 
photoprotective processes (Rastogi et al. 2014).
In practice, interpretation of pigment changes is 
complicated by the fact that responses with similar 
directionality can arise via different mechanisms and 
on different time horizons. Therefore, correct analysis 
requires distinguishing acclimation from adaptation. 
Acclimation refers to reversible changes in pigment 
parameters within the cell life cycle or short-term culture 
dynamics. Adaptation denotes stable intergenerational 
shifts that are fixed by selection and reproducible upon 
repeated exposures, manifested as stabilization of pigment 
phenotypes and regulatory regimes across generations 
(Hohmann-Marriott and Blankenship 2011, Patel et al. 
2022). A complementary and equally critical interpretive 
axis runs between acclimatory and damage-mode responses. 
Both can produce externally similar pigment changes 
(e.g., declining chlorophyll, elevated carotenoid:chlorophyll 
ratios), but they differ fundamentally in the direction of 
cellular control. In the acclimatory mode, pigment changes 
are regulatory in origin: they are controlled by defined 
molecular circuits, proceed alongside maintained or 
improved photosystem performance (stable or recovering 
Fv/Fm), and are typically reversible when the stressor is 
removed. In the damage mode, photodamage accumulates 
faster than the capacity for repair and regulated dissipation; 
pigment changes are accompanied by declining F v/Fm, 
rising lipid peroxidation markers such as malondialdehyde 
(MDA), and progressive loss of photosystem function. 
The transition between modes is determined by the balance 
between the rate of photodamage and the combined rate 
of photoprotection and PSII repair, and therefore depends 
on stress intensity, duration, and the combination of 
concurrent stressors (Muramatsu and Hihara 2012, Song 
et al. 2026). This distinction is particularly consequential 
under changing stress conditions: fluctuating irradiance, 
escalating pollutant doses, or simultaneous shifts in 
temperature and nutrients, because such dynamics can 
drive the system across the acclimation-damage threshold 
repeatedly and in both directions on timescales of minutes 
to days. Throughout this review, distinguishing these 
modes and identifying the multiparameter combinations 
of pigment, photobiophysical, and oxidative-damage 
indicators that operationalize the distinction is a unifying 
analytical objective.

In applied contexts, the same pigment and redox-linked

This narrative review examines systematically how 
photopigment systems in microalgae and cyanobacteria are 
remodeled under different stressors. Rather than providing 
a purely descriptive overview, we present a mechanistic 
synthesis of how pigment systems regulate excitation 
pressure, photoprotection, and redox balance. We examine 
how pigment modules shape energy partitioning, reactive 
oxygen species formation, and photosystem stability, 
and why similar pigment responses may reflect different 
physiological scenarios across taxa and conditions. 
The review is organized into functional blocks, including 
chlorophylls, carotenoids, phycobiliproteins, and 
extracellular UV screens, and integrates evolutionary 
perspectives and experimental-evolution evidence. 
We conclude by outlining key remodeling patterns, current 
limitations, and priorities for improving comparability and 
causal interpretation.
Chlorophyll system and regulation of light harvesting 
under photooxidative load
Chlorophylls form the core of the photochemical apparatus 
in microalgae and define the link between light-field 
characteristics and the metabolic output of photosynthesis. 
As constituents of light-harvesting complexes and reaction 
centers, they enable transfer of excitation energy to 
the primary charge-separation reactions (Hill et al. 2005). 
At the same time, the chlorophyll system is coupled 
to the risk of photodamage. Under excess absorbed 
energy, the probability of intersystem crossing and 
the formation of reactive oxygen species, primarily singlet 
oxygen, increases, thereby making chlorophyll a potential 
source of photooxidative load (Ruban 2016). Therefore, 
photosynthetic resilience is determined not by maximizing 
the chlorophyll pool, but by fine-tuning its amount and 
organization toward a balance between light harvesting 
and photoprotection, which depends on the structural and 
functional state of the antenna system.
Observed ranges of chlorophyll content confirm 
the high plasticity of this system. Under favorable 
conditions, the total chlorophyll fraction often falls 
within 0.5–1.0% of dry biomass, although substantially 
higher values have been reported for some fast-growing 
chlorophytes under optimized cultivation; for Chlorella 
vulgaris, chlorophyll accumulation up to 7% of dry mass 
has been reported (D’Alessandro and Antoniosi Filho 
2016). These parameters are sensitive to light intensity 
and spectral composition, temperature, pH, salinity, 
nutrient availability, and the presence of xenobiotics and 
heavy metals. For this reason, chlorophyll is widely used 
as a quantitative indicator of physiological state. 
As a component of aquatic environmental quality 
assessment, its interpretation requires accounting for the 
stress regime and the culture growth stage (Koutra et al. 
2018).
Under stress, chlorophyll dynamics do not follow 
a single direction because they reflect the relationship 
between growth limitation and redistribution of resources. 
For example, in Scenedesmus obliquus, exposure to 
the dye Brilliant Green was accompanied by partial growth 
inhibition and pronounced changes in chlorophyll content 
together with increased protein and lipid fractions, which 
indicates stress-induced redistribution of carbon and 
energy fluxes (Rajamanickam and Selvasembian 2026). 
In other cases, sublethal xenobiotic concentrations may 
temporarily increase chlorophyll content and biomass. For 
Chlorella sp., exposure to imidacloprid at 10–50 mg L –1 
was associated with increased chlorophyll content and dry 
mass after 12 d of cultivation, which is consistent with 
a hormetic scenario until a toxicity threshold is reached 
(Deng et al. 2022). Therefore, changes in the chlorophyll 
pool should be evaluated together with markers of 
oxidative stress and indicators of metabolic reallocation, 
because an externally similar trend may correspond either 
to damage or to compensatory remodeling. At the gene-
regulatory level, chlorophyll biosynthesis is gated at 
the Mg-chelatase step, where the catalytic subunit CHLH 
(GUN5 in eukaryotes) inserts Mg²⁺ into protoporphyrin IX 
to commit metabolic flux irreversibly to the chlorophyll 
branch; loss-of-function gun5/cch mutations abolish 
Mg-chelatase activity and drastically reduce chlorophyll 
accumulation, confirming CHLH as the principal 
regulatory node of the pathway (Beale 2009). Activity 
of this step is enhanced by GUN4, a porphyrin-binding 
protein that associates with both protoporphyrin IX and 
Mg-protoporphyrin IX, stimulates Mg-chelatase in vitro 
and in vivo by promoting CHLH membrane association, 
and facilitates substrate-to-product transfer through 
the Mg-branch, thereby coupling enzymatic activation to 
metabolic flux control. Additionally, GUN4–porphyrin 
complexes generate 1O2 under illumination, positioning 
GUN4 at the interface between chlorophyll biosynthesis 
and retrograde signaling (Adhikari et al. 2011). Under 
excess light, both H 2O2 and 1O2 function as plastid-to-
nucleus retrograde messengers: H2O2 exits the chloroplast 
to modulate nuclear gene expression during high-light 
stress, while ¹O 2, acting through the EX1/EX2-mediated 
pathway, broadly represses chloroplast-function and 
tetrapyrrole-biosynthesis genes, with CHLH/GUN5 itself 
participating in this signaling network through physical 
interaction with EX1/EX2, such that gun5 mutations 
suppress 1O2-triggered retrograde responses and alter 
the downregulation of nuclear photosynthetic genes, 
although direct experimental evidence that chlH transcript 
levels are preferentially repressed by H 2O2 or 1O2 has not 
yet been explicitly documented, the available evidence 
positions retrograde ROS signaling as a mechanistically 
coherent suppressor of chlH transcription independently 
of growth rate (Leister 2019). This retrograde regulatory 
logic extends to the transcriptional level, where sigma 
factor identity determines which subset of photosynthetic 
and chlorophyll-related genes is activated in response to

Plasticity of the chlorophyll system is determined 
not only by quantity, but also by chlorophyll type, which 
expands the spectral capabilities of photosynthesis 
and affects excitation partitioning. In photosynthetic 
eukaryotes and cyanobacteria, chlorophylls a, b, c, d, 
and f have been described, differing in spectral properties 
and taxonomic distribution (Gisriel 2024, Tamiaki and 
Kichishima 2025). Chlorophyll a is universal and serves 
as the primary photochemical pigment. Chlorophyll b is 
characterized by a formyl group at the C-7 position and 
is among the most abundant chlorophylls in oxygenic 
phototrophs after chlorophyll a. Both pigments are 
embedded in photosystems, large supramolecular protein–
pigment complexes that support primary photochemical 
reactions (Kume et al. 2018). Chlorophyll c, typical of 
diatoms, corresponds to aquatic optical conditions with 
a shift of the available spectrum toward the blue region 
(Büchel 2020). The most specialized forms, chlorophylls d 
and f, have been identified in a limited number of 
cyanobacteria and enable use of the far-red range above 
700 nm, which increases photosynthetic performance in 
spectrally filtered habitats where the visible component 
of light is attenuated (Elias et al. 2024). Thus, the 
taxonomic mosaic of chlorophylls reflects evolutionarily 
fixed solutions that broaden the spectral window of 
photosynthesis and reduce the risk of excitation imbalance 
by redistributing absorption across wavelengths. Table 1

PSII is the primary site of photodamage under 
abiotic stress in both groups, but the mechanisms and 
downstream consequences differ in instructive ways. 
In cyanobacteria, high light, ROS, chemical pollutants, 
and acidification converge on PSII by disrupting D1 
protein integrity and impairing excitation delivery from 
the phycobilisome core, thereby causing a rapid decline 
in PSII activity that precedes broader metabolic collapse. 
This reduction in antenna input occurs in a defined 
sequence, as phycobilisome disassembly is characterized 
by the preferential release of rod pigments before 
degradation of the allophycocyanin core, with this process 
being mediated by the NblA proteolytic adaptor (Tokodi 
et al. 2025). PSI, by contrast, shows greater stability 
under most stress conditions and may even maintain 
or upregulate associated gene expression when PSII is 
impaired, though iron limitation overrides this pattern by 
specifically targeting the chlorophyll-rich PSI reaction 
centre. State transitions provide a further regulatory 
dimension unique to cyanobacteria: phycobilisome 
migration between PSII and PSI redistributes excitation 
energy on timescales of seconds to minutes, allowing 
the cell to balance photosystem loading before 
transcriptional or proteolytic remodeling is engaged 
(Steiner et al. 2026). In eukaryotic microalgae, PSII–LHCII 
connectivity is regulated through LHCII phosphorylation, 
a process triggered by plastoquinol accumulation under 
excess light or elevated CO 2. Phosphorylation promotes 
dissociation of LHCII from PSII, thereby reducing PSII 
quantum yield and redistributing antenna excitation 
toward PSI. This mechanism functionally resembles 
a state transition, although it is mediated through covalent 
modification of antenna proteins rather than through 
the physical migration of a soluble light-harvesting 
complex (Scarponi et al. 2025). Chemical stress and metal 
toxicity in microalgae produce PSII damage accompanied 
by chlorophyll loss, paralleling the cyanobacterial pattern, 
but without the ordered phycobilisome disassembly 
sequence; instead, loss of Chl b and peripheral LHCII 
components precedes core antenna degradation, reflecting 
the structural logic of the LHC system. Across both 
groups, the consistent pattern is that PSII antenna capacity 
is reduced before reaction-centre integrity is lost, whether 
through carotenoid-dependent quenching, antenna protein 
remodeling, or phycobilisome proteolysis – a convergent 
response that limits singlet-oxygen production at the cost 
of reduced photochemical throughput (Sharma et al. 2025).
Beyond changes in chlorophyll content, some 
phototrophs exhibit qualitative remodeling of chlorophyll 
composition, enabling spectral tuning of light absorption 
and maintenance of photosynthetic function under 
spectrally constrained environments. Chlorophyll f is 
the most illustrative example: it extends the operational 
range of oxygenic photosynthesis into the far-red region 
above 700 nm and makes available photons that are poorly 
usable for most oxygenic phototrophs (Tros et al. 2021). 
It was described in cyanobacteria associated with Shark 
Bay stromatolites and is considered a rare biochemical 
innovation that is functionally relevant under pronounced 
spectral filtering. The producer of chlorophyll f was 
identified as Halomicronema hongdechloris (Chen et al. 
2012). The spectral shift is linked to a formyl group at 
the C-2 position and allows chlorophyll f to be classified 
as 2-formyl-chlorophyll a, which is accompanied by 
pronounced photobiophysical consequences with minimal 
chemical modification of the chlorophyll scaffold.
Chlorophyll f function is associated with the far-red 
light acclimation program Far-Red Light Photoacclimation 
(FaRLiP), in which approximately 8–12.5% of cellular 
chlorophyll a is reversibly replaced by chlorophyll f. 
The remodeling affects not only antenna regions, but also 
components of both photosystems, including the primary 
electron donors. Importantly, FaRLiP is an inducible mode 
and differs from constitutive far-red photosynthesis based 
on chlorophyll d in Acaryochloris (Patel et al. 2025). 
As a result, a mixed system is formed with dominance 
of chlorophyll a and a functionally critical contribution 
of chlorophyll f despite its low abundance, sometimes 
with the presence of trace amounts of chlorophyll d 
(Antonaru et al. 2025). This principle emphasizes that 
spectral adaptation is implemented through remodeling 
of photosynthetic apparatus architecture rather than 
through simple accumulation of a single pigment. 
The ecological logic of FaRLiP matches conditions in 
which the visible part of the spectrum is attenuated due to 
shading, competition among phototrophs, or light passage 
through absorbing matrices. Such systems include dense 
microbial mats and stromatolites, shaded rock surfaces, 
soil crusts, and some thermal habitats, where the far-red 
range becomes relatively enriched (Murray et al. 2022).

genetic cluster that includes approximately 15–21 genes

and metagenomic studies, identification of far-red light-
adapted cyanobacteria most often uses detection of 
the gene chlF encoding chlorophyll f synthase (Di Stefano 
et al. 2024). Proteomic data show that upon a shift to far-
red light, the primary remodeling occurs in photosynthetic 
complexes, whereas central metabolic pathways remain 
relatively stable, which is consistent with a narrowly 
directed program of spectral acclimation (Chen et al. 
2019). Transcription of the FaRLiP cluster is controlled 
by the dedicated far-red-sensing phosphorelay system 
RfpA/RfpB/RfpC. The phytochrome-class photoreceptor 
RfpA detects far-red light and initiates signal transduction 
through autophosphorylation, followed by phosphotransfer 
to the shuttle protein RfpC and subsequently to 
the response regulator RfpB. Once phosphorylated, 
RfpB activates transcription of FaRLiP-associated genes, 
thereby establishing one of the best-characterized light-
quality-responsive regulatory programmes described in 
cyanobacteria (Ho et al. 2017).
An important implication is the diagnostic value of 
chlorophyll f as a marker of far-red light adaptation. 
Its synthesis is induced by far-red wavelengths and 
reverses upon return to white light, enabling detection of 
FaRLiP activity in cultures and environmental samples 
(MacGregor-Chatwin et al. 2022). Taken together, 
the strongest evidence indicates that chlorophyll-based 
acclimation depends primarily on antenna organization, 
pigment distribution between antennae and reaction 
centers, and PSI–PSII stoichiometry rather than on total 
chlorophyll content alone. Consequently, changes in bulk 
chlorophyll cannot independently distinguish regulated 
acclimation from pigment degradation and photodamage. 
Although antenna resizing and FaRLiP are well-established 
mechanisms, direct causal links between ROS signaling 
and the transcriptional control of individual chlorophyll-
biosynthesis genes remain less clearly demonstrated 
across taxa. Future studies should therefore combine 
pigment composition with antenna cross-section, PSI/PSII 
performance, repair kinetics, and oxidative-damage 
markers.
Carotenoid system of photoprotection and redox
buffering under stress factors
Carotenoids represent one of the most functionally 
versatile pigment classes in microalgae and cyanobacteria. 
Their role is not limited to auxiliary light harvesting, 
because carotenoids account for a major fraction of 
the molecular protection of the photosynthetic apparatus 
against the consequences of excess excitation (García-
Oneto et al. 2024, Mostafa and Hashem 2025). 
By chemical criteria, carotenoids are typically subdivided 
into carotenes, including α-carotene and β-carotene, and 
xanthophylls, including lutein and violaxanthin, reflecting 
the presence of oxygen-containing groups and differences 
in membrane distribution and functional contributions to 
photoprotection and redox balance (Kolašinac et al. 2021). 
Within light-harvesting complexes, carotenoids participate 
in energy transfer, and as excitation pressure increases, 
they support dissipation of excess energy, quenching of 
chlorophyll triplet states, and neutralization of radical 
species via electron transfer, radical adduct formation, 
and hydrogen atom transfer. For correct interpretation of 
stress responses, it is useful to distinguish carotenoids 
that are constitutively associated with photosynthetic 
complexes from accumulation-type carotenoids that 
are stress-induced. Primary carotenoids are integral to 
antennae and the microenvironment of reaction centers 
and directly contribute to matching light harvesting to 
the capacity for photochemical energy use. Secondary 
carotenoids accumulate under prolonged adverse exposure 
and function as optical and redox reserves, often being 
deposited in specialized intracellular structures (Shi et al. 
2020).
In green algae, experimental reduction of antenna size 
improved light-use efficiency and biomass productivity, 
indicating that pigment organization directly affects the 
distribution of absorbed energy within the photosynthetic 
apparatus (Cazzaniga et al. 2014, Negi et al. 2020). Under 
excess light, photoprotective dissipation also helps limit PSI 
photoinhibition and the associated risk of reactive oxygen 
species formation (Takagi et al. 2019). In cyanobacteria, 
carotenoid-triggered quenching in phycobilisomes has 
been shown experimentally to divert excitation away 
from both photosystems (Rakhimberdieva et al. 2010). 
In this sense, pigment-dependent energy dissipation 
should be viewed not only as photoprotection, but also 
as part of the regulation of electron flow. This distinction 
helps explain why the total carotenoid pool does not show 
a universal increase under higher irradiance. In a study on 
Pseudoneochloris marina, carotenoid content decreased 
as light intensity increased (Gonçalves et al. 2019). Such 
results indicate that response direction is determined not 
only by light load, but also by exposure duration, by which 
carotenoid subclasses are included in the total estimate, 
and by how antenna complex composition changes during 
acclimation.
Notably, carotenoid regulation is often branch-
specific and reflects functional partitioning between light 
harvesting and photoprotection. In the euryhaline microalga 
Tetraselmis sp. CTP4, different branches of the carotenoid 
pathway respond in opposite directions to changes in 
irradiance. β-carotene content was up to threefold higher 
under low light at 33 µmol(photon) m –2 s –1 than under 
higher irradiance, which is consistent with its contribution 
to maintaining efficient energy capture under photon 
limitation. In contrast, lutein increased by approximately 
1.5-fold under high light at 170–280 µmol(photon) m–2 s–1, 
which corresponds to strengthened nonphotochemical 
protection. At the same time, neoxanthin and violaxanthin 
showed levels that were 2–3-fold higher under low light, 
indicating that even within the xanthophyll pool, regulation 
can reflect distinct functional demands depending on 
the photobiophysical regime. These data emphasize that 
interpreting total carotenoids as a single indicator of 
stress tolerance is often insufficient without considering 
the specific carotenoid profile and the conditions under

photoprotection is associated with xanthophyll cycles, 
which set the potential for nonphotochemical quenching 
(NPQ) and limit photoinhibition. In higher plants, 
the xanthophyll cycle is triggered by lumen acidification 
and, via violaxanthin de-epoxidase (VDE), converts 
violaxanthin to zeaxanthin through the V AZ cycle 
(Ruban 2016). The cycle is initiated when light-driven 
proton pumping acidifies the thylakoid lumen to pH 6.2, 
at which point VDE undergoes protonation-dependent 
conformational opening, dimerizes, and associates with 
MGDG-rich domains of the lumenal membrane surface to 
gain access to its lipophilic substrates; the enzyme then 
catalyzes stepwise de-epoxidation utilizing ascorbate as 
the obligate electron donor (Jahns et al. 2009, Fufezan 
et al. 2012). The zeaxanthin produced is incorporated 
into LHC complexes, where it modifies pigment–protein 
interactions to activate NPQ pathways that dissipate excess 
excitation energy as heat, while simultaneously protecting 
thylakoid membrane lipids from peroxidation. Upon 
a decrease in irradiance and the consequent rise in lumenal 
pH, stromal ZEP catalyzes the back-reaction [zeaxanthin 
(Z)-antheraxanthin (A)-violaxanthin (V)] using NADPH 
and O2 as co-substrates. The intrinsically slower kinetics 
of ZEP relative to VDE ensure a controlled relaxation 
of the NPQ state, preventing premature restoration of 
full antenna absorption capacity before irradiance has 
sufficiently declined, which collectively enhances NPQ 
and reduces reactive oxygen species formation (Berne et al. 
2018, Zhou et al. 2020). In microalgae, the contribution of 
this cycle is taxon-specific. In diatoms, NPQ is linked to 
the diadinoxanthin (DD) diatoxanthin (Dt) cycle, in which 
DD is de-epoxidized to Dt by a VDE-type diadinoxanthin 
de-epoxidase that can operate at higher lumenal pH 
than canonical VDE, including in model species such 
as Phaeodactylum tricornutum and Thalassiosira 
pseudonana, whereas in chlorophytes alternative 
de-epoxidases may occur and zeaxanthin can have a more 
pronounced antioxidant role (Jakob et al. 2001, Girolomoni 
et al. 2020). In both cases, shortening the lifetime of excited 
states and reducing effective absorption of PSII decrease 
the probability of singlet oxygen formation and thereby 
limit the development of photooxidative cascades (Giossi 
et al. 2020). When comparing studies, it is important to 
consider that NPQ measurements alone do not resolve 
the mechanism if data on the sizes of relevant xanthophyll 
pools and interconversion kinetics are lacking, because 
the limiting factor may be pigment availability, enzymatic 
capacity, or antenna architecture.
Carotenoids also perform structural and protective 
functions that are particularly relevant under salt 
stress, temperature extremes, and nutrient limitation. 
Located in thylakoid membranes and pigment–
protein complexes, carotenoids influence the physical 
properties of the membrane phase and help maintain 
supercomplex integrity. This effect is strengthened when 
lipid composition is remodeled and membrane viscosity 
changes in a coordinated manner (Joshi et al. 2023, Song 
et al. 2024). Under nutrient limitation, when degradation 
of thylakoid lipids and proteins increases photosystem 
vulnerability, carotenoid-dependent excitation quenching 
and ROS detoxification can slow the development 
of photoinhibition and support functional membrane 
organization (Muzzopappa and Kirilovsky 2020). Thus, 
the carotenoid profile reflects an indicator linked not only 
to photochemical parameters, but also to the stability of 
membrane architecture.
A distinct form of carotenoid adaptation is stress-
induced hyperaccumulation of secondary carotenoids, 
well documented for astaxanthin in Haematococcus 
pluvialis and β-carotene in Dunaliella salina. In both 
systems, high light and nutrient deficiency activate 
carotenogenesis coupled to triacylglycerol accumulation, 
with lipid droplets serving as the primary depot for 
carotenoid sequestration (Aditi et al. 2025). In H. pluvialis, 
this response is driven by transcriptional upregulation 
of MEP-pathway genes, including DXS, CMS, CMK, 
HDS, and HDR, which supply isoprenoid precursors 
for the sequential oxidative conversion of β-carotene 
to astaxanthin: β-carotene ketolase (BKT, encoded by 
stress-induced paralogs bkt1, bkt2, and bkt3, upregulated 
up to 52-fold) catalyzes ketolation through echinenone 
to canthaxanthin, while β-carotene hydroxylase (CrtR-b, 
with the additionally characterized CrtR-b2 isoform) 
drives parallel hydroxylation through β-cryptoxanthin 
and zeaxanthin, with convergent intermediates yielding 
adonixanthin and finally astaxanthin (Liang et al. 2016). 
In D. salina, β-carotene accumulates within chloroplast 
lipid globules (plastoglobuli) at concentrations reaching 
up to 10% of dry mass under high-light and nutrient 
stress, with expansion of this storage compartment tightly 
correlated with de novo pathway upregulation and fatty 
acid remodeling; plastoquinone-derived isoprenoid 
antioxidants, including plastochromanol-8, contribute 
to redox buffering and oxidative stress protection in 
this membrane environment, though their role as direct 
coordinators of β-carotene partitioning to plastoglobuli 
has not been experimentally established (Kim et al. 2024). 
The practice of two-stage cultivation that includes biomass 
growth under favorable conditions followed by controlled 
stress induction reflects a trade-off between cell division 
and redirection of metabolic fluxes into protective reserves, 
which increases final pigment productivity (Gao et al. 
2017). To compare responses across taxa, Table 2 provides 
examples of carotenoid shifts under different stressors, 
indicating response direction and functional interpretation 
at the level of photoprotection and redox buffering.
Carotenoids are involved not only in effector, but also in 
regulatory layers of the stress response. They are linked to 
signaling cascades, including via hormone-like derivatives 
and via redox-dependent tuning of carotenogenesis (Sun 
et al. 2022). In Dunaliella salina, ROS associated with 
the activity of NADPH oxidase homologs were linked to 
induction of β-carotene biosynthesis under high irradiance, 
which supports the view of ROS as regulatory signals 
that set the scale of pigment investment when conditions 
change (Kim et al. 2024). As a result, the carotenoid 
phenotype is shaped as an outcome of feedback 
between excitation pressure, redox state, and metabolic 
fluxes, rather than as an isolated marker of exposure.

At the transcriptional level, oxidative signals reach 
carotenogenic promoters through a 2-Cys peroxiredoxin 
(2-Cys Prx)-mediated redox relay: 2-Cys Prx reacts 
rapidly with H 2O2, undergoes an oxidation-dependent 
conformational change, and transfers oxidative equivalents 
to thioredoxin (Trx), thereby modulating the redox state 
of Trx-dependent transcriptional regulators (Winterbourn

upregulation of psy (phytoene synthase), pds (phytoene 
desaturase), and bkt (β-carotene ketolase) observed 
in Haematococcus pluvialis under high light, nsPEF 
treatment, and chemical oxidative inducers, although 
direct experimental evidence that oxidized 2-Cys Prx 
interacts with or modulates transcription specifically 
from psy, pds, or bkt promoters in this organism has 
not yet been reported (Mallén-Ponce et al. 2024). 
A complementary post-translational layer is provided 
by the ORANGE (Or) protein, a plastid-localized 
chaperone that stabilizes phytoene synthase independently 
of transcript abundance, ensuring that carotenoid flux 
increases before full transcriptional upregulation is 
established (Kumari et al. 2020).
Overall, the most firmly established carotenoid 
functions are xanthophyll-cycle-dependent energy 
dissipation, chlorophyll-triplet quenching, and protection 
against oxidative damage. By contrast, total carotenoid 
content is often an ambiguous stress indicator because 
individual carotenoid branches may respond in opposite 
directions and on different timescales. Stress-induced 
hyperaccumulation of astaxanthin or β-carotene 
represents prolonged metabolic reallocation rather than 
the rapid photoprotective response mediated by primary 
xanthophylls. Regulatory connections involving ROS, 
2-Cys peroxiredoxin, and the ORANGE protein remain 
promising but are not equally supported by direct evidence 
in all microalgal taxa. Pigment-resolved and time-
resolved analyses are therefore required for mechanistic 
interpretation.
Phycobiliproteins and plasticity of the light-harvesting 
antenna under stress conditions
Phycobiliproteins (PBPs) represent a specialized class 
of photopigments that largely determine acclimation and 
resilience strategies in cyanobacteria and in some algal 
lineages. In contrast to membrane-bound chlorophylls 
and carotenoids, PBPs are water-soluble chromoproteins 
that contain open-chain tetrapyrrole chromophores 
(phycobilins), a structural organization that defines 
their spectral properties and functional role in light 
harvesting (Dumay and Morançais 2016). This molecular 
organization also confers high structural flexibility to 
the phycobiliprotein antenna and enables rapid adjustments 
of its quantitative parameters and functional properties in 
response to changes in irradiance, spectral regime, and 
chemical stressors (Stadnichuk 2025).

Phycobilisomes are not restricted to cyanobacteria 
but also constitute major peripheral antenna systems in 
red algae and glaucophytes. In red algae, the antenna is 
generally enriched in phycoerythrin, with phycocyanin 
occupying intermediate positions and allophycocyanin 
forming the core that transfers excitation toward 
the photosystems. Changes in the PE ratio, bilin composition, 
phycobilisome size, and coupling to the photosystems 
enable acclimation to variations in irradiance and spectral 
quality. Under low-light or spectrally filtered conditions, 
an increased contribution of phycoerythrin enhances 
the capture of green and blue-green photons. Red algae lack 
the cyanobacterial Orange Carotenoid Protein-dependent 
quenching system and instead employ lineage-dependent 
photoprotective mechanisms, including phycobilisome 
mobility and state transitions in some mesophilic 
species, partial energetic decoupling of the antenna, and 
ΔpH-dependent excitation quenching associated 
predominantly with the PSII core antenna.
Glaucophytes retain cyanobacteria-like phycobilisomes 
within their plastids, or cyanelles, with phycocyanin 
and allophycocyanin serving as the principal antenna 
pigments in well-studied species such as Cyanophora 
paradoxa. These antennae transfer excitation to both 
PSII and PSI. In C. paradoxa, redox-dependent state 
transitions and redistribution of phycobilisome excitation 
between the two photosystems represent major short-term 
mechanisms for limiting excitation imbalance, whereas 
long-term acclimation to different light spectra involves 
adjustment of the phycobilisome-to-photosystem ratio and 
modification of energy-transfer pathways. Thus, although 
phycobilisome-based light harvesting is conserved 
among cyanobacteria, red algae, and glaucophytes, 
the mechanisms used to regulate antenna excitation and 
prevent photodamage are strongly lineage-specific (Kaňa 
et al. 2014, Misumi and Sonoike 2017, Green 2019, Fang 
et al. 2022, V oerman et al. 2022, Ueno and Akimoto 2024).
A well-characterized mechanism is complementary 
chromatic acclimation (CCA), in which the bilin-bearing 
phytochrome-class sensor kinase RcaE detects light 
quality by reversible photoswitching between green-
absorbing Pg and red-absorbing Pr states. The signal is 
then transmitted through a multistep RcaE/RcaF/RcaC 
phosphorelay, ultimately regulating transcription through 
the DNA-binding response regulator RcaC. Under red 
light, phosphorylated RcaC activates transcription of 
the inducible cpcB2A2 operon through L-box elements, 
thereby promoting PC synthesis. Under green light, 
dephosphorylated RcaC releases repression of cpeC, 
which initiates CpeR-mediated induction of cpeBA and 
leads to PE apoprotein accumulation (Ariyanti et al. 2021, 
Espinoza-Corral et al. 2024). This reciprocal regulatory 
pattern, in which red light favors PC accumulation 
whereas green light favors PE accumulation, was initially 
demonstrated in Fremyella diplosiphon and later confirmed 
in Calothrix and other filamentous cyanobacteria. These 
findings support CCA as a conserved strategy for spectral 
acclimation in cyanobacteria (Terauchi et al. 2004, Prabha 
et al. 2023). These responses include adjustments to 
photon limitation and spectral shifts that affect excitation 
distribution within the antenna system.
Under low irradiance, enhanced PE synthesis in 
Rhodomonas salina is viewed as a mechanism that maintains 
photosynthetic efficiency under energy limitation, when 
higher light collection efficiency compensates for reduced 
photon flux (Xie et al. 2021). In addition, PE has been 
associated with antioxidant effects that may increase 
tolerance to oxidative and temperature stress (Hatha and 
Sumayya 2023). Notably, thermophilic cyanobacteria 
from hot springs produce substantially more chlorophyll a, 
carotenoids, and phycocyanin than mesophilic strains 
(Jaiswal et al. 2024). This example indicates that 
PE-containing complexes can retain functional and applied 
relevance under regimes where temperature becomes 
a defining element of the stress profile.
In scenarios accompanied by excess excitation energy, 
the role of PBPs shifts from compensating energy deficit 
to limiting photodestructive consequences of overload. 
High irradiance, nutrient imbalance, and the presence of 
pollutants increase the risk of photoinhibition and reactive 
oxygen species formation, especially in organisms with 
a large antenna. Under these conditions, adaptive value lies 
in controlling energy transfer at the antenna level, where 
part of the excitation is redistributed or dissipated before 
reaching reaction centers, thereby reducing the probability 
of initiating photooxidative cascades (Grettenberger et al. 
2024, van Stokkum et al. 2025). Further evidence for 
functional plasticity of the phycobiliprotein system 
is provided by reports of APC variants that absorb in 
a more far-red region, expanding possible trajectories of 
energy redistribution in specialized cyanobacteria adapted 
to shaded and spectrally filtered habitats (Soulier and 
Bryant 2023). These findings indicate that PBPs function 
as a regulated antenna layer capable of modulating 
the efficiency and direction of excitation transfer in 
response to changing environmental constraints.
PBPs are also integrated into stress responses through 
redox-associated effects and the metabolic economy of 
the cell. PC and PE exhibit antioxidant activity and may 
contribute to maintaining cellular redox homeostasis under 
oxidative pressure. In experiments involving heavy metals, 
xenobiotics, microplastics, and elevated temperature, 
changes in PBP content often correlate with tolerance, yet 
the direction of the effect depends on exposure intensity 
and duration. A biphasic pattern in which PBPs are 
maintained or increase at early stress stages, but undergo 
selective degradation under prolonged or strong exposure, 
is consistent with a trade-off strategy. Within this logic, 
PBPs initially support energy transfer and redox buffering, 
and are later mobilized as a nitrogen and carbon source 
during a transition toward resource reallocation (Kesheri 
et al. 2021). Thus, shifts in the phycobiliprotein pool reflect 
not only photobiophysical tuning, but also a regulated

balance between preserving photosynthetic function and 
sustaining metabolic resilience.
The structural implementation of PBP functions 
is linked to their assembly into large light-harvesting 
complexes, phycobilisomes, which couple the phycobili-
protein antenna to photosystems. These assemblies 
are characterized by large molecular size and by high 
architectural variability depending on environmental 
conditions (Zheng et al. 2021). Canonically, APC forms 
central antenna components, whereas PC and PE occupy 
more peripheral positions. Linker proteins define complex 
geometry and strongly influence energy transfer efficiency 
(Espinoza-Corral et al. 2024). Changes in light intensity, 
spectrum, and nutrient availability drive remodeling of 
antenna size, PBP composition, and expression of linker 
components, which alters energy partitioning between PSI 
and PSII (Kirilovsky and Büchel 2019). Experiments with 
mutations in linker components such as ApcE show that 
disrupting antenna attachment to the thylakoid membrane 
leads to redistribution of excitation energy, which supports 
a regulatory role of phycobiliprotein organization in 
controlling photosynthetic fluxes (Zlenko et al. 2019). 
Phycobiliprotein gene expression in cyanobacteria 
is subject to global nitrogen-responsive transcriptional 
control centred on NtcA, a CAP/CRP-family regulator 
that binds the palindromic consensus sequence TGTA-
(N8)-TACA within promoters of nitrogen-assimilation 
genes, including glnA, and co-activates operons such as 
the nitrate assimilation nir cluster in concert with NtcB 
(Jiang et al. 2000). Under conditions of nitrogen sufficiency, 
NtcA represses the GS-inactivating factor genes gifA 
and gifB, maintaining glutamine synthetase activity 
and nitrogen flux; as nitrogen status deteriorates, NtcA-
dependent transcriptional reprogramming contributes to 
the downregulation of biosynthetic capacity, coordinated 
with the NblS–NblR cascade that drives phycobilisome 
degradation (García-Domínguez et al. 2000). Although 
NtcA recognition motifs have been identified upstream of 
the cpcBA, cpeBA, and apcAB operons, direct evidence 
demonstrating NtcA binding to these promoters and 
activation of their transcription remains limited. Similarly, 
while 2-oxoglutarate (2-OG) is widely recognized as a key 
signal of carbon–nitrogen imbalance and an activator of 
NtcA, its role as the immediate trigger of phycobiliprotein 
gene expression has not been experimentally confirmed 
in the studies examined here. Therefore, the proposed 
regulatory relationship warrants further experimental 
validation (Luque et al. 2001). The proteolytic arm 
of this regulatory network operates through the NblS–
NblR–NblA cascade. NblS, a membrane-associated 
PAS-domain histidine kinase, integrates high-irradiance 
and macronutrient-deprivation signals and transmits 
bleaching cues through interactions with response 
regulators RpaB and SrrA; nblS mutants fail to regulate 
hliA, psbA, and cpcBA expression and cannot degrade 
phycobilisomes under nutrient stress (van Waasbergen 
et al. 2002). NblR promotes nblA transcription and is 
essential for phycobilisome degradation. Its regulatory 
activity, however, appears to be phosphorylation-
independent, as replacement of Asp57 with Ala preserves 
normal nblA induction and bleaching. Under nitrogen 
stress, full induction of nblA requires coordinated regulation 
by NblR and NtcA, thereby linking phycobilisome 
degradation to the NtcA-centered network that senses 
cellular nitrogen status (Kato et al. 2008). NblA1/NblA2 
then act as an obligate heterodimeric proteolytic adaptor, 
binding phycocyanin β-subunits within peripheral rods 
through conserved N- and C-terminal motifs while 
simultaneously recruiting ClpC to deliver rod substrates 
for degradation, producing temporally ordered antenna 
dismantling in which PC-containing rods are selectively 
eliminated before NblA-dependent APC core degradation 
proceeds. Cyanobacteria deficient in nblA1 and nblA2 
retain intact phycobilisomes during nitrogen starvation, 
enter a nondividing dormant state, and exhibit pronounced 
susceptibility to photoinhibition. This phenotype 
underscores the functional necessity of regulated antenna 
downsizing under conditions of sustained excitation 
pressure (Karradt et al. 2008).
Across oxygenic phototrophs, similar nonphoto-
chemical quenching phenotypes can arise from 
fundamentally different antenna-control modules, and this 
distinction is essential when cyanobacterial responses are 
compared with those of plants and green algae.
In plants, the dominant energy-dependent NPQ 
component is driven by lumen acidification: ΔpH 
formation under excess light conditions has been 
experimentally linked to activation of NPQ (Steen et al. 
2020), while mechanistic analysis shows that this process 
depends on pH-sensitive antenna regulation (Nicol and 
Croce 2021). Low pH triggers a PsbS conformational 
response (Krishnan-Schmieden et al. 2021), consistent 
with structural determinants resolved for PsbS (Fan et al. 
2015), and promotes PSII–antenna interaction changes 
associated with energy dissipation (Correa-Galvis 
et al. 2016), while xanthophyll-cycle pigments tune 
the amplitude and kinetics of quenching under fluctuating 
light (Steen et al. 2020). 
In green algae, lumen acidification also gates NPQ 
(Dinc et al. 2016), but pH sensing and dissipation 
are mechanistically integrated into LHCSR proteins 
(Ballottari et al. 2016). LHCSR1/LHCSR3 impose 
pH-dependent dissipation in vivo (Dinc et al. 2016, Troiano 
et al. 2021), and zeaxanthin can provide an additional, 
partly independent quenching route. Thus, convergent 
photoprotective outcomes arise from fundamentally 
different antenna architectures and molecular control 
points across oxygenic phototrophs.

a molecular basis for OCP-mediated quenching (Liguori 
et al. 2024, Sauer et al. 2024). The contribution of this 
mechanism increases under high irradiance and oxidative 
stress, when the probability of photoinhibition and ROS 
load rises, and experimental studies show that higher 
OCP levels enhance antenna-dependent photoprotection 
(Tiwari et al. 2025). Increased sensitivity of OCP-deficient 
strains to photoinhibition and accelerated phycobiliprotein 
degradation further support the view that the OCP module 
is a critical element of antenna stabilization under stress 
exposure (Niziński et al. 2025).
Quantitative in vivo studies show that OCP-dependent 
quenching of phycobilisomes is not limited to a decrease 
in fluorescence, but leads to a measurable redistribution 
of excitation energy reaching the photosystems. OCP 
activation reduces the effective absorption cross-section of 
PSII and modifies Q A redox kinetics, indicating changes 
in excitation pressure within the photosynthetic apparatus. 
Importantly, at physiological OCP levels, this regulation 
involves only part of the antenna, with approximately half 
of the phycobilisomes associated with PSII participating, 
which sets an upper limit to antenna-based energy 
dissipation (Protasova 2021).
Changes in OCP abundance demonstrate that 
cyanobacterial photoprotection is constrained by a balance 
between energy dissipation and photosynthetic efficiency. 
Elevated OCP levels strengthen thermal dissipation, 
suppress singlet oxygen formation, and facilitate PSII 
repair under high-light stress (Sedoud et al. 2014, 
Takahashi et al. 2019). In contrast, excessive quenching 
decreases excitation transfer from phycobilisomes to 
PSII and consequently limits photosynthetic performance 
(Ogawa et al. 2025). On short timescales following 
illumination, OCP-dependent NPQ develops in parallel 
with adjustments in linear and cyclic electron flow, 
accompanied by measurable shifts in P 700 redox state, 
directly linking antenna regulation to electron transport 
control (Elanskaya et al. 2024). The cellular pool of OCP 
available for photoprotection is subject to multilayered 
regulatory control. In Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803, 
ocp expression is regulated by RpaB, an OmpR-family 
response regulator that functions within the NblS/
Hik33–RpaB signaling pathway and controls numerous 
genes involved in stress acclimation, photoprotection, 
and pigment metabolism. Under low-light conditions, 
phosphorylated RpaB binds HLR1 promoter motifs and 
represses target gene expression, whereas high-light 
exposure promotes RpaB dephosphorylation and rapid 
transcriptional derepression. This regulatory mechanism 
is supported by ChIP analyses and by the rapid induction 
of well-characterized RpaB target genes, including hliA 
and rpoD3 (Bilger et al. 2024). Direct evidence linking 
RpaB occupancy to the ocp promoter has not been 
reported, and post-transcriptional repression through 
the sRNA ApcZ provides an independent layer of control 
over OCP accumulation under non-stress conditions. 
Regardless, the primary mechanism by which high 
light scales OCP activity is post-translational: blue-
green photon absorption drives structural conversion of 
OCP° to OCPr, making the photoprotective response 
reactive in character and governed by direct photon 
sensing at the protein level rather than by transcriptional 
pre-amplification of the OCP pool (Salvadori et al. 
2024). The best-supported mechanisms of phycobilisome 
regulation include complementary chromatic acclimation, 
OCP-dependent excitation quenching, state transitions, 
and NblA-mediated antenna degradation. However, 
similar decreases in phycobiliprotein content may indicate 
regulated antenna downsizing, nutrient remobilization, 
or irreversible damage, and these alternatives cannot 
be distinguished from pigment measurements alone. 
Functional phycobilisome uncoupling and the distinct 
strategies of red algae, glaucophytes, and phycobilisome-
lacking cyanobacteria further demonstrate that no 
single photoprotective model applies to all lineages. 
Future studies should combine spectroscopy, antenna–
photosystem coupling measurements, photosystem 
kinetics, and oxidative-stress indicators.
Functional uncoupling of phycobilisomes from 
the photosystems represents an additional photoprotective 
mechanism in cyanobacteria. Under high-light stress, 
reduced energetic coupling limits excitation transfer from 
the antenna to the reaction centers, thereby decreasing 
excitation pressure and photodamage, although at 
the cost of reduced light-harvesting efficiency. This 
response is distinct from OCP-mediated thermal quenching, 
even though both mechanisms reduce excitation delivery 
to the photosystems (Tamary et al. 2012).
Not all cyanobacteria possess phycobilisomes. 
Prochlorococcus instead uses chlorophyll a2/b2-binding 
Pcb antenna proteins and therefore lacks phycobilisome-
dependent OCP quenching. In this genus, zeaxanthin 
accumulation contributes to photoprotection through 
antioxidant activity and possibly through a screening 
function associated with pigment located outside 
the photosynthetic thylakoid complexes. Diel increases in 
the zeaxanthin-to-chlorophyll ratio support its protective 
role under high daytime irradiance, although the precise 
cellular localization and mechanism require further 
confirmation (Claustre et al. 2002).
Scytonemin as an extracellular UV protection
mechanism in cyanobacteria
Scytonemin is a specialized photoprotective pigment of 
cyanobacteria and fundamentally differs from intracellular 
systems that regulate light harvesting or excitation

quenching within the photosynthetic apparatus. It is 
a yellow-brown lipophilic indole pigment deposited not in 
thylakoids but in the extracellular polysaccharide sheath 
of some cyanobacteria, especially in lineages inhabiting 
environments with high and prolonged solar radiation 
(Mazur and Ślesak 2025). Due to this localization, 
scytonemin performs a preventive function. It intercepts 
ultraviolet radiation before it reaches photosynthetic 
complexes and genomic DNA, thereby reducing 
the incoming dose of damaging light already at the cell 
boundary. Phylogenetically, scytonemin occurs across 
diverse cyanobacterial lineages and has long been 
recognized as a widespread protective pigment associated 
with exposure to high ultraviolet radiation (Brenowitz and 
Castenholz 1997).
The high effectiveness of this strategy is linked to 
the chemical nature of scytonemin and its optical 
properties. The scytonemin molecule is a homodimer 
built from two cyclopentyl indole fragments connected by 
a carbon–carbon bond. The conjugated ring system provides 
intense absorption from UV-A to the violet-blue region, 
approximately 325–425 nm. The absorption maximum is 
observed at around 370 nm in vivo and around 384 nm in 
organic solvents (Gao et al. 2021). Scytonemin can absorb 
up to 90% of incident UV-A, which is associated with 
reduced UV-induced inhibition of photosynthetic oxygen 
evolution (Mazur and Ślesak 2025). Although the main 
contribution is in UV-A, scytonemin can also substantially 
attenuate shorter-wavelength regions, including UV-B and 
even UV-C, which broadens its protective range under 
natural conditions where radiation spectrum and intensity 
vary over time and space (Edwards et al. 2000).
A key advantage of scytonemin is the combination 
of shielding capacity and exceptional photostability. 
Long-term irradiation experiments show that scytonemin 
persists after months of continuous UV-A exposure, with 
a degradation rate that is several orders of magnitude 
lower than that of chlorophylls (Assunção et al. 2022). 
This has direct physiological relevance, because prolonged 
episodes of high radiation often coincide with desiccation 
or other states in which energy-dependent photoprotection 
and enzymatic antioxidant systems operate in a limited 
manner. Under such regimes, a stable extracellular filter 
continues to reduce the damaging light dose without 
requiring active metabolism (Fuentes-Tristan et al. 2019).
The ecological role of scytonemin is expressed 
not only through self-shielding, but also through its 
integration into cellular stress response pathways. Its 
synthesis is induced by UV-A and can be enhanced under 
additional physicochemical stressors, including high 
salinity, elevated temperature, and desiccation, which 
indicates coordination of this protection with broader 
regulatory networks of resilience (Rastogi et al. 2015, 
Cepoi 2024). At the molecular level, UV-A induction in 
Nostoc punctiforme is regulated by the two-component 
system encoded by Npun_F1277 and Npun_F1278, 
which is located immediately upstream of the scytonemin 
biosynthetic cluster. The response regulator Npun_F1278 
is essential for scytonemin production, since deletion of 
Npun_F1278 completely abolishes its synthesis. Both 
Npun_F1277 and Npun_F1278 are co-transcribed and 
induced by UV-A, UV-B, and high visible light. A related 
UV-A-responsive pathway operates through UirS, 
an atypical membrane-bound CBCR-class photoreceptor 
that absorbs UV-A, autophosphorylates, and transfers 
the phosphoryl group to the AraC-family response regulator 
UirR, which binds DNA and regulates downstream 
transcriptional programmes; although published literature 
connects this phosphorelay to scytonemin cluster 
activation, direct experimental evidence linking UirR 
occupancy to scyABCDE promoters was not recovered in 
the primary dataset and warrants independent verification 
(Soule et al. 2009). Scytonemin accumulation is enhanced 
not only by UV radiation but also by oxidative and 
osmotic stress. Synergistic induction under combined 
UV-A and photooxidative conditions has been reported 
in Chroococcidiopsis and Scytonema , suggesting 
that scytonemin biosynthesis is regulated by multiple 
environmental inputs rather than by light signals alone. 
These observations support the existence of convergent 
regulatory pathways through which redox-related signals 
may influence the scytonemin biosynthetic programme in 
addition to the primary light-responsive two-component 
system. Although a MarR-family redox-sensing regulator 
(RosR) has been proposed as a potential mediator of 
H2O2-dependent activation, direct evidence linking 
RosR or other MarR-family regulators to scytonemin 
cluster expression in cyanobacteria remains lacking. 
Consequently, this component of the regulatory model 
requires further experimental validation (Rastogi et al. 
2014). Genera that are rich in scytonemin include Nostoc, 
Scytonema, Calothrix, Lyngbya, Anabaena, Nodularia, 
Chroococcidiopsis, Gloeocapsa, and Gloeocapsopsis 
(Casero et al. 2024).
For correct interpretation, it is important to distinguish 
scytonemin from other UV-protective metabolites of 
cyanobacteria, primarily mycosporine-like amino acids. 
These compounds are water-soluble, colorless, and 
efficiently absorb in the UV-A and UV-B regions, dissipating 
energy as heat without generating reactive oxygen species, 
and they may contribute not only to photoprotection, but 
also to antioxidant defense and osmoregulation (Peng 
et al. 2023). Scytonemin, in contrast, is lipophilic, localized 
extracellularly, and is particularly effective as an entry 
barrier, which becomes critical under dehydration and 
metabolic pause when intracellular protective mechanisms 
function in a limited manner. Therefore, these compound 
classes should be viewed not as interchangeable, but as 
potentially complementary components of a multilevel 
defense distributed between the extracellular sheath and 
intracellular compartments.
Beyond its ecological function, scytonemin attracts 
attention due to biological activity and potential applied 
value. Alongside its role as a physical UV filter, it exhibits 
antioxidant activity and can limit UV-induced DNA 
damage (Pathak et al. 2020). Effects in biomedical models 
have also been reported, including anti-inflammatory 
and antiproliferative activity, which motivates interest in 
dermatological and pharmaceutical applications (Rastogi 
et al. 2015). These observations support the idea that

Evolutionary principles of pigment system remodeling 
under changing environmental conditions
Natural selection has repeatedly remodeled pigment 
systems in algae and cyanobacteria, because pigments sit at 
the interface between energy capture and the constraints of 
oxygenic photosynthesis. Any increase in absorbed energy 
raises the risk of photodamage and redox imbalance and 
therefore makes pigment composition and organization 
a direct target of selection. In natural habitats, this selection 
operates under the combined influence of irradiance and 
photoperiod, nutrient availability, temperature, pH, and 
salinity, and toxicants, including heavy metals, pesticides, 
and emerging contaminants. Under such conditions, 
oxidative stress and disruption of redox homeostasis 
become critical constraints for photosystem stability and 
cellular resilience (Nowicka 2022). Context dependence 
is especially evident in that superficially similar pigment 
changes may reflect different biological causes. Under 
moderate stress pressure, remodeling of the pigment 
profile often performs a compensatory function and 
maintains photosynthetic apparatus performance (Foo 
et al. 2020). However, once protective thresholds are 
exceeded, a different trajectory is observed in which 
pigment pools decrease due to biosynthetic constraints 
and increasing damage to antennae by uncontrolled ROS 
(Anand et al. 2021). Therefore, interpretation of pigment 
shifts along natural gradients requires consideration not 
only of response direction, but also of load level, exposure 
duration, and the combination of factors that determine 
whether the system remains in an acclimation mode or 
transitions to a degradation mode.
The light environment is the most predictable and 
persistent source of selective gradients. Its spatial structure 
is shaped by depth, turbidity, and eutrophication, whereas 
its temporal structure reflects seasonality, diel cycles, and 
rapid irradiance fluctuations (Litchman 2000, Tsakalakis 
et al. 2022). Along these gradients, differences in 
chlorophyll ratios and in accessory pigment composition 
have evolved, because changes in light spectrum and 
intensity directly alter the balance between the need for 
light harvesting and the need to limit excitation pressure 
(Kübler and Raven 1996). More recent work further 
shows that variability in the ocean light spectrum can 
differentially favor phytoplankton with flexible pigment 
content and chromatic acclimation capacity (Mattei et al. 
2026). 
Subsequent field and laboratory studies expanded 
this framework by incorporating ultraviolet exposure, 
life-cycle stage dependence, and stressor interactions. 
Notably, ultraviolet-driven pigment responses have 
been documented in natural seasonal settings (Ha et al. 
2015), whereas combined exposure regimes, including 
interactions with light–dark cycling, can modify not 
only the magnitude of the response but also its direction 
(Aguilera et al. 2008).
Photoprotection across these gradients is supported not 
only by carotenoid mechanisms. Mycosporine-like amino 
acids and other secondary metabolites, including phenolic 
compounds, show pronounced sensitivity to ultraviolet 
regime and seasonality (Dunlap and Shick 1998, Korbee 
et al. 2005). In some cases, such compounds may also 
extend protective effects beyond the cell surface through 
release into the surrounding environment, creating 
an additional layer of ecological resilience at 
the community level (Swanson and Druehl 2002). This 
indicates that selection acts not on individual molecules, 
but on integrated strategies in which different pigment and 
metabolite classes functionally complement each other 
depending on the nature of light and chemical pressure.
Situations in which stress regimes recur consistently 
and become background conditions for stable 
differentiation among populations and strains merit special 
attention. However, the mechanistic links between short-
term phenotypic acclimation and long-term genotypic

In Haematococcus lacustris, nitrogen starvation combined

culminates in the formation of astaxanthin-enriched cysts. 
However, early protective stages at the onset of stress

not a universal response following a single regulatory 
template, but a product of selection acting on functional 
trade-offs among light harvesting, excitation limitation, 
redox stabilization, and maintenance of structural integrity 
of the photosynthetic apparatus. Recurrent general

the same pigment markers can indicate either adaptation 
or damage depending on context (Maltsev et al. 2021,

Natural models of long-term multifactor selection in 
extremophilic phototrophic microorganisms
Extremophilic phototrophs provide a window into how 
pigment systems are shaped under long-term and recurrent 
exposure to multiple factors acting simultaneously. 
In contrast to short-term acclimation, such conditions 
impose stable selection regimes in which value lies not 
in a single pigment, but in coordinated operation of light 
harvesting, photoprotection, and redox control across 
repeated cycles of damage and recovery. Therefore, 
extreme habitats serve as natural analogs of multifactor 
stress in which it is possible to trace which elements of the 
pigment apparatus become prioritized, and which recede 
while population viability is maintained (Yuan et al. 2024).
Desert biological soil crusts illustrate one of the most 
stringent scenarios of combined pressure. Cyanobacteria 
in these systems simultaneously experience high UV 
exposure, chronic desiccation with episodic rehydration, 
sharp diel and seasonal temperature fluctuations, and 
often elevated salinity. Resilience is typically provided by 
a suite of traits that includes extracellular UV-shielding 
compounds, antioxidant defenses involving carotenoids 
and enzymatic systems, and extracellular polymeric 
substances that increase water retention and contribute to 
salt tolerance (Xu et al. 2021). An additional requirement 
is the ability to enter a state of metabolic dormancy 
during desiccation and rapidly restore activity after 
wetting, a feature that has been extensively documented 
in cyanobacteria inhabiting arid environments (Williams 
2006). More recent studies further show that repeated 
cycles of desiccation and rehydration impose constraints 
on photosynthetic recovery and require stabilization 
of pigment systems and photoprotective mechanisms 
(Romero et al. 2020, Ye et al. 2021).
The ecological role of biocrusts further links 
pigment phenotypes to measurable fitness outcomes. 
Cyanobacteria act as pioneer colonizers, stabilize 
the substrate through filamentous growth and EPS, 
facilitate succession, and contribute to nitrogen and 
carbon cycling (Sepehr et al. 2019, Fattahi et al. 2020). 
Their dominance in arid landscapes indicates that the 
combination of sheath-based and pigment-based strategies, 
including UV shielding, has direct functional value under 
extreme insolation and water deficit. At the same time, 
biocrust communities show specialization. Genera such 
as Microcoleus and Coleofasciculus primarily contribute 
to structural stabilization of the substrate, whereas Nostoc 
and Scytonema are associated with nitrogen fixation and 
enhanced UV tolerance (Zhang et al. 2021, Etesami 2025). 
This pattern emphasizes that pigment adaptations are 
selected in a community context, where complementary 
strategies collectively determine ecosystem resilience.
At the level of pigment traits, arid systems show 
a recurrent evolutionary pattern consistent with the 
threshold logic discussed above. When the combination 
of UV exposure and dehydration makes photosynthesis 
especially risky, selection may favor reduced chlorophyll 
a together with stronger passive screens and protective 
molecules. In cyanobacteria from biocrusts, drought is 
often associated with lower chlorophyll a and higher 
scytonemin. In Scytonema, pronounced induction of 
scytonemin has been reported under combined drought 
and UV exposure (Pathak et al. 2022). Under moderate 
load, compensatory pigment remodeling can occur, 
whereas extreme or prolonged exposure shifts the system 
toward antenna limitation and reduction of pigment pools 
to decrease uncontrolled ROS formation. Similar threshold 
behavior is also observed in experiments with toxicants. 
Low doses sometimes maintain or increase pigment levels 
via compensatory or hormetic responses, whereas higher 
doses reduce pigments due to damage and biosynthetic 
constraints (Ismaiel and Piercey-Normore 2024, Li et al. 
2025).
Temperature extremes impose a different type of 
multifactor pressure because they change the relationship 
between energy input and energy use and thereby alter 
the probability of redox imbalance. At low temperatures, 
photochemical excitation may continue while downstream 
energy use and repair become constrained, increasing 
the risk of overexcitation and photooxidative damage, 
especially under photochilling conditions (Míguez et al. 
2017, Ermilova 2020). Comparative studies report 
that cold stress is often associated with activation of 
photoprotective cycles and changes in pigment ratios, 
including xanthophyll-cycle indices (Míguez et al. 2017).

transmembrane, HAMP, and PAS domains that detects 
low-temperature-induced rigidification of the lipid 
bilayer, initiates cold perception at the membrane – 
a mechanism confirmed by FTIR analysis and by 
the loss of cold-gene induction in desaturase-deficient 
backgrounds where membrane fluidity cannot change 
appropriately (Los and Leusenko 2025). Upon activation,

impose another stable selection regime in which osmotic 
stress is tightly linked to redox signaling and membrane 
organization and is therefore especially informative for 
understanding the evolution of carotenoid solutions. 
Dunaliella salina remains a classic model in which 
the combination of high salinity, high irradiance, and 
nutrient limitation leads to pronounced β-carotene 
accumulation (Goswami et al. 2022). Importantly, 
not only synthesis but also the mode of deposition matters. 
In Dunaliella, stress-induced β-carotene is compartmented 
in β-carotene-rich plastoglobuli/lipid droplets within the 
plastid system (Davidi et al. 2014). This storage mode 
functionally connects carotenoid accumulation with 
triacylglycerol metabolism and carbon-sink formation 
under nitrogen limitation (Bonnefond et al. 2017). Variation 
among strains is also informative. Carotenogenic and 
non-carotenogenic lineages can achieve photoprotection 
via different routes, combining β-carotene deposition with 
different degrees of reliance on xanthophyll mechanisms, 
which highlights multiple evolutionary trajectories toward 
a similar functional task (Sousa et al. 2024). For D. salina, 
links between ROS and induction of β-carotene under high 
irradiance have been described, which supports the view 
of carotenoid phenotypes as products of redox-regulated 
feedback rather than passive consequences of damage 
(Ye et al. 2008).
Metal-enriched and acidic ecosystems add an additional 
layer of multifactor pressure because they intensify 
ROS chemistry and affect metal-sensitive elements of 
the photosynthetic apparatus. Heavy metals, irrespective 
of their redox activity, cause toxicity predominantly by 
inducing oxidative stress via direct or indirect mechanisms 
(Nowicka 2022). For pigment systems, this is especially 
critical because tetrapyrroles and iron–sulfur centers are 
vulnerable targets, and their oxidative destabilization 
disrupts electron transport and amplifies redox imbalance 
via positive feedback (Asril et al. 2025). Under such 
conditions, pigments contribute to resilience through 
antioxidant and photoprotective functions, yet once 
protective capacity is exceeded, pigment loss becomes 
likely because of damage and biosynthetic limitation.
Within this set of models, Galdieria sulphuraria 
is particularly valuable because it combines tolerance 
to extreme acidity and high temperature (Thangaraj 
et al. 2011) with resistance to a range of heavy metals 
(Kharel et al. 2024). These conditions increase pressure 
on photosynthetic functions dependent on chlorophyll/
tetrapyrrole integrity and Fe–S cluster assembly, processes 
that are widely metal-sensitive and closely linked to 
photosynthetic stability. Model relevance is also linked to 
features of photosynthetic-apparatus organization that can 
support efficiency under low irradiance and, under resource 
limitation, may be accompanied by a reduced share of 
photosynthetic components. This phenotype is consistent 
with a conservative logic: under resource scarcity and high 
redox load, the system limits photosynthetic investment 
to keep photooxidation risk within tolerable bounds 
(Stadnichuk et al. 2011).
In natural extreme habitats, long-term multifactorial 
stress selects not for a high-pigment phenotype, but 
for a stable pigment-system configuration in which 
light harvesting is continuously matched to cellular 
redox capacity and thylakoid membrane state. The most 
reproducible outcome is a shift in emphasis from antenna 
maximization to risk control through radiation screening, 
switchable energy dissipation, antioxidant buffering, and 
structural stabilization. Therefore, pigment variation across 
natural gradients should be interpreted as a marker of 
selected survival strategies that distinguish compensatory 
remodeling modes from protective limitation modes, 
rather than as a universal indicator of photosynthesis 
enhancement or suppression.
Experimental evolution as a causal approach to identify 
heritable remodeling of pigment systems
Long-term evolution experiments (LTEE) and adaptive 
laboratory evolution (ALE) provide a methodologically 
rigorous opportunity to distinguish short-term acclimation 
of pigment systems from heritable adaptation that is fixed 
across generations. In natural gradients of combined 
stress, light regime, nutrient availability, temperature, 
salinity, and toxicants often change jointly; therefore, 
observed changes in pigments and photobiophysical 
parameters often remain correlational and allow multiple 
interpretations. In ALE and LTEE, selective pressure is

predefined and maintained over long periods, which makes 
it possible to test causal links between a stress regime, 
pigment phenotype dynamics, and trait stability after 
re-adaptation or transfer between conditions (Sun et al. 
2018, LaPanse et al. 2021).
A key advantage of ALE and LTEE is their temporal 
resolving power. Early phases are dominated by rapid 
physiological responses, including remodeling of light-
harvesting architecture and changes in nonphotochemical 
quenching (NPQ), comprising the fast-reversing energy-
dependent component (q E; ΔpH/xanthophyll-dependent), 
state transitions (q T; LHCII phosphorylation via STN7/
STT7 kinase), photoinhibitory quenching (q I; slow-
reversing, linked to D1 turnover), and the PsbS-independent 
zeaxanthin-dependent component (q Z), whereas at later 
stages under sustained stress, stable changes in regulation 
and metabolic coupling can become fixed, appearing 
as stable pigment ratios and reproducible profiles of 
individual pigments. For this reason, causal interpretation 
requires no single time points but comparable time series, 
replicate lines, and standardized tests of reversibility 
(Lakeman et al. 2009, Zhao and Huang 2021). This logic is 
summarized in Fig. 2, which shows how a defined stressor 
is sequentially translated into proximate constraints on 
photosynthesis, triggers remodeling of pigment modules, 
and is then fixed as a heritable phenotype in ALE and 
LTEE designs.
Empirical LTEE outcomes under high irradiance 
indicate that stable phenotypes often arise not through 
simple increases in the total pigment pool, but through 
optimization of light harvesting relative to photodamage 
risk. Selection under extremely high irradiance up to 
3,000 µmol(photon) m –2 s –1 led to tolerant lines, which 
indicates a directed nature of long-term light pressure 
(He et al. 2025). In the study by Bonnefond et al. (2022), 
after 160 d of selection under dynamic light, productivity 
increased by 77% together with an 85% reduction in 
antenna cross-section and the appearance of 2,700 new 
alleles, which is consistent with selection for architectural 
solutions that reduce excitation pressure. Complementarily, 
Russell et al. (2022) showed a 1.9-fold increase in 
β-carotene production in Tetraselmis tetrathele under high 
light, which corresponds to strengthened photoprotective 
carotenoid buffering under chronic photooxidative load.

are highly informative as early stress responses; however, 
without a multigenerational selection framework, they 
should not be interpreted as evidence of heritable pigment 
adaptation.
Metal stress is particularly informative for causal 
analysis because it directly perturbs photosynthetic 
electron transport, enhances ROS generation, and 
destabilizes pigment homeostasis. Under these conditions, 
pigment metabolism represents part of redox homeostasis 
rather than only a damage target (Tripathi and Poluri 
2021, Nowicka 2022). At the same time, declines in 
pigment content do not necessarily scale linearly with PSII 
functional impairment, because toxic metals can affect 
different components of the photosynthetic apparatus 
with unequal sensitivity, and fluorescence parameters 
may change even when some pigment pools remain 
comparatively stable (Kumar et al. 2014, Dong et al. 
2022). Nanda et al. (2021) reported that under exposure 
to Cd(II) and Pb(II), carotenoids could be relatively better 
preserved and, in some settings, could increase at later time 
points compared with chlorophylls, while pigment ratios 
changed over time in line with activation of antioxidant 
programs. Such examples indicate that long-term selection 
and temporal dynamics are necessary for interpreting 
pigment phenotypes as elements of stable adaptation.
Current evidence strongly supports scytonemin as 
a stable extracellular barrier against chronic UV-A 
exposure, particularly when dehydration or metabolic 
limitation restricts energy-dependent protection. Its 
broader regulation by oxidative, osmotic, and temperature 
stress is plausible, but several proposed transcriptional 
and redox-control pathways still lack direct experimental 
validation. We therefore consider scytonemin a long-
term protective investment that complements, rather than 
replaces, rapid intracellular quenching and antioxidant 
mechanisms. Important unresolved questions include 
its biosynthetic cost, induction threshold, ecological trade-
offs, and quantitative contribution relative to mycosporine-
like amino acids under combined environmental stresses.
Convergent functional trajectories of pigment system 
remodeling based on ALE and LTEE
ALE and LTEE data allow discussion of recurrent 
trajectories of pigment-system remodeling as convergent 
solutions that arise under chronic stressor exposure. 
Convergence here refers to the functional logic of 
adaptation rather than to a fixed pigment profile across 
taxa, because under similar selective pressure different 
biochemical implementations can achieve equivalent 
regulation of excitation pressure while maintaining 
acceptable levels of photooxidative risk.
In a thermally driven ALE of Picochlorum sp. 
BPE23 conducted in a turbidostat under diel light and 
temperature cycles with stepwise increases of the daytime 
temperature peak up to 49°C, direct measurements of 
chlorophylls and carotenoids were not performed, and the 
pigment component was assessed via the specific optical 
absorption cross-section over 400–700 nm. By the end 
of selection, this value decreased from 221.9 ± 18.9 to 
143.4 ± 23.3 m2 kg–1, which was interpreted as a regulated 
reduction of light absorption under thermal stress together 
with an increased upper temperature limit for growth in 
the isolated clones (Barten et al. 2022). 
In an ALE of Chlorella vulgaris under stepwise 
hypersalinity in serial transfer cycles, selected lines E2 
and E2.5 were characterized by an approximately 2.2-fold 
increase in total lipid content while maintaining growth 
and biomass relative to the control. Total chlorophyll in 
the best lines did not show a pronounced decline, and 
oxidative stress markers did not indicate increased lipid 
peroxidation, which is consistent with resilience forming 
as a systemic metabolic and redox adaptation rather than 
as an isolated remodeling of the pigment pool (Varunraj 
et al. 2023).
In a high-light ALE of Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803, 
selection across increasing irradiance levels produced 
populations able to grow under light intensities that 
were lethal for the ancestral laboratory type, including 
population-level resilience at 3,000 µmol(photon) m –2 s–1. 
The genetic architecture of the trait was polygenic, and 
causal testing of individual reconstructed mutations 
showed increased growth and carotenoid content at 
700 µmol(photon) m –2 s –1, while single mutations did 
not enable survival in 2,000 µmol(photon) m –2 s–1, which 
indicates that combinations of changes are required for 
the extreme phenotype (Dann et al. 2021).
In a salt ALE of Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803 in which 
ten independent lines S1–S10 evolved from a single 
clone under constant 3% NaCl, lines obtained after 303 d 
of selection showed improved growth under salt, but 
divergent metabolomic and transcriptomic response 
programs. Such divergence under the same selective factor 
supports the interpretation that convergence can occur 
at the level of phenotypic function, whereas molecular 
solutions, including components linked to pigment and 
redox tuning, remain multiple and line-specific (Hu et al. 
2020).
Thus, under prolonged selective exposure, different 
taxa can reach the same functional state in which light-
harvesting intensity is adjusted to the limits of stable 
photochemistry and redox homeostasis. Under thermal 
regimes, this is expressed as reduced effective light 
absorption with preserved photosystem performance 
under elevated temperature load, whereas under 
extremely high light it is expressed as heritable retuning 
of photosystem organization and electron transport, often 
polygenic in nature and linked to the photoprotective 
contribution of carotenoids. Under salt stress, resilience

reallocation and increased antioxidant capacity, with 
divergence of molecular programs among lines; therefore, 
pigment metrics are more appropriately treated as part of 
coordinated systemic adaptation rather than as the sole 
target of selection.
Pigment metrics in pollution monitoring and control 
of culture resilience
Pigment profiles of microalgae and cyanobacteria are 
among the most responsive physiological indicators 
under heavy metal exposure because they closely track 
the state of the photosynthetic apparatus, redox balance, 
and photooxidative load. Toxic metal stress rapidly affects 
chlorophylls and accessory pigments, as pigment–protein 
complexes are highly sensitive to disturbances in electron 
transport and ROS overproduction (Nowicka 2022). 
Under heavy metal exposure, cells mitigate toxicity and 
preserve photochemical function through a combination of 
reduced uptake, active export, intracellular sequestration, 
and metal chelation, including phytochelatin-based 
detoxification, together with reinforcement of antioxidant 
defence (Tripathi and Poluri 2021, Chakravorty et al. 
2023). These responses are commonly accompanied 
by changes in antioxidant enzyme activities such as 
SOD, CAT, and APX, as well as by the mobilization of 
low-molecular-mass redox buffers, including glutathione, 
ascorbate, tocopherols, and carotenoids (Tripathi and 
Poluri 2021, Nowicka 2022). As a result, a measurable 
physiological response pattern emerges in which changes 
in the pigment pool represent an integrated consequence 
of redox-homeostasis disruption and altered energy 
partitioning rather than a single isolated reaction.
The kinetics of pigment responses make them 
particularly valuable for early detection of environmental 
change. Compared with higher organisms and with 
endpoints that require prolonged effect accumulation, 
microalgae show rapid shifts in pigment composition 
as pollutant concentrations change, which supports 
their use as early indicators of metal load and toxicity 
levels (Carfagna et al. 2013, Wang et al. 2019). 
At the same time, interpretation of pigment metrics 
requires consideration of dose, duration, and multifactor 
exposure structure. Inhibitory pollutant concentrations 
often reduce chlorophyll and carotenoid contents due 
to biosynthesis suppression, membrane damage, and 
photosystem destabilization, which is accompanied by 
increased oxidative damage to lipids and macromolecules 
(Jamers et al. 2013, Japar et al. 2021). Nonlinear scenarios 
can also occur under sublethal loads, where early time 
points show transient compensatory shifts, including 
temporary increases in pigment levels and light absorption 
capacity. Such responses are consistent with hormetic 
mechanisms and reflect an early phase of the stress 
program rather than improved ecosystem physiological 
status; therefore, pigment increases at low doses cannot 
be treated as a simple equivalent of optimal cultivation 
conditions (Agathokleous et al. 2022, Zhang et al. 2024). 
Under mixed pollutants and concurrent factors such as 
changes in light, trophic status, and ionic strength, this 
dose-dependent ambiguity becomes especially important.
The most reliable diagnostic solutions arise when moving 
from single indicators to multiparameter panels in which 
pigments are interpreted together with redox-stress markers 
and photochemical parameters. Chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, 
and carotenoids remain core metrics because they are 
linked to carbon fixation efficiency and ATP synthesis and 
therefore to functional preservation of the photosynthetic 
apparatus (Chakravorty et al. 2023). Diagnostic accuracy 
increases when these metrics are complemented with lipid 
peroxidation markers, antioxidant activity parameters, 
and measures of photosystem efficiency (Chen et al. 
2022). This approach helps distinguish compensatory 
acclimation, in which pigment levels remain stable despite 
elevated redox markers, from conditions in which pigment 
decline is accompanied by membrane destabilization 
and reduced photosynthetic activity (Liu et al. 2022). 
Toxicological assessment is therefore more robust when 
pigment responses are interpreted together with redox and 
photosynthetic parameters rather than in isolation.
Practical applicability of pigment profiles is evident in 
both biomonitoring and culture control in complex media. 
In biosensor configurations, changes in physiological 
activity, including pigment-linked responses and 
photochemical parameters, are used as signals of 
inhibitory factors, and applied systems widely use 
genera such as Chlorella , Chlamydomonas, Arthrospira, 
Nannochloropsis, and Scenedesmus (Zeraatkar et al. 
2016). Reliability of such approaches depends on 
calibration to realistic pollutant mixtures and control of 
factors that can distort the signal, including shading effects 
and physicochemical properties of nanomaterials whose 
toxicity strongly depends on size, surface chemistry, 
aggregation, and exposure duration. In technological 
systems oriented toward cultivation on wastewater, 
pigment monitoring can serve as an operational 
physiological control tool that helps track transitions from 
a productive state to a pronounced stress mode.
Pigment profiles provide a robust indicator layer 
because they integrate the consequences of photochemical 
disruption, redox imbalance, and changes in membrane 
stability into quantitatively measurable traits. Maximum 
reliability is achieved when pigment data are interpreted 
within multiparameter panels aligned with oxidative 
damage markers and photosynthetic parameters. This 
approach enables correct handling of nonlinear dose-
dependent responses, discrimination of early compensatory 
shifts from developed damage, and use of pigment data 
as an early warning and controllable monitoring tool in 
polluted and wastewater media. 
Conclusion
Combined environmental stresses disrupt the balance 
between absorbed light energy and the capacity of 
microalgae and cyanobacteria to use or dissipate 
excitation safely. Pigment remodeling is therefore a central 
component of stress acclimation rather than merely 
an indicator of exposure. Chlorophyll systems adjust

antenna size and pigment organization, carotenoids enhance 
nonphotochemical quenching, antioxidant protection, 
and membrane stability, while phycobiliproteins regulate 
spectral light capture, excitation transfer, and resource 
allocation. Specialized pigments such as scytonemin 
provide an additional extracellular barrier against 
chronic ultraviolet radiation. The strongest evidence 
supports rapid excitation dissipation, antenna resizing, 
xanthophyll-cycle activity, OCP-mediated quenching, and 
regulated phycobilisome degradation as major protective 
mechanisms. However, similar pigment changes may 
also result from nutrient remobilization or irreversible 
photodamage. Consequently, total pigment content 
or a single pigment ratio cannot reliably distinguish 
acclimation from damage without corresponding 
measurements of photosystem performance and oxidative 
injury. The major unresolved challenge is to connect 
pigment phenotypes with regulatory mechanisms and 
ecological context. Future studies should use time-
resolved, multiparametric designs that combine pigment 
profiles with PSI and PSII performance, nonphotochemical 
quenching, ROS, lipid peroxidation, redox status, 
and transcriptomic or proteomic data under realistic 
combinations of light, temperature, nutrients, salinity, and 
pollutants. Such approaches will clarify when pigment 
remodeling preserves photosynthetic function and when 
it indicates that cellular protective capacity has been 
exceeded.
