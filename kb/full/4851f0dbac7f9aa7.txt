Integrated nitrogen and NPSB fertilization enhances 
productivity, nutrient-use efficiency, and profitability 
of tef (Eragrostis tef (Zucc.) Trotter) in Southwestern 
Ethiopia
Adisu Akalu · Solomon Tulu · Amsalu Nebiyu · Garome Shifaraw
Received: 31 May 2026 / Accepted: 7 September 2026
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract
The field experiment was carried out during the 2022 main cropping season under rainfed conditions on a 
farmer’s field in Gechi District, southwestern Ethiopia. The objective was to evaluate the effects of different 
rates of N combined with NPSB fertilizer on the growth, yield, and yield components of tef varieties, and to 
identify the economically optimum fertilizer rate for maximizing tef production under the local conditions. A 
factorial experiment comprising three tef varieties and six nitrogen–NPSB fertilizer combinations (3 × 6) was 
conducted using a randomized complete block design with three replications. Except for days to 50% flower -
ing (DF), days to 90% physiological maturity (DPM), and harvest index (HI), both the main effects and the 
interaction effects of variety and fertilizer rate significantly ( p < 0.05) influenced all measured parameters. The 
interaction of tef variety and fertilizer rate significantly ( p < 0.05) affected grain yield, thousand-seed weight, 
harvest index, and net benefit. Based on the present study, the Kora variety supplied with 34.5 kg N + 200 kg 
NPSB ha⁻ 1 achieved the highest grain yield (2565.4 kg ha⁻1), representing approximately a 162% increase 
over the unfertilized control and a 40% increase over the currently recommended fertilizer treatment, together 
with the greatest net economic return. Although the Kora variety supplied with 34.5 kg N + 200 kg NPSB ha⁻1 
produced the highest grain yield, agronomic efficiency, and economic return under the conditions of this study, 
these findings are based on a single-location, single-season experiment. Therefore, the recommendation should 
be considered preliminary and applicable primarily to agroecological conditions similar to those of Gechi Dis -
trict until validated through multi-location and multi-season experiments.

Introduction
Tef [Eragrostis tef (Zucc.) Trotter] is an indigenous Ethiopian cereal crop belonging to the family Poaceae, 
subfamily Chloridoideae, tribe Eragrostideae, and genus Eragrostis, which comprises approximately 350 spe -
cies, with tef being the only species extensively cultivated for human consumption 1. It is a warm-season, self-
pollinated, C4 annual grass that typically grows between 40 and 80 cm in height under normal field conditions2. 
Ethiopia is recognized as the center of origin, domestication, and genetic diversity of tef, where the crop has been 
cultivated for thousands of years and remains a staple food for millions of people1,3.
Tef is highly adaptable to diverse agroecological conditions and is cultivated across a broad altitudinal range, 
from near sea level to about 3000 m above sea level, with optimum production occurring between 1100 and 
2950 m1,2. The crop performs well under temperatures ranging from 10 to 27 °C and annual rainfall of 450–
850 mm and can be grown on a wide range of soil types, including sandy loam and heavy clay soils1. Its tolerance 
to both drought stress and temporary waterlogging makes tef an important crop for climate-resilient agriculture 
and food security2,3.
Tef has immense cultural, economic, and nutritional importance in Ethiopia, where it is cultivated by more 
than seven million smallholder households and occupies the largest share of cereal acreage 1,2. The grain is the 
principal ingredient used for preparing injera, the staple food of much of the Ethiopian population, and is also 
used for porridge, bread, and traditional beverages such as tella and katikala1. Nutritionally, tef is rich in protein, 
dietary fiber, essential amino acids, and minerals such as iron, calcium, zinc, and magnesium. In addition, its 
gluten-free nature has increased its popularity in international health-food markets3,4. Tef straw is also highly val-
ued as livestock feed and as a construction material for traditional houses and grain storage structures (gotera)2,5.
Despite its importance, tef productivity in Ethiopia remains relatively low compared with its potential. Recent 
agricultural statistics indicate that tef occupies more than 3.1 million hectares of land annually, making it the 
most widely cultivated cereal crop in the country 6. However, national average productivity remains about 2.0–
2.2 t ha⁻1, whereas yields exceeding 4.0 t ha⁻ 1 have been achieved under recommended management practices 
and more than 6.0 t ha⁻ 1 under experimental conditions 1,7. In Oromia region, tef is among the dominant cereal 
crops and serves as an important source of food and household income. Likewise, in Gechi District, tef is one of 
the major cereal crops cultivated for both home consumption and market purposes. During the 2020 main crop -
ping season, tef covered about 7,796 ha, accounting for 37.5% of the total cereal area, with an average productiv-
ity of only 1.68 t ha⁻1, indicating substantial scope for yield improvement.
Low tef productivity is attributed to several biophysical and management constraints, including soil fertility 
depletion, imbalanced fertilizer use, recurrent moisture stress, weed infestation, lodging, and limited adoption 
of improved varieties 1,8. Among these factors, declining soil fertility and inadequate nutrient management are 
widely recognized as major constraints to sustainable crop production. National soil fertility assessments have 
revealed widespread deficiencies of nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), sulfur (S), boron (B), zinc (Zn), and other 
essential nutrients in Ethiopian agricultural soils9,10. These nutrient deficiencies have been aggravated by continu-
ous cultivation, soil erosion, inadequate organic matter replenishment, and long-term nutrient mining.
Historically, fertilizer recommendations in Ethiopia emphasized only nitrogen and phosphorus through the 
application of urea and diammonium phosphate (DAP). Although these recommendations contributed to increased 
crop yields, they failed to account for variations in soil fertility status, agroecology, and crop nutrient require -
ments10. Consequently, Ethiopia has shifted toward balanced and site-specific nutrient management approaches 
based on the Ethiopian Soil Information System (EthioSIS), promoting the use of blended fertilizers containing 
both macro- and micronutrients, such as NPSB and NPSZnB 9,10.Although blended fertilizers have improved 
nutrient supply, the nitrogen content contained in NPSB fertilizer is often insufficient to meet the nutrient demand 
of high-yielding tef varieties.
Previous studies have demonstrated that nitrogen and blended fertilizer applications significantly improve 
tef growth, yield, and yield components. However, the optimum fertilizer rates vary considerably across

agroecological zones due to differences in soil characteristics, climatic conditions, nutrient availability, and 
varietal responses. Therefore, site-specific fertilizer recommendations are essential to maximize productivity, 
enhance nutrient use efficiency, and ensure sustainable tef production11,12
Most previous studies have focused either on the effects of fertilizer application on tef productivity or on the 
performance of improved varieties under specific nutrient management practices. Limited attention has been 
given to the interaction between varietal differences and fertilizer regimes. Recent studies have shown that tef 
varieties differ in their response to nutrient application rates, indicating that varietal characteristics play a signifi-
cant role in determining nutrient use efficiency and yield performance11. However, information on the combined 
effects of improved tef varieties and varying rates of nitrogen and blended fertilizers, particularly NPSB, remains 
inadequate under diverse agro ecological conditions, including the humid and sub-humid environments of South-
western Ethiopia. Consequently, location-specific studies are required to develop integrated variety fertilizer rec-
ommendations that enhance productivity, nutrient use efficiency, and the sustainability of tef production systems.
In Gechi District, where tef production plays an increasingly important role in food security and household 
income generation, fertilizer recommendations are often based on generalized national guidelines or research 
findings from other agroecological zones with different soil and climatic conditions. However, fertilizer responses 
in tef vary substantially across locations due to differences in soil fertility status, rainfall patterns, and manage -
ment practices, highlighting the need for site-specific recommendations 13,14. Consequently, farmers in the area 
lack locally validated recommendations that integrate improved tef varieties with balanced nutrient management 
strategies. Furthermore, limited information is available regarding the agronomic efficiency and economic profit-
ability of different nitrogen and blended NPSB fertilizer combinations for recently released tef varieties under the 
humid and sub-humid conditions of Southwestern Ethiopia11,13.
Although balanced fertilizer recommendations have recently been introduced in Ethiopia through the Ethio -
pian Soil Information System (EthioSIS), most recommendations remain generalized and do not adequately 
account for varietal differences in nutrient responsiveness15,16. Previous studies have primarily focused either on 
evaluating fertilizer rates or comparing improved tef varieties independently17,18. Consequently, limited informa-
tion exists regarding the interaction between genotype and balanced nutrient management, particularly under the 
humid Nitisol environments of southwestern Ethiopia. Because nutrient use efficiency varies among genotypes 
due to differences in root architecture, nutrient uptake capacity, assimilate partitioning, and physiological effi -
ciency, identifying optimum genotype × fertilizer combinations is essential for improving fertilizer-use efficiency, 
economic profitability, and sustainable tef production19. Therefore, this study addresses an important knowledge 
gap by integrating varietal evaluation with balanced N and NPSB fertilization under location-specific conditions. 
Therefore, this study was conducted to evaluate the effects of different nitrogen and NPSB fertilizer rates on the 
growth, yield, yield components, agronomic efficiency, and economic returns of selected tef varieties in Gechi 
District, Southwestern Ethiopia Accordingly, this study was undertaken to evaluate the effects of different rates of 
nitrogen combined with NPSB fertilizer on the growth, yield, and yield components of tef varieties in Gechi Dis-
trict, southwestern Ethiopia. The specific objectives were to: (i) determine the optimum combination of nitrogen 
and NPSB fertilizer rates for improving the growth, yield, and yield components of tef varieties; and (ii) identify 
the agronomically efficient and economically feasible nitrogen and NPSB fertilizer combination for sustainable 
tef production in the study area.

Gechi District, Buno Bedele Zone, Oromia Regional State, Southwestern Ethiopia. The experimental site is geo-
graphically located at 8°18′39″ N latitude and 36°26′29″ E longitude, at an altitude of 2,132 m above sea level

Treatments and experimental design
The treatments consisted of a factorial combination of three tef varieties (Boset, Dagim, and Kora) and six 
nitrogen NPSB fertilizer combinations. The treatments were arranged in a randomized complete block design 
(RCBD) with three replications in a 3 × 6 factorial arrangement, giving a total of 18 treatment combinations. The 
fertilizer treatments included an unfertilized control, 11.5 kg N + 100 kg NPSB ha⁻ 1, 23 kg N + 150 kg NPSB 
ha⁻1, 34.5 kg N + 200 kg NPSB ha⁻1, 46 kg N + 100 kg NPSB ha⁻1, and 46 kg N + 250 kg NPSB ha⁻1. The cur-
rently recommended fertilizer rate for the study area, 46 kg N + 100 kg NPSB ha⁻1, was included as a standard 
comparison treatment. The fertilizer treatments were selected based on regional fertilizer recommendations, pre-
liminary soil fertility analysis, and nutrient deficiencies identified through the Ethiopian Soil Information System 
(EthioSIS)15,21. The inclusion of fertilizer rates both below and above the currently recommended application rate 
enabled evaluation of crop response under deficient, optimum, and excessive nutrient supply conditions, a widely 
adopted approach in fertilizer calibration and nutrient response studies22.This experimental design also facilitated 
identification of the agronomic optimum, biological optimum, and economically optimum fertilizer combinations 
for different tef varieties by quantifying yield response across a range of nutrient application levels23.
Experimental procedures and crop management
The field layout was prepared according to the experimental design specifications. The total experimental area 
measured 10 m × 45.5 m (455 m2). Each gross plot had a size of 2 m × 2 m (4 m2), while the net plot size was 
1.6 m × 1.6 m (2.56 m2). Blocks were separated by 1.0 m-wide alleys, and plots within each block were spaced 
0.5 m apart. Tef seeds were sown manually in rows at a seed rate of 15 kg ha⁻1 with 20 cm spacing between rows, 
following the agronomic recommendations for tef production in Ethiopia 24,25. Each plot consisted of ten rows. 
Nitrogen fertilizer was applied in split doses, with one-third applied two weeks after crop emergence and the 
remaining two-thirds applied before the booting stage in all treatments except the control. For data collection, 
the two outermost rows on each side of the plot and 0.4 m from both ends of each row were considered border 
rows and excluded from measurements to minimize border effects. Weeds were controlled manually as required. 
All other agronomic practices, including land preparation, sowing, and crop management, were undertaken in 
accordance with local recommendations for tef production in the study area24.
Soil sampling and analysis
Prior to sowing, composite soil samples were collected from the experimental field at a depth of 0–20 cm using 
an auger following a W-shaped sampling pattern across the experimental area. The collected samples were thor-
oughly mixed to obtain a representative composite sample. The soil samples were air-dried and gently crushed. 
Samples intended for the determination of soil texture, pH, cation exchange capacity (CEC), available phospho-
rus (P), sulfur (S), and boron (B) were passed through a 2 mm sieve, whereas samples for organic carbon (OC) 
and total nitrogen (TN) analyses were further ground and passed through a 0.5 mm sieve.
The prepared samples were analyzed at the Bedele Soil Research Laboratory Center following standard labora-
tory procedures. Particle size distribution was determined using the hydrometer method as described by Gee and 
Or26. Soil pH was measured in a 1:2.5 soil-to-water suspension using a digital pH meter following procedures 
outlined by Hazelton and Murphy 27. Organic carbon was determined using the Walkley–Black wet oxidation 
method, while total nitrogen was analyzed using the Kjeldahl digestion-distillation method according to the pro-
cedures described by Estefan et al.28.Cation exchange capacity (CEC) was determined using the ammonium ace-
tate extraction method at pH 7.0 following FAO29, laboratory guidelines. Available phosphorus was determined 
using the Bray II extraction method, which is recommended for acidic soils, including Nitisols28. Available sulfur 
was determined using monocalcium phosphate extraction followed by turbidimetric measurement, whereas avail-
able boron was determined using the hot-water extraction method according to procedures described by Jones30.

Statistical analysis
Prior to analysis of variance (ANOV A), all response variables were examined for compliance with the assump-
tions of normality, homogeneity of variance, and independence of residuals. Normality was evaluated using 
the Shapiro–Wilk test together with visual inspection of residual plots 33,34, while homogeneity of variance was 
assessed using Levene's test35.Where these assumptions were satisfied, the data were subjected to factorial analy-
sis of variance (ANOV A) using the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure of SAS version 9.3 36. The LSD 
procedure was selected because the experiment involved a balanced factorial design with a significant overall 
ANOV A, and the treatment comparisons were predefined based on the experimental objectives. Under these 
conditions, the LSD test is widely used in agronomic research for detecting biologically meaningful differences 
among treatment means.Therefore; the treatment means were separated using the Least Significant Difference 
(LSD) test at the 5% probability level following a significant F-test37. For the analysis, the ANOV A model was:

Partial budget analysis was performed to evaluate the profitability of applying N + blended NPSB fertilizers 
across the three tef varieties, as summarized in Table 8. The analysis considered all relevant costs, including labor 
for fertilizer application, the market prices of fertilizers and seeds at sowing, and the prices of tef grain and straw

were adjusted downward by 10%. Dominance analysis was used to eliminate treatments with higher variable 
costs and lower net benefits. A treatment was considered economically profitable if its MRR was 100% or higher.

Results and discussion
Selected physicochemical properties of experimental site before sowing
The experimental soil was analyzed for its key physicochemical properties prior to sowing. The soil texture con-
sisted of 15.2% sand, 29.5% silt, and 55.3% clay (Table 2), classifying it as a clay-dominated soil. Soil texture 
significantly influences water-holding capacity, infiltration rate, aeration, root penetration, and overall soil fertil-
ity. The soil pH was measured at 5.9, indicating moderately acidic Nitisols, which can affect nutrient availability 
and crop growth. The cation exchange capacity (CEC) of the site was 34.21 cmol kg⁻ 1 (Table 2), reflecting the 
soil’s capacity to retain essential nutrients and providing insight into its clay mineral composition and organic 
matter content.Soil organic carbon (OC) was found to be in the medium range. According to Boix-Fayos et al.38 
a threshold of 3–3.5% OC is needed to improve aggregate stability; soils below this level show limited improve-
ment in soil structure. Total nitrogen content was measured at 0.124% (Table 2), classifying the soil as low in 
nitrogen. For most Ethiopian soils, nitrogen content below 0.2% is considered suboptimal for crop production15. 
The low nitrogen level may be attributed to continuous cultivation, poor vegetation cover, and limited return of 
crop residues to the soil.

Available phosphorus (P), determined using the Bray II method, was extremely low, indicating a strong need 
for phosphorus fertilizer application. Phosphorus availability is categorized as very low (< 7 mg kg⁻1), low 
(8–19 mg kg⁻1), medium (20–39 mg kg⁻1), high (40–58 mg kg⁻1), and very high (> 59 mg kg⁻1)39 Available sulfur 
(S) was 7.04 ppm (Table 2), placing it in the very low category (< 9 mg kg⁻1) according to EthioSIS15, suggesting 
that sulfur-containing fertilizers are required to improve crop nutrition. Available boron (B) was 0.67 ppm (Table 
2), below the critical level of 0.8 ppm for most Ethiopian soils 40, indicating boron deficiency likely caused by 
continuous cultivation and long-term use of fertilizers lacking micronutrients. Overall, these soil properties are 
critical for determining and adjusting fertilizer treatments in the experiment. They also provide essential baseline 
information about the experimental site, which is important for interpreting the results of the current study and 
for planning future research.
The pre-planting soil analysis demonstrated that the experimental field was characterized by low total nitrogen 
(0.124%), very low available phosphorus (5.54 mg kg⁻1), very low sulfur (7.04 mg kg⁻1), and deficient boron 
(0.67 ppm). These deficiencies justify the evaluation of balanced NPSB fertilizer rather than nitrogen fertilizer 
alone. Furthermore, the moderately acidic soil pH (5.9) may reduce phosphorus availability through fixation by 
iron and aluminum oxides, reinforcing the importance of balanced nutrient management under Nitisol condi -
tions. Therefore, the observed crop responses are biologically consistent with the initial nutrient limitations of 
the experimental soil.
Effect of N and NPSB fertilizer rates on phenological parameters of tef
Days to 50% flowering
The main effect of nitrogen (N) significantly ( p < 0.05) influenced the number of days to 50% flowering in tef, 
while the effects of nitrogen and NPSB fertilizer rates and the interaction between variety and fertilizer were not 
significant (p > 0.05). Among the varieties, Guduru exhibited the longest duration to 50% flowering (64 days), 
whereas the local variety flowered earliest (52 days) (Table 3). Compared with Kora and the local variety, Guduru 
required two and twelve additional days, respectively, reaching 50% flowering, likely reflecting inherent genetic 
differences and varietal responses to environmental conditions.
Regarding fertilizer treatments, the shortest time to 50% flowering (57 days) was recorded at 34.5 kg N + 200 kg 
NPSB ha⁻1, which was statistically similar to 46 kg N + 250 kg NPSB (57 days), 23 kg N + 150 kg NPSB (58 days), 
and 11.5 kg N + 100 kg NPSB (59.4 days). In contrast, the longest flowering period (63 days) was observed in 
the unfertilized control, which was comparable to 46 kg N + 100 kg NPSB (61 days) and 11.5 kg N + 100 kg 
NPSB (59 days). Increasing nitrogen and NPSB fertilizer rates generally accelerated phenological development, 
leading to earlier flowering. This response is attributed to improved nutrient availability that enhances early 
vegetative growth and physiological development. Similarly, Abichu et al. 41 observed that increasing nitrogen

by increasing grain yield proportionally more than total biomass production.
Agronomic efficiency
Agronomic nutrient use efficiency (ANUE) refers to the additional yield obtained per unit of nutrient applied. 
The results of this study indicated that ANUE of tef was significantly influenced by varying rates of N + NPSB 
blended fertilizer. The highest ANUE (13.7 kg/kg) was recorded for the Kora variety at 23 kg N + 150 kg NPSB 
ha⁻1, whereas the lowest ANUE (3.43 kg/kg) was observed for the Local variety at 46 kg N + 250 kg NPSB ha⁻1 
(Table 7 and Fig. 2).
The superior economic performance of Kora was not solely attributable to higher grain yield but also to its 
greater agronomic nutrient-use efficiency. Higher nutrient-use efficiency indicates that additional fertilizer inputs 
were converted into proportionally greater grain production, thereby increasing economic return per unit of fer -
tilizer applied. This finding suggests that varietal differences in nutrient responsiveness should be considered 
when formulating fertilizer recommendations because economically optimum nutrient rates may differ among 
genotypes. Similar genotype-dependent differences in nutrient-use efficiency have been reported in cereal crops, 
where improved cultivars generally exhibit superior recovery and utilization of applied nutrients compared with 
traditional varieties62,63.
Agronomic efficiency declined progressively with increasing fertilizer rate despite higher grain yields, indicat-
ing diminishing nutrient-use efficiency under excessive fertilizer application. This pattern reflects the well-estab-
lished principle that fertilizer recovery efficiency decreases once crop nutrient demand approaches saturation. 
Consequently, maximizing grain yield does not necessarily maximize nutrient-use efficiency or economic prof -
itability. These findings demonstrate that optimum fertilizer recommendations should simultaneously consider 
biological yield, nutrient-use efficiency, and economic return.Similar trends have been widely documented in 
recent agronomic research, indicating that nutrient use efficiency decreases when fertilizer application exceeds 
crop demand.

The present economic analysis is consistent with recent studies on tef fertilizer management in Ethiopia, 
which have demonstrated that optimum rather than maximum fertilizer rates often provide the greatest economic 
return. The combination of 34.5 kg N + 200 kg NPSB ha⁻1 with the Kora variety generated the highest net benefit 
and marginal rate of return, indicating that balanced nutrient application maximized profitability while avoid -
ing unnecessary fertilizer costs. Similar findings were reported by Tesfaye et al. 68, who observed that applica -
tion of 150 kg NPS ha⁻1 resulted in the highest net benefit and a marginal rate of return of 876%, making it the 
most economically viable fertilizer rate for tef production. Likewise, Mekonene et al.50 found that the combined 
application of 92 kg N ha⁻ 1 and 46 kg P₂O₅ ha⁻ 1 produced the highest net benefit and marginal rate of return 
(1684.97%), demonstrating the importance of balanced nutrient management for maximizing economic returns 
in tef production. Furthermore, Abiro et al. 69 reported that landscape-specific nutrient management strategies 
consistently generated higher benefit–cost ratios and net returns than blanket fertilizer recommendations across 
diverse Ethiopian agro ecologies. Similar conclusions were drawn by Teshome et al.48, who found that integrated 
fertilizer management practices significantly improved both grain yield and profitability of tef under farmers’ 
field conditions. The current results also agree with earlier findings by Asfaw et al.70, who reported that moderate 
nitrogen rates provided higher value-to-cost ratios than excessive fertilizer applications, emphasizing that eco -
nomic optimum fertilizer rates are often lower than agronomic maximum rates. These findings collectively sup -
port the recommendation of 34.5 kg N + 200 kg NPSB ha⁻1 for the Kora variety as an economically sound nutrient 
management option for tef production in the study area.Although the highest fertilizer rate produced greater bio-
mass accumulation, the additional grain yield obtained was insufficient to compensate for the increased fertilizer 
cost. Consequently, net economic return declined despite increased biological production. This finding illustrates 
the difference between agronomic optimum and economic optimum fertilizer rates. Therefore, fertilizer recom -
mendations should be based not only on maximum yield but also on profitability and resource-use efficiency 
under smallholder farming systems.
Study limitations
Although this study generated valuable information on genotype × fertilizer interactions in tef, several limitations 
should be acknowledged. First, the experiment was conducted during a single cropping season at one experi -
mental location, limiting extrapolation of the findings across diverse agro ecological conditions. Second, nutri -
ent uptake, nitrogen recovery efficiency, root characteristics, chlorophyll content, and post-harvest soil nutrient 
dynamics were not evaluated. Consequently, the physiological mechanisms responsible for varietal differences in 
nutrient-use efficiency could not be directly quantified. Third, seasonal climatic variation and genotype × environ-
ment interactions were not assessed. Future research should therefore validate these findings using multi-location 
and multi-season experiments while incorporating physiological, biochemical, and soil fertility measurements to 
improve understanding of nutrient response mechanisms. Therefore, the findings should be regarded as location-
specific and serve as a basis for future adaptive research under diverse production environments.
Conclusion
The present study demonstrated that tef varieties differ significantly in their response to balanced nitrogen and 
NPSB fertilization. Among the evaluated combinations, the Kora variety supplied with 34.5 kg N + 200 kg NPSB 
ha⁻1 consistently produced superior grain yield, harvest index, agronomic efficiency, net benefit, and marginal 
rate of return under the conditions of Gechi District. These findings emphasize that integrating genotype selec -
tion with balanced nutrient management is more effective than applying generalized fertilizer recommendations. 
Agronomic nutrient use efficiency was highest at moderate fertilizer rates, and partial budget analysis revealed

rate of return (1082.4%), indicating economic viability. These results demonstrate that applying N-supplemented 
blended NPSB fertilizer substantially improves tef growth, yield, and profitability, with the Kora variety showing 
the greatest responsiveness.
These findings indicate that this fertilizer combination represents a promising nutrient management option for 
humid Nitisol environments with agroecological conditions similar to those of the study area. However, because 
the experiment was conducted at a single location during one growing season, the recommendation should be 
considered preliminary rather than broadly applicable. Further multi-location and multi-season studies across 
diverse soil types, climatic conditions, and management systems are required to validate the consistency, stabil -
ity, and wider applicability of this fertilizer recommendation before it can be recommended for national-scale tef 
production in Ethiopia.
