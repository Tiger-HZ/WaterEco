 A B S T R A C T 
Air pollution is a complex process that requires statistical modeling and forecasting due to 
the temporal variability in the concentrations of different pollutant components in the 
atmosphere. This study analyzes monthly concentrations of PM 10, PM2.5, SO2, NO2, and O 3 
measured in Çanakkale between 2019 and 2024 using time series methods to generate 
future forecasts. Factor analysis was first applied to identify the underlying structure 
among pollutants and to select the most influential variables for modeling; as a result, PM10 
and SO 2 were found to load most strongly on the primary factor associated with air 
pollution in the region. For these two pollutants, which w ere examined in terms of 
stationarity, trend, and seasonal components, forecasting models were developed based 
on Exponential Smoothing techniques (Simple, Holt, Damped, Holt –Winters additive and 
multiplicative) and the Box –Jenkins methodology (ARIMA, SARI MA). Model performance 
was evaluated using RMSE, MAE and MAPE for both in-sample and out-of-sample periods. 
The Holt –Winters multiplicative model achieved the highest forecasting accuracy, with 
MAPE values of 11.040% for PM₁₀ and 20.253% for SO₂ by effectively capturing trend and 
proportional seasonality. This study is distinctive in that it provides a comparative 
evaluation of ETS and Box –Jenkins approaches for multiple air pollutants and develops 
region-specific forecasting models for Çanakkale, thereby o ffering novel methodological 
and practical insights for air quality assessment in Türkiye.

INTRODUCTION 
 
Air pollution is recognized as one of the most critical 
environmental issues worldwide due to its adverse impacts 
on human health and ecosystems. According to the World 
Health Organization, pollutants such as particulate matter 
(PM), nitrogen dioxide (NO 2), sulfur dioxide (SO 2), carbon 
monoxide (CO), and ozone (O3) are among the leading causes 
of respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, contributing to 
millions of premature deaths [1]. Air quality is not only a key 
determinant of public health but also plays a significant role 
in climate change, agricultural productivity, and ecosystem 
balance [2]. Therefore, modeling pollutant concentrations 
and forecasting their future levels is of paramou nt 
importance for both scientific research and environmental 
planning. 
In Turkey, air quality management is regulated under the Air 
Quality Assessment and Management Regulation, which sets 
limit values aimed at protecting public health. Çanakkale is 
among the regions requiring regular monitoring due to 
emissions from industrial activities, heating, and traffic, as it 
is strategically located at the intersection of road, air, and sea 
transport between the Marmara and Aegean regions and 
 
* Corresponding Author: t.sokut@comu.edu.tr (Tuğba Söküt Açar) 
hosts numerous industrial facilities operating in sectors such 
as food, furniture, metal, chemical, plastic, tobacco, and 
machinery manufacturing. These characteristics make 
Çanakkale a strategically important area where both regional 
transport of pollutants and local emission sources interact, 
and therefore a suitable case study for air pollution modeling. 
Data from the Marmara Clean Air Center and Continuous 
Monitoring Stations in Çanakkale indicate periodic increases 
in parameters such as PM10 and NOx [2, 3]. 
Among the pollutants examined in this study, PM 10 and PM2.5 
belong to the particulate matter class and pose significant 
health risks to the respiratory system. NO 2 formed primarily 
from fossil fuel combustion, are nitrogen oxide compounds 
that play an important role in atmospheric chemistry and the 
formation of secondary pollutants. SO 2 is mainly released 
during coal-based energy production and industrial activities 
and is a major contributor to acid rain. O3, when formed at the 
tropospheric level, acts as a harmful pollu tant and tends to 
increase during summer months due to photochemical 
reactions [4, 5]. The concentrations of these pollutants are 
strongly influenced by meteorological factors such as 
temperature, humidity, pressure, wind speed, precipitation, 
and sunshine duration. Since air pollution can result from

multiple factors, it is essential to examine and analyze the 
origins of air pollutants and the relationships among them [6]. 
Recent studies on air pollutants in Çanakkale province were 
also examined. For the Çanakkale region, Aydın and Menteşe 
[7] investigated air quality in the Gelibolu district based on 
VOC measurements and examined both spatial and temporal 
(daily and seasonal) variations, showing that routine road 
traffic and ferry -related vehicle loading s ubstantially 
increased VOC levels. In addition, Akyürek [8] analyzed the 
forest fire that occurred in Çanakkale between 22–24 August 
2023 using Sentinel -2 and Sentinel -5P satellite images and 
Geographic Information Systems, and reported that the fire 
affected an area of 4191.93 hectares and significantly 
increased atmospheric NO₂ concentrations, thereby 
deteriorating air quality. At the local scale, these recent 
studies have mainly focused on monitoring air quality, 
mapping its spatial distribution and iden tifying emission 
sources; by contrast, studies providing a comprehensive 
framework based on statistical time series modelling and 
forecasting remain relatively scarce. 
In the literature on air pollution forecasting, both classical 
time series models and ar tificial intelligence -based 
approaches have been employed. The Box -Jenkins 
methodology (ARIMA, SARIMA) is widely recognized as a 
robust statistical approach for modeling series with trend and 
seasonality [9]. Existing literature shows that classical time 
series models such as ARIMA and SARIMA are widely applied 
for forecasting environmental and climate -related variables. 
Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of 
SARIMA models in capturing seasonality and improving 
forecast accuracy for climate variables such as temperature 
and precipitation [10]. Temiz and Turgut [11] performed a 
time series analysis of PM₁₀ concentrations in Ankara using 
the Box –Jenkins methodology and identified an ARIMA 
(3,0,0) model as the most appropriate specification for 
forecasting future PM 10 levels. In a comprehensive national -
level study, Cekim [12] compared ARIMA, ETS, and Singular 
Spectrum Analysis (SSA) models to forecast PM₁₀ 
concentrations across Turkey’s most polluted cities. The 
results demonstrated that while E TS provided satisfactory 
forecasts in some regions, non -parametric SSA achieved the 
lowest overall prediction errors, highlighting the importance 
of comparing traditional and state -space approaches in air 
quality modeling. 
Exponential smoothing methods, su ch as Holt -Winters, have 
also proven effective, particularly for short - and medium -
term forecasts [13]. For instance, Wu et al. [14] applied the 
Holt-Winters method for predicting the Air Quality Index in 
China and demonstrated its capability in capturing seasonal 
components. Similarly, Setiawan [15] utilized ARIMA for PM 
forecasting in Madrid, while Hansun et al. [16] implemented 
modified exponential smoothing techniques for Jakarta. 
Studies conducted in Turkey have generally focused on either 
a single pollutant or a single modeling approach [4, 5, 17, 18]. 
Comprehensive studies that simultaneously compare ETS 
and ARIMA methods across multiple pollutants remain 
limited in the existing literature. 
The objective of this study is to model the major air pollutants 
measured in Çanakkale (PM10, PM2.5, SO2, NO2, and O3) for the 
period 2019–2024 using time series methods and to produce 
forecasts for future periods. For this purpose, the study was 
organized as follows: First, time series plots of air quality 
variables were generated, and the variables were 
descriptively analyzed. Next, factor analysis was applied to 
identify the most influential pollution parameters in 
Çanakkale. Based on the results of the factor analysis, selected 
pollutants were modeled using both Exponential Smoothing 
and Box -Jenkins methodologies. In this context, the central 
research question of the study is whether, owing to their 
more flexible treatment of trend and especially seasonal 
components, ETS models outperform Box –Jenkins models in 
forecasting the air pollutions. The performance of the 
resulting models was then evaluated using various error 
metrics to identify the best-performing models. This study is 
distinctive in that it comparatively examines multiple 
pollutants using both ETS and B ox-Jenkins approaches and 
provides forecasts for the Çanakkale region —a topic rarely 
addressed in the Turkish literature. In this context, the 
research stands out as one of the first comprehensive studies 
to compare the performance of two different classic al time 
series approaches in detail. 
 
MATERIALS AND METHODS 
 
In this study, future forecasts were generated using major air 
pollutants (PM 10, PM 2.5, SO 2, NO 2, and O 3) measured in 
Çanakkale between 2019 and 2024. The forecasting process 
involved a comparative application of two classical time 
series approaches: ETS methods and the Box -Jenkins 
methodology. During the modeling phase, the stationarity of 
the series was examined using the Augmen ted Dickey-Fuller 
(ADF) test, and trend and seasonality components were 
analyzed. The predictive performance of the models was 
evaluated using metrics such as RMSE, MAE and MAPE. 
All statistical analyses and forecasting procedures in this 
study were condu cted using R statistical software (RStudio 
IDE, version 2024.04.1 Build 748), with relevant packages for 
time series modeling and visualization, including forecast (for 
auto.arima(), ets(), and accuracy() functions), tseries (for 
adf.test()) and ggplot2 (for graphical representation of time 
series and forecasts). 
 
Forecasting with Exponential Smoothing Methods 
 
Exponential smoothing methods are highly effective and 
widely used approaches for forecasting in time series 
analysis. The fundamental principle of these methods is to 
assign greater weights to more recent observations in the 
series when predicting future values. In this way, the most 
recent data points are considered more significant, thereby 
improving forecast accuracy. The main types of exponential 
smoothing methods include: Simple Exponential Smoothing 
(SES), Holt’s Exponential Smoothing, Damped Exponen tial 
Smoothing, and Holt-Winters Exponential Smoothing. 
 
Simple exponential smoothing, SES 
 
SES used when the time series does not contain components 
such as trend or seasonality. In other words, the goal of this 
method is to predict the current level of t he time series in 
cases where there is no general trend or seasonal effect. For 
future forecasts, greater weight is assigned to the most recent 
observations of the series.

time 𝑡 be 𝑦𝑡. The forecast for ℎ periods ahead made at time 𝑡 
is given by: 
Forecast Equation: 𝑦̂𝑡+ℎ|𝑡 = ℓ𝑡 
Level Equation: ℓ𝑡 = 𝛼𝑦𝑡 + (1 − 𝛼)ℓ𝑡−1 
The initial forecast value is taken as the arithmetic mean of 
the series. Here, 𝛼 is the smoothing parameter, which takes a 
value between 0 and 1, and its optimal value is determined by 
minimizing the mean squared error (MSE).

Holt exponential smoothing 
 
If the time series to be forecasted contains only a trend 
component, this method is preferred. In addition to the 
Simple Exponential Smoothing model, a trend term is 
included in the forecasting model since the data exhibit a 
trend. This method uses two smo othing parameters, both 
ranging between 0 and 1, which are determined by 
minimizing the mean squared error [19]. To enable 
forecasting of trend -containing data, Holt extended the 
simple exponential smoothing method as follows: 
Forecast Equation: 𝑦̂𝑡+ℎ|𝑡 = ℓ𝑡 + ℎ𝑏𝑡 
Level Equation: ℓ𝑡 = 𝛼𝑦𝑡 + (1 − 𝛼)(ℓ𝑡−1 + 𝑏𝑡−1) 
Trend Equation: 𝑏𝑡 = 𝛽(ℓ𝑡 − ℓ𝑡−1) + (1 − 𝛽)𝑏𝑡−1 
Here, 𝛼 and 𝛽 are the smoothing parameters, and 𝑏𝑡 
represents an estimate of the trend (slope) at time t. In this 
model, 𝛼 controls the smoothing of the level, while 𝛽 controls 
the smoothing of the trend. Both parameters are optimized 
within the range (0,1) by minimizing the mean squared error 
[20]. The initial values for level and trend are typically 
obtained using parameter estimates from a simple linear 
regression applied to the series [21]. 
 
Damped exponential smoothing 
 
Proposed by Gardner and McKenzie [22], damped 
exponential smoothing extends Holt’s method by introducing 
a damping parameter that gradually reduces the influence of 
the trend over time. This approach allows for more stable and 
reliable forecasts in the long term. Particularly for 
multiplicative models, where the trend is expected to 
diminish or exhibit dynamic changes over time, the damped 
method often produces more robust results [23]. In addition 
to the smoothing parameters, this method incorporates a 
damping parameter, 𝜃, which lies within the interval 0 < 𝜃 <
1 [24]. 
Forecast Equation: 𝑦̂𝑡+ℎ|𝑡 = ℓ𝑡 + (𝜃 + 𝜃2 + ⋯ + 𝜃ℎ)𝑏𝑡 
Level Equation: ℓ𝑡 = 𝛼𝑦𝑡 + (1 − 𝛼)(ℓ𝑡−1 + 𝜃𝑏𝑡−1) 
Trend Equation:𝑏𝑡 = 𝛽(ℓ𝑡 − ℓ𝑡−1) + (1 − 𝛽)𝜃𝑏𝑡−1 
 
It is evident that when 𝜃 = 1, the model reduces to Holt’s 
linear exponential smoothing method. 
 
Holt-Winters exponential smoothing 
 
The Holt-Winters method is an extension of the simple and 
Holt exponential smoothing methods, specifically designed 
for time series with seasonality. To account for seasonal 
effects, this method can be applied using two different models 
depending on the structure of the series: additive and 
multiplicative seasonal models. In the additive model, 
seasonal fluctuations remain constant regardless of the 
overall level of the series, while in the multiplicative model, 
the magnitude of seasonal variations depends on the level of 
the series [25]. A key feature of the Holt -Winters method is 
that it involves three smoothing parameters: 𝛼 (level), 𝛽 
(trend), and 𝛾 (seasonality). These parameters are optimized 
to minimize the forecast error of the model [26]. 
Here, 𝑚 denotes the length of the seasonal cycle, and 𝑆𝑡−𝑚 
represents the seasonal component from the corresponding 
period in the previous cycle. Additionally, 𝑘 refers to the 
integer part of (ℎ − 1)/𝑚, which ensures that the seasonal 
indices used in the forecasts correspond to those from the 
most recent seasonal cycle in the sample [27]. In Winters 
models, the seasonal components are normalized, meaning 
that the seasonal factors are adjusted so that their average 
equals 1. This implies that the sum of the seasonal 
components is equal to 1, ensuring that seasonal effects are 
appropriately scaled to the overall level of the series [28]. 
In summary, the four exponential smoothing methods differ 
mainly in the components they account for. The SES model 
considers only the level component and is suitable for series 
without trend or seasonality. The Holt method extends SES by 
including a trend component, allowing forecasts for data 
exhibiting a systematic increase or decrease. The Damped 
trend model further refines Holt’s approach by introducing a 
damping parameter that gr adually reduces the trend effect 
over time, providing more stable long-term forecasts. Finally, 
the Holt –Winters model incorporates both trend and 
seasonality, with additive and multiplicative formulations 
depending on whether seasonal fluctuations remain constant 
or vary in proportion to the series level.

Forecasting with Box-Jenkins Methods 
 
Box-Jenkins models are considered a generalized form of 
exponential smoothing methods. While exponential 
smoothing focuses on modeling trend and seasonality 
components in the data, Box -Jenkins models aim to capture 
the autocorrelation structure within the series. These models 
are categorized as either seasonal or non -seasonal. Non -
seasonal Box-Jenkins models are expressed as ARIMA(p,d,q), 
where p represents the order of the autoregressive (AR) 
component, d denotes the degree of differencing required to 
achieve stationarity, and q indicates the order of the movin g 
average (MA) component. Seasonal Box -Jenkins models are 
expressed as SARIMA(p, d, q)(P, D, Q)m, where P, D, and Q 
represent the seasonal autoregressive, seasonal differencing, 
and seasonal moving average orders, respectively, and 𝑚 
denotes the seasonal period. 
For Box-Jenkins models to be applicable, the time series must 
be stationary, meaning it s hould be free from trend and 
seasonal fluctuations. To achieve stationarity in the mean, 
differencing is applied, while variance stationarity is typically 
addressed using logarithmic or power transformations [9].

Non-seasonal Box-Jenkins models 
 
Non-seasonal Box-Jenkins models include the autoregressive 
model (AR(p)), the moving average model (MA(q)), and their 
combination, ARMA(p,q). For non -seasonal series, if the 
series is not stationary (i.e., exhibits a trend), differencing is 
applied to achi eve stationarity. In such cases, an integrated 
component I(d) is added to the model, resulting in the general 
form ARIMA(p,d,q).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
 
In this study, air quality data for Çanakkale were obtained 
from continuous monitoring stations under the Marmara 
Clean Air Center Directorate. The study covers the period 
from January 2019 to June 2024. The raw data were collected 
on a daily basis and converted into monthly averages for 
analysis and modeling. The air pollutants considered in the 
study include PM10, PM2.5, SO2, NO2, and O3. Time series plots 
and boxplots for these pollutants are presented in Figure 1.

Among the air pollutants examined over the 2019 –2024 
period, the most evident trend structure was observed in the 
SO2 series. SO 2 levels exhibited a steady decline over time, 
reaching minimum values particularly in 2023 and 2024, 
indicating a potential reduction in emissions from heating 
and industrial activities. On the other hand, the most 
prominent seasonal pat tern was identified in the O 3 series.

average value was 51.259 μg/m³, while the maximum was 
recorded as 84.928 μg/m³, remaining below the 8 -hour limit

relationships among pollutants and uncover potential 
common source structures, factor analysis was applied. The 
analysis employed the prin cipal components method, and 
factor loadings were made interpretable using varimax 
rotation.

In time series modeling, it is crucial to determine whether the 
series is stationary to ensure that the assumptions of the 
applied methods are satisfied. Stationarity is defined as the 
condition in which the mean and variance of the series remain 
constant over time, and the autocovariances depend only on

the lag length. When working with a non -stationary time 
series, regression analysis involving such series may lead to 
the problem of spurious or m eaningless regression [3 1]. 
Although stationarity can be preliminarily assessed through 
graphical analysis, more advanced detection methods involve 
the use of autocorrelation functions (ACF), partial 
autocorrelation functions (PACF), and unit root tests (such as 
the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test). In this study, stationarity 
analysis was performed using the ADF test. The hypotheses 
for the ADF test were formulated as follows: H 0: The series is 
non-stationary, H 1: The series is stationary. Failure to reject 
the null hypothesis (when 𝑝 ≥ 0.05 at a Type I error level of 
0.05) indicates that the series is non-stationary. The results of 
the ADF test are presented in Table 4. 
According to the ADF test results, the PM₁₀ series was found 
to be non -stationary (p≥0.05), whereas the SO 2 series was 
considered stationary at the 95% confidence level, as its p -
value was below 0.05 (Table 4). For the non-stationary series, 
first-order differencing was applied to achieve stationarity, 
and the transformed series were then incorp orated into the 
modeling stage.

sample (January 2019 –June 2024) and out -of-sample (July–
December 2024) periods, and model performance was 
evaluated using RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. The resulting best -
performing models and their parameter specifications for

components, which allows them to capture even weak 
seasonal effects, leading to better predictive performance. In 
contrast, within the Box -Jenkins framework, seasonality 
contributes to the model only if it is pronounced and regular; 
therefore, the SARIMA model did not provide additional 
benefits over the non-seasonal ARIMA model.

An examination of the out -of-sample results presented in 
Table 8 reveals that the lowest error values for the SO2 series 
were obtained from the Holt-Winters (multiplicative) model. 
While the Holt -Winters (additive) model produced better 
results during the in-sample period, the multiplicative model 
outperformed it in the out-of-sample period primarily due to 
changes i n the amplitude of the seasonal fluctuations. The 
additive model assumes that seasonal effects remain constant 
in magnitude, whereas the multiplicative model allows these 
effects to vary in proportion to the series level. During the test 
period, the increa se in SO 2 levels was accompanied by a 
proportional increase in the magnitude of seasonal 
fluctuations. Consequently, the multiplicative model was 
better able to capture this changing amplitude pattern, 
resulting in lower error values. Therefore, the Holt -Winters 
(multiplicative) model, which effectively accounts for both 
trend and proportional seasonality components, was 
ultimately identified as the most suitable model for 
forecasting the SO2 series.

Overall, while direct numerical comparison with previous 
studies such as Temiz and Turgut [11] is not feasible due to 
differences in data scope and modeling frameworks, the 
present findings conceptually complement earlier Box –
Jenkins–based research by expanding the analysis to multiple 
pollutants and confirming the consisten t superiority of the 
Holt–Winters multiplicative model in capturing both trend 
and proportional seasonality. Moreover, the results are in line 
with those of Cekim [12], who compared ETS and ARIMA 
models for PM₁₀ forecasting across Turkey’s most polluted 
cities and found that ETS models could outperform ARIMA 
under certain urban conditions. In this sense, the present 
study contributes uniquely to the Turkish air quality 
literature by providing a detailed ETS –Box–Jenkins 
comparison for both PM₁₀ and SO₂ speci fically in the 
Çanakkale region, thereby offering a region –focused 
statistical framework that can serve as a reference for future 
local air pollution forecasting studies. 
 
CONCLUSIONS 
 
This study conducted a comprehensive comparison of 
Exponential Smoothing and Box -Jenkins models for 
forecasting air pollution in Çanakkale, focusing on PM 10 and 
SO2, which were identified as the most influential pollutants 
through factor analysis. Stationarity tests revealed that PM₁₀ 
required first-order differencing, while SO2 was stationary at 
level. The forecasting results demonstrated that ETS methods 
generally outperformed Box -Jenkins models in terms of 
predictive accuracy. For PM₁₀, the Holt -Winters 
multiplicative model achieved the lowest RMSE and MAPE 
values, effectively capturing both trend and seasonal 
variations. Similarly, for SO 2, the Holt-Winters multiplicative 
model provided the best performance, indicating that even 
weak seasonal components, when modeled appropriately, 
enhance forecast accuracy. Conversely, the Box -Jenkins 
methodology showed no difference between ARIMA and 
SARIMA models, highlighting the limited role of seasonality in 
these series. Overall, the findings emphasize that ETS 
methods offer greater flexibility in incorporating trend and 
seasonal patterns, making them superior for forecasting air 
pollutant concentrations in regions with complex temporal 
dynamics.
