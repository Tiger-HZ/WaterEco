Environmental contamination and human 
health risk assessment of trace element 
exposure through diverse water sources

Abstract 
Human activities such as industrial effluents, agricultural run-off, and other land-
based sources of pollution contaminate water with trace elements, posing threats 
to both the environment and human health. This study investigates concentrations 
of three trace elements, namely nickel (Ni), lead (Pb), and copper (Cu), in an assessment 
of health risks. The study comprised three different sources of water, namely surface 
water (SW), groundwater (GW), and industrial water (IW), sampled from six tehsils 
of District Bahawalpur, namely Bahawalpur, Bahawalpur Saddar, Hasilpur, Ahmedpur 
East, Yazman, and Khairpur Tamewali. A total of thirty water sampling sites, ten sites 
for each of the three types of water sources, were selected, and water samples 
were collected in the months of August, September, and October 2025. Results 
of the studied trace elements showed the highest concentrations in industrial water 
samples, followed by GW and SW samples (p < 0.001), with the highest concentration 
of Ni being 1.99 ± 0.20 mg/L, while that of Pb was 2.55 ± 0.11 mg/L and that of 
Cu was 3.24 ± 0.18 mg/L in IW samples. Significant differences were observed 
in the concentrations of the three studied trace elements in water samples as well 
as among the sampling sites of three types of water sources (p < 0.001). Estimated 
oral and dermal exposure (EEoral and EEderm) indicated low health exposure risks. 
However, THQoral values for Pb in GW and Ni, Pb and Cu in IW samples were found 
to exceed the acceptable threshold values. In addition to THQoral values, estimated 
cumulative non-carcinogenic health risks in the form of TTHQoral values for all three 
types of water sources under study were > 1 in all three water sources, suggesting 
potential risks. While, estimated carcinogenic health risks for the three types of water 
sources under study were found to be within the acceptable limits for all the studied 
health risks. This study provided a better understanding of various pollutants and their 
concentrations in water sources at the studied location.

1 Introduction
Water is an indispensable natural resource that sustains human health, ecosystem 
integrity, agricultural productivity, industrial development, and socioeconomic growth 
[11]. Although nearly 71% of the Earth’s surface is covered by water, freshwater accounts 
for less than 3% of the global water reserves, and only a small fraction is readily accessible 
for human consumption and domestic use [64]. Rapid population growth, urbanization, 
industrial expansion, agricultural intensification, and climate change have substantially 
increased the demand for freshwater resources while simultaneously accelerating their 
deterioration in quality [64]. Consequently, ensuring access to safe drinking water has 
become one of the foremost environmental and public health challenges worldwide 
and remains a central objective of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 6 
(SDG-6), which aims to ensure universal access to safe and clean water [28].
Among the numerous contaminants threatening freshwater resources, trace 
elements have received considerable scientific attention because of their persistence, 
non-biodegradability, environmental mobility, and potential to accumulate in aquatic 
ecosystems and living organisms. Unlike many organic pollutants that undergo 
degradation over time, trace elements remain in environmental compartments for 
prolonged periods and continuously cycle through water, sediments, soils, and biota. 
Their accumulation in drinking water has therefore become a major environmental 
concern owing to their adverse ecological impacts and their ability to cause long-term 
human exposure [3, 10], Vijay [63]. Increasing concentrations of trace elements in 
freshwater ecosystems have been associated with declining water quality, disruption of 
aquatic biodiversity, ecological imbalance, and growing risks to human health [41, 66].
Trace elements in aquatic environments are contributed by geogenic (natural) 
processes as well as by anthropogenic sources. Naturally, the metal-bearing rocks are 
weathered, minerals are dissolved, volcanic material is emitted, and soil is eroded to 
contribute to the surface water as well as to the groundwater. However, in recent decades, 
the anthropogenic sources have contributed to the majority of the contamination of the 
aquatic environments, i.e. industrial effluents, mining activities, municipal wastewater, 
agricultural runoff, fossil fuel combustion, metal processing industries, application of 
fertilizers and pesticides, landfill leachates, and rapid urbanization ecosystems [10, 52, 
62]. These contaminants, introduced into the environment through various sources, are 
transported to different aquatic environments through runoff, atmospheric deposition, 
infiltration and groundwater recharge, thereby degrading both surface and groundwaters 
[62]. The untreated industrial and municipal wastewaters are continuously being 
discharged into the environment, and in developing countries, the lack of wastewater 
treatment facilities adds to the contamination in aquatic environments [39].
The contamination of trace elements in water is creating serious health problems 
as these elements can easily enter the human body through water. Some of the health 
effects caused by chronic exposure to trace elements are neurological problems, kidney 
problems, cardiovascular diseases, liver damage, skeletal problems, reproductive 
problems, developmental abnormalities and various types of cancer [16, 17], Vijay [63]. 
Therefore, to ensure the health of the people and to provide safe drinking water, guideline 
values for trace elements in drinking water have been established by international 
organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO) [43].

Different trace elements have different biological functions and therefore they 
cannot be considered in a single group. While copper (Cu) is required for the human 
body in small quantities to carry out various physiological and biochemical activities 
like enzymatic activities, cellular respiration, antioxidant activities, formation of 
connective tissues, etc., high levels of this element can cause toxicity. It can cause 
disturbances in cellular activities, can cause increased oxidative stress, and can cause 
toxicity to the liver, kidneys, and gastrointestinal tract [19]. On the other hand, lead 
(Pb) is a non-essential element and has no known biological function in the human 
body. It is considered to be one of the most toxic elements found on the earth, and 
it causes cumulative toxicity to the human body. Even low levels of this element can 
cause toxicity to the human body after prolonged exposure. It can cause toxicity to 
the nervous system, blood, kidneys, heart, and reproductive organs [50]. While nickel 
(Ni) is naturally occurring and is widely used in various industrial applications, it 
is not required for the human body and can cause toxicity to the human body after 
prolonged exposure. It can cause various health problems like allergic skin reactions, 
respiratory problems, immunotoxicity, and carcinogenicity [16]. Therefore, it is very 
important to distinguish between essential and non-essential trace elements to find 
out their environmental behaviour, toxicological properties, and potential human 
health risks.
There is a constant struggle to provide safe drinking water in Pakistan as the country 
grapples to cope with the effects of rapid urbanization, industrial growth, lack of 
effective wastewater management, intensification of agricultural activities, and a surging 
population. Most of the country’s drinking water is sourced from groundwater, which is 
being continuously threatened and has thus become a matter of national public health 
concern, requiring adequate protection [46, 49]. Water from different sources, including 
surface water, groundwater, wastewater, and industrial effluent, is being contaminated 
with industrial waste, agricultural runoff containing a mix of agricultural chemicals, 
sewage from cities and mines, and geological processes of the natural environment [21, 
23]. Furthermore, the large volume of water being generated from seasonal flooding 
and uncontrolled urban growth is being freely discharged into all types of freshwater 
resources of the country and causing unprecedented water pollution, leading to 
widespread occurrence of water-borne diseases and serious chemical contamination 
[1]. National assessments conducted by the Pakistan Council of Research in Water 
Resources (PCRWR) have also demonstrated that a considerable proportion of drinking 
water sources fail to comply with recommended national and international water 
quality standards, highlighting the urgent need for continuous monitoring and effective 
management of drinking water resources [24].
Recent international studies have considerably advanced drinking water quality 
assessment by integrating trace element determination with pollution indices, 
multivariate statistical analyses, geospatial techniques, and quantitative human health 
risk assessment models. Such integrated approaches have enabled researchers to 
identify contamination sources, distinguish between geogenic and anthropogenic 
inputs, and evaluate both non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks associated with 
long-term exposure to contaminated drinking water. These comprehensive assessment 
frameworks provide a more robust understanding of the relationships between trace

element contamination and public health and have become increasingly important for 
developing evidence-based environmental management strategies [42, 53].
In Pakistan, numerous investigations have assessed drinking water quality and the 
occurrence of trace elements in different provinces, including Punjab, Sindh, Khyber 
Pakhtunkhwa, and Balochistan. These studies have consistently demonstrated that 
industrial effluents, agricultural runoff, municipal wastewater, mining activities, and 
natural geological processes contribute substantially to trace element contamination 
in both groundwater and surface water [12]. Several investigations have further 
evaluated the potential human health risks associated with contaminated drinking water 
using hazard quotient (HQ), hazard index (HI), and carcinogenic risk (CR) models. 
While these studies have significantly improved the understanding of drinking water 
contamination in Pakistan, many have been limited to specific geographical regions, 
individual water sources, or selected contaminants [13]. Moreover, most investigations 
have primarily focused on reporting contaminant concentrations and basic health risk 
indices, whereas comprehensive comparisons among multiple water sources using 
integrated health risk assessment frameworks remain relatively limited [39].
Research worldwide is utilizing trace element determination, water pollution 
assessment indices, water quality statistics analysis, GIS spatial analysis, and human 
health risk assessment, etc., to evaluate drinking water source contamination, its 
causes, geogenic vs. anthropogenic, water quality health risks, etc. [42, 56, 58]. 
Comprehensive studies on drinking water source contamination assessment utilizing 
the above approaches have not only supported scientists and decision-makers in 
better understanding the relationship between water contamination by trace elements 
and water-borne health risks but also have enabled them in planning, designing, and 
implementing effective environment-friendly health management measures.
Nevertheless, contamination characteristics vary considerably among different 
geographical regions because of differences in geology, land use, industrial development, 
agricultural practices, and population density. Consequently, region-specific 
investigations remain essential for generating reliable baseline data and supporting local 
water quality management [7]. Despite these important advances, significant knowledge 
gaps remain regarding the distribution of trace elements and their associated human 
health risks in several rapidly developing regions of Pakistan. Southern Punjab, despite 
experiencing increasing urbanization, agricultural intensification, industrial growth, and 
expanding municipal activities, has received comparatively less attention than many 
other parts of the country. Bahawalpur District relies predominantly on groundwater 
for domestic consumption, while surface water and industrial water also contribute to 
meeting municipal, agricultural, and industrial demands. However, comprehensive 
information regarding trace element contamination across these different water 
sources and its potential implications for human health remains limited. To the best of 
our knowledge, no previous study has systematically compared groundwater, surface 
water, and industrial water within Bahawalpur District while simultaneously evaluating 
trace element contamination together with quantitative human health risk assessment 
using internationally accepted risk assessment models. This represents an important 
knowledge gap that must be addressed to support evidence-based environmental 
management and public health protection in the region.

Copper (Cu), lead (Pb) and nickel (Ni) are among the most common trace elements, 
which have been reported in various studies conducted on drinking water quality 
around the world. These trace elements occur in the environment due to geogenic as 
well as anthropogenic sources, such as industrial effluents, metal processing units, 
municipal wastewater, agricultural activities, corrosion of water distribution pipes, fossil 
fuel combustion and weathering of metal bearing rocks [37]. These elements can enter 
drinking water sources from these sources and can be used as indicators of water quality 
with reference to environmental hazards. Concentrations of these trace elements in 
drinking water are reported in the form of guidelines by national as well as international 
organizations. Other trace elements such as cadmium (Cd), arsenic (As) and mercury 
(Hg) are also considered as very toxic elements but have not been included in the present 
study, as Cu, Pb and Ni are very common, persistent in environment, having pronounced 
adverse health effects in human being and have been reported in vast number of studies 
all over the world. Moreover, these three elements are included in the drinking water 
quality guidelines all over the world and, therefore, inclusion of these three elements 
in the present study would provide a solid base to correlate concentrations of these 
elements with contamination as well as health risks to the consumers of water under 
study.
Unlike many previous investigations conducted in Pakistan that evaluated either 
groundwater or surface water independently or primarily reported contaminant 
concentrations, the present study adopts a comprehensive approach by simultaneously 
assessing trace element, copper (Cu), lead (Pb), and nickel (Ni) concentrations in 
groundwater, surface water, and industrial water collected from six tehsils of Bahawalpur 
District. Furthermore, quantitative human health risk assessment models were 
employed to evaluate the potential non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks associated 
with oral and dermal exposure to these trace elements through drinking water 
consumption. The findings provide a comprehensive baseline dataset for Bahawalpur 
District and contribute valuable scientific evidence for improving drinking water quality 
management, protecting public health, and supporting the sustainable management of 
freshwater resources in southern Punjab, Pakistan.
2 Materials and methods
2.1 Study area
This study investigated the concentrations of nickel (Ni), lead (Pb), and copper (Cu) in 
water samples collected from three major water sources, namely surface water (SW), 
groundwater (GW), and industrial water (IW), and evaluated the associated human 
health risks. Water samples were collected from representative groundwater, surface 
water, and industrial water sources located across six tehsils of District Bahawalpur, 
Punjab, Pakistan, namely Bahawalpur City, Bahawalpur Saddar, Ahmadpur East, 
Yazman, Hasilpur, and Khairpur Tamewali. The geographic coordinates of all 
sampling locations were recorded using a Global Positioning System (GPS) to 
facilitate site identification, spatial visualization, and future monitoring (Table  1). 
The corresponding tehsil for each sampling location was identified using reverse 
geocoding based on the recorded geographic coordinates. In total, the 30 sampling 
sites were distributed among Bahawalpur City (n = 6), Bahawalpur Saddar (n = 6), 
Page 6 of 23Naz et al. Discover Environment (2026) 4:515 
Ahmadpur East (n = 6), Yazman (n = 6), Hasilpur (n = 3), and Khairpur Tamewali 
(n = 3). The study area map (Fig.  1) was prepared using QGIS version 4.2.1 (Belém 
do Pará). Administrative boundary datasets at the provincial, district, and tehsil 
levels were obtained from the Humanitarian Data Exchange (HDX) database (https:// 
data. humda ta. org/), while the hydrographic network (major rivers and canals) was 
incorporated using publicly available HDX waterway datasets. The georeferenced 
sampling locations were overlaid on the administrative boundary and hydrographic 
layers, and distinct symbols and colors were used to differentiate surface water, 
groundwater, and industrial water sampling sites. The final figure layout, including 
map composition, cartographic elements, annotations, and graphical refinements, 
was completed using Inkscape version 1.4.2 (f4327f4, 2025-05-13). Ten sampling sites 
were selected for each water source (SW, GW, and IW), and three replicate samples 
were collected from each site. Sampling was conducted between August and October 
2025.

2.2 Collection, transportation and storage of water samples
A representative sampling approach was adopted to collect water samples from 
three different water sources. Groundwater, surface water, and industrial water were 
selected as representative water sources because groundwater is extensively used 
for drinking purposes, surface water supports aquatic ecosystems and is widely 
used for domestic and livestock activities, while industrial effluents discharged into 
drainage channels ultimately enter freshwater systems and constitute a major source 
of trace element contamination. For each water source, ten sampling points were 
selected from different locations across the study area based on their accessibility 
and suitability for sample collection and transportation while ensuring representative 
coverage of the respective water source. Groundwater sampling points were selected 
from water pumps located within mosque premises because these water sources are 
routinely used by local communities for drinking and ablution purposes. Sampling 
points of surface water sources were selected from major canals in different 
geographical locations of the study area. Sampling points of industrial water sources 
were chosen from discharge points of active sugar mills, textile mills, flour mills, and 
oil mills. Industrial water samples were collected at the discharge outlets in the drain 
or the water body. This was done right after the water had been discharged into the 
drain or water body. The surface water and industrial water samples were collected 
at a depth of approximately 150 cm below water surface to minimize the influence of 
surface debris and floating contaminants.
The collected samples of water were stored in pre-cleaned high-density polyethylene 
(HDPE) bottles. Before the samples were collected, the HDPE bottles were cleaned 
with laboratory detergent and then rinsed with tap water, immersed in 5% (v/v) nitric 
acid solution for more than 24 h, and rinsed again several times with double-distilled 
deionized water. Each sample bottle was rinsed two to three times with water from the 
sampling site immediately prior to sampling in order to condition the bottle without 
diluting the sample because there might be some deionized water left in the bottle 
after cleaning. Field rinsing was done purely for conditioning purposes and not for the 
purpose of changing the concentration of trace elements. After collection of samples, 
bottles were closed, placed in a portable insulated ice box containing ice packs, and 
maintained at temperatures 6 ± 3 °C using ice packs during transportation. Samples 
were stored at appropriate conditions in the Zoology Laboratory, Government Sadiq 
College Women University, Bahawalpur for subsequent analysis.
2.3 Sample processing and digestion
A total of 90 water samples (30 samples from each water source) were collected from 
the selected sampling sites. These samples were analyzed for the concentration of Ni, 
Pb, and Cu to carry out human health risk assessment.
50 ml of each water sample was placed in a beaker and left to cool down. 1 ml of 
concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃; 69–72%) and 2.5 ml of concentrated hydrochloric 
acid (HCl; 30%) were added to the beaker, and the beaker was placed on a hot plate 
(Westpoint Double Hot Plate WF-262). The mixture was then heated to 90 °C and left 
to mix until the volume of the mixture had decreased to approximately 20 ml.

After cooling, the solutions were filtered through Whatman No. 42 filter paper in 
a 100 mL volumetric flask. The solutions were then diluted to volume with deionized 
water. 2 mL of concentrated HNO₃ was added to the solutions to ensure complete 
acidification and to obtain clear solutions. The solutions were then diluted with 
3% (v/v) nitric acid according to the USEPA digestion protocol. After filtration and 
settling, the solutions were transferred into pre-cleaned sample containers which 
had been sterilized at 135°C. The samples were then sealed properly and stored until 
the start of the trace element analysis. For trace element analysis of digested samples 
Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (AAS), Analytik Jena NovAA 400P , at Central 
Laboratory, Muhammad Nawaz Sharif University of Agriculture, Multan, was used.
2.4 Determination of trace elements
Prior to analysis, the Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (AJ NovAA 400P , Analytik 
Jena, Germany) was optimized under the manufacturer’s recommended flame conditions 
for the determination of Ni, Pb, and Cu. Double-distilled and deionized water was used 
to prepare all working solutions and served as the reagent blank. Calibration standards 
of 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 mg L–1 were prepared from certified stock standard solutions. 
Calibration curves were generated by plotting absorbance against the corresponding 
standard concentrations and fitting the data using linear least-squares regression (y = a 
+ bx). Blank correction was applied prior to the quantification of sample concentrations. 
The concentrations of Ni, Pb, and Cu were determined according to the Standard 
Methods for the Examination of Water and Wastewater [4], Methods 3500-Ni B, 3500-Pb 
B, and 3500-Cu B). The trace element analyses were performed at an external analytical 
laboratory following the standard analytical protocol. Three independent water samples 
collected from each sampling location were analysed separately. One concentration value 
was obtained from each independently collected sample using AAS, and the results are 
presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of the three independent samples.

3 Results
The concentrations of Ni, Pb, and Cu in surface water (SW), groundwater (GW), and 
industrial water (IW) at ten sampling sites in District Bahawalpur, Punjab, Pakistan are 
presented in Table  2. Spatial variation in trace element concentrations at the sampling 
sites within the three water types was observed.
The range of concentrations for Ni, Pb, and Cu in surface water samples is shown 
in Table  2. The highest concentration of Ni was found at Site 5, with a concentration 
of 0.43 ± 0.02 mg/L, whereas Sites 6 and 10 had the lowest concentrations with values 
of 0.08 ± 0.02  mg/L. For Pb, the highest concentration was found at Site 3 with a 
concentration of 0.44 ± 0.01  mg/L. The lowest concentration of Pb was found at Site 
5. For Cu, the highest concentration was found at Site 10 with a concentration of 
0.63 ± 0.04  mg/L, whereas the lowest concentration of Cu was found at Site 4 with a 
concentration of 0.45 ± 0.03 mg/L.
Concentrations of trace elements in groundwater at the study area showed 
considerable spatial variations. Nickel concentrations in the groundwater samples ranged 
from 0.38 ± 0.02  mg/L to 0.48 ± 0.03  mg/L. The highest concentration of nickel was 
found in the sample collected from Site 8, whereas the lowest concentration was found 
in the sample collected from Site 7. Lead concentrations in the groundwater samples 
ranged from 0.68 ± 0.05 mg/L to 0.75 ± 0.06 mg/L. The highest concentration of lead in 
the groundwater samples was found to be 0.75 ± 0.06 mg/L at Sites 9 and 4. The samples 
from Sites 1, 4 and 9 contained 0.74 ± 0.03 mg/L, 0.75 ± 0.04 mg/L and 0.75 ± 0.06 mg/L 
of lead, respectively. The lowest concentration of lead in the groundwater samples was 
found to be 0.68 ± 0.05 mg/L at Sites 2 and 8. Copper concentrations in the groundwater 
samples ranged from 0.86 ± 0.04 mg/L to 0.96 ± 0.08 mg/L. The highest concentration of 
copper was found to be 0.96 ± 0.08 mg/L at Site 8. The lowest concentration of copper 
was found to be 0.86 ± 0.04 mg/L at Site 5.
A very large number of samples from industry contained high concentrations of 
the three investigated trace elements. The concentration of the element nickel in all

4 Discussion
In the present study, the concentration of Ni, Pb and Cu in surface water, groundwater 
and industrial water from six different locations in District Bahawalpur, Punjab, Pakistan 
was assessed, and their potential health risk to humans was determined. Our results 
showed a significant difference in the concentration of trace elements in various samples 
of water, and the highest concentration of trace elements was found in industrial water, 
followed by groundwater and surface water. Among the three trace elements, the highest 
concentration of Cu was found in industrial water and groundwater samples, followed 
by Pb in surface water samples. The highest concentration of Pb was found in surface 
water samples.
There is abundant evidence from Pakistan to support the fact that high concentrations 
of trace elements in groundwater and industrial water are because of high levels of 
industrial effluents, municipal wastewater, agricultural runoff, and natural geochemical 
processes [6, 32]. In developing countries like Pakistan, groundwater is the main source 
of drinking water for the majority of the population, whereas surface water is used for 
various purposes including domestic use, agriculture, and irrigation [38, 51]. Therefore,

as long-term exposure to high concentrations of trace elements could lead to health 
problems. Relatively low concentrations of trace elements in surface water may be due 
to dilution, high flow rates and dispersion of contaminants, whereas high concentrations 
in groundwater and industrial water may be due to accumulation of contaminants 
over long periods, limited natural dilution, and continued input of contaminants from 
anthropogenic activities [40].
The comparatively lower concentrations observed in surface water may be attributed 
to continuous water movement, dilution by rainfall and canal inflows, sedimentation 
processes, and shorter residence times, all of which reduce dissolved trace element 
concentrations. Nevertheless, surface water remains vulnerable to contamination 
through agricultural runoff, municipal wastewater discharge, atmospheric deposition, 
and urban stormwater, particularly during periods of intensive irrigation and seasonal 
rainfall [52]. These findings align with prior research that has reported similar results 
in various districts and regions across Pakistan. Numerous previous studies conducted 
within the country have also revealed the presence of high concentrations of dangerous 
metals in groundwater that is typically used for drinking [34, 36, 48].
Groundwater contamination is likely associated with the gradual migration of 
dissolved trace elements through soil profiles and aquifer materials. Leaching from 
contaminated surface environments, infiltration of agricultural runoff, seepage from 
industrial disposal sites, leakage from domestic wastewater systems, and weathering of 
local geological formations may collectively contribute to groundwater contamination. 
Because groundwater generally has a longer residence time than surface water, dissolved 
trace elements may accumulate over extended periods, resulting in comparatively 
higher concentrations. Furthermore, hydrogeological characteristics, including aquifer 
composition, soil permeability, groundwater flow dynamics, and recharge conditions, 
strongly influence the transport and persistence of trace elements within groundwater 
systems [62].
Agricultural activities also represent an important non-point source of trace element 
contamination within the study area. Long-term application of phosphate fertilizers, 
pesticides, livestock manure, and irrigation return flows may gradually enrich 
agricultural soils with trace elements that are subsequently transported into nearby 
surface waters through runoff or infiltrate groundwater through percolation. Therefore, 
the contamination observed in the present study is likely the combined result of 
industrial emissions, agricultural practices, domestic wastewater discharge, and natural 
geological processes rather than a single pollution source [10, 52, 55].
Environmental contamination by heavy metals is considered a major environmental 
problem, particularly in aquatic environments, due to their toxic nature. Moreover, 
the toxic effects of heavy metals increase with the presence of other heavy metals 
[33, 39]. In the present study, the atomic absorption spectrophotometric method was 
used to determine the concentration of heavy metals including Chromium (Cr), Iron 
(Fe), Nickel (Ni), Cadmium (Cd), Lead (Pb), Zinc (Zn), Cobalt (Co) and Manganese 
(Mn) in the water samples collected from the groundwater of industrial area of Kasur, 
Pakistan. The concentrations of these heavy metals were found to be higher than the 
water quality standards for these parameters as specified by World Health Organization 
Page 17 of 23
Naz et al. Discover Environment (2026) 4:515 
 
(WHO). The concentrations of heavy metals in the water samples of Kasur industrial 
area were found to be in the following order: Chromium (Cr) > Lead (Pb) > Iron (Fe) 
> Cadmium (Cd) > Nickel (Ni) > Cobalt (Co) > Manganese (Mn) > Zinc (Zn). Among 
these heavy metals, the concentration of Chromium (Cr) was found to be highest 
with an average value of 1.320 mg/L. The industrial area of Kasur is reported to be the 
major source of most of these toxic heavy metals [41]. In a study conducted in Sialkot, 
Pakistan, the concentrations of various heavy metals in the groundwater were found to 
be higher than the acceptable values specified by World Health Organization (WHO) 
for these parameters. These included concentrations of Nickel (Ni) at 0.10 mg/L, Copper 
(Cu) at 0.06 mg/L, Lead (Pb) at 0.49 mg/L, Manganese (Mn) at 0.03 mg/L, Iron (Fe) at 
0.30  mg/L, Chromium (Cr) at 0.03  mg/L and Zinc (Zn) at 0.16  mg/L. The area under 
study was divided into six regions. Based on the results, it was observed that the extent 
and causes of groundwater pollution were different in all the regions of the study area. 
In another study, the concentrations of lead in the untreated and treated surface water 
samples collected from 18 districts of Karachi were found to be within the permissible 
limits. The mean concentrations of lead in untreated and treated surface water samples 
were found to be higher in groundwater than in surface water (Haq et al. 2011).
This work found elevated levels of trace elements similar to a number of earlier 
surveys conducted in Pakistan. All of these studies reported widespread contamination 
of groundwater by human activities. Trace elements are among the more persistent 
environmental contaminants and can have toxicity for ecological organisms and human 
health. However, impact can vary greatly when more than one element is present at the 
same time [18, 39, 54]. Water from tubewells in Kasur industrial area contained levels 
of Cr, Fe, Ni, Cd, Pb, Zn, Co and Mn that were higher than the WHO recommended 
levels for drinking water by a factor of four to twenty-six. Industrial activities were 
the principal cause of these high levels [41]. Groundwater from Sialkot contained high 
levels of Ni, Cu, Pb, Mn, Fe, Cr and Zn that were above the WHO recommended levels 
for drinking water. The levels of these trace elements in groundwater from different 
parts of Sialkot were found to vary greatly (Ul Haq et  al. 2011). Seasonal variation in 
levels of trace elements in groundwater was reported in another study. In that work 
Pb, Zn and Cu were found to be the most persistent contaminating the groundwater 
during winter, while Ni and Cr were found to be the most persistent during summer; 
thus, hydrogeological factors as well as season could affect the distribution of these 
contaminants in groundwater [26]. Recently, it was reported that the levels of several 
trace elements in tube-well water from southern Punjab were at levels that could pose 
serious risks to human health [47]. A study that used water samples from rural tube-
wells in Tehsil Jhang found that 67% of the water samples from these locations contained 
levels of trace elements that exceeded the WHO recommended limits for these 
contaminants in drinking water [31, 33].
The significantly higher concentrations of Ni, Pb, and Cu observed in industrial 
water indicate that anthropogenic activities are the dominant contributors to trace 
element contamination in the study area. Bahawalpur has experienced increasing 
industrialization, including textile processing, metal fabrication, agro-based industries, 
fertilizer production, engineering workshops, and municipal wastewater discharge. 
Effluents generated from these activities may contain elevated concentrations of trace 
Page 18 of 23Naz et al. Discover Environment (2026) 4:515 
elements that are discharged directly or indirectly into nearby drains and water bodies. 
The elevated concentrations observed in industrial water therefore reflect continuous 
inputs from untreated or partially treated industrial effluents, whereas comparatively 
lower concentrations in surface and groundwater suggest dilution, natural attenuation, 
and geochemical filtration processes. Ni and Cu levels in the different water samples 
of the investigated area were found to be above the WHO recommended values for 
drinking water, for most of the samples (Fig. 2 and Table 2). These levels of contamination 
would mostly be due to increased industrial activities of industrialization, increased 
agricultural activities, the presence of untreated wastewater, and others that are known 
to cause the pollution of trace elements in Pakistan [2, 36, 57].
Jehan et al. [27] reported elevated levels of Pb and Cu in water samples from industrial 
estates of Pakistan. Concentrations of trace elements in industrial water are substantially 
higher than in surface water and groundwater. Industrial effluents are mostly untreated 
or are being treated inadequately, leading to the release of contaminated water into 
the environment. In aquatic systems, trace elements can accumulate in sediments and 
persist for long periods of time. These elements can be taken up by benthic organisms 
and get transferred to the upper food chain through aquatic food webs, posing ecological 
as well as human health risks [2, 22]. High levels of trace elements in sediments of Nullah 
Aik have been reported by Qadir et al. [45]. In the midstream section of the Nullah, the 
highest levels of trace elements were found in industrial effluents. In the present study, 
it was observed that industrial water contained higher levels of Ni, Pb, and Cu than 
groundwater and surface water, indicating that industrial activities are a major source of 
trace elements in the study area.
Similar contamination patterns have been reported in mining and industrial 
regions worldwide, where elevated concentrations of Ni, Pb, and Cu have been linked

Elevated concentrations of trace elements may adversely affect aquatic ecosystems 
through their accumulation in sediments and subsequent transfer into aquatic 
food webs. Because trace elements are persistent and non-biodegradable, they 
may bioaccumulate in aquatic organisms and biomagnify through higher trophic 
levels, thereby impairing growth, reproduction, physiological functions, and overall 
ecosystem stability. Consequently, industrial wastewater not only represents a 
potential risk to human health but also threatens freshwater biodiversity and 
ecological integrity [9 , 22].
This study demonstrated that the trace elements in ground and surface water 
have a direct impact on human consumption, while the impact of industrial water is 
indirectly effective. This is because the surface and ground water are directly used by 
the human population. Industrial effluents can enter receiving surface-water systems, 
contaminate sediments, and potentially contribute to groundwater contamination 
through infiltration, thereby creating indirect pathways for ecological and human 
exposure. Trace elements released from industrial and other anthropogenic sources 
may also accumulate in aquatic organisms and move through aquatic food webs, 
potentially increasing ecological and human health risks [12, 20]. In coherence with 
the current study, the target hazard quotient (THQoral) for Pb in drinking water from 
the groundwater was recorded to be higher, indicating a significant risk as reported 
by Boumaza et al. [8 ] and Naz et al. [41].
The elevated THQ values observed for Pb in groundwater and for all three 
investigated trace elements in industrial water indicate that prolonged exposure 
may pose unacceptable non-carcinogenic health risks, particularly through oral 
consumption of contaminated water. Children are especially vulnerable because of 
their higher water intake relative to body weight and their developing nervous and 
immune systems. Although carcinogenic risk values remained below internationally 
accepted thresholds, continuous exposure to trace elements may increase cumulative 
health burdens over time, emphasizing the need for regular monitoring, effective 
water treatment, and preventive environmental management [30].
Overall, the findings indicate that anthropogenic activities, particularly industrial 
discharges and intensive agricultural practices, are the principal drivers of trace 
element contamination in the investigated water sources. The observed differences 
among industrial, groundwater, and surface water reflect variations in contaminant 
inputs, hydrogeological transport processes, and natural attenuation mechanisms. 
These findings emphasize the need for routine monitoring, effective wastewater 
treatment, stricter industrial discharge regulations, and sustainable agricultural 
management to protect drinking water resources and minimize long-term ecological 
and human health risks.

5 Conclusion
This study evaluated the concentration of nickel (Ni), lead (Pb), and copper (Cu) in 
surface water, groundwater, and industrial water collected from 6th tehsils of District 
Bahawalpur, Punjab, Pakistan, and assessed the associated human health risks. The 
results demonstrated significant differences among the three water sources, with 
industrial water exhibiting the highest trace element concentrations, followed by 
groundwater and surface water. Although dermal exposure and carcinogenic risk 
remained within acceptable limits, the THQ and TTHQ values for oral exposure 
exceeded the recommended safety threshold in several water sources, indicating 
potential non-carcinogenic health risks associated with the long-term consumption of 
untreated water. These findings emphasize the importance of continuous monitoring 
and effective management of drinking water quality in the study area.
The findings provide valuable baseline information for regional water quality 
management. Policymakers and environmental authorities should strengthen and 
routine water quality surveillance, enforce sticker regulations on industrial effluent 
discharge, and implement integrated pollution control strategies to minimize trace 
element contamination from industrial, agricultural, and domestic sources. Water 
treatment agencies should prioritize appropriate treatment technologies and regular 
monitoring to ensure compliance with the national and international drinking water 
quality standards, while public awareness programs should be promoted to reduce 
human exposure to contaminated water.
The present study provides important baseline information on the occurrence of Ni, 
Pb, and Cu and their associated human health risk in the water sources investigated 
of district Bahawalpur. Future studies could further strength these findings by 
incorporating seasonal monitoring, a broader range of potentially toxic elements and 
microbial contaminants, child-specific exposure assessment, hydrogeochemical, and 
source apportionment analyses, and advanced health risk assessment approaches to 
provide a more comprehensive understanding of water contamination and support 
evidence-based water resource management in Bahawalpur and other regions facing 
similar environmental challenge.
