Abstract
Zinc (Zn) deficiency limits plant growth and productivity, yet the role of arbuscular 
mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) in Zn-deficiency tolerance in pea plants remains unclear. 
In this study, we investigated the effect of Rhizophagus irregularis on pea plants 
exposed to Zn deficiency in hydroponic conditions. Microscopic observation and 
qPCR-based quantification of AMF abundance confirmed successful colonization 
only in AMF-inoculated plants, accompanied by the presence of different AMF 
structures in the roots. Zn deficiency severely impaired plant health, resulting in 
reduced biomass, photosynthetic parameters, and Zn status in tissues. Zn deficiency 
also increased hydrogen peroxide accumulation, electrolyte leakage, and altered 
mineral homeostasis. However, AMF inoculation significantly alleviated these 
detrimental effects by improving growth performance, photosynthetic traits, and Zn 
accumulation under Zn-deficient conditions. AMF further induced the expression of 
Zn transporter-related genes ( ZIP1 and ZIP2), suggesting enhanced Zn acquisition 
in inoculated plants. In addition, Zn deficiency caused substantial reprogramming 
of amino acid metabolism, characterized by increased stress-associated amino acids 
and reductions in several growth-related amino acids, including leucine, isoleucine, 
glycine, and tyrosine. AMF inoculation restored several of these metabolites under 
Zn-deficient conditions, indicating metabolic adjustment associated with stress 
recovery and physiological stabilization. Importantly, split-root analysis showed 
improved shoot growth, biomass, and chlorophyll status even when AMF colonized 
only one portion of the root system, providing evidence for systemic regulation. 
Overall, these findings demonstrate that AMF alleviate Zn deficiency through 
coordinated regulation of Zn acquisition, metabolic adjustment, and systemic 
physiological responses, highlighting their potential for improving plant performance 
under Zn-limited conditions.

 1 Introduction
Zinc (Zn) participates in various physiological and biochemical processes that facilitate 
plant growth and survival. Although needed in small amounts, its deficiency or excess 
can significantly impact plant metabolism [7]. Zn is necessary for healthy root and shoot 
development, seed production, and stress tolerance [ 3]. Zn naturally occurs in soils of 
terrestrial ecosystems and is indispensable for plant growth due to its involvement in 
enzyme activation, protein synthesis, membrane integrity, and regulation of gene expres-
sion [8, 22].
Plants obtain Zn mainly from the soil solution, where Zn occurs predominantly as free 
Zn2⁺ (and, especially at higher pH, as hydroxo/carbonate/bicarbonate species), which 
is transported to root surfaces by mass flow and diffusion and then taken up by roots 
[27]. However, Zn availability is often limited in calcareous and alkaline soils, which are 
widespread in many agricultural regions, leading to widespread Zn deficiency in crops 
and consequent yield and nutritional losses [ 3, 8]. Zn deficiency is recognized as one 
of the most prevalent micronutrient constraints affecting crop productivity worldwide, 
particularly in legume-based systems where nutrient demand is high [ 3, 7]. In plants, 
Zn deficiency results in reduced chlorophyll synthesis, impaired photosynthesis, stunted 
growth, and disruption of hormonal balance, ultimately affecting both biomass accumu -
lation and reproductive development [24].
Certain fungi, particularly mycorrhizal fungi, play an important role in increasing Zn 
uptake in plants. Numerous symbiotic relationships between fungi and plants are essen -
tial to the stability of ecosystems, nutrient intake, and plant health [ 20, 21, 29]. Particu-
larly, arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) are essential for enhancing plant Zn nutrition 
and reducing the negative effects of Zn stress on plants [ 26, 29]. AMF form mutualistic 
associations with the roots of most terrestrial plants, extending their extraradical hyphal 
networks into the soil, thereby increasing the effective root surface area for nutrient 
acquisition [29]. This hyphal network allows plants to access sparingly available Zn pools 
beyond the rhizosphere depletion zone, which are otherwise inaccessible to non-mycor -
rhizal roots [ 21]. In addition to improving Zn uptake, AMF enhance plant tolerance to 
both biotic and abiotic stresses by improving soil structure, water relations, and nutrient 
balance [5, 6]. AMF colonization has also been shown to regulate the expression of Zn 
transporter genes (e.g., ZIP family transporters), thereby facilitating efficient Zn translo -
cation from fungal hyphae to plant root cortical cells [ 9, 31]. By facilitating the transfer 
of Zn from fungal hyphae to plant root cells, these transporters aid plants in overcoming 
Zn shortage. Furthermore, AMF can alter rhizosphere chemistry by releasing organic 
acids and chelating compounds that increase Zn solubility and bioavailability [ 29]. 
Therefore, AMF are widely recognized as promising biofertilizers for sustainable agricul-
ture, contributing to improved nutrient use efficiency and reduced reliance on chemical 
fertilizers [6].
Pea (Pisum sativum L.) is a major grain legume and an important plant-based protein 
source for both human and animal nutrition. It is valued for its high protein content, 
essential amino acids, and its ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiosis with 
rhizobia, making it a key component of sustainable cropping systems [ 12, 30]. However, 
pea is particularly sensitive to micronutrient deficiencies, including Zn deficiency, which 
can significantly reduce nodulation efficiency, nitrogen fixation, and overall productivity 
[24]. Despite the recognized benefits of AMF in improving micronutrient uptake, the

pea remains limited. Therefore, this study explores the role of AMF in mitigating Zn

acid profiles, and nutrient dynamics.
2 Materials and methods
2.1 Plant cultivation and AMF treatments
Pea seeds were obtained from the commercial seed company Circle Seed (Dhaka, Ban -
gladesh). The seeds were surface sterilized with 70% ethanol for 30  min, followed by 
three rinses with distilled water. They were then germinated on tissue paper in a ger -
mination tray at ambient temperature. Seeds were allowed to sprout on damp tissue 
paper, maintained with deionized water, in a dark atmosphere at room temperature for 
three days. Only healthy, uniform seedlings were selected for transplantation. In hydro -
ponic system, a half-strength basal nutrient solution [ 18] was used, with the following 
nutrient concentrations (μM): KNO 3 (16,000), Ca(NO 3)2.4H2O (6000), NH4H 2PO4 
(4000), MgSO 4.7H2O (2000), KCl (50), H3BO 3 (25), Fe-EDTA (25), MnSO 4.7H2O (2), 
Na2MoO4.2H2O (0.5), CuSO 4.5H2O (0.5), and Zn as ZnSO 4 at two levels, viz. 0.1  μM 
(Zn-deficient) and 2.0 μM (Zn sufficient/control). The AMF inoculum consisted of 500 
spores from Rhizophagus irregularis (Organic Gardening, New South Wales, Australia) 
as previously described with slight modification [ 13]. There were four treatments: con -
trol (2.0 μM ZnSO4); Zn- (0.1 μM ZnSO4); Zn-AMF+ (0.1 μM ZnSO4 + 0.2 g AMF); and 
AMF+ (2.0 μM ZnSO 4 + 0.2 g AMF). The pH of the medium was adjusted to 6.0 using 
KOH (1 M). Plants were kept in a growth chamber at 25 °C with 60% relative humidity, 
200  μmol  m−2 s −1 light intensity, and long-day light conditions (14  h light/10  h dark). 
The experiment was arranged in a completely randomized design (CRD) with three 
independent biological replicates per treatment. Each independently maintained hydro -
ponic culture served as an experimental unit, and the experimental units were randomly 
assigned to the four treatments and randomly positioned in the growth chamber. Plants 
were harvested five weeks after transfer to the hydroponic culture.
2.2 Morphological features
Morphological growth parameters, such as shoot height, root length, shoot fresh weight, 
and root fresh weight were measured on harvested plants grown on solution culture. 
Roots developed by each plant sample were washed in distilled water and then quickly 
blotted in tissue paper before taking weight by electronic balance. The fresh weight of 
the shoot was directly weighed by electronic balance after harvesting. For turgid weight, 
the fresh shoots were then immersed in deionized water overnight at 4  °C until full 
hydration was achieved. After surface moisture was carefully removed with tissue paper, 
the fully hydrated shoots were weighed to obtain the turgid weight. Furthermore, shoot 
height and root length were measured by cm measuring scale. The roots and leaves of 
the plants were separated manually and dried in an oven at 80 °C for 3 days before mea -
suring the dry weight.
2.3 Determination of chlorophyll score and photosynthetic parameters
The chlorophyll content of young, fully expanded leaves was measured by SPAD (Soil 
Plant Analysis Development) meter (Minolta, Japan). Furthermore, FluorPen FP 100

(Photon Systems Instruments, Czech Republic) was used to investigate the chlorophyll 
fluorescence characteristics, such as Fv/Fm (quantum efficiency of photosystem II) and 
Pi_ABS (photosynthesis performance index) of young, fully expanded leaves adapted at 
dark for 1 h.
2.4 Visualization of AMF colonization in the roots
Roots from meristematic zone were cut into 1 cm segments and cleared in 5% KOH at 
60 °C for 5 min. After rinsing, samples were acidified in 1% HCl for 5 min, stained with 
0.05% trypan blue at 60 °C for 10 min, and blotted to remove excess stain. Roots were 
then incubated in lactoglycerol for 40  min, mounted on slides with lactoglycerol, and 
examined under a digital microscope at 40×. These staining and microscopy methods 
are widely used to identify AMF structures in roots and quantify the extent of mycor -
rhizal colonization [32].
2.5 Molecular detection and relative abundance of AMF in roots
We assessed AMF colonization in roots collected from the meristematic zone using con-
ventional PCR (polymerase chain reaction). Briefly, DNA was extracted from roots using 
the CTAB method [ 10]. Prior to PCR, the DNA was diluted to equal concentration. 
For molecular confirmation of AMF colonization, genomic DNA was amplified with R. 
irregularis 18S rRNA primers (F: A T C A A C T T T C G A T G G T A G G A T A G A, R: G A A C C C 
A A A C A C T T T G G T T T C C) (Badri et al. 2016). PCR products were mixed with loading 
dye and loaded into gel wells alongside a DNA ladder. The gel run was carried out at 
120 V for 30 min. Bands were then visualized under UV and sized by comparison with 
the DNA ladder.
For determination of relative AMF abundance, DNA-based quantitative real-time PCR 
(qPCR) was performed using the same R. irregularis 18S rRNA primers on a CFX96 
Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA). The qPCR program consisted of an initial dena -
turation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 39 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 55 °C for 30 s , with 
a final extension at 60 °C for 5 min . Relative AMF abundance was calculated using the 
2−ΔΔCt method, with PsGAPDH as the internal reference for normalization. Three inde -
pendent biological replicates were analyzed, with two technical replicates for each bio -
logical replicate.
2.6 Determination of electrolyte leakage in roots
Electrolyte leakage was measured in roots using an electrical conductivity meter to 
assess membrane integrity. Briefly, root samples from one randomly chosen plant per 
replicate were cut into 1  cm segments. Samples were then placed in individual stop -
pered vials containing 20 ml of non-ionized water after three washes with distilled water 
to remove surface contamination. These samples were incubated at room temperature 
(25 °C) on a shaker (100 rpm) for 2 h. Electrical conductivity (EC) of bathing solution 
was read after incubation [23].
2.7 Determination of hydrogen peroxide in roots
The roots were homogenized in 0.1% trichloroacetic acid using mortar and pestle for 
hydrogen peroxide analysis. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min 
to remove cellular debris. The resulting supernatant was mixed with potassium iodide

Absorbance was measured at 390 nm using a Shimadzu UV-1650PC spectrophotometer 
[2].
2.8 Elemental analysis in root and leaf
Multiple mineral elements were quantified by Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass 
Spectrometry (ICP-MS; PerkinElmer, NexION 2000, USA) and Zn, Fe, Cu, and Mn 
were selected for presentation since they exhibited significant treatment-dependent 
responses. Briefly, nutrient analysis was conducted using root and young leaf tis -
sues. Root samples were carefully excised, rinsed under running tap water, immersed 
in 0.1 mM CaSO₄ solution for 10 min, and subsequently washed with deionized water. 
Young leaf samples were separately rinsed with deionized water. All samples were 
transferred into labeled envelopes and oven-dried at 75  °C for 3  days. The dried sam -
ples were then finely powdered. Approximately 0.5  g of powdered sample was trans -
ferred into a clean, sterile 50 mL beaker. Ten milliliters of HNO 3 followed by 2.5 mL of 
H2O2 were added to each sample, which was then kept overnight at room temperature. 
Three blank samples were prepared simultaneously to account for possible contami -
nation from reagents and instruments. Afterward, sample digestion was performed in 
a fume hood chamber, beginning at 100  °C, with the temperature gradually increased 
to 150 °C, and then to 200 °C until the solution was clear. After cooling to room tem -
perature, the digested samples were diluted to a final volume of 50 mL with ultra-pure 
deionized water using volumetric flasks. All samples were filtered through Whatman fil -
ter paper to remove particulates prior to analysis. During analysis, the nebulized sample 
(~ 0.85 mL min−1) entered the core of the argon plasma of the ICP-MS system.
2.9 Expression pattern of Zn transporter genes in the roots
We performed real-time qPCR analysis to validate the expression of Zn transporter 
genes, such as ZIP1 and ZIP2 in the roots of pea on the CFX96 Real-time System (Bio-
Rad, USA) with gene-specific primers. Briefly, the first-strand cDNA was synthesized 
from total RNA using the GoScript Reverse Transcription System (Promega, USA). The 
qPCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95  °C for 2  min, fol -
lowed by 42 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 
60 °C for 5 min. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, 
with PsGAPDH serving as the internal reference gene. The real-time PCR experiment 
included three independent biological replicates, with two technical replicates per -
formed for each. The primers used for qPCR experiments were as follows: PsGAPDH 
(fw: G T G G T C T C C A C T G A C T T T A T T G G T, Rv: T T C C T G C C T T G G C A T C A A A), ZIP1 
(F: T G A G G G A A T C G C C A T T G G T C, R: A G A G A T C G C G T A G G T C C A G T) and ZIP2 (F: 
C C C A T A G G T G T C G G G A T T G G, R: G G C C C A T A G A G A T C G C G T A G).
2.10 Amino acid profiling in pea roots and leaves
The amino acid composition of pea root and leaf tissues was determined using the chro -
matographic method [ 25]. Briefly, approximately 0.40  g of each dry powdered sample 
was treated with 12 mL of 7.0 N HCl, 36 mL of deionized water, and 0.1 mg phenol for 
acid digestion, followed by thorough homogenization in sealed glass tubes and incuba -
tion at 102  °C for 24  h for hydrolysis. Following hydrolysis, the samples were filtered

and diluted to 100  mL with deionized water. The diluted solutions were subsequently 
filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane filter prior to amino acid determination. Amino 
acid analysis was performed using an amino acid analyzer (Model: S433-D, Sykam AAA, 
Germany). The amino acid analyzer was equipped with an autoinjector and a capillary 
column (150 mm × 4.26 mm). The column temperature was maintained at 57 °C, with 
a flow rate of 0.45 mL min −1 at a pressure of 43 bar. The total run time for the analysis 
was 45 min, with amino acid detection performed at wavelengths of 440 nm and 570 nm 
using a Dual-Channel Photometer. The amino acid content was quantified by comparing 
the sample peak areas to those of amino acid standards, based on their respective reten -
tion times.
2.11 Determination of symbiotic signalling using split root assay
A split root system is a setup where a plant’s root system is divided into two or more sep-
arate compartments separated by a sealed plastic barrier, allowing different treatments 
to be applied to different root zones of the same plant. The plants were cultivated for 
one week in a sterile basal nutrient solution [18] before conducting the split-root assay in 
treatment pots. The taproot of each seedling was cut in the middle, and the lateral root 
system was evenly divided between two compartments within a single pot filled with 
different solutions. The compartments were separated by a solid plastic barrier. Each 
split-root system was subjected to the same growth conditions, including the presence 
or absence of Zn deficiency. In one compartment of each split-root system, AMF was 
inoculated, while the other compartment remained uninoculated, based on the treat -
ment. The split-root experiment was conducted using a completely randomized design 
with three independent biological replicates per treatment combination. Plants were 
cultivated for an additional four weeks before data collection. The split-root assay was 
performed under six conditions: control/control, Zn-/Zn-, Zn-/Zn-AMF+, Zn-AMF+/
Zn- AMF+, control/AMF+, and AMF+/AMF+.
2.12 Statistical analysis
All experimental data were analysed using a completely randomized design (CRD), with 
three independent biological replicates per treatment. The statistical significance of each 
variable was evaluated by analysis of variance (ANOVA), where appropriate. Where sig -
nificant differences were detected by ANOVA ( P < 0.05), Duncan’s multiple range test 
was employed as a post-hoc analysis to identify specific group differences. All statistical 
analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics 20.0 (IBM Corporation, USA). Graphical 
representations were generated using the R package ggplot2.

3.1 AMF colonization and abundance in the roots
Microscopic observation of stained roots showed no visible AMF colonization in the 
control and Zn-deficient plants without AMF inoculation (Fig.  1A, B). In contrast, roots 
inoculated with AMF under Zn-deficient and Zn-sufficient conditions showed clear 
fungal structures, including mycelia, arbuscules, and vesicles (Fig.  1C, D). PCR amplifi -
cation further confirmed AMF colonization, with visible bands detected in AMF-inoc -
ulated samples, particularly in lanes 3 and 4, while no clear amplification was observed 
in the non-inoculated samples (Fig.  1E). Consistent with the microscopic and molecular

AMF+ plants compared with the control and Zn- plants (Fig.  1F). Interestingly, no sig -

plants (Fig. 1F).
3.2 AMF inoculation improves morphological features under Zn deficiency

and root development compared to the control plants (Fig.  2A). However, AMF inocu -
lation restored plant growth under Zn-deficient conditions, while AMF+ plants under 
Zn-sufficient conditions exhibited the greatest overall growth performance (Fig.  2A).

the control treatment (Fig.  2B). In contrast, Zn-deficient plants inoculated with AMF

inoculation restored shoot height to levels statistically comparable to the control plants 
(Fig. 2C). AMF+ plants exhibited the highest shoot height among all treatment groups 
(Fig. 2C).

shoot dry weight, where Zn deficiency caused a marked reduction compared to con -
trols, while AMF inoculation showed a non-significant increase in shoot dry weight 
under Zn-deficient conditions (Fig.  2E). Plants inoculated with AMF without Zn defi -
ciency showed similar shoot dry weight to those of controls (Fig.  2E). Turgid weight

4 Discussion
Zn deficiency severely impaired plant growth and physiology, as evidenced by reduced 
growth and photosynthetic parameters in pea plants. These findings are consistent with 
the well-established role of Zn as an essential micronutrient involved in chlorophyll 
biosynthesis, photosynthetic electron transport, membrane stability, and enzyme acti -
vation [3, 7]. Zn deficiency also increased hydrogen peroxide accumulation and electro -
lyte leakage, indicating enhanced oxidative stress and membrane injury. Previous studies 
have shown that Zn deficiency disrupts redox homeostasis and promotes reactive oxy -
gen species (ROS) accumulation, ultimately leading to cellular damage and growth inhi -
bition [8].
AMF inoculation markedly alleviated these detrimental effects by improving growth 
performance, chlorophyll content, photosynthetic efficiency, and biomass accumulation 
under Zn-deficient conditions. The improvement in photosynthetic parameters suggests 
that AMF protected the photosynthetic apparatus from Zn deficiency-induced photoin -
hibition. AMF are known to improve nutrient acquisition through extensive extraradical 
hyphal networks that increase the effective absorptive interface and facilitate micro -
nutrient uptake beyond the rhizosphere depletion zone [ 1, 29]. In addition, AMF can 
enhance antioxidant defense systems and stabilize cellular membranes under nutrient 
stress conditions [6]. The reduced hydrogen peroxide and electrolyte leakage observed in 
AMF-inoculated plants further support the role of AMF in mitigating oxidative damage 
under Zn stress.
Zn deficiency significantly altered mineral homeostasis in both roots and leaves, par -
ticularly affecting Zn, Fe, Cu, and Mn accumulation. The decline in Zn concentration 
under Zn deficiency confirms impaired micronutrient availability and uptake. Inter -
estingly, AMF inoculation restored Zn levels in both roots and leaves, demonstrating 
the ability of AMF to facilitate Zn acquisition. A few studies have reported that AMF 
improve Zn uptake by increasing absorptive surface area and mobilizing sparingly avail -
able Zn in soil through hyphal transport [ 4, 11]. Although other mineral elements were 
also quantified (data not presented), their levels did not differ significantly among treat -
ments, indicating that the effects of AMF and Zn supplementation on plant mineral 
nutrition were element-specific rather than reflecting a general alteration of ion homeo -
stasis. Recent studies demonstrated that AMF-mediated stress alleviation was accom -
panied by improved nutrient acquisition and ion homeostasis, including increased P , K, 
and Fe accumulation under cold stress [ 33]. Thus, although other measured elements 
remained unchanged in the present study, they may still contribute to plant physiologi -
cal functions under our experimental conditions.
The increased expression of Zn transporter genes ( ZIP1 and ZIP2) under AMF inoc -
ulation further suggests that AMF-mediated Zn acquisition may involve molecular 
regulation of Zn transport systems. Zn transporter proteins, particularly ZIP-family 
transporters, play critical roles in Zn uptake and redistribution during micronutrient

deficiency [16]. The strong induction of these transporter genes under Zn-AMF+ condi -
tions indicates that AMF not only improve Zn availability in the rhizosphere but may 
also activate host Zn transport pathways to enhance Zn uptake efficiency. Interestingly, 
Zn deficiency increased Fe and Cu accumulation, while AMF inoculation partially nor -
malized their levels. This phenomenon may reflect the competitive and interconnected 
relationships among micronutrient transport systems. Excessive Fe accumulation during 
Zn deficiency has been previously reported and may occur due to non-specific metal 
transporter activity and disrupted ion homeostasis [ 28]. Thus, it appears that AMF-
mediated restoration of nutrient balance likely contributed to improved physiological 
stability under Zn stress in pea plants.
Zn deficiency caused substantial changes in amino acid metabolism, particularly 
increasing stress-associated amino acids such as histidine, lysine, threonine, glutamic 
acid, and aspartic acid, while reducing several growth-related amino acids including 
isoleucine, leucine, glycine, and tyrosine. Amino acid reprogramming is a common 
response to nutrient stress and often reflects metabolic adjustments associated with 
osmoprotection, nitrogen remobilization, and stress signaling [ 19]. The accumulation 
of glutamic acid and aspartic acid under Zn deficiency may indicate enhanced nitro -
gen redistribution and stress-associated metabolic activity. AMF inoculation restored 
several amino acids that declined during Zn deficiency, particularly leucine, isoleucine, 
glycine, and tyrosine. Branched-chain amino acids such as leucine and isoleucine are 
associated with energy metabolism and stress recovery, while glycine and tyrosine con -
tribute to antioxidative metabolism and secondary metabolite biosynthesis [ 14]. These 
findings suggest that AMF modulate metabolic pathways to support cellular recovery 
and growth under Zn deficiency.
The split-root experiment strongly suggests that AMF-mediated Zn deficiency toler -
ance operates predominantly through systemic regulation rather than solely through 
localized root colonization. Although AMF-associated compartments exhibited greater 
root growth and reduced hydrogen peroxide accumulation, the most striking observa -
tion was that improvements in shoot height, shoot dry weight, and chlorophyll score 
occurred even when only one portion of the root system was inoculated with AMF. This 
indicates that the beneficial effects of AMF were transmitted beyond the colonized root 
sector and influenced whole-plant physiology through long-distance signaling pathways. 
The recovery of shoot growth and chlorophyll status under partial AMF colonization 
suggests that AMF-induced signaling can systemically regulate photosynthetic perfor -
mance and biomass accumulation under Zn deficiency. Similar systemic responses have 
been observed in AMF-associated plants exposed to nutrient and abiotic stresses, where 
colonized roots trigger whole-plant adaptive mechanisms involving phytohormones, 
ROS signaling, calcium signaling, and nutrient-responsive pathways [15, 17].
In our study, compartment-specific differences in root traits were more limited com -
pared to the strong shoot-level responses. While some localized increases in root length 
and root dry weight were detected in AMF-associated compartments, these differences 
were relatively modest compared to the substantial improvements observed in aboveg -
round traits. Likewise, hydrogen peroxide accumulation was reduced in AMF-colonized 
compartments, but the reduction in oxidative stress at the whole-plant level suggests 
systemic regulation of antioxidant responses rather than exclusively local detoxification. 
These findings imply that direct fungal-root interaction alone cannot fully explain the

observed stress alleviation. Instead, AMF likely induced systemic signaling cascades that 
coordinated physiological adaptation throughout the plant. In addition, AMF can induce 
systemic antioxidant responses by modulating ROS-scavenging enzymes and redox sig -
naling pathways, thereby improving membrane stability and reducing oxidative damage 
under micronutrient stress conditions [6]. The improved shoot performance observed in 
this study may therefore result from coordinated systemic regulation of nutrient trans -
port, carbon assimilation, and oxidative stress tolerance. Thus, the present study pro -
vides evidence that AMF-mediated Zn deficiency tolerance extends beyond localized 
nutrient acquisition and involves integrated systemic physiological regulation through -
out the plant. Despite these findings, several limitations should be considered. All exper-
iments were conducted using pea seedlings under controlled hydroponic conditions, 
which enabled precise manipulation of Zn availability and AMF inoculation but may not 
fully represent the complexity of soil-grown plants under field conditions. Moreover, as 
pea is a legume, nutrient acquisition and stress responses may vary across developmental 
stages, particularly following the establishment of rhizobial nodulation and symbiotic N 
fixation, which can substantially alter plant nutrient demand and root physiology. There-
fore, the responses observed at the seedling stage may not fully reflect AMF-mediated 
Zn-deficiency tolerance during later vegetative and reproductive stages. Future studies 
should validate these responses across different developmental stages and under soil and 
field conditions. In particular, investigating the interactions among AMF colonization, 
rhizobial nodulation, N fixation, and Zn homeostasis would provide a more comprehen -
sive understanding of AMF-mediated nutrient regulation in pea.
5 Conclusions
R. irregularis , a known AMF, substantially improved Zn-deficiency tolerance in pea 
plants through coordinated regulation of nutrient uptake, physiological performance, 
and metabolic adjustment. AMF inoculation enhanced plant growth, chlorophyll sta -
tus, photosynthetic efficiency, and Zn accumulation while reducing oxidative damage 
and membrane instability under Zn-deficient conditions. The induction of Zn trans -
porter-related genes further suggests that AMF promote Zn acquisition and internal 
nutrient redistribution in stressed plants (Fig.  7). In addition, AMF-mediated modula -
tion of amino acid profiles indicates an important role of metabolic reprogramming in 
maintaining cellular stability and stress adaptation during Zn deficiency. The split-root 
experiment demonstrated that these beneficial effects were largely mediated through 
systemic signaling, where AMF colonization on one side of the root system improved 
the physiological performance of the entire plant. Collectively, these findings highlight 
the multifaceted role of AMF in enhancing plant resilience to micronutrient deficiency 
and provide new insight into the systemic nature of AMF-mediated stress tolerance. The 
study further highlights the potential of AMF-based bioinoculants as an environmen -
tally sustainable strategy for improving plant performance under Zn deficiency, although 
their effectiveness should be validated under soil and field conditions.
