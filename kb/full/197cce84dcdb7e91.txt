Abstract 15 
 16 
Food production and consumption affect environmental sustainability, also causing water pollution. 17 
This study assesses the relationship between food consumption patterns and water pollution using China 18 
as the case study, with a specific emphasis on pesticide -related pollution using the grey WF as the 19 
indicator. It includes the whole production chain, from fork to farm, considering food waste and losses. 20 
It assesses grey WFs for the current Chinese food consumption pattern, the recommended pattern of the 21 
Chinese government, the ‘planetary health diet’ and a plant-based diet. The current pattern, high in 22 
animal foods, has the largest grey WF (42 m³ /day), despite animal foods providing less energy 23 
compared to crop-based foods. Pork, beef and potato, only 8% of energy consumption, contribute 73% 24 
to total grey WFs , indicating the highest potential water pollution impact. The recommended pattern 25 
has a grey WF of 26, the planetary health diet of 21, and the plant-based pattern of 11 m³ /day. The 26 
temporal grey WFs peak during the cropping season. The current food consumption pattern has the 27 
highest peak, the plant-based pattern the lowest. Especially animal foods substantially increase grey 28

1 Introduction 41 
 42 
Globally, food production and consumption affect environmental sustainability, also causing water 43 
pollution (FAO, 2012) . Water pollution refers to the introduction of harmful substances, such as 44 
nutrients, pesticides, and other chemicals, into freshwater systems, degrading water quality and 45 
threatening human and ecological health. Freshwater of sufficient quality is essential for human health 46 
and well -being, but anthropogenic water pollution, such as contamination by pesticides applied in 47 
agriculture, significantly degrades water quality and poses risks to both aquatic ecosystems and human 48 
health (Schwarzenbach et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2024) . While previous studies have extensively 49 
examined GHG emissions (e.g., Crippa et al., 2021), land use (e.g., Kastner et al., 2012), and nutrient-50 
related water pollution (e.g., Mekonnen & Hoekstra, 2015, 2018), the role of pesticides in water 51 
pollution from food systems remains underexplored. The Chinese Ministry of Agriculture, for example, 52 
agreed on the strategy ‘Zero Growth of Pesticide till 2020’ (MOA, 2015). 53 
 With the increasing global population, food security issues and food production within the planetary 54 
boundaries have become important concerns (e.g., Foley et al., 2011; Godfray et al., 2010; Tilman et 55

causes emissions to the environment, e.g. greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions or agricultural emissions to 57 
water bodies (e.g., nitrogen, phosphorus or pesticides). However, different food commodities affect 58

there are huge differences among the environmental impacts of food consumption patterns depending 60

contribution of animal foods have larger impacts than plant -based patterns. Indicators applied include 62 
the ecological footprint, land requirements, GHG emissions or water footprint, among others. The 63 
ecological footprint developed by Wackernagel and Rees (1998) addresses the need for biophysical 64 
approaches to achieve environmental sustainability indicating the large land use especially related to 65 
food consumption. Gerbens-Leenes and Nonhebel (2002) have shown that different food consumption 66 
patterns have different agricultural land requirements, while dietary changes are the predominant driver 67 
of future land needs for food (Kastner et al., 2012) . The intensification of food systems and shifts of 68 
human diets to more animal foods have caused an increase of GHG emissions. For example, Steinfeld 69 
et al. (2006) revealed that animal production is responsible for approximately 18% of global GHG 70 
emissions. Recently, Crippa et al. (2021) have reported that food systems contribute 34% to global 71 
GHG emissions. Also water use and water pollution related to food consumption and production are 72 
relevant for environmental sustainability. For instance, Hoekstra and Mekonnen (2012) quantified the 73 
green, blue and grey water footprints (WFs) of humanity, including water for food. Mekonnen and 74 
Hoekstra (2015, 2018,) assessed global water pollution caused by nitrogen and phosphorus, fertilizers 75 
used for food production. For the assessment, they applied the grey WF, defined as the hypothetical 76 
volume of freshwater required to assimilate a pollutant load to the extent that ambient water quality 77 
standards are not violated. It serves as a volumetric indicator of water pollution, not as the actual volume 78 
of water polluted or required for dilution (Hoekstra et al., 2011). Franke et al. (2013) updated the grey 79 
WF methodology, also taking, among other things, pesticides into consideration. 80 
 To assess water pollution, the methods of the grey water footprint (WF) and life cycle assessment 81 
(LCA) are useful and complementary tools. The grey WF is able to measure total pollution levels , 82 
whereas LCA supports comprehensive sustainability assessments (Berger et al., 2025). Where the grey 83

In this study, we aimed to estimate the impact of different food consumption patterns on water pollution 87

 Recently, Yi et al. (2024a) have assessed grey WFs related to pesticides for the main crops produced 89 
in China, indicating not only that the current crop grey WFs are dominated by pesticides, and not by 90 
nitrogen or phosphorus, but also that there are huge differences among crops. Moreover, animal 91 
production systems convert large quantities of feed crops into animal foods (meat, milk and eggs), so 92 
that their water pollution is probably larger than pollution caused by vegetal foods (e.g. grains, 93 
vegetables and fruits). This means that differences in the composition of food consumption patterns 94 
might have different effects on water pollution. At present, there is no quantification of water pollution 95 
caused by food consumption patterns. To achieve a more sustainable food consumption pattern, 96 
addressing environmental and health concerns, Duchin (2005), for example, proposed a predominantly 97 
plant-based Mediterranean-type pattern. Willett et al. (2019) proposed the ‘planetary health diet’, a food 98 
consumption pattern with a small contribution of animal foods and relatively small environmental 99 
footprints, including GHGs, water, land, N and P use, and biodiversity loss. An important question 100 
would be whether or not food consumption patterns dominated by animal foods would also cause more 101 
water pollution than patterns mostly based on vegetal foods. 102

consumption of animal foods went up (FAO, 2024). The Chinese government recommends healthy food 104 
consumption patterns including milk and meat (Wang et al., 2016) . Importantly, this study adopts a 105 
national systems perspective rather than focusing on regional heterogeneity within China. While 106 
regional dietary variations exist, the central government's unified food and nutrition guidelines provide 107 
a coherent national policy framework. This study aims to find the relationship between food 108 
consumption patterns and water pollution using China as the case study area. It adopts the grey WF 109 
(Franke et al., 2013) to assess water pollution for four different food consumption patterns, the current 110 
Chinese food consumption pattern (averaged on the national level for the year 2020), the recommended 111

2 Grey WFs related to food supply chains 121 
 122 
Food commodities produced in agriculture show losses in the supply chain from the farm gate to final 123 
consumption. This study employs a consumption-based accounting framework to assess water pollution 124 
associated with food consumption. Unlike production-based assessments, which allocate environmental 125 
impacts solely to the point of emission, a consumption -based perspective traces impacts back to the 126 
final consumer , thereby revealing how downstream dietary choices drive upstream agricultural 127 
pollution. Assessments of environmental sustainability of food consumption should include the gap 128 
between food consumption and production. For example, Song et al. (2015) quantified the embedded 129 
GHG and water use caused by food waste in households and Xue et al. (2021) assessed the food losses 130 
and waste for the whole food supply chain, from the farm gate, the retail gate, to final consumption 131 
(eating of the food, e.g. a strawberry), including the main crops and animal and aquatic foods in China. 132 
That study has shown that different foods have different losses in their supply chain. Supply chain losses 133 
cause an increase of grey WFs per unit of product. When food is lost or wasted, the environmental 134 
burdens embedded in the lost portion —including pesticide -related water pollution —are effectively 135 
allocated to the actual food consumed, amplifying the per-unit environmental footprint. Figure 1 shows 136 
an example of a grey water footprint (WF) increase for strawberries due to food losses along a food 137 
supply chain. 138

Figure 1 illustrates the grey WF increase per unit of food as it progresses from farmers to retailers and 144 
finally to consumers. This amplification effect has critical policy implications: when consumers choose 145 
foods with high supply chain losses, they indirectly cause a larger environmental impact than reflected 146 
in farm-gate assessments. Using strawberries as an example: farmers produce six strawberries (at the 147 
farm gate), with a grey WF of Y m³ per kg of strawberry. As the strawberries move through the supply 148 
chain, at the retail gate, there are only four left, causing the grey WF to increase to 6/4 Y m³ per kg. By 149 
the time the strawberries reach the consumer’s mouth, only three strawberries remain, resulting in a 150 
grey WF of 6/3 Y m³ per kg. Overall, the stages of food production, supply, and consumption 151 
significantly impact the grey WF. Additionally, consumer choices regarding different foods with 152 
different initial grey WFs and different losses will result in varying grey WFs. 153

2.2 Retailers and food supply 165 
 166 
After leaving the farm gate, food commodities often undergo processing and distribution to retailers. 167 
During this process, food conversions and food waste and loss (FWL) occur. In China, different food 168 
commodities have different FWLs. For example, fresh strawberries have a FWL of around 33% from 169 
farm gate to retail gate, while the FWL for cereals is about 10%, and for vegetables around 50% (Xue 170 
et al., 2021). However, only 45% of the live-weight of beef cows is converted into beef after slaughter, 171 
with an average FWL for Chinese beef of around 20% at the retail gate (FAO, 2003; Xue et al., 2021). 172 
 173 
2.3 Consumers and food consumption 174 
 175 
Finally, consumers buy the foods at the retail. There are also conversions, wastes and losses in the 176 
consumer stage. These conversions vary among different foods. Some foods, like rice, wheat and 177 
soybean based foods (e.g., boiled rice, noodles and home-made soy milk) increase their weight, because 178 
they take up water. Other foods, like beef, lose weight when cooked or by peeling, like potato or orange. 179 
Additionally, between the food at the retail gate and the food actually consumed, there is also FWL. 180 
 Consumers face thousands of food choices for their food consumption pattern. These patterns have 181 
different grey WFs, depending on the composition. 182

The system included in this study covers the full food supply chain from agricultural production (farm) 186 
through processing and distribution (food industry and retail) to final consumption (fork). The 187 
assessment includes: (1) direct pesticide, nitrogen, and phosphorus emissions from crop production; (2) 188 
indirect emissions embedded in feed crops used for animal and aquatic production; and (3) amplification 189 
effects from supply chain losses and conversions. The following two aspects that are not taken into 190 
account: (1) Regional heterogeneity: While China exhibits regional variations in dietary patterns and 191 
agricultural practices, this study adopts a national aggregate perspective. This approach aligns with the 192 
central government's unified food and nutrition policy framework; (2) Agricultural international trade: 193 
China's staple foods (rice, wheat and potato), fruits and vegetables, animal feed (e.g. maize) and 194 
livestock foods (pork, poultry, beef, milk) are largely produced in China itself (FAO, 2024). The study 195 
focuses on domestic consumption-production linkages, and trade-related impacts in other countries are 196 
not considered. This exclusion is partially justified by China's national food security strategy, which 197 
prioritizes domestic production for staple crops. However, we acknowledge that China is a significant 198 
importer of soybeans and other feed crops, meaning that a portion of the grey WF embodied in Chinese 199 
consumption may occur outside China's borders and is not captured in this analysis. 200

5.1 Study relevancy 444 
 445 
This study establishes, for the first time, the consumption -to-production perspective for pesticide -446 
related water pollution using a consumption -based grey water footprint (WF) framework using China 447 
as the case study area. The analysis builds on two earlier studies on water pollution from agriculture in 448 
China (Yi et al., 2024a, 2024b), incorporating information on food waste and losses in production chains 449 
(Xue et al., 2021) and data on four different food consumption patterns. The 18 pesticides were selected 450 
based on farmer surveys as the most frequently applied pesticides for these crops rather than on the 451 
share of total pesticide use, with the underlying data drawn from Zhang et al. (2015) and Yi et al. 452 
(2024a). We estimated that the present pesticides accounted for around 60% of the total use of pesticide 453 
in China (Jing et al., 2022). The 12 crops (maize, rice, wheat, soybean, potato, peanut, rapeseed, 454 
sugarcane, sugar beet, tea, cotton, and tobacco) and 2 crop categories (vegetables and fruits) together 455 
account for 97% of China's national crop planting area and represent 95% of total crop production. The 456 
14 crop-based foods analyzed in Section 3 are covered by these 12 crops and 2 categories, with the 457 
exception of tea, cotton, and tobacco which are not food commodities and are therefore excluded from 458 
the dietary analysis. The four key findings are: (i) Pesticides—not nitrogen or phosphorus—dominate 459

Several methodological choices and data limitations warrant discussion regarding their potential 485 
influence on our findings. 486 
 487 
5.2.1 Data sources and representativeness 488 
 489 
This study applied data sources for agricultural production from the main producing areas assuming 490 
they are representative for China as a whole. This was done to indicate the relation between 491 
consumption patterns and grey WFs. We compiled feed ingredient data from farmer surveys conducted 492 
in the largest feed producing region, i.e., Northern China (Lu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2007; Yi et al., 493 
2023), assuming these data represent national average practices. A limitation is the temporal mismatch 494 
between the feed composition data (primarily from Wang et al., 2007) and the 2020 consumption 495 
patterns analyzed. While feed efficiency in China has improved since 2007, we lack updated national 496 
feed composition data; this uncertainty should be addressed in future research. The study selected the 497 
typical modern animal production systems that dominate animal production. They use feed ingredients, 498 
like maize and soybean, mostly grown in Northern China. We assumed that the contribution from 499 
traditional systems is very small. 500 
 501 
5.2.2 Manure-related nutrient pollution 502 
 503 
Our grey WF calculations for animal products exclude nitrogen and phosphorus leakage from animal 504 
manure, which in China may be discharged directly to water bodies or improperly managed. This 505 
exclusion is consistent with the pesticide-focused scope of our study, as manure-related pollution falls 506 
under nutrient (nitrogen and phosphorus) rather than pesticide pollution. It l ikely leads to 507 
underestimation of nutrient-related grey WFs for animal products. We acknowledge this as a scoping 508 
limitation and recommend that future research integrate both pesticide and nutrient pollution pathways 509 
for a more comprehensive assessment of water pollution from livestock systems. 510

5.2.3 Pesticide environmental fate 512 
 513 
Our calculations are based on conservative behavior for pesticides assuming that no degradation occurs 514 
before they reach water bodies. This assumption is consistent with the grey WF accounting guidelines 515 
(Franke et al., 2013). This approach provides a worst-case estimate of pesticide-related water pollution. 516 
In reality, pesticide environmental behavior varies considerably: some pesticides (e.g., glyphosate) are 517 
moderately mobile and degrade relatively quickly, while others (e.g., atrazine) are persistent and highly 518 
mobile (Franke et al., 2013). More refined methods that incorporate pesticide-specific degradation rates 519 
and soil adsorption coefficients would reduce absolute grey WF estimates. In our temporal analysis 520 
(Figure 6), we already apply pesticide -specific degradation half -lives, which are embedded in the 521 
dynamic grey WF calculations to provide a more realistic scenario for seasonal patterns. Regarding 522 
water quality standards, our previous work (Yi et al., 2024a) examined the effect of alternative standards: 523 
if the maximum permissible concentration (MPC) were applied instead of our chosen standard, the total 524 
grey WF for crops would be fifteen times larger. This indicates that using a more stringent standard 525 
would strengthen rather than weaken the conclusion that pesticides dominate water pollution from 526 
Chinese food production systems. We acknowledge that we have not tested the opposite direction —a 527 
less stringent drinking -water-type standard—as such standards are designed for human health rather 528 
than aquatic ecosystem protection and are therefore less relevant for our assessment. However, we 529 
recognize that a formal bounded sensitivity analysis across alternative standards would strengthen the 530 
study; we have identified this as a priority for future research. 531

5.3 Comparison with earlier studies 566 
 567 
Our study supports previous findings, suggesting that such dietary shifts can reduce land use (Kastner 568 
et al., 2012), greenhouse gas emissions (Tilman and Clark, 2014), and water pollution related to nitrogen, 569 
phosphorus (Mekonnen and Hoekstra, 2015, 2018), and pesticides (Yi et al., 2024ab). Consistent with 570 
the framework proposed by Duchin (2005) for sustainable food consumption patterns, our results 571 
confirm that plant-based dietary patterns have lower environmental impacts. More broadly, our study 572 
is in line with earlier research across multiple environmental dimensions, including GHG emissions 573 
(e.g., Guzmán et al., 2021), land and water use (e.g., Kastner et al. 2012 ), nitrogen and phosphorus 574 
pollution (e.g., Mekonnen and Hoekstra, 2015, 2018), and water footprints of agricultural products (e.g., 575 
Berger et al., 2025). Th ose studies consistently indicate that animal -based foods contribute 576 
disproportionately to total environmental pressure. 577

5.4 Policy implications 579 
 580 
In China, rapid urbanization and economic growth have led to a shift towards higher consumption of 581 
especially animal foods ( meat, eggs and milk products) , deviating from the traditional Chinese diet 582 
based on vegetables, whole grains, and crop based proteins (Huang et al., 2024). Our study shows that 583 
the present Chinese food consumption pattern has the largest grey WF, suggesting the greatest potential 584 
impact on water pollution and that a shift towards consumption of less animal products, e.g. the 585 
planetary health diet or a plant -based diet, decreases pollution. Our findings suggest four policy 586 
priorities. First, dietary structure optimization. Chinese nutrition experts should advocate a more 587 
balanced diet aligned with the principles of the planetary health or plant-based diet (Willett et al., 2019), 588 
which emphasizes diverse options such as legumes, nuts, and whole grains while reducing meat and 589

5.5 Future research directions 618 
 619 
Several directions for future research emerge from this study. First, while our analysis relies on 620 
secondary data from farmer surveys and national statistics, future research could benefit from primary 621 
data collection across multiple regions and production scales to improve accuracy. Second, our study 622 
focused on pesticides, but excluded other pollutants such as antibiotics, which have emerged as a 623 
significant environmental concern (Wang et al., 2024). Future studies should expand the pollutant scope 624 
for a more comprehensive assessment. Third, we adopted a national systems perspective, but China 625 
exhibits significant regional variation in dietary patterns and pesticide use intensity (Yi et al., 2024b). 626 
Future research should incorporate regional heterogeneity to support localized implementation 627 
strategies. Fourth, our grey WF calculations assumed conservative behavior for pesticides; future 628 
studies could refine these estimates by incorporating pesticide -specific degradation rates. Fifth, we 629 
excluded agricultural trade from our analysis, but China is a significant importer of feed crops, which 630 
may indirectly contribute to water pollution in exporting countries. Future research should adopt a 631 
global perspective to assess the full environmental footprint of Chinese food consumption. 632
