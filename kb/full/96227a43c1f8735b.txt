Abstract
The pressure of rapid urbanisation has led to the rapid transformation of wetlands 
and vegetation, causing major problems for ecological sustainability in fast-growing 
cities. This study assesses the spatiotemporal dynamics of wetland, vegetation, and 
built-up surfaces in Ara City, Bihar, India, from 1995 to 2025 using Landsat 5, 8, and 9 
imageries. The following spectral indices were used to assess the seasonal and long-
term environmental changes as a result of urban expansion: Normalized Difference 
Vegetation Index (NDVI), Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI), Modified 
Normalized Difference Water Index (MNDWI), Normalized Difference Built-up Index 
(NDBI), Urban Index (UI), Normalized Difference Bareness Index (NDBal), Bareness 
Index (BI), and Enhanced Built-up and Bareness Index (EBBI). The anthropogenic 
impact on vegetation and water resources around Ara city in middle of Bihar-Uttar 
Pradesh border in Bhojpur district of north-eastern part of Bihar, India surrounding 
by the Ganga and Son rivers was studied. The results reveal a progressive decline in 
vegetation cover and open-water features, areas experiencing rapid urban expansion. 
Pre-monsoon NDVI exhibited lower spatial variability than post-monsoon NDVI, 
indicating seasonal differences in vegetation dynamics. MNDWI indicated a reduction 
in water-related features, whereas higher NDWI values in 2025 suggest an increase 
in water-associated surfaces within the urban landscape. The results of the Pearson’s 
correlation analysis indicate negative correlation between built-up indices and NDVI 
and negative correlation between built-up indices and MNDWI, showing the inverse 
relationship between urbanization and ecological health indicators, while positive 
correlation between built-up indices and NDWI shows the positive relationship 
between built-up and water. The study underscores that wetlands, which serve as 
critical habitats for migratory birds, are increasingly threatened by human activities. 
The novel nature of the study is the integration of multi-season and multi-index 
analyses of the dynamics of wetland, vegetation, and urban surfaces over 30 years. 
The results offer insights for sustainable urban planning, wetland conservation, 
ecosystem management and may have implications for wetland-dependent 
biodiversity.

 1 Introduction
Urban encroachment reduces vegetation and wetland coverage in urban environments. 
The wetland and the vegetation coverage are two significant components of our ecosys -
tem. The first one acts as a kidney [ 5] and another one acts as a water purifying system 
and green lung [ 30]. Both of these are habitats for many living species on our planet. 
Man-made activities that gain onto the natural environment form a fragmented network 
of different organs in our ecosystem. For the betterment of our future generation in the 
context of contemporary resource utilization, a blueprint was prepared, known as the 
Sustainable Development Goal [41]. Urban greening is negatively redistributive in global 
cities [15].
Urban land alteration is a major threat to global terrestrial biodiversity; losses of native 
biodiversity abundance and species abundance are estimated at 34% and 52% per 1 km 
grid-cell respectively, and an average loss of 7–9 species per 10 km grid-cell [ 22]. In this 
context, open spaces in urban built-up areas contribute a critical part to regulating the 
microclimate, primarily in the regulation of urban temperature [ 33]. Deforestation and 
urban gentrification caused environmental contamination to spread quickly through -
out Asia, which may have long-term effects on sustainable development and economic 
growth [3]. Land use/land cover (LULC) modifications have been accelerated by rapid 
urbanization, as evidenced by a 9.36% uplift of built-up zones that affects land surface 
temperature (LST), a 5.73% reduction of cropland, and diminishing vegetation cover by 
3.56%, as a result of decreased Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) values 
and a push-up to the urban heat island (UHI), as well as a reduction of the anthropo -
genic factor of the COVID-19 lockdown in 2020, which temporarily increased NDVI 
values and decreased LST [17]. Numerous habitats, such as parks, cemeteries, urban for-
ests, vacant lots, campus areas, gardens and yards, and landfills, as well as blue spaces 
like lakes, streams, artificial swales, ponds, and storm water retention ponds, generate 
urban ecosystem services. It is predicted that between 2000 and 2030, over 60% of the 
urban land that will exist in 2030 will be constructed [ 11, 31]. The urban “green and 
blue infrastructure” has a critical contribution towards enhancing adjustment capabil -
ity and survival strength in contemporary climatic uncertainty situations and economi -
cally long-lasting, multiservice civilian life in our society [ 12, 19]. The key contribution 
of flora and fauna in urban areas is innumerable. The open green and blue space has 
a center of aesthetic demand and recreational needs of maximum urban citizens. In a 
deeper sense, these elements serve important functions such as replenishing ground -
water, reducing pollution, setting pollution benchmarks, controlling climate effects and 
flood damage, providing shade for vendors and travelers, and mitigating heat during the 
sunny season [10].
According to MoEFCC, wetlands have the following values and benefits, e.g., wet -
lands as sources of water, wetlands as flood and storm buffers, wetland products, wet -
lands as water purifiers, wetlands for recreation and tourism, wetlands and climate 
change through CO₂ sinks, and wetlands as habitats for migratory birds and biodiver -
sity hotspots [27]. Rural-to-urban migration leads to expanded anthropogenic activities,

Although remote sensing techniques have been used to monitor urban environmen -
tal changes and significant progress has been made, the existing studies have focused 
mainly on one type of urban features at a time (e.g., vegetation, wetlands, built-up areas), 
and/or included only a few spectral indices or a limited number of temporal observa -
tions. The comprehensive assessment of vegetation, wetland, barren and built-up land 
dynamics over several seasons and over a longer time scale has thus been constrained, 
especially for medium sized cities in eastern India that are rapidly urbanizing. In addi -
tion, few studies have quantitatively compared the relationships among several spectral 
indices to increase an understanding of the ecological response of urban landscape to 
rapid urbanization. Ara city, situated between Ganga and Son rivers has marked urban -
ization in the past few decades, but there is a lack of complete geospatial assessment 
of the wetland and vegetation dynamics of the city. The origins of the name Ara were 
believed to come from “ Aaranno, ” which means forest or vegetated area, which is reflect-
ing the historically close connection of the region to the presence of plenty of vegetation 
and wetlands. These ecosystems have traditionally been able to sustain the local diver -
sity of wildlife and can offer appropriate habitat for wetland dependent species, such as 
migratory birds. The present study aims to mitigate the identified research gap by using 
multi-temporal Landsat data (1995–2025) and eight spectral indices (NDVI, NDWI, 
MNDWI, NDBI, UI, NDBal, BI and EBBI) to explore the long-term and seasonal evolu -
tion of wetlands, vegetation, barren and built-up land. Pearson’s correlation analysis is 
also utilized to further measure relations among these indices and assess their responses 
to urbanization. The integrated multi-season, multi-index approach used in this study 
is novel and offers a holistic view of long-term urban ecological changes, which will 
generate scientific evidence to guide sustainable urban planning, wetland conservation 
and ecosystem management. Changes in wetlands and vegetation observed are also dis -
cussed in terms of their potential ecological impact on wetland dependent biodiversity, 
including migratory bird habitat, although no direct impacts are assumed in the absence 
of field-based ecological observations.
1.1 Objective
1. To evaluate the spatiotemporal variation of wetland and vegetation cover from 1995 
to 2025.
2. To examine the impact of urbanization on wetland and vegetation cover and discuss 
its potential implications for migratory bird habitats.
3. To identify the relation of different surface indices from 1995 to 2025.
Hypothesis (H0) There is no correlation of different surface indices from 1995 to 2025.

2.3.1 Satellite data selection, image preprocessing, and data processing workflow
The Multi-temporal Landsat satellite imagery was retrieved from the United States 
Geological Survey (USGS) Earth Explorer archive to monitor the changes in wetland, 
vegetation and built-up surfaces in Ara city between 1995 and 2025. The Landsat 5 
TM imagery was used for 1995 while the Landsat 8 OLI/TIRS and Landsat 9 OLI-2/
TIRS-2 imagery was used for 2025. Images were obtained in April-May (pre-mon -
soon) and October-November (post-monsoon) to reflect the seasonal variations in the 
study area. The pre-monsoon period is comparatively a dry period with lesser vegeta -
tion greenness and surface water availability, while the post-monsoon period follows 
the southwest monsoon, during which vegetation is at its peak growth and wetlands are 
refilling. The comparison of these two seasons will allow for a full evaluation of seasonal 
changes in vegetation, surface water and urban land cover changes and will reduce the 
impact of short-term climatic variability. Those images that lack a maximum cloud cover 
(below 10%) were chosen to minimize atmospheric interference. Geometrically and

2.3.2 Vegetation index
Normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI)
For vegetation analysis, the NDVI is a globally recognized spectral index [ 7, 20]. 
Because growing plants retain more red light and reflect near-infrared radiation, first 
developed the NDVI equation, or the ratio of red to near-infrared radiation in 1974 [ 34]. 
Water bodies, bare terrain, and populated places are indicated by values between zero 
and negative, and positive values show thin to thick vegetation cover. NDVI levels vary 
from − 1 to 1 [25].
 NDVI = ((NIRr − REDr)) / ((NIRr + REDr)) (1)
REDr and NIRr indicate the red and near-infrared band reflectance values, whereas 
NDVI stands for normalized difference vegetation index.
2.3.3 Water indices
Normalized difference water index (NDWI)
The NDWI is a useful spectral index for delineating open water body aspects devel -
oped by McFeeters [ 26]. Shortwave-infrared (SWIR) and near-infrared (NIR) radiation 
are absorbed by water bodies. NIRr and Greenr are the reflectances of the near-infrared 
and green bands, respectively, and McFeeters defined it as
 NDWI = (GREENr − NIRr) / (GREENr + NIRr) .
First NDWI was introduce in 1996, a remote sensing technique to estimate the canopy 
level vegetation water content from space using SWIR and NIR bands [ 13]. SWIR and 
Red bands (bands 5 and 3 in Landsat TM) were employed by Rogers and Kearney to 
generate NDWI [18]. The surface is covered with non-water bodies if NDWI ≤ 0, while it 
is filled with water if NDWI > 0. The NDWI value ranges from − 1 to 1, with zero serving 
as a threshold value. In this research, the Eq. 2 has been used for NDWI calculation [26]. 
i.e.,
 NDWI = ((GREENr − NIRr)) / ((GREENr + NIRr)) (2)
where NIRr and GREENr represent the reflectance values of the near-infrared and green 
bands, respectively, and NDWI stands for normalized difference water index.

Modified normalized difference water index (MNDWI)
The combination of open water bodies and ponds is shown by the MNDWI values. A 
normalized difference pond index (NDPI) has been invented in 2007 to categorize ponds 
in West Africa [21]. Compared to the NDWI, the MNDWI shows more open water and 
is more suited for improving water with a lot of built-up land in the background [ 44]. 
Water bodies and built-up objects cannot be completely distinguished by McFeeters’s 
NDWI. This problem was addressed in 2006, and the Eq.  3 has been used for calculation 
of MNDWI [44]. The modified normalized difference water index is known as MNDWI. 
This equation was further used in 2007 [21, 23].
 MNDWI = ((GREENr − SWIRr)) / ((GREENr + SWIRr)) (3)

2.3.4 Built-up and bareness indices
There are many indices for analysis of built-up and bareness areas. This study has calcu -
lated NDBI, EBBI, UI, and BI for comparative analysis of different built-up and bareness 
indices on urban density measures. Each of them has an owned equation, i.e.,
Normalized difference built-up index (NDBI)
For mapping built-up regions automatically, NDBI was first developed by a Chinese 
geographer, in 2003 [45]. Thereafter, it was frequently used to calculate the built-up area 
of cities. Water bodies are indicated by a negative NDBI value, built-up areas are indi -
cated by a greater positive NDBI value, and vegetation coverage is indicated by a lower 
positive NDBI value. NDBI is an outcome of the transformation of NDVI for analyzing 
built-up region; thereafter, it has been used for the intensity of urban construction [ 28]. 
The equation follows for calculating NDBI.
 NDBI = ((SWIR1r − NIRr)) / ((SWIR1r + NIRr)) (4)
where SWIR1r and NIRr represent the reflectance values of the short-wave infrared/
middle-infrared, and near-infrared channels of various bands in Landsat imagery, and 
NDBI stands for normalized difference built-up index.

 UI = ((SWIR2r − NIRr)) / ((SWIR2r + NIRr)) (5)
where UI means urban index: SWIR2 r, and NIRr represent mid-wave infrared channel 2

3.3 Analysis of built-up and bareness surface areas
Five spectral indices were calculated to evaluate the built-up and bareness characteristics 
of Ara city, namely NDBI, UI, NDBal, BI, and EBBI (Table  4; Fig.  4d–h (i–iv)). Overall, 
the indices show significant urbanization and land use change from 1995 to 2025 both 
in pre-monsoon and post-monsoon period. During this 30-year period, the Urban Index 
(UI) recorded the highest growth, from − 0.0419 in 1995 to 0.0946 in 2025 during the 
pre-monsoon season and from − 0.2816 to 0.0865 in the post-monsoon season (Table 4), 
suggesting significant expansion of built-up areas in the 30 years. The statistical mea -
sures also show changes in the spatial distribution of built up and barren surfaces. UI has 
lower standard deviation in 2025 than in 1995 in both seasons, indicating greater con -
sistency of urban growth across space. Likewise, the variation of NDBal was relatively 
low in the pre-monsoon season of 2025, suggesting that the distribution of barren land 
related to construction work and land excavation was more uniform. During the post-
monsoon season, greater variability is observed, with the seasonal influence of rainfall 
causing temporary flooding and changing the spectral properties of the low-lying barren 
surfaces (Table 4). These temporal changes are also reflected in the spatial distribution of 
the built-up and bareness indices (Fig. 4d–h (i–iv)). The built-up area grew continuously 
from the urban center to the outer areas from 1995 to 2025 with an increasing con -
centration of barren land around construction sites and new developments at the out -
skirts of the city. The spatial patterns suggest that the land surface has been significantly 
altered by rapid urbanization, characterized by the conversion of vegetation and wet -
lands with impervious surfaces and bare soils. Overall, the built-up and bareness indices 
show continuous increase in urbanization and land-cover changes in Ara city during the 
last 30 years (Table  4; Fig.  4d–h (i–iv)) The combined use of multiple indices provides 
complementary evidence of increasing urbanization, expansion of barren land, and the 
associated alteration of the urban landscape.

6 Discussion
The present study shows that there are considerable seasonal and temporal deviations 
in water, vegetation and built-up surface characteristics in Ara city during the period 
of 1995 to 2025. The observed deterioration of the vegetation condition in the post-
monsoon period, increased fragmentation of green spaces and the expansion of built-
up surfaces indicate a substantial alteration of the landscape over the study period. The 
results are similar to those of previous research, which has shown that urbanisation and 
land-use/land-cover (LULC) change led to a decline in vegetation cover and changes in 
urban ecological conditions [ 17, 29, 35]. The same patterns have been found in recent 
urban remote sensing studies using long-term satellite observations of urban growth, 
which suggest that the rapid expansion of an urban area has a significant impact on 
vegetation distribution and urban landscape fragmentation, but that the extent of the 
change depends on the local environmental conditions and the type of urban planning 
[16]. The present results are similar to the studies conducted in Indore [1] and Guwahati 
[17] where reduction in NDVI due to growing urbanization led to alteration in urban 
environmental conditions. Similarly, recent research has shown that decreases in urban 
vegetation often occur simultaneously with increased impervious surfaces and land -
scape fragmentation in rapidly expanding cities. The findings, however, contrast with 
the findings reported around the Bandel Thermal Power Station where the reduction in 
Page 21 of 28
Rahaman and Sadaf Discover Geoscience (2026) 4:380 
atmospheric particulate matter after the improvement in environmental conditions was 
the basis for stating that the vegetation health has improved [ 25]. The difference indi -
cates that in Ara, a number of factors other than urbanization such as seasonal climatic 
variability, land-use change, environmental management, and urban development may 
be affecting vegetation dynamics [16].
Analysis of NDWI and MNDWI further showed temporal variations in surface water 
features. The relatively greater temporal stability of the MNDWI compared to the 
NDWI, and the strong negative correlation of MNDWI with built-up indices, are con -
sistent with previous GIS-based analyses of wetland loss due to land filling and urban 
sprawl [24, 38]. Studies on urban landscape dynamics have also shown that urban growth 
has an impact on wetland ecosystems, and on the diversity of the landscape [ 2, 29]. The 
satellite-based investigations have also revealed how water bodies in urban areas and 
wetlands are often altered by urban growth, especially in areas of land conversion and 
infrastructure development, over the past few years [9].
However, the existing study is based on spectral indices derived only from remotely 
sensed data and these relationships must be viewed with caution. Possible modification 
of wetlands with urban development is noted in the observed spatial patterns, but hydro-
logical variation, seasonal rainfall variation, alteration of drainage patterns, and local 
water-management practices may also influence changes observed. The study found that 
there were strong negative correlations between NDVI and built-up indices (NDBI, UI, 
BI, and EBBI), which align with other studies that found an inverse relationship between 
vegetation cover and urbanization [ 17, 36]. These results have also been observed in 
recent remote sensing research, where the conversion of built-up areas was associated 
with a decay in vegetation and fluctuations in the ecological conditions of cities.
Moreover, the better performance of EBBI in identifying built-up and barren surfaces 
was found to be consistent than other built-up index which showed that EBBI outper -
forms traditional built-up indices like NDBI and UI in diverse urban environments [ 4]. 
Previous GIS-based research also demonstrated the effectiveness of using multi-tempo -
ral NDVI for vegetation dynamic monitoring [ 14, 32] and recent studies highlighted the 
need for combining multiple spectral indices to achieve a more complete understanding 
of urban environmental dynamics. The present study is different from the previous stud-
ies which mainly focused on individual indices like NDWI, NDVI, or NDBI [ 1, 14, 17] as 
it uses eight complementary spectral indices for the simultaneous assessment of vegeta -
tion, water bodies, built-up land, and barren surfaces under both pre- and post-monsoon 
conditions. The multi-index framework is an integrated, comprehensive assessment 
of the seasonal land surface dynamics in Ara, a fast-expanding medium-sized city, for 
which similar geospatial studies are limited.
Positive NDVI values are typically linked with improved vegetation health, which 
can lead to lower urban heat island effect, better soil moisture storage, and ecological 
habitats [8, 33, 43]. In contrast, reduction in vegetation and addition of impervious sur -
faces are strongly associated with urban environmental degradation [ 35]. The same, it 
has been reported in previous studies that urbanization leads to a reduction in the area 
of rivers, wetlands and khals and rise in the risk of floods and water logging [ 24, 38]. 
The current work found similar spatial relationships among vegetation loss, urbaniza -
tion growth, and changes in water-related variables. Although direct hydrological and 
Page 22 of 28
Rahaman and Sadaf Discover Geoscience (2026) 4:380 
ecological observations were not the focus of this research, these relationships may be 
interpreted as statistical associations, not direct cause-effect relationships.
The results of this study have a relevance to sustainable urban planning and the man -
agement of wetlands in rapid urbanization cities like Ara. The spatially explicit frame -
work of integrated use of NDVI, NDWI, MNDWI, NDBI, UI, NDBal, BI and EBBI 
facilitates the identification of vegetation loss, wetland degradation and built-up expan -
sion areas. These can be used to help urban planners and local authorities consider the 
conservation of wetlands, control land reclamation and landfill operations, preserve 
urban green spaces, and integrate blue-green infrastructure into future development 
plans. In addition, frequent monitoring with multi-temporal satellite data can support 
policy makers to assess the success of restoration measures and develop evidence-based 
approaches for sustainable use of the land in growing cities like Ara. Recent research 
also has shown the potential of satellite-derived vegetation and urban indicators in the 
background of sustainable urban planning and climate adaptation [37].
Finally, the changes in the wetland-related indices observed could have wider ecologi -
cal implications. Research has previously been conducted on the loss of migratory birds 
due to wetlands, mangroves and tidal flats degradation in South and Southeast Asia [40]. 
Field observations of bird abundance and biodiversity were not conducted in the pres -
ent study, so it is not possible to make any direct inferences about migratory birds in 
Ara. Rather, these results highlight areas where further ecological research combining 
remote-sensing techniques and biodiversity sampling can benefit the understanding of 
how urban development affects wetland-breeding species. Additional ecological studies 
combining field studies and remote sensing are required to assess these potential effects 
(Figs. 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9).

8 Conclusions
This study evaluated the spatio-temporal variations of vegetation, wetlands, and built-up 
areas at Ara city from 1995 to 2025 using eight spectral indices extracted from multi-
temporal Landsat images (NDVI, NDWI, MNDWI, NDBI, UI, NDBal, BI and EBBI). 
The outcomes show a clearly marked seasonal pattern of environmental conditions. The 
pre-monsoon NDVI values increased slightly throughout the study period while the 
post monsoon values decreased, suggesting poorer peak NDVI values and higher spa -
tial variation in 2025 than in 1995. Pearson’s correlation analysis also discovered signifi -
cant negative associations between NDVI and all built-up indices ( p ≤ 0.05), indicating 
that built-up areas were associated with spatial variability of vegetation. The analysis of 
water-related indices indicates temporal changes in wetland and surface-water charac -
teristics. The inverse relationship between the built-up indices and MNDWI as well as 
the contrasting behaviour of NDWI and MNDWI indicate changes in wetlands during 
the study period.
The combination of several spectral indices during the pre-monsoon and post-mon -
soon period serves as a holistic approach to monitoring long-term environmental 
change in a rapidly increasing urbanizing region. The outcomes could help urban plan -
ners and environmental managers determine where vegetation loss, wetland alteration, 
and urban development may be occurring, which could help guide sustainable land-
use planning, wetland conservation, and ecosystem management. It is recognized that 
the changes observed in the wetlands could have ecological consequences for wetland 
dependent biodiversity, such as migratory birds, but the current study did not incorpo -
rate field-based biodiversity or data on bird populations. For this reason, these implica -
tions are preliminary and should be considered as yet to be validated with subsequent 
ecological studies. Integrating these techniques of remote sensing, land-use analysis, and 
field-based ecological surveys will give a more all-inclusive understanding of the rela -
tionships amongst urbanization, wetland dynamics, vegetation change, and the potential 
for preserving biodiversity in these wetlands.
