Hyperuricemia, characterized by elevated serum uric acid 
levels, is a biochemical precursor of various medical condi-
tions, including gout, uric acid nephropathy, and the formation 
of uric acid stones. It is intricately associated with metabolic 
disorders (e.g., diabetes, obesity, and dyslipidemia), chronic 
kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease (e.g., hypertension, 
coronary artery disease, and stroke). Although the prevalence of 
hyperuricemia varies by region and ethnicity, the global burden 
has increased substantially over the past two decades [1,2]. Hy-
peruricemia and gout are more common in men than women; 
however, uric acid levels increase in women after menopause. 
Hyperuricemia should be understood as a condition affected 
by a complex array of factors, such as metabolic health, dietary 
patterns, renal efficiency, and hormonal dynamics, rather than 
being solely attributed to excessive food consumption.
In this issue of the Journal of Rheumatic Diseases, Lee and 
Kim [3] analyzed data from the 2019 to 2021 Korea National 
Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) involv-
ing a cohort of 14,305 individuals aged ≥20 years. Dietary qual-
ity was assessed using the Korean Healthy Eating Index (KHEI). 
This study employed the KHEI to thoroughly evaluate dietary 
quality, thereby providing a framework for discussing the re-
lationship between overall dietary quality and hyperuricemia 
within Korean dietary practices. The KHEI is a comprehensive 
measure of dietary quality that includes 14 components, includ-
ing breakfast, whole grains, fruits, vegetables, protein foods, 
dairy, saturated fat, sodium, sugars, and beverages, and the bal-
ance of carbohydrates, fats, and energy. In the real world, uric 
acid metabolism is influenced by complex dietary patterns, rath-
er than by individual nutrients. This offers the advantage of eval-
uating hyperuricemia in the broader context of overall dietary 
patterns and metabolic health rather than solely attributing it 
to the intake of specific high-purine foods. Recent studies using 
the KHEI have consistently shown that overall dietary quality is 
associated with hyperuricemia. Using a large-scale KNHANES 
dataset, Son et al. [4] demonstrated that higher KHEI scores 
were associated with lower odds of hyperuricemia. Kim et al. [5] 
applied propensity score matching to further compare nutrient 
intake and dietary quality between individuals with and without 
hyperuricemia. Based on the KHEI evaluation, it was docu-
mented that individuals with hyperuricemia had significantly 
lower scores than the healthy controls. Nonetheless, the KHEI 
may not fully capture alcohol intake and specific subtypes of 
purine-rich foods. The reported studies collectively affirm the 
significance of the KHEI as a comprehensive dietary quality in-
dex in hyperuricemia research. Furthermore, the findings high-
light the potential relevance of overall dietary quality in dietary 
counseling for individuals with or at risk of hyperuricemia.
Recent population-based studies have suggested a sex-depen-
dent relationship between dietary quality and hyperuricemia [5-
7]. Adherence to healthy dietary patterns, such as the Dietary 
Approaches to Stop Hypertension and Mediterranean diets, was 
associated with a reduced risk of hyperuricemia in both sexes.

able data originate from observational studies and systematic 
reviews, which indicate that the association between dietary 
patterns, overall dietary quality, and hyperuricemia demon-
strates sex-specific differences [3,5,7-9]. Notwithstanding these 
earlier findings, a definitive consensus on sex-specific dietary 
recommendations remains elusive, largely because of the scar-
city of randomized controlled trials that directly evaluate sex-
based differences in urate-lowering efficacy. Consequently, well-
designed, sex-specific clinical trials are crucial for providing the 
evidence required for sex-specific dietary recommendations.
Lee and Kim [3] underscore the importance of sex-specific 
analysis as a primary research question. Their work makes a 
significant contribution by employing separate stratified analy-
ses, rather than merely treating sex as a covariate, to reevaluate 
the relationship between dietary quality and hyperuricemia 
in Korean adults. In men, higher KHEI scores were associated 
with a gradual reduction in the risk of hyperuricemia, whereas 
no significant trend was observed in women. In a multivari-
able logistic regression model with restricted cubic splines, men 
showed a consistent reduction in risk as KHEI scores increased, 
whereas women exhibited a U-shaped risk pattern [3]. This 
study demonstrates that improving the overall dietary qual-
ity may serve as a potentially modifiable factor associated with 
lower odds of hyperuricemia in men. In contrast, the findings 
highlighted the complexity of uric acid metabolism in women, 
which cannot be solely attributed to dietary factors. Although 
these findings do not warrant distinct dietary recommendations 
for men and women, they strongly support the need for sex-
stratified analyses and personalized interpretations in future di-
etary research on hyperuricemia. Although Lee and Kim’s study 
[3] demonstrates considerable strengths, its findings should be 
interpreted cautiously owing to its inherent limitations, includ-
ing the cross-sectional study design, recall bias, and insufficient 
adjustment for confounding factors. Nonetheless, utilizing a 
large-scale Korean cohort, this study holds significant clinical 
value by reaffirming the potential relevance of overall dietary 
quality to hyperuricemia while highlighting the necessity of a 
sex-specific approach. These findings suggest the importance of 
considering sex when interpreting the relationship between di-
etary quality and hyperuricemia. In men, dietary patterns were 
directly correlated with the risk of hyperuricemia. In contrast, 
in women, non-dietary factors, such as hormonal shifts, meta-
bolic status, medication, and renal function, are likely to exert a 
greater influence. Therefore, these findings shift the discussion 
from generic dietary considerations toward a more nuanced, 
sex-specific interpretation of the association between dietary 
quality and hyperuricemia. Ultimately, these findings should 
be viewed as hypothesis-generating evidence supporting sex-
stratified dietary research. Future prospective cohort studies and 
randomized controlled trials are required to determine whether 
personalized, sex-specific dietary interventions can improve 
long-term clinical outcomes.
