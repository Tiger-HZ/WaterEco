Intermittent water supply is common across the Global South. In Multi-Village Schemes, one source
supplies several village storage tanks within ﬁxed supply periods, making balanced allocation difﬁcult
when ﬂow information is limited. This study presents an integrated framework for multilingual demand
input, data-driven scheduling, and automatic valve operation. A voice interface collects tank
demands, the scheduling model generates a valve schedule, and a programmable logic controller
executes the schedule without manual transfer between stages. The framework adapts an existing
discrete-event model, mixed-integer linear programming formulation, and ε-greedy state-selection
strategy. It uses measured state-speci ﬁc ﬂows instead of hydraulic equations. On an eight-tank
laboratory network with 256 valve states, ﬂow measurements from 33 states (13%) produced a
schedule with an absolute solver-predicted deviation below 2.6% for every tank. Across eight physical
trials, the largest absolute mean experimental deviation was 5.55%. The optimized schedules
produced more balanced allocations than the basic operating policies. In simulation, ε-greedy and
pure greedy performed similarly for the eight-tank network, while ε-greedy required fewer states on
average for the ten-tank network. The voice interface achieved 97 –98% entity-extraction accuracy
under the tested clean and noisy conditions, with no hallucinated demand assignments observed.
These results demonstrate an integrated demand-to-actuation work ﬂow on a laboratory-scale
intermittent water-supply testbed.
Reliable access to safe drinking water remains a persistent global challenge,
particularly in low and middle-incom e regions across the Global South.
Despite substantial investments in water infrastructure, intermittent water
supply (IWS) continues to be widely practiced due to source limitations,
infrastructure constraints, and operational challenges1–3. In IWS systems,
water is delivered only during limited time windows, leading to supply
unreliability, pressure variability, and inequitable distribution among
consumers2,4.
In many water scarce regions,particularly in South Asia–rural and peri
urban supply systems are organized as shared-source networks in which a
single reservoir supplies multiple geographically dispersed villages or sto-
rage tanks through a common transmission system as shown in Fig.1.T h e s e
conﬁgurations are commonly referred toa sM u l t i - V i l l a g eS c h e m e s( M V S )
or Regional Rural Water Supply Schemes (RRWSS) 5. Because all storage
tanks depend on the same upstream source, the timing and duration of
supply to one village directly affect availability elsewhere. Under limited
supply conditions, increasing deliveryto one settlement necessarily reduces
availability to others. Coordinated scheduling of valve operations is there-
fore essential to ensure equitable allocation across villages.
Unlike continuously operated wate r distribution networks, where
operational objectives emphasize pressure regulation and energy efﬁciency6,
MVS networks regulate service primarily through time-based control of
inlet valves. The central operational objective shifts toward equity— deﬁned
as uniformity in proportional demand satisfaction across villages when total
available supply is insuf ﬁcient7. Achieving this requires systematic sche-
duling strategies rather than heuristic or experience-based operation.

Optimization-based formulations have been proposed to address
equitable allocation in intermittent systems7,8. Scheduling in such networks
involves binary valve decisions coupled with nonlinear hydraulic relation-
ships governing ﬂow and pressure, resulting in mixed-integer nonlinear
programming (MINLP) problems 6,9. These formulations are computa-
tionally intensive and require accura te hydraulic models that are rarely
available in practice.
Rural water supply networks in th e Global South exhibit pressure-
dependent demand behavior, transientﬁlling dynamics, and highly variable
boundary conditions, complicatingsteady-state hydraulic modeling10.E v e n
recent calibration approaches develo ped for data-scarce environments
continue to rely on detailed network topology, measuredﬂows, and pressure
observations11. In many MVS networks, such data are unavailable or out-
dated, limiting the practical applicability of model-dependent optimization
approaches.
A data-driven alternative that avoids the need for calibrated hydraulic
models reformulates the scheduling problem using a Discrete Event Model
(DEM)12. In the DEM representation, each distinct valve con ﬁguration is
treated as a system state with aﬁxed experimentally measuredﬂow vector,
replacing nonlinear hydraulic equations with pre-measured values and
reducing the MINLP to a tractable mixed integer linear programming
(MILP) problem. A practical limitation of this approach is the exponential
growth of the state space with the number of valves (2n states for n valves),
making exhaustive characterization infeasible for larger networks. To
address this limitation, anε-greedy exploration strategy was introduced to
identify a reduced set of informative valve states by prioritizing conﬁgura-
tions that serve tanks with current supply deﬁcits13. This approach enables
low-deviation schedules to be obtained without measuring the complete
valve-state space.
Surrogate-based active learning provides another approach for redu-
cing the number of costly state evaluations. Gaussian-process models pre-
dict both the response and uncertainty of unmeasured candidates, while an
acquisition function balances the use of promising candidates with the
exploration of uncertain regions. Similar Bayesian optimization methods
have been applied to pump scheduling and control in water distribution
networks14,15.T h i sp r o v i d e sab a s i sf o re v a l u ating Gaussian-process-based
active learning as an alternative valve-state selection method in the
present study.
A complementary challenge concerns execution. Existing reviews
indicate that much of the scheduling literature emphasizes simulation and
benchmark studies, while practical implementation remains constrained by
computational demands, modeling requirements, and control integration
challenges6. The gap between a computed optimal schedule and its reliable
physical execution in theﬁeld has received limited attention.
Reservoirs in Indian MVS networks are typically of theﬁll-and-draw
type, with separate inlets at the highest level and outlets at the lowest level16.
Inlet valves are regulated as binary devices — fully open or fully closed —
using multi-turn manual valves that require signiﬁcant physical effort to
operate (more than 45 turns to open or close a valve on a 10-inch line) 17.
Retroﬁtting existing valves with electrical actuators and remote actuation
through IoT-enabled devices has been explored, though operator interac-
tion typically remains through conventional smartphone displays or
Human Machine Interfaces (HMIs)18.
In MVS networks, ﬁeld studies report that routine oversight is often
carried out by local caretakers rather than trained hydraulic operators19.
These personnel communicate storage status or daily demand estimates to a
central operator, who then opens or closes valves according to heuristic
plans. Demand articulation, therefore, forms an integral part of the opera-
tional workﬂow, since allocation outcomes depend directly on the accuracy
of reported inputs.
Speech-based human–computer interaction has been widely studied as
a mechanism to reduce barriers associated with text literacy, keyboard
proﬁciency, and language constraints indiverse socio-technical contexts20.
In low-resource and multilingual env ironments, speech interfaces lower
articulation and cognitive burdens relative to text-dominant systems21.E a r l y
deployments in rural India demonstrated that voice-based systems enable
inclusive interaction among semi-literate and multilingual communities22,
and subsequent studies indicate th at voice interaction is embedded in
routine smartphone practice across socio-economic groups in India23.T h e s e
characteristics make voice-based demand acquisition a natural ﬁtf o rt h e
MVS operational context, where operators communicate in multiple
regional languages.

Results
The proposed framework was evalu ated experimentally on an eight-
demand-tank subnetwork of the Reconﬁgurable Testbed for the Control
and Operation of Water Distribution Networks (RTCOP-WDN), where a
single overhead tank (OHT) supplied gravity-driven ﬂow through inde-
pendently actuated inlet valves. The optimized schedule was implemented
on the testbed and benchmarked against two basic operating heuristics—
demand-proportional time allocation and operator based operation — as
well as schedules obtained using pure-greedy and Gaussian-process-based
state selection. Because the network has 2 8 = 256 possible valve states,
exhaustive experimental characterization was impractical; the proposedϵ-
greedy strategy required measurements from only 33 states. A broader
comparison of ϵ-greedy, pure-greedy, random, and Gaussian-process
methods was conducted separately using EPANET/WNTR-simulatedﬂows
for eight- and ten-tank networks.
Scheduling performance and convergence
The scheduling process was initialized withM random valve states, whereM
is the number of valves. Multiple valves could be open in each state, and the
initial set included every valve open at least once. Thus, eight initial states
were used for the eight-valve network. Given the limitedﬂow information
available initially, the schedule obtained at the ﬁrst iteration exhibited
relatively large deviations from the speciﬁed demand targets. To improve
the allocation performance, additionalvalve states were selected iteratively
using the ϵ-greedy heuristic described in the Methods section. At each
iteration, the selected state and its correspondingﬂow rates were appended
to theﬂow table, after which the scheduling problem was re-solved using the
updated information.
The objective function represents the aggregate absolute deviation
between the supplied and target demands, together with a maximum-
deviation term that promotes equitable allocation among the tanks. Our
aim is to get that objective function close to zero, which indicates
relative deviation are very less for all the tanks. Fig. 2 illustrates the
reduction in the objective function value as additional valve states were
incorporated.
Using 33 of the 256 possible valve states (12.9% of the full state space),
the adapted ε-greedy method reached an objective value of 0.103. At this
point, the absolute relative deviation of each tank was below 2.6%. This
objective value was used as the common threshold for all state-selection
methods. Sampling efﬁciency was de ﬁned as the total number of states,
including the initial states, required for the objective to ﬁrst satisfy
J ≤ Jref = 0.103.
Figure 3 compares the tank-level relative deviations at the initial and
ﬁnal iterations. The initial schedule showed a wide range of deviations
because the ﬂow table contained limited state information. As additional
valve states were included, both the magnitude and spread of the deviations
decreased. At theﬁnal iteration, the absolute relative deviation of each tank
was below 2.6%, indicating amore balanced allocation.

Performance under supply shortage conditions
Intermittent water distribution systems frequently operate under conditions
where the available water is insufﬁcient to satisfy total consumer demand.
To evaluate the proposed schedulin g framework under such conditions,
additional experiments were conducted by proportionally scaling all tank
demands by the same factor, thereby preserving the relative demand dis-
tribution while reﬂecting the reduced available supply. Schedules were then
generated using the proposed methodology with these revised targets.
Two shortage scenarios were investigated: a 10% and a 15% reduction
in total available supply. The originalt o t a ld e m a n do ft h e8 - t a n kn e t w o r k
was 81.28 L, reduced to 73.16 L under the 10% scenario and 69.09 L under
the 15% scenario.
The results under a 10% supply reduction are presented in Table3.A l l
experimentally delivered volumes closely matched the revised target
demands, with implementation deviations ranging from− 5.03% to 4.79%,
well within the ± 5% acceptance criterion. The experimental supplies were
also in close agreement with solver predictions, con ﬁrming successful
implementation of the generated schedule under moderate shortage
conditions.
Table 4 presents results under the more severe 15% reduction.
Implementation deviations ranged from− 4.99% to 5.67%. The marginal
exceedance observed for T6 (5.67%) is attributed to the errors from the level
sensor, and equitable allocation was broadly maintained across all tanks.
Solver predictions and experimental outcomes remained in close agree-
ment, demonstrating the framework’s ability to generate realizable sche-
dules even under tighter supply constraints.
Taken together, both shortage scenarios con ﬁrm that the proposed
framework maintains equitable distribution under supply-limited condi-
tions. The proportional demand scaling approach allows the optimization to
directly accommodate shortage scenarios without structural modiﬁcation,
making the methodology well suited to the variable supply conditions
characteristic of intermittent water distribution systems.
Sensitivity analysis for the exploration parameterε
The exploration parameter ε governs the balance between random
exploration and deﬁcit-based exploitation in the state selection heuristic.
A higher ε increases the probability of selecting a valve state at random,
promoting broader exploration of the state space, while a lower ε biases
selection towards states that serve the tanks with the greatest supply
deﬁcit. Since the number of states required to reach a target objective can
vary between runs due to the stochastic nature of the selection process, a
sensitivity analysis was conducted to identify a suitable value of ε prior to
the main experiments.
Four candidate values were evaluated:ε ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15}. For
each value, ﬁve independent trials were performed, each starting from the
same initial working set of valve states. The number of states added to the
working set before the target objective function value was achieved was
recorded for each trial. The results are summarized in Table 5 and visua-
lized Fig. 6.
The results indicate that the mean number of states explored is broadly
similar across all four values, ranging from 34.6 (ε = 0.01) to 37.0 (ε = 0.10).
This suggests that, within the investigated range, the search effort required to
reach the target objective was relatively insensitive to the exact choice ofε.
The standard deviation varied more noticeably. The highest variability
was observed forε = 0.15 (10.01 states), whereasε = 0.10 exhibited the lowest
variability (5.87 states), indicating more consistent convergence behavior
across repeated trials. The value ε = 0.05 provides a moderate level of
exploration with a mean search effort (35.2 states) comparable to the best-
performing conﬁguration and a moderate standard deviation (7.29 states).
Accordingly,ε = 0.05 was selected for all subsequent experiments.

The operator based operation method 24 was implemented by
opening all valves simultaneously at the beginning of the 24-min sche-
duling horizon. The cumulative volume supplied to each tank was
monitored during operation. When the supplied volume of a tank
reached its speciﬁed demand, the corresponding valve was closed, while
the valves associated with the remaining unsatisﬁed tanks remained open.
The procedure continued until all demands were satis ﬁed or the 24-min

characterization of the combined valve states was used. Table 7 presents
the demand, experimentally received volume, and resulting relative

tank-level inequality, with a maximum absolute deviation of 44.9%. Because

For the state-selection benchmark comparison, a consistent sampling-
efﬁciency criterion was used. The objective value achieved by theϵ-greedy
method, Jref = 0.103, was adopted as the common performance threshold.
Starting from the same initial ﬂow table, each state-selection method was
applied iteratively until the objectiveﬁrst satisﬁed J ≤ Jref.S a m p l i n ge fﬁciency
was quantiﬁed by the total number of measured valve states required to
reach this threshold, including the initial states.
The pure-greedy valve-state selection method iteratively selected valve
states based on the deﬁcits obtained from the current schedule. After solving
the scheduling problem using the availableﬂow table, under-supplied tanks
were ranked according to their relative deﬁcits, and the next unmeasured
state was selected to prioritizeﬂow to those tanks. The correspondingﬂow
rates were measured and added to the ﬂow table before the scheduling
problem was re-solved. Unlike the ϵ-greedy method, pure greedy did not
include an explicit exploration step.
The pure-greedy method attained the reference objective, J ≤ 0.103,
after 33 measured states. Althoughthis was the same number required by
the ϵ-greedy method, the two methods selected different sequences of valve
states. Table 8 compares the solver-predicted allocation obtained from the
pure-greedyﬂow table with its physical implementation on the eight-tank
testbed.
The solver-predicted deviations ranged from − 1.58% to + 1.37%.
Following physical implementation, the deviations ranged from− 4.77% to
+ 3.94%. The experimental results therefore remained reasonably close to
the solver predictions, with the largest absolute experimental deviation
observed for T6.
The Gaussian-process (GP)-based active learning method used a data-
driven surrogate model to select informative valve states. A separate GP was
trained for each tank using the measured valve states and their corre-
sponding ﬂow rates. For every unmeasured state, the models predicted the
tank-levelﬂows and their associated uncertainties. An acquisition function
combined the predicted ability of each state to reduce the current demand
deﬁcits with an uncertainty-based exploration term. The highest-scoring
state was measured and added to theﬂow table, after which the scheduling
problem was re-solved. This process continued until the reference objective
was attained.
The GP-based method attained J ≤ 0.103 after 38 measured states.

Discussion
This study demonstrates the end-to-end implementation of a data-driven
scheduling framework that connects m ultilingual demand acquisition,
schedule generation, and automatic PLC-controlled valve actuation on an
eight-tank laboratory network. Using measuredﬂows from 33 of the 256
possible valve states, the framework produced a low-deviation allocation
while satisfying the imposed switching and convenient-hours constraints.
This study builds on the DEM–MILP formulation andε-greedy state-
selection strategy developed in earlier work 12,13. Here, these methods are
adapted and integrated with multilingual demand input and PLC-based
valve operation. The integrated system is evaluated through physical trials,
comparisons with operating and state-selection methods, model-based
benchmarking, and simulations on a larger network.
The comparison with the basic ope rating policies demonstrates the
importance of accounting for tank-speciﬁc ﬂow characteristics. Allocating
valve-open durations solely in proportion to demand resulted in substantial
under-supply because the policy did not account for differences in theﬂow
delivered to each tank. Operator based operation method delivered a large
fraction of the aggregate dema nd but produced substantial de ﬁcits in
individual tanks. Thus, high aggregate water delivery did not ensure equi-
table tank-level demand satisfaction. In comparison, the schedules gener-
ated using measured state-speci ﬁc ﬂows produced more balanced
allocations under the speciﬁed operational constraints.
The experimental results show th at effective scheduling can be
achieved without measuring every possible valve conﬁguration. However,
because all 256 states were not characterized experimentally, the minimum
attainable objective value for the physical network remains unknown. The
result is therefore interpreted as successful reduced-state scheduling rather
than as evidence of near-optimality.
The ε-greedy strategy performed comparably to pure greedy for the
eight-tank network. In the experimental study, both methods required 33
measured states to attain the reference objective threshold, although they did
not select the same states. In the simulation study, pure greedy andε-greedy
required averages of 39 and 41 states, respectively, to meet the 5% deviation
criterion. For the ten-tank network, ε-greedy required fewer states on
average and met the criterion in all ﬁve runs. Its exploration component
periodically selected conﬁgurations outside those favored by the current
tank deﬁcits, which may reduce the risk of restricting the search to a limited
region. These results indicate that ε-greedy performed on par with pure
greedy in the eight-tank network and provided a clearer advantage in the
larger ten-tank state space.
GP based method implemented on experimental set up required 5
more states than the ε-greedy method and pure greedy method. In
numerical simulations, the GP based selection method explores similar
number of states as compared to theϵ greedy method. However, it requires
estimation of the kernel parameters during each iteration which is a clear
disadvantage as compared to the ϵ greedy method which requires only
simple calculations.
The calibrated WNTR model provided a model-based reference using
simulatedﬂows for all valve states. The resulting schedules had low solver-
predicted deviations, but their deviations increased when implemented on
the physical network. The model used measurements from the same
33 states to estimate the unknown hydraulic loss parameters because no
other calibration data were available. Therefore, this comparison tests how
well the calibrated model predictsﬂows outside these 33 states.
The model-based comparison also shows the information needed to
build a hydraulic model. Even in the controlled laboratory network, the
unknown roughness and minor-loss parameters had to be estimated from
ﬂow measurements. This task can be more difﬁcult in ﬁeld networks where
network data and operating conditions may be incomplete or uncertain. The
data-driven formulation does not require these parameters because it uses
measured ﬂow rates directly. However, it still requiresﬂow measurements
from a representative set of valve states.
The ten-tank simulation provides evidence that a scheduling-relevant
subset can be identi ﬁed without incorporating the complete state space.
Although the number of possible conﬁgurations increased fourfold relative
to the eight-tank system, the adaptedε- g r e e d ys t r a t e g yi d e n t iﬁed schedules
satisfying the selected deviation criteria using only a fraction of the available
states. The observed MILP solution times also showed that reducing the
number of states does not necessarily reduce computation time, because a
larger state set can provide the solver with greaterﬂexibility in satisfying the
scheduling constraints. The principalpurpose of state selection is therefore
to reduce the number of physical ﬂow measurements rather than the
computational size of the scheduling problem.
The shortfall experiments showed that the scheduler continued to
produce feasible allocations when the available water was below the total
demand. Tank-level deviations increased as water availability decreased.
These ﬁndings apply only to the two tested shortfall conditions.
The present sensitivity analyses varied ε and β separately. They
therefore do not measure the combined effects of algorithmic, hydraulic,
and operational inputs. Future work will apply global sensitivity analysis by
varying different inputs together. First-order and total-order indices can
then be used to measure their direct and interaction effects26,27.
The multilingual voice interface connects demand input with
schedule generation and execution. It maintained high entity-extraction
accuracy under the tested clean, noisy, and code-mixed conditions, with
no hallucinated demand assignments observed. The code-mixed results
also showed that entity extraction remained reliable despite lower tran-
scription accuracy. Further testing with more speakers, dialects, acoustic
conditions, and alternative input methods will support its use in ﬁeld
applications.
The RTCOP-WDN testbed represents key features of gravity-fed
intermittent water-supply systems, including a common elevated source,
separate supply branches, binary va lve operation, demand targets, and
restricted supply periods. However, the laboratory network does not
reproduce the scale, leakage, infrastructure condition, or operating uncer-
tainty of a ﬁeld network. Field validation is therefore required to assess
performance under real operating c onditions. Direct comparison with
published scheduling studies is also difﬁcult because the networks, objec-
tives, constraints, and evaluation conditions differ.
The present system uses binary valve operation and an open-loop
schedule based on ﬁxed demand inputs. Binary operation represents the
targeted ﬁeld condition, where inlet valves are either fully open or fully
closed. Continuous valve control could provideﬁner ﬂow adjustment where
suitable equipment is available. The current schedule does not respond to
changes in demand or water availability during operation. Future work will
include ﬁeld testing, further hydraulic-model validation, comparison with
additional scheduling and demand-input methods, and an Economic
Model Predictive Control formulation using real-time tank-level feedback
and a shrinking scheduling horizon28.

The proposed framework integrates three functional components —
demand acquisition, optimization-based scheduling, and physical control
execution — into a uni ﬁed pipeline for equitable water distribution in
intermittently operated rural and peri-urban networks. Figure8 provides an
overview of the complete system, illustrating the data and controlﬂow from
spoken user input through to valve actuation at the laboratory testbed. The
following subsections describe each component in detail, beginning with the

System architecture
T h ed e m a n da c q u i s i t i o nl a y e ra c c e p t ss p o k e nc o m m a n d sf r o mﬁeld
operators through the multilingual voice interface. Recognized speech is

laboratory network under controlled valve states serve as the hydraulic input

At the computational scheduling layer, the demand vector and ﬂow
parameters are passed to the MILP solver (Gurobi 12.0, via the PuLP
interface). The solver operates over a24-slot scheduling horizon and returns
an optimal sequence of valve states— one active conﬁguration per time slot
— that minimizes relative supply deviations across all tanks subject to the
operational constraints.
The resulting schedule is translated into a binary open-close actuation
sequence and transmitted to a PLC through intranet. The PLC executes each
valve transition deterministically at the corresponding time interval, com-
pleting the decision-to-actuation loop from spoken demand to physical
supply.
Scheduling problem formulation
Equitable water distribution is formulated as an optimization problem that
allocates supply over aﬁnite scheduling horizon by selecting valve states that
minimize relative deviation betwe en supplied and demanded volumes
across all demand tanks.
RRWSS/MVS-type systems typically follow a ﬁll-and-draw conﬁg-
uration in which water from a centralized source is conveyed to overhead
storage tanks through inlet pipes at the top, while withdrawal occurs
through separate outlet pipes at the bottom 16. This structural separation
hydraulically decouples the supply side from the downstream distribution
network: inﬂow to a tank during a supply interval is governed entirely by
upstream head conditions and valve conﬁguration, and is independent of
the storage level within the tank.
Exploiting this property, Kurian et al. [ref.12] introduced a data-driven
optimization framework for MVS networks. Each unique combination of
ON/OFF valve positions de ﬁnes a state characterized by ﬁxed ﬂow dis-
tributions, forming a Discrete Event Model (DEM). Flows associated with
each state are determined either through hydraulic simulation or direct
experimental measurement. By treating these as known parameters, the
nonlinear hydraulic equations are decoupled from the scheduling problem,
transforming the original MINLP in to a mixed-integer linear program
(MILP) that is substantially more tractable. A detailed derivation of the
DEM formulation and the correspon ding reduction from the original
MINLP to the MILP scheduling model is provided in Supplementary
Section S2.
Although the DEM approach eliminates full state-space enumeration,
the number of feasible valve states grows exponentially with the number of
valves (2n for n valves). To mitigate this combinatorial growth, a de ﬁcit-
driven ε-greedy exploration strategy proposed in Adhithyan et al. [ref.13]
iteratively constructs a reduced yet informative subset of valve states by
prioritizing states that improve supply to villages experiencing the largest
relative de ﬁcits, enabling near-optimal s cheduling without exhaustive
characterization of the state space.

Multi-Village Scheme networks operating under intermittent supply con-
ditions. The platform comprises four overhead tanks (OHTs) and twenty
storage tanks (STs), interconnected through a reconﬁg u r a b l ep i p en e t w o r k .

village-level household or community storage points, consistent with the

For the experiments reported her e, an eight-tank subnetwork was
conﬁgured: one OHT serving as the source and eight demand tanks (T1–T8)
as consumers. The OHT was positioned approximately 2.6 m above the
demand tanks, providing the hydrauli c head required for gravity-driven
distribution— a deliberate design choice to replicate the conditions of
gravity-fed water schemes common in rural India12. The water level in the
OHT was maintained within a controlled range of 46.5 ± 2.5 cm throughout
all experiments to ensure consistent supply-side head pressure across trials.
Each supply pipe was equipped with a Bürkert Model 3280 motorized
ball valve, operated in binary ON/OFF mode via analog control signals
(4–20 mA) from the automation system. These valves allow rapid, repea-
table switching between open and closed states, enabling precise control of
the valve conﬁguration at each scheduling interval.
The Automation system was implemented using an AXC F 2152
PLCnext controller along with AXL F AO8 1F and AXL F AO4 1H Axioline
F I/O modules manufactured by Phoenix Contact, Germany.
Tank water levels were measured using HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensors
mounted on each tank connected to an Arduino Mega. The water level data
was logged at a rate of one reading for everyﬁve seconds in each tank; raw
distance readings were low-passﬁltered and converted to volume estimates
based on the known tank cross-sectional area.
The distribution network consists of 3.5 mm diameter stainless steel
tubes connecting a 10.5 mm diameter main trunk from the OHT to each
demand tank via individual supply branches. The OHT has a diameter of
45 cm and height of 75 cm, while each demand tank is constructed from
acrylic glass with a diameter of 30 cm and height of 50 cm. Each branch is
independently controlled by its dedicated Bürkert 3280 valve, so that
opening or closing a valve fully enables or isolatesﬂow to the corresponding
tank. There are no shared branch segments between demand tanks,
ensuring that theﬂow delivered to any tank depends only on the state of its
own valve and the prevailing OHT head— a structural property that makes
each valve con ﬁguration’s ﬂow vector reproducible under constant head
conditions.
The laboratory scheduling horizon consists of N =2 4o n e - m i n u t e
intervals, giving a total experime ntal window of 24 min per trial. This
compact horizon is a laboratory-scale analog of a real-world supply window,
which typically spans 6 –24 hours discretised into hourly intervals. The
capacity of the demand tanks is 35 l and the averageﬂow rate in the tank is
1.5 lpm (liter per minute). The slot duration of 1 minute is chosen to match
the hydraulic time constants of the scaled system.
A four-tank sub-con ﬁguration of the same platform was used for
preliminary validation of the scheduling framework. Results of that study,
including convergence behavior and demand tracking accuracy for the
reduced system, are provided in Supplementary Section S1.

Baseline operating and state-selection methods
Two basic operating policies and three alternative state-selection methods
were used for comparison. The operating policies were time-proportional
demand allocation and operator-based operation, while the state-selection
methods were random state selection, pure-greedy state selection, and
Gaussian-process-based active learning. The time-proportional demand
allocation and operator-based operation policies were implemented on the
physical eight-tank testbed. Pure-greedy and GP-based state selection were
evaluated experimentally on the eight-tank testbed and through WNTR/
EPANET simulations of the eight- and ten-tank networks. The adaptedε-
greedy strategy was evaluated under the same experimental and simulation
conditions. Random state selection was evaluated only in simulation
because its random behavior required several independent runs, making
repeated physical measurements and implementations impractical within
the experimental scope.
The time-proportional demand allocation method opened each valve
once, with its open duration assigned in proportion to the corresponding
tank demand. The valves were operated sequentially. When one valve was

open, all other valves remained closed. No ﬂow information, hydraulic
model, or optimization was used to generate the schedule.
The operator-based operation method opened all valves at the begin-
ning of the supply period24. When the cumulative volume supplied to a tank
reached its demand, the corresponding valve was closed, while the valves of
the remaining unsatisﬁed tanks remained open. The process continued until
all demands were satisﬁed or the supply period ended.
In random state selection, at each iteration, one valve state was selected
uniformly at random from the remaining unobserved states, without
replacement. Five simulation runs were conducted using different
random seeds.

adapted ε-greedy strategy but with exploration disabled (ε =0 ) .E a c hn e w
