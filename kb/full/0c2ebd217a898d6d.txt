Urban new particle formation (NPF) is shaped by meteorology and pollution, yet their stage-speci ﬁc
roles across particle sizes remain difﬁcult to quantify. Here we combine size-resolved particle number
size distributions from polluted Beijing and cleaner coastal Qingdao with XGBoost modeling and
SHAP attribution to resolve environmental contributions across 10–500 nm particles and three modes.
The models reproduced observed particle number concentrations with high skill (mean testR² = 0.95 in
Beijing and 0.88 in Qingdao), enabling size- and mode-speci ﬁc attribution of model predictions.
Meteorological variables, especially relative humidity and solar radiation, accounted for the largest
share of nucleation-mode variability (~56–58%), and their contributions intensiﬁed during NPF events,
reaching ~70% in Qingdao. By contrast, pollution-related variables gained importance in growth-
related size modes, with city-speci ﬁc differences. These results show that meteorological conditions
were most strongly associated with observable nucleation-mode bursts, whereas pollution-related
conditions gained importance during subsequent growth, providing observational benchmarks for
urban aerosol formation and size-resolved aerosol-model sensitivities.
New particle formation (NPF) converts gaseous precursors into atmo-
spheric particles and is an important source of urban ultraﬁne particles and
climate-relevant aerosol populations 1–5. Although NPF is episodic, its
impact on urban particle burdens can be substantial: long-term urban
observations have reported localized formation-and-growth processes on
about 7% of days, with ultraﬁne particle number concentrations reaching
60 × 10³ cm⁻³6. The atmospheric relevance of NPF therefore depends not
only on the appearance of nanometer-sized particles, but also on their
persistence and growth into larger size ranges. NPF is thus a coupled
formation-and-growth process, not only an event-occurrence problem.
The formation and growth stages are in ﬂuenced by related but dis-
tinct processes. Nucleation involves molecular clusters formed from
precursors such as sulfuric acid, amines and low-volatility organic com-
pounds, whereas subsequent growth depends on condensable vapors,
coagulation and interactions with pre-existing particles 7–13. This distinc-
tion is central because newly formed particles must grow rapidly enough
to avoid scavenging by larger aerosols, especially in the smallest size
ranges, before they can contribute to larger aerosol populations12,13. Field
observations have linked NPF events to strong solar radiation, low relative
humidity, low background aerosol loading and changes in trace gases such
as NO₂,O ₃, and SO₂14–18. These observations show that both meteorology
and atmospheric composition are relevant, but they do not directly
quantify how their relative roles vary across particle sizes and stages. Large
observational analyses have identi ﬁed several common environmental
conditions associated with NPF. For example, a study covering more than
85 site-years at 16 European sites identi ﬁed 1952 NPF events and exam-
ined their frequency, formation rate and growth rate in relation to
meteorology and atmospheric composition 14. Solar radiation, tempera-
ture and pressure were generally positively associated with event fre-
quency, whereas relative humidity was generally negatively associated;
however, exceptions occurred among sites, and both the magnitude and
direction of some relationships varied with local conditions. These ﬁnd-
ings show that the ﬁeld has moved beyond asking whether meteorology
and composition matter. The more speci ﬁc question is how their

This question is difﬁcult to answer because urban NPF is nonlinear, size-
dependent and embedded in covarying atmospheric conditions. Meteor-
ological variables can affect photochemistry, humidity-dependent sinks and
dilution, while pollution-related variables can serve as proxies for precursor
availability, oxidant chemistry, emissions and pre-existing particle sinks19–26.
Event classiﬁcation, correlation analysis and formation- or growth-rate
diagnostics provide essential constraints on NPF occurrence and develop-
ment27,28. However, these approaches usually summarize NPF using event
labels or a small number of process metrics, and are less suited to assigning
measured environmental contributions continuously across the 10–500 nm
size range and across nucleation, Aitken and accumulation modes.
Interpretable machine learning provides one route to this attribution
problem, provided that its outputs are interpreted within the limits of
observational data. Nonlinear models can reproduce complex relationships
among atmospheric variables, while interpretation methods can decompose
model predictions into variable-level contributions29–32. Recent studies using
physically informed or interpretable learning have shown how predictive
models can be linked to process-relevant variables rather than used as black
boxes33–36. Here, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) is used not as a
causal test, but as a quantitative tool for comparing how measured
meteorological and pollution-related variables contribute to modeled par-
ticle number concentrations across sizes and atmospheric states31,32.
Here we apply a size- and stage-resolved explainable machine-learning
approach to urban NPF using particle number size distributions measured in
two contrasting urban environments in China: polluted Beijing and cleaner
coastal Qingdao (Fig. S1). XGBoost models were trained to predict particle
number concentrations across 10–500 nm and within nucleation, Aitken and
accumulation modes37; SHAP was then used to attribute model predictions to

Meteorological and pollution-related contributions partition
strongly by particle size
We next applied SHAP to each size-resolved XGBoost model to quantify
how the 13 environmental variables contributed to modeled PNC variability
across 10–500 nm (Fig. 2). The attribution patterns showed strong size
dependence and clear differences between the two cities. In Qingdao, rela-
tive humidity (RH) was a leading con tributor to the smallest particles,
accounting for ~20–24% of model-explained variability in the 10 –25 nm
range, but its contribution dropped to <8% above 25 nm. In contrast, NO₂
showed a pronounced contribution between 60 and 180 nm, reaching
~40%, while remaining <10% outside this interval. In Beijing, RH also
contributed strongly at small sizes, with relative importance of ~10 –27%
below 20 nm, but it remained inﬂuential over a wider size range and reached
56.8% across 100–500 nm. SO₂ was negligible in Qingdao, with contribu-
tions ≤5% across sizes, but exceeded ~10% near 15 nm and again around
80 nm in Beijing.
To clarify how individual variables combine into broader environ-
mental categories, we grouped predictors into meteorological variables,
trace gases and background particulate matter. The grouped SHAP
contributions revealed a consistent size-dependent partitioning.
Meteorological variables accounted for the largest contribution at the
smallest sizes, reaching ~55 –80% below 50 nm. Pollution-related vari-
ables, including trace gases and particulate matter, became more
important in larger size ranges, especially near the transition from Aitken
to accumulation-mode sizes. In Qingdao, RH, pressure and temperature
were the leading contributors below 50 nm, whereas O ₃,N O₂ and PM₂.₅
became more important above 50 nm. In Beijing, RH and solar radiation
remained important across a broader size range, while O ₃,N O ₂ and
PM₂.₅ contributed most strongly within a narrower mid-size window.
These results show that the relative importance of meteorology and
pollution-related variables is not ﬁxed, but shifts systematically with
particle size.
Together, the size-resolved results show that urban PNC variability
cannot be explained by a single dominant environmental factor. Instead, the
attribution pattern changes with particle diameter: meteorological variables
are most inﬂuential in the smallest size range associated with nucleation and
early growth, whereas pollution-related variables gain importance as par-
ticles grow into larger modes. This size dependence provides theﬁrst layer of
evidence that meteorological and pollution-related in ﬂuences partition
differently across the NPF sequence.

Mode-resolved attribution separates nucleation-mode variability
from growth-mode variability
To connect the size-resolved results to the NPF formation –growth
sequence, we further grouped particles into nucleation, Aitken and accu-
mulation modes and trained separate XGBoost models for each mode in
both cities (Fig. 3 and Table S4). This mode-based analysis condenses the
continuous size-resolved pattern into process-relevant particle classes and
enables direct comparison of environmental contributions during nuclea-
tion and subsequent growth.
In the nucleation mode, meteorological variables accounted for the
largest share of model-explained variability in both cities. Their combined
contribution was ~58% in Qingdao and ~56% in Beijing (Fig.3a, b). RH was
consistently the leading meteorolog ical contributor, although its effect
depended on humidity level. Low RH was associated with positive SHAP
contributions to nucleation-mode PNCs, whereas higher RH was associated
with smaller or negative contributions, with suppression beginning above
~50% RH in Qingdao and above ~20% RH in Beijing (Figs. S6 and S7). Solar
radiation was also important in Beijing, ranking second with a relative
contribution of ~16%, consistent w ith the role of photochemistry in
producing low-volatility vapors. These results indicate that meteorological
variability provides the strongest mod el-attributed signal in nucleation-
mode PNCs.

Finally, to complement the attribution-based analyses with a conven-
tional event-level descriptor, apparentparticle growth rates were calculated
for all classiﬁed NPF events (Table S5). Apparent GR values ranged from
2.91 to 3.96 nm h⁻¹ for the three Beijing events and from 0.73 to 3.25 nm h⁻¹
for the ﬁve Qingdao events. These estimates provide a quantitative
description of the observed modal displacement but do not identify the
vapors or processes responsible for particle growth. Because only eight
events were available and the campaigns differed in season, instrumenta-
tion, and atmospheric background, the GR values are interpreted descrip-
tively rather than as evidence for a statistically general relationship between
particle growth and any individual environmental variable. Taken together,
the event-level analyses show that meteorological variables, particularly RH
and solar radiation, were most stro ngly associated with observable
nucleation-mode enhancements, whereas pollution-related variables con-
tributed more conditionally during individual events and became more
important during later modal development and at larger particle sizes.

pollution-related environmental states modulate subsequent growth.
These ﬁndings support and reﬁne previous ﬁeld observations. Earlier
studies have shown that NPF occurrence is often favored by strong solar
radiation, low relative humidity and low background aerosol loading, while
growth rates and particle survival depend on condensable vapors and pre-
existing particle sinks12–14,21. Large multi-site analyses have also emphasized
that the direction and magnitude of meteorological and compositional
effects vary among locations14. Our contribution is to move beyond event-
level associations by quantifying how these environmental contributions
partition continuously across particle sizes and particle modes. The results
do not overturn the established view that NPF requires chemical precursors;
rather, they clarify when and where measured meteorological and pollution-
related variables become most important in explaining observed particle
number variability.
We use “gatekeeper” in an observational sense: favorable meteor-
ological states mark the conditions under which precursor chemistry is
more likely to appear as an observable nucleation-mode particle burst.
Low relative humidity can reduce particle- and vapor-sink effects and is
frequently associated with conditions favorable for NPF, whereas strong
solar radiation enhances photochemical production of low-volatility
vapors that can participate in cluster formation and early growth 14–18.I n
our analysis, these conditions were re ﬂected by large positive SHAP
contributions from RH- and radiation-related variables during
nucleation-mode events. This interpretation should be understood as
process-consistent attribution rather than direct causal proof. The che-
mical basis of nucleation remains essential, including sulfuric acid, bases
and low-volatility organic compounds 7–13. The present results therefore
suggest that meteorology helps set the conditions under which available
precursor chemistry becomes expressed as an observable particle burst,
rather than replacing precursor chemistry itself.
The increasing importance of pollution-related variables at larger
particle sizes is consistent with current understanding, but it should not be
interpreted as evidence for a single particle-growth pathway. NO₂,O ₃,C O ,
and PM₂.₅ are not direct measurements of the molecular species responsible
for particle growth; rather, they provide indirect information on emissions,
oxidative conditions, combustion inﬂuence, atmospheric aging, and pre-
existing aerosol loading (Table S6). The meteorology–pollution grouping is
therefore analytical rather than mechanistic, because meteorological and
chemical processes remain closely coupled in the atmosphere. Accordingly,
the larger SHAP contributions of pollution-related variables in the Aitken
and accumulation modes indicate that model predictions at these sizes were
more sensitive to pollution-related atmospheric states than were predictions
of nucleation-mode PNC. This pattern is consistent with studies showing
that urban emissions can supply condensable organic vapors and that
combustion-related chemistry may contribute to nanoparticle growth and
survival21–26. Conversely, PM₂.₅ showed predominantly negative contribu-
tions to nucleation-mode PNC in the SHAP dependence analysis. Because
PM₂.₅ is a bulk aerosol-loading proxy rather than a direct measure of con-
densation or coagulation sink, this result is consistent with stronger pre-
existing particle and condensable-vapor sinks but does not establish either
process independently. Together, these patterns point to competing size-
dependent in ﬂuences: polluted atmospheric states may coincide with
stronger sinks that limit the observable emergence of new particles, while
also providing precursor- and oxidati on-rich conditions associated with
particle growth at larger sizes.

The contrasting attribution patterns in Qingdao and Beijing indicate
that the meteorology–pollution balance was context dependent rather than
uniform across particle sizes. In the Qingdao campaigns, which generally
represented lower-pollution coasta l conditions, the attribution shifted
relatively clearly from meteorology-dominated nucleation-mode variability
to stronger pollution-related contr ibutions in the accumulation mode.
Beijing showed a different size dependence: meteorological variables
dominated the nucleation mode, pollution-related variables accounted for
the larger share of attribution in theAitken mode, and RH again became the
leading predictor in the accumulation mode. The two datasets therefore
provide useful contrasting observational cases, but the differences should
not be interpreted as a controlled city-to-city response to pollution
background alone.

The results provide observational benchmarks for interpreting urban
aerosol observations and evaluating size-resolved aerosol-model sensitiv-
ities. First, they show that total par ticle number concentration or binary
event classiﬁcation alone is insufﬁcient to describe urban NPF. The same

environmental variable can differ in importance across nucleation, Aitken,
and accumulation modes, and its contribution may change between event
and non-event states. Second, the size-resolved SHAP framework provides a
way to compare modeled particle-number variability with physically
interpretable environmental variables. Such information can help assess
whether size-resolved aerosol model s show the expected sensitivities to
meteorology and pollution-related proxies, even before all molecular
pathways are simulated explicitly. Third, the results suggest that anticipating
nucleation bursts requires high-qual ity meteorological information,
whereas interpreting subsequent growth requires better representation of
emissions, oxidation chemistry, and pre-existing particle sinks. These
implications are best viewed as process-level constraints for observation-
based interpretation and model sensitivity evaluation, not as a direct forecast
system or a direct estimate of climate effects.
Several limitations de ﬁne the scope of these conclusions. SHAP
quantiﬁes how measured variables contribute to the predictions of a trained
model; it does not establish causal pathways on its own 31,32. The available
covariates also do not include several key molecular precursors and growth
agents, such as sulfuric acid, ammonia,amines, iodine species, highly oxy-
genated organic molecules or size-resolved condensation sink. As a result,
the pollutant variables used here should be interpreted as proxies for broader
chemical and physical environments. In addition, the Beijing and Qingdao
datasets differ in observation perio d, instrumentation and urban back-
ground, which limits direct generalization to all cities. Direct trafﬁc counts,
black-carbon measurements, size-resolved condensation-sink estimates
and air-mass back trajectories were not available. The present analysis
therefore cannot provide trafﬁc-source apportionment, isolate individual
molecular growth pathways or quantitatively separate local processing from
regional transport. These limitations do not weaken the central attribution
result, but they deﬁne its proper interpretation: the study identiﬁes robust,
size- and stage-dependent statistic al contributions within the measured
environmental space.
Future work can build on this framework in three directions. First,
coupling size-resolved explainable ML with direct measurements of H₂SO₄,
NH₃, amines, VOCs, oxygenated organic molecules and condensation sink
would help connect environmental attribution with molecular mechanisms.
Second, applying the same framework to longer multi-city datasets would
test whether the meteorology-to-pollution transition identi ﬁed here is
common across urban environments or speciﬁc to particular pollution and
climate regimes. Third, integrating SHAP-based environmental sensitivities
with process models could help assess whether aerosol microphysical
models reproduce not only particle number concentrations, but also the
correct size-dependent responses to meteorology and emissions. Such
integration would make explainable ML a complement to mechanistic
modeling rather than a replacement for it.
Overall, this work shows that the environmental controls of urban NPF
are best understood as size- and stage-dependent attributions rather than a
single meteorology-versus-pollution rule. Meteorology provides the stron-
gest model-explained signal for obs ervable nucleation-mode bursts,
whereas pollution-related condit ions become more important during
growth, with city-speciﬁc expression. This attrib ution framework offers
observational benchmarks for size-re solved aerosol-model sensitivities
while linking routine urban observations to process-level interpretation.
Methods
Sampling sites and measurements
Measurements were conducted at an urban site in Beijing (116.31°E,
39.99°N) and at two suburban sites in Qingdao (Site A: 120.69°E, 36.37°N;
Site B: 120.67°E, 36.35°N) (Fig. S16). These datasets represent two con-
trasting urban atmospheric regimes: a heavily polluted inland megacity and
a cleaner coastal urban environment. In Beijing, PNSDs were measured with
a Fast Mobility Particle Sizer (FMPS; 5.6–560 nm) at 1-s resolution. After
data-quality screening and synchronization with the environmental mea-
surements, the analysis retained observations from 16 and 21–23 December
2011 and from 13–25 April 2012, excluding 20 and 24 April. In Qingdao,
PNSDs were measured with a Scanning Mobility Particle Sizer (SMPS;
10–500 nm) at 4-min resolution. The analysis retained observations from
Site A on 30 July and 1–2, 4, 6–7, and 13 August 2019, and from Site B on 10
November, 12–30 November, and 1–31 December 2019, 1 January 2020,
and 6–30 June 2020. Instrument details and calibration are provided in the
Supporting Information.Because the Beijing and Qingdao campaigns were
asynchronous and used different pa rticle-sizing instruments, the two
datasets are treated as contrasting observational cases rather than as a
controlled inter-city experiment. Campaign-speciﬁc particle, pollutant and
meteorological statistics are provided in Table S7.
Meteorological variables included temperature (T), RH, pressure (P),
wind speed (WS), wind direction (WD), and solar radiation (SNSR).

Event classiﬁcation and operational size modes
Daily time-resolved PNSD contour plots were examined over the complete
observation day using the same conceptual classi ﬁcation criteria at both
sites. A day was classiﬁed as an NPF event only when both of the following
conditions were satisﬁed: (i) a distinct particle mode emerged within the
smallest instrumentally resolved size interval below 25 nm, corresponding
to 5.6 –25 nm in Beijing and 10 –25 nm in Qingdao; and (ii) the modal
diameter subsequently exhibited a co herent and sustained shift toward
larger sizes over several hours, consistent with established observational
criteria27,28. Short-lived particle-number concentration spikes, enhance-
ments conﬁned to approximately constant diameters, and small-particle
increases without a continuous growth trajectory were classi ﬁed as non-
events. A banana-shaped trajectory in the PNSD contour plot was regarded
as a characteristic pattern supportingsustained modal growth, rather than
as an independent classiﬁcation criterion.
Because event identiﬁcation was limited by the lower detection dia-
meter of each instrument, this procedure identiﬁes formation-and-growth
events only after the developing particle mode enters the measured size
range. Nucleation episodes that rema in below the lower detection limit
cannot be detected, and molecular nucleation itself is therefore not directly
observed. Representative NPF even t and non-event days are shown in
Fig. S17, and the numbers of valid observation days and classiﬁed NPF event
days are summarized in Table S7. For th e mode-based analyses, particles
were grouped into three operational size ranges: nucleation mode, deﬁned as
5.6–25 nm in Beijing and 10–25 nm in Qingdao; Aitken mode, deﬁned as
25–100 nm; and accumulation mode, deﬁned as 100–500 nm. Throughout
this study, “nucleation-mode particles” therefore refers to the smallest
instrumentally measured particle-size range and does not imply direct

Machine learning framework
We modeled particle number concentration in each size bin using 13
environmental predictors (Table S1). Four algorithms were evaluated: Ridge
regression, a fully connected neural network, Random Forest, and XGBoost.
Data were divided into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets. Hyper-
parameters for Ridge regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost were opti-
mized by grid search with 3-fold cross-validation within the training data.
The neural network included two hidden layers with 256 and 128 nodes.
Model performance was evaluated using R² and root-mean-square error
(RMSE) on the test data. Because the train–test split evaluates out-of-sample
reconstruction within the observed campaigns, the reported skill should not
be interpreted as prospective forecasting performance under independent
future conditions.

Separate models were trained for each size channel, yielding 109
models for Qingdao and 31 models for Beijing. Additional XGBoost models
were trained for the total number concentrations of the nucleation, Aitken

models followed the algorithm of Breiman40.
