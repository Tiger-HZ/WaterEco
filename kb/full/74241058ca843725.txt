AUTOMORPHISMS OF THE PANTS GRAPH OF A NONORIENTABLE
SURFACE
MICHA L STUKOW AND B LA˙ZEJ SZEPIETOWSKI
Abstract. We prove that, except in certain low-complexity cases, the automorphism group
of the graph of pants decompositions of a nonorientable surface is isomorphic to the mapping
class group of that surface.
1. Introduction
Let S be a compact surface, possibly nonorientable and with boundary. We define the
mapping class group Mod(S) of S to be the group of isotopy classes of all homeomorphisms
ofS. If S is orientable then Mod( S) is usually called the extended mapping class group and
we denote by Mod+(S) the subgroup of Mod(S) consisting of orientation preserving mapping
classes.
A connected orientable (resp. nonorientable) surface of genus g with b boundary compo-
nents will be denoted by Sg,b (resp. Ng,b). If b = 0 we drop it from the notation. Note that
Ng,b is obtained fromS0,g+b by gluingg M¨ obius bands (also called crosscaps) alongg distinct
boundary components of S0,g+b.
One of the most important breakthroughs in the study of mapping class groups Mod+(Sg,b)
of oriented surfaces was the introduction of the so-called cut-system complex by Hatcher and
Thurston in [7]. They proved that this 2-dimensional complex is connected and simply-
connected. Moreover, the action of Mod +(Sg,b) on cut-system complex is cocompact, so the
full finite presentation of Mod +(Sg) can be derived from the presentations of the stabilizers
of vertices and edges. This program was initiated by Hatcher and Thurston in [7], completed
by Harer [4], and later greatly simplified by Wajnryb [16, 17] – see [17] for more details.
1.1. The pants complex. In Appendix of [7] Hatcher and Thurston introduced another
important concept – the graphP1(Sg,b) of maximal cut systems of an oriented surface. The
vertices of this graph are pants decompositions of Sg,b and edges correspond to two types of
elementary moves (see Section 3). Later, Hatcher, Lochak and Schneps [5, 6] proved that
P1(Sg,b) is connected and added five types of 2-dimensional cells to turn the graph P1(Sg,b)
into 2-dimensional complexP(Sg,b) and they proved thatP(Sg,b) is simply-connected. This
complex is now called the pants complex of Sg,b.
There are a number of interesting results concerningP(Sg,b). Brock [3] proved thatP1(Sg,b)
is quasi-isometric to Teichm¨ uller space with the Weil-Petersson metric. Margalit [12] proved
the following characterization of Aut(P(Sg,b)).
Theorem 1.1. The natural map θ : Mod(Sg,b) → Aut(P(Sg,b)) is surjective. Moreover,
kerθ ∼= Z2 for S ∈ {S1,1,S 1,2,S 2}, kerθ ∼= Z2⊕ Z2 for S = S0,4, kerθ = Mod(S) for
S =S0,3, and kerθ is trivial otherwise.

In contrast to the orientable case, much less is known about the pants complex of a nonori-
entable surface. The most significant result to date is due to Papadopoulos and Penner [13],
who introduced two new elementary moves between pants decompositions of a nonorientable
surface (see Section 3) and proved that the resulting pants graph P(Ng,b) is connected.
1.2. The main theorems. The main goal of this paper is to extend Theorem 1.1 to the
case of a nonorientable surface (Theorem 1.4). However, as a first step we need the following
nonorientable version of Theorem 1.2.
Theorem 1.3. IfN is a nonorientable surface withχ(N)< 0 andη : Mod(N)→ Aut(Curv(N))
is the natural map, then
(1) η is surjective;
(2) kerη∼= Z2⊕ Z2 for N = N1,2, kerη∼= Z2 for N∈{ N2,1,N 3}, and kerη is trivial
otherwise.
The above theorem was proved by Atalan and Korkmaz [2] for nonorientable surfacesNg,b
withg+b̸= 4, the caseN =N1,3 was settled in [15], and the proof for the remaining surfaces
N∈{N2,2,N 3,1,N 4,0} is given in the present article (Theorem 9.1).
The following theorem is the main result of this paper.
Theorem 1.4. IfN is a nonorientable surface withχ(N)< 0 andθ : Mod(N)→ Aut(P(N))
is the natural map, then
(1) θ is surjective;
(2) kerθ = kerη, where η : Mod(N)→ Aut(Curv(N)) is the map from Theorem 1.3.
As an immediate corollary, we get
Corollary 1.5. If N =Ng,b is a nonorientable surface with χ(N)< 0, then
(1) Aut(P(N))∼= Aut(Curv(N)),
(2) Aut(P(N))∼= Aut(Curv(N))∼= Mod(N), for g +b≥ 4.
We also prove that if two nonorientable surfaces have isomorphic pants graphs, then they
are homeomorphic – see Theorem 8.4. Finally, we show that it is enough to add 3 types
of 2-dimensional cells (two types of triangles and one type of pentagonal cells) to P(N3) to
obtain a connected and simply-connected complex P(N3)⊃P (N3) – see Theorem 10.15.

1.3. Comparison with the case of an orientable surface. Our proof of Theorem 1.4
has a similar structure as the proof of Theorem 1.1 in [12] however, there are some important
differences.
If S is an oriented surface, then to each edge of P(S) there is an associated Farey graph
and these graphs are the main tool to control the action of elements of Aut( P(S)). If N
is nonorientable, then the situation is more complicated, because there are different kinds
of subgraphs associated to edges in P(N) – see Proposition 4.23. What is more, there are
edges inP(N) that are not contained in any triangle – see Example 4.12.
Another new phenomena in a nonorientable case is the fact that two pants decompositions
of the same surface can have a different number of components (see for example Figure 1).
The existence of moves of type 4 (Figure 2) implies that ifN has a pants decomposition with
p≥ 2 one-sided and q two-sided curves, then N has also a pants decomposition with p− 2
one-sided and q + 1 two-sided curves. This significantly complicates the notion of 2-tight
paths inP(N) (corresponding to the notion of 2-curve small paths in [12]) – see Definition
5.5.
Finally, in an oriented case, the pants graph P1(S) is embedded in a simply-connected
pants complexP(S) and we have a short list of types of 2-cells in that complex. It turns out
that these 2-dimensional cells play a crucial role in the proof of Theorem 1.1. On the other
hand, if N is nonorientable, then we only have a connected graph P(N) =P1(N) and the
full list of types of 2-dimensional cells are yet to be discovered. In fact, in the present paper
we discovered three interesting 2-cells: pentagon in N3, tame alternating hexagon inP(N1,3)
and standard heptagon in P(N2,2) – see Figures 16, 9 and 8. We expect that this kind of
cells will be crucial in constructing the 2-dimensional structure of P(N), but we leave this
for future considerations.
1.4. Outline of the paper. In Section 3 we review the definition of the pants graph P(N)
of a nonorientable surface N =Ng,b. We also identify this graph for surfaces of some small
values ofg andb. In Section 4 we identify the subgraphs of P(N) corresponding to edges of
P(N) (Remark 4.7). Then we classify the triangles in P(N) (Proposition 4.9) and we prove
the abstract characterization of moves (Proposition 4.23). In Section 5 we study properties of
short loops (circuits) inP(N) and we introduce the circuit corresponding to 2-cell inP(N2,2)
(standard heptagon). The analysis of short circuits is continued in Section 6 and we prove
that (in most cases) every edge ofP(N) can be extended to a short circuit with some special
properties (Proposition 6.4). This construction is a crucial step towards the proof that there
is a well-defined correspondence between Aut( P(N)) and Aut(Curv( N)) – we prove this
in Section 7, Theorem 7.5. In the same section, we finish the proof of our main Theorem
1.4. In Section 8 we prove that if two nonorientable surfaces have isomorphic pants graphs,
then they are homeomorphic (Theorem 8.4). Section 9 contains the proof of Theorem 1.3 if
g +b = 4 (Theorem 9.1). Finally, in Section 10 we focus on the case N =N3. We describe
the structure of P(N) and construct an abstract graph isomorphic to it. Additionally, we
define a simply-connected 2-dimensional complexP(N), whose 1-skeleton isP(N), and such
that Aut(P(N)) = Aut(P(N)) (Theorem 10.15).

2. Preliminaries
Let S be a connected compact surface of negative Euler characteristic. Recall that
χ(Sg,b) = 2− 2g−b, whereas χ(Ng,b) = 2−g−b. A non-trivial curve on S is an embedded
simple closed curve which does not bound a disc nor a M¨ obius band in S and which is not
homotopic to a boundary component of S. By a curve we always understand a non-trivial
curve. A curve is either two- or one-sided depending on whether its regular neighbourhood
is an annulus or a M¨ obius band respectively. To simplify the notation, we identify a curve
with its isotopy class. We denote by i(α,β ) the geometric intersection number of two curves
α,β . If i(α,β ) = 0 and α̸=β, then we say that α and β are disjoint.
A (finite) set of pairwise disjoint curves on S is called a multicurve. For a multicurve X
we denote by X+ (resp. X−) the set of two-sided (resp. one-sided) curves of X, and by|X|
the number of curves in X.
The curve complex Curv(S) is a simplicial complex whose k-simplices are multicurves of
cardinalityk + 1 on S. The k-skeleton of Curv(S) is denoted by Curvk(S).
IfS =S0,4 orS =S1,1, the definition of Curv(S) is modified by declaring α,β ∈ Curv0(S)
to be adjacent in Curv( S) whenever i(α,β ) = 2 or i(α,β ) = 1 respectively. The resulting
connected graph is isomorphic to the Farey graph – see Section 3 of [11]. For the description
of Curv(N) for nonorientable surfaces of small complexity see [14].
For a multicurveX on S we denote by SX the surface obtained by cutting S along X. A
component of SX is trivial if it is homeomorphic to S0,3. A pants decomposition of S is a
multicurveX such that all components of SX are trivial. Note that a pants decomposition
is a maximal multicurve.
Any pants decomposition of an orientable surface Sg,b consists of 3g +b− 3 curves. The
number of curves in a pants decomposition of a nonorientable surface Ng,b varies. For any
integerm such that 0≤m≤g and m≡g (mod 2) there is a pants decoposition of Ng,b of

Theorem 10.15. The 2-dimensional complex P(N) obtained by attaching 2-cells to P(N)
along each triangle and each standard pentagon is simply-connected.
Proof. Let L be a loop in P(N) based at X0∈ V1. We can assume that L is a simplicial

contain edges from E33. Let n be the length of L, i.e. the number of edges contained in L.
We prove by induction on n that L is null-homotopic inP(N). If n = 0 then L is obviously
null-homotopic, so assumen≥ 1 and that all loops of length less than n are null-homotopic.
IfL is contained in the Farey subgraph Γ1, thenL is null-homotopic, because the complex
obtained by attaching 2-cells to Γ 1 along each Farey triangle is simply-connected.
IfL is not contained in Γ1, then it contains at least one vertexX1∈V2 and we can assume
thatX1 is joined to X0 by an edge fromE12 and this edge is contained in L. If n = 2 thenL
travels from X0 to X1 and then back to X0 along the same edge, so it is null-homotopic. If
n≥ 3 then L must contain X2∈V3, X3∈V2, and edges X1X2∈E23, X2X3∈E23 (because
we assumed that L contains no edges from E33). Consider the following segment L′ of L:
X0−−3 X1−→4 X2←−4 X3.
