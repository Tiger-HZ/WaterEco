Algal species as substrates for cultivating potentially pathogenic 
free‑living amoebae: a novel in vitro strategy

Abstract
Free-living amoebae (FLA) are ubiquitous protists of ecological and clinical importance whose in vitro cultivation tradition-
ally relies on bacterial food sources, most commonly Escherichia coli on non-nutrient agar. Although this approach is widely 
used, bacteria-based substrates may introduce experimental and biosafety-related limitations because some bacteria can 
survive phagocytosis and persist within amoebae, which may act as intracellular reservoirs that protect endocytobionts from 
environmental stress and antimicrobial treatments and may also promote bacterial interactions associated with horizontal gene 
transfer and the dissemination of virulence- or resistance-related traits. To evaluate a non-bacterial alternative for laboratory 
cultivation, this study assessed three green algal species — Scenedesmus obliquus, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, and Chlorella 
vulgaris — as trophic substrates for three potentially pathogenic FLA on non-nutrient agar, using E. coli as the conventional 
positive control. Among the tested amoebae, Acanthamoeba genotype T3 (accession no. OR568985) exhibited the great-
est growth across all tested substrates, with the highest cell densities recorded on S. obliquus, followed by C. reinhardtii 
and E. coli by day 7. Naegleria clarki (accession no. OR568992) and Vermamoeba vermiformis (accession no. OR568993) 
also proliferated on algal substrates, although growth was generally higher on E. coli. Strong positive correlations between 
algal- and bacterial-based growth patterns indicated broadly similar temporal trends in amoebal proliferation. Overall, these 
findings demonstrate that selected green algae, particularly S. obliquus and C. reinhardtii, can support measurable growth 
of potentially pathogenic FLA and represent promising non-bacterial substrates for laboratory cultivation.

Introduction
Free-living amoebae (FLA) are ubiquitous microorganisms 
distributed across a wide range of natural and engineered 
environments, including soil, freshwater, wastewater, and 
air (Fonseca et al. 2020; Fan et al. 2024; Gad et al. 2024a , 
b; Ghadesi et al. 2024; Shaheen et al. 2024). They contrib-
ute to microbial grazing and nutrient turnover and thus play 
important ecological roles in regulating microbial commu-
nity structure (Delafont et al. 2016; Archibald et al. 2017). 
Despite their ecological significance, FLA are also recog-
nized as opportunistic pathogens capable of causing severe

2003; Król-Turmińska and Olender 2017). For instance, 
species belonging to genera Acanthamoeba, Naegleria, and 
Vermamoeba are implicated in human infections. Acantham-
oeba species are associated with a range of clinical mani-
festations, including Acanthamoeba keratitis (AK), granu-
lomatous amoebic encephalitis (GAE), and disseminated 
infections in immunocompromised hosts (Marciano-Cabral 
and Cabral 2003; Visvesvara et al. 2007; Belaidi et al. 2025).
The public health importance of FLA extends beyond 
their direct pathogenicity. Numerous studies have shown 
that bacteria engulfed by amoebae may survive intracellu-
lar digestion and persist as endocytobionts (Mungroo et al. 
2021). In this protected intracellular niche, amoebae may 
function as environmental reservoirs or “Trojan horses,” 
shielding internalized bacteria from harsh conditions such 
as desiccation, disinfectants, antibiotics, and other biocidal 
treatments (Barker et al. 1999; Scheid 2014). Moreover, resi-
dence within amoebae may enhance bacterial persistence, 
promote virulence-related adaptation, and create conditions 
favorable for interactions associated with horizontal gene 
transfer and the dissemination of resistance- or virulence-
related traits (Mungroo et al. 2021). Accordingly, the use of 
bacterial prey in laboratory cultivation systems may intro-
duce biological and interpretive complexities that extend 
beyond a simple nutritional role.
Prey-cell-free cultivation of FLA is also possible using 
enriched axenic media. PYG is well established for many 
adapted Acanthamoeba strains, whereas Naegleria, Ver-
mamoeba, and other FLA may require different supple-
mented formulations. Because nutritional requirements 
and adaptation to axenic growth vary among genera and 
strains, no single prey-free medium can be assumed to sup-
port diverse FLA taxa equally (Schuster 2002). The present 
study therefore evaluated green algal cells as non-bacterial 
particulate substrates in an agar-based cultivation system 
comparable with the conventional bacterial-food approach.
Despite the recognized ecological and clinical impor -
tance of FLA, culture-based methods remain fundamental 
for their isolation, maintenance, and experimental study. 
Among available approaches, non-nutrient agar (NNA) 
seeded with bacteria, particularly E. coli, is widely used 
as the conventional cultivation method (Khan 2006). This 
system is practical, effective, and well established, and E. 
coli therefore represents an appropriate positive control for 
comparative cultivation studies. However, although bacteria-
based feeding systems are highly useful for routine amoeba 
growth, they may not always be optimal when a more con-
trolled (Martinez 2019; Mungroo et al. 2022), non-bacterial 
trophic substrate is desired, especially in experiments where 
intracellular bacteria-associated interactions may confound 
downstream observations or biosafety considerations.
In this context algae represent an attractive alternative 
trophic resource. As photosynthetic eukaryotes, algae are 
phylogenetically distinct from bacteria and may provide an 
alternative non-bacterial food source for amoeba cultiva -
tion, potentially reducing experimental and biosafety con-
cerns associated with bacteria–amoeba interactions, includ-
ing intracellular bacterial persistence and adaptation (Van 
Wichelen et al. 2010; Mungroo et al. 2021; Weger et al. 
2024). In addition, replacing bacterial prey with algal sub-
strates may reduce the likelihood that cultivation systems 
unintentionally support amoeba-associated bacterial persis-
tence or bacteria-mediated interactions linked to intracellular 
survival (Mungroo et al. 2021). Previous ecological studies 
have shown that protozoa can exploit algal resources under 
various environmental conditions (Boenigk and Arndt 2002; 
Van Wichelen et al. 2010; Weger et al. 2024); however, the 
use of algae as intentional non-bacterial substrates for cul-
tivating potentially pathogenic FLA on NNA has remained 
insufficiently explored.
Previous studies have demonstrated that certain amoe-
bae can utilize selected green algae as food sources; how -
ever, successful growth appears to depend on the specific 
amoeba–alga combination. Early cultivation experiments 
showed considerable variation in the ability of different algal 
species to support amoeba growth (Ho and Alexander 1974). 
More recently, Weger et al. (2024) tested several green algae 
against three amoebae and found that only one combina-
tion, Cochliopodium sp. with Scenedesmus vacuolatus, sup-
ported substantial growth. These observations indicate that 
green algae should not be considered universally suitable 
substrates for amoebae and highlight the need to evaluate 
defined algal species against individual FLA taxa under 
standardized cultivation conditions.
Therefore, the present study evaluated three green algal 
species, Scenedesmus obliquus, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, 
and Chlorella vulgaris, as alternative trophic substrates for 
the cultivation of three potentially pathogenic FLA, namely 
Acanthamoeba genotype T3, Naegleria clarki, and Ver -
mamoeba vermiformis, using E. coli as the conventional 
positive control. We hypothesized that selected algal sub-
strates could support measurable amoebal growth and thus 
provide promising non-bacterial alternatives for laboratory 
cultivation.
Materials and methods
The source of FLA, bacteria, and algae strains
Three FLA species, Acanthamoeba genotype T3 (acces-
sion no. OR568985), Naegleria clarki (OR568992), and 
Vermamoeba vermiformis (OR568993), were isolated and 
identified from the outlets of reverse osmosis point-of-use 
Journal of Applied Phycology 
systems (Gad et al. 2024a). Sequencing of the isolates was 
performed using the QPinnacle2 platform (QitanTech, 
China). These FLA species were selected for use in the pre-
sent work as they are globally and locally dominant taxa 
in natural and engineered freshwater systems. Moreover, 
they have documented clinical relevance, and are known to 
harbor and interact with a range of intracellular pathogenic 
bacteria under environmental and in vitro conditions (Tra -
belsi et al. 2012; Salahuldeen et al. 2020; Gad et al. 2024b). 
These FLA species were used in subsequent trials with 
monoxenic media to explore a novel food source. Professor 
Gamila Hussien Ali kindly provided the algal strains from 
the National Laboratory of Algae at the National Research 
Centre (Egypt). Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922) bacteria 
were kindly provided by Professor Gamila El-Taweel, Envi-
ronmental Microbiology Laboratory, National Research 
Centre, Egypt.
Preparation of FLA cysts
The previously isolated and molecularly identified strains 
of FLA (Gad et al. 2024a) were separately propagated on 
NNA seeded with heat killed E. coli and allowed to encyst 
at room temperature. The cysts were scraped from the agar 
surface, transferred into tubes, and washed five times with 
sterile Page's Amoebae Saline (PAS) to get rid of suspend-
ing bacteria in the supernatant. After each wash, the tubes 
containing cysts of amoebae were centrifuged at 1500 rpm 
for 10 min and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet, 
containing amoebae cysts, was then re-suspended in 1 mL 
of sterile PAS.
To ensure the absence of bacteria after the last wash, the 
obtained cysts were cultivated on non-nutrient agar without 
any food source. If cultured amoebic cysts did not trans-
form to active trophozoites, this is an indication to ensure 
the complete absence of bacteria from washed cysts (i.e., 
washed amoebic cysts became free from bacteria that could 
interfere with the inoculated food sources).
Counting of the three different strains of washed amoe -
bic cysts was carefully performed by three independent 
researchers. Counted cysts were dispensed in Eppendorf 
tubes (each having a mean count of 10 ± 2 cysts/tube) to be 
ready for use in subsequent experiments.
Use of algal species for FLA cultivation
Three algal species, Scenedesmus obliquus, Chlamydomonas 
reinhardtii, and Chlorella vulgaris were separately tested as 
alternative food sources to replace E. coli, which is conven-
tionally used for culturing FLA (Salahuldeen et al. 2020; 
Gad et al. 2024a). The selected algal taxa represent com-
mon freshwater chlorophytes frequently detected in aquatic 
systems. Chlamydomonas reinhardtii is a well-established 
laboratory model organism with extensive physiological 
characterization; S. obliquus is a widespread colonial green 
algal species commonly present in biofilms and surface 
waters; and C. vulgaris is widely used in ecological and 
applied research because of its robust growth and ease of 
cultivation and is characterized by broad environmental dis-
tribution and tolerance to environmental stress. These algal 
species differ in morphology, cell size, and cell wall com-
position, allowing assessment of potential species-specific 
growth responses of FLA (Hodač et al. 2016; Lamminen 
2021; Oliveira et al. 2021; Calatrava et al. 2023). Previous 
ecological studies have demonstrated that protists, including 
amoebae and other microeukaryotes, can utilize green algae 
as food sources (Martinez 1996; Schuster 2002; Wang et al. 
2014), supporting their relevance for evaluating algal-based 
cultivation approaches.
Non-nutrient agar (NNA) in sterile 24-well plates was 
seeded with either heat-killed E. coli (forming a bacterial 
lawn) or algal suspensions. For bacterial controls, E. coli 
was applied to NNA to support trophozoite growth and 
incubated at 37 °C following established protocols (Schus-
ter 2002; Al-Herrawy and Gad 2015). The algal strains were 
maintained in BG-11 medium under controlled laboratory 
conditions at 25 ± 2 °C under continuous illumination until 
they reached the exponential growth phase (Andersen and 
Kawachi 2005). Algal cultures were washed three times with 
sterile distilled water prior to use as food source for FLA 
to reduce potential bacterial contamination. Each well was 
inoculated with 10 cysts, as described above. Experiments 
were conducted in triplicate wells per condition and E. coli 
controls were included in parallel.
Two substrate volumes (50 µL and 100 µL) from each 
species were separately tested to explore potential dose-
dependent effects of food availability for FLA proliferation. 
These volumes corresponded to approximately 5 × 103 and 
1 × 104 cells for algal suspensions and 2.1 × 104 and 1 × 10⁶ 
cells for E. coli. Given the exploratory nature of this study, 
food substrates were standardized based on cell counts rather 
than biomass or carbon content. Because algal and bacte-
rial cells differ in size and biochemical composition, strict 
nutritional equivalence was not established; therefore, the 
aim was to evaluate comparative growth responses under 
controlled experimental conditions rather than quantitative 
nutritional equivalence.
For growth assessment, three separate replicate wells 
were prepared for each experimental condition and sam-
pling day. At each time point, the entire contents of each 
well were collected independently in 1 mL of sterile Page’s 
Amoebae Saline (PAS), fixed with Lugol’s iodine, and 
enumerated using a Neubauer hemocytometer (Koyun 
et al. 2020). All quantitative results are expressed as cells 
 mL−1 of the resuspended well contents. In this study, the 
term “cell” refers to either a trophozoite (T) or a cyst (C), 
 Journal of Applied Phycology
as identified morphologically under light microscopy. 
Where indicated, total growth (T + C) represents the com-
bined number of trophozoites and cysts per well, normal-
ized to 1 mL.
Cell enumeration was conducted in an ISO/IEC 
17025-accredited laboratory under documented standard 
operating procedures and periodic external evaluation. 
Each of the three replicate wells was independently evalu-
ated by one of three skilled and experienced researchers 
trained in FLA morphological identification (Koyun et al. 
2020). The reported values represent the mean ± standard 
deviation calculated from the three replicate wells (n = 3) 
for each experimental condition and sampling day. Sepa-
rate sets of replicate wells were used for each sampling 
day, and growth was assessed from day 0 to day 7.
Statistical analysis
The differences in FLA growth on various food sources 
were assessed using Friedman, Kruskal–Wallis, and 
Mann–Whitney U tests. Additionally, Spearman correla-
tion analysis was employed to evaluate the relationship 
between FLA growth on algal species and E. coli. All 
statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted 
using R v4.1.0 (https:// www.r- proje ct. org/).
Results
Cultivation of FLA on non‑nutrient agar seeded 
with C. reinhardtii
The phylogenetic analysis confirmed the taxonomic place-
ment of the three studied free-living amoebae, with N. clarki 
OR568992.1, Acanthamoeba genotype T3 OR568985.1, and 
V. vermiformis OR568993.1 clustering within their corre-
sponding GenBank reference groups (Fig.  1). The growth 
patterns of FLA revealed differences across species and food 
sources, highlighting variations in adaptability and nutrient 
preferences. Among the tested species, Acanthamoeba geno-
type T3 exhibited the highest overall growth, demonstrating 
its remarkable (Friedman test; P < 0.0001) adaptability to 
diverse substrates. When cultivated with Chlamydomonas. 
reinhardtii, its growth increased exponentially from a 
mean of 10 on Day 0 to 2228 ± 47.52 cells mL−1 on Day 7 
(Table S1, Fig. 3). In comparison, its growth means with E. 
coli reached 2171.6 ± 79.10 cells mL−1 on Day 7 (Table S2).

These findings emphasize that C. reinhardtii holds promise 
as an alternative to E. coli for FLA cultivation, particularly

This observation has significant implications for sustainable 
laboratory practices and the development of algal-based cul-
tivation techniques.
Cultivation of FLA on non‑nutrient agar seeded 
with S. obliquus
The results demonstrate significant growth trends among dif-
ferent FLA types (Acanthamoeba genotype T3, N. clarki, 
and V. vermiformis) when cultivated with S. obliquus and 
E. coli. Overall, Acanthamoeba genotype T3 exhibited 
the highest growth compared to N. clarki and V. vermi-
formis across both food sources, with a final mean growth 
of 2423.3 ± 99.50 (Table S10) cells mL−1 on day 7 with 
S. obliquus (Friedman test; P = 0.0012), compared to 
3088.3 ± 30.55 cells mL−1 (Table S11) with E. coli (Fried-
man test; P = 0.0001). Statistical analysis revealed that the 
food source significantly influenced growth, with Acantham-
oeba genotype T3 showing higher early-stage growth with 
E. coli and S. obliquus. Similarly, N. clarki showed robust 
growth dynamics, achieving 1376.6 ± 25.17 cells mL−1 with 
S. obliquus (Table S12) and 1455 ± 32.78 cells mL−1 with E. 
coli on day 7 (Table S13), indicating its adaptability to dif-
ferent nutrient sources. However, V. vermiformis exhibited 
moderate growth, reaching 1160 ± 22.91 cells mL−1 with S. 
obliquus (Table S14) and 1076.6 ± 22.54 cells mL−1 with 
E. coli (Table S6), with no significant difference observed 
between food sources (Wilcoxon test, P = 0.06). The growth 
trajectories for all FLA with S. obliquus displayed a steady 
increase after day 3 (Figs.  3 and 4 ). FLA growth on S. 
obliquus followed temporal patterns broadly comparable to 
those observed on E. coli (Spearman, r = 0.99, P < 0.0001) 
(Fig. 3, Table S15, S16, S17). These findings suggest that S. 
obliquus is a promising non-bacterial alternative to bacterial 
substrates for FLA cultivation. Its use may provide a more 
controlled cultivation platform for laboratory studies and 
may help reduce bacteria-associated complications in exper-
imental investigations of FLA ecology and pathogenicity.

Discussion
The present study demonstrates that selected green algae can 
support the in vitro growth of potentially pathogenic free-
living amoebae (FLA) on non-nutrient agar, thereby broad-
ening the range of trophic substrates available for laboratory 
cultivation. Importantly, the significance of these findings 
lies not in suggesting that algal substrates universally outper-
form the conventional bacterial control, but rather in show-
ing that algal-based systems can sustain measurable amoebal 
proliferation under controlled conditions. This distinction 
is critical, because the value of algal substrates is primarily 
methodological: they provide an alternative non-bacterial 
trophic platform for cultivating FLA in experimental settings

The use of bacteria-seeded non-nutrient agar, particularly 
with E. coli, remains the standard approach for cultivating 
FLA and has proven highly effective for isolation and rou-
tine maintenance (Khan 2006; Lorenzo-Morales et al. 2006). 
However, bacteria in amoebal culture systems may play roles 
that extend beyond nutrition alone. Many studies have shown 
that bacteria engulfed by amoebae can survive intracellular 
digestion, persist as endocytobionts, and exploit amoebae as 
protective environmental reservoirs (Greub and Raoult 2004; 
Thomas et al. 2010; Balczun and Scheid 2017). In particular, 
FLA have been described as “Trojan horses” that can shel-
ter internalized microorganisms from adverse environmen-
tal conditions, disinfectants, antibiotics, and other biocidal 
agents, while also facilitating persistence, transmission, and 
adaptation of associated bacteria (Greub and Raoult 2004; 
Mungroo et al. 2021). Residence within amoebae may also 
promote bacterial interactions linked to virulence modu-
lation and horizontal gene transfer, thereby complicating 
the interpretation of bacteria-fed cultivation systems in 
downstream ecological or pathogenicity studies (Greub and 
Raoult 2004; Thomas et al. 2010; Mungroo et al. 2021). 
Within this context, algal substrates are particularly attrac-
tive because they may reduce bacteria-associated experimen-
tal confounding while still supporting amoeba growth.
Among the tested amoebae, Acanthamoeba genotype 
T3 showed the strongest and most consistent prolifera -
tion across substrates, particularly on S. obliquus and C. 
reinhardtii. This pattern is biologically plausible given the 
broad trophic flexibility and ecological adaptability of pro-
tozoa, which is well known for exploiting diverse food par-
ticles and microbial resources in natural and experimental 
systems (Boenigk and Arndt 2002; Van Wichelen et al. 
2010; Weger et al. 2024; Bilden et al. 2025). Amoebae do 
not merely ingest particles indiscriminately; rather, post-
ingestion processing depends on prey properties such as 
size, digestibility, and structural composition (Bowers and 
Olszewski 1983). Thus, the stronger growth observed on 
S. obliquus and C. reinhardtii compared with C. vulgaris 
may be associated with differences in cell wall proper -
ties among the tested algal species. The cell wall of Chlo-
rella species has been reported to be particularly robust 
and recalcitrant to disruption (Voigt et al. 2014; Weber 
et al. 2022), which may reduce substrate accessibility to 
FLA under the experimental conditions tested. However, 
because cell wall digestibility and feeding mechanisms 
were not directly investigated in the present study, this 
interpretation should be considered a plausible hypothesis 
requiring further experimental validation.
In contrast to Acanthamoeba genotype T3, which con-
sistently achieved the highest cell densities across all tested 
substrates, N. clarki and V. vermiformis exhibited lower 
overall growth irrespective of the food source. Neverthe -
less, both species showed measurable growth on all three 
green algal substrates, with growth levels that were generally 
comparable to those observed on the E. coli control. These 
findings suggest that the tested algal species can support the 
growth of multiple FLA taxa, although the magnitude of 
growth appears to be species dependent. Such interspecific 
differences are consistent with previous studies showing that 
amoebae growth responses can vary among taxa and may be 
influenced by both predator-specific characteristics and prey-
related properties (Weekers et al. 1993; Pickup et al. 2007). 
The observed interspecific differences should not be inter -
preted solely on the basis of amoeba taxonomy. Previous 
evidence indicates that grazing and growth are often deter -
mined by specific interactions between individual amoeba 
and prey species or strains rather than by broad taxonomic 
affiliation alone (Weger et al. 2024).
It should also be noted that, although all three organ-
isms are referred to as free-living amoebae because of their 
amoeboid morphology and mode of locomotion, they repre-
sent two distantly related evolutionary lineages: Amoebozoa 
(Acanthamoeba and Vermamoeba) and Discoba (Naegleria) 
(Adl et al. 2019). Accordingly, algal suitability should not

that algal-supported proliferation broadly followed similar 
temporal trends to those obtained with E. coli, although such 
correlations should be interpreted cautiously and not as evi-
dence of full nutritional equivalence.
The broader implication of these findings lies in the pos-
sibility of developing more controlled non-bacterial culti-
vation systems for FLA. In studies concerned with amoeba 
physiology, ecology, pathogenicity, or interactions with 
microorganisms, the use of bacterial prey may unintention-
ally create a biologically complex system in which intracel-
lular bacterial survival and host–microbe interactions influ-
ence amoeba physiology and experimental outcomes (Matz 
et al. 2008; Thomas et al. 2010; Karnkowska et al. 2026). 
Previous work has shown, for example, that microorganisms 
surviving passage through protozoa may acquire enhanced 
tolerance to chlorination and other stresses, and may display 
altered phenotypic traits relevant to persistence and pub-
lic health risk (Mattana et al. 2006; Mungroo et al. 2021). 
Against this background, the use of algae as a non-bacterial 
food source may offer a more controlled experimental plat-
form for culturing amoebae while limiting the introduction 
of bacterial prey that could persist intracellularly.
These findings should be interpreted within the meth-
odological scope of the study. Cell quantification was based 
on manual hemocytometer counting under microscopy, and 
although counts were performed independently by three 
skilled observers using standardized morphological criteria 
and triplicate measurements, some observer-dependent vari-
ability cannot be excluded, particularly at higher cell densi-
ties. The analyses were conducted in an ISO 17025-accred-
ited laboratory operating under standardized procedures 
and external quality evaluation; however, formal statistical 
assessment of inter-observer agreement and blinded evalua-
tion were not performed. In addition, inoculum standardiza-
tion was based on cell counts rather than biomass, carbon 
content, or digestible nutritional value, and because algal 
and bacterial cells differ in size and biochemical composi-
tion, strict nutritional equivalence among substrates was not 
established. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted 
as demonstrating growth support and comparative response 
patterns rather than precise quantitative nutritional compa-
rability. Although full axenity of the algal cultures was not 
independently verified, the algal cells were washed three 
times with PAS prior to use, and the wash solution alone did 
not support FLA growth, suggesting that the observed pro-
liferation was unlikely to result from the washing medium 
or loosely associated carryover, even though the presence of 
trace associated microorganisms cannot be excluded entirely. 
Microscopic observations further indicated active ingestion 
of algal cells by the amoebae; however, ingestion was not 
quantified, and neither digestion efficiency nor algal deple-
tion was measured. Likewise, physiological and molecu-
lar responses to the different substrates were not assessed. 
Taken together, these constraints indicate that the present 
study should be regarded as a proof-of-feasibility rather 
than a mechanistic analysis of amoeba algivory. Future work 
should therefore integrate rigorously controlled algal cul-
tures, biomass-standardized feeding experiments, quantita-
tive assessment of ingestion and substrate utilization, and 
physiological or transcriptomic approaches to clarify which 
algal traits most effectively support amoeba growth and to 
better evaluate the broader utility of algal-based cultivation 
systems.
Overall, the study shows that selected green algae, par -
ticularly S. obliquus and C. reinhardtii, can sustain measur-
able growth of potentially pathogenic FLA under labora -
tory conditions. These findings expand the methodological 
options available for amoeba cultivation and support the use 
of algal substrates as promising non-bacterial alternatives in 
experimental systems where bacterial prey may introduce 
undesirable ecological or interpretive complexity. Framed 
in this way, algal-based cultivation represents a useful and 
conceptually important direction for future research on FLA 
biology, ecology, and pathogenicity.
Conclusions
This study provides preliminary evidence that selected 
green algae can support the in vitro cultivation of poten-
tially pathogenic free-living amoebae under controlled labo-
ratory conditions. All tested amoeba species showed meas-
urable growth on algal substrates, although the magnitude 
of the response varied among both amoebae and algal taxa. 
Among the examined strains, Acanthamoeba genotype T3 
exhibited the most consistent and pronounced proliferation, 
particularly on S. obliquus and C. reinhardtii, indicating that 
algal suitability as a trophic substrate is species dependent 
rather than uniform across FLA. These findings highlight 
the potential of algal substrates to serve as promising non-
bacterial alternatives for amoeba cultivation in experimen-
tal systems where bacterial prey may introduce additional 
ecological or interpretive complexity. At the same time, 
the observed growth patterns should not be interpreted as 
evidence of strict nutritional equivalence with conventional 
bacterial substrates, because the study was designed as a 
feasibility assessment rather than a mechanistic or biomass-
normalized comparison. Overall, this work establishes a 
proof-of-concept foundation for algal-based cultivation of 
FLA and supports further investigation using rigorously

controlled algal cultures, quantitative feeding analyses, and 
physiological or molecular approaches to determine the 
broader applicability of this strategy in laboratory research 
on amoeba biology, ecology, and pathogenicity.
