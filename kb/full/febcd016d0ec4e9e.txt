Introduction
Liquid and solid animal manure can be utilized for 
biogas production, a sustainable energy source when 
implemented at high technological level and using 
waste biomass as feedstock (Khoshnevisan et  al. 
2021; Lamolinara et  al. 2022; Weiland 2009). After 
biogas production, the resulting digested slurry is typ-
ically applied to fields as an organic fertilizer (Lamo-
linara et al. 2022; Möller & Müller 2012; Nkoa 2013; 
Zilio et al. 2021).
Abstract Anaerobic digestion alters slurry proper -
ties with implications for ammonia (NH3) emission 
after field application. While pH and dry matter (DM) 
are known drivers, they do not fully explain observed 
variability. This study quantified the extent to which 
chemical and physical characteristics explain NH3 
emission from digestates and untreated pig and cat-
tle slurry. Five field trials were conducted over two 
years using nine digestates and six untreated slurries. 
Emission was measured over 150  h following sur -
face band application using dynamic flux chambers 
and cavity ring-down spectroscopy. Slurries were 
characterized for pH, DM, rheological parameters 
(consistency coefficient K ; flow behavior index m ), 
particle size distribution, and exposed surface area. 
Mixed-effects models were used to evaluate correla-
tion between subsets of these variables and cumula-
tive emission. Cumulative NH₃ emission was 41% of 
applied TAN on average for digestate, 27% for cattle, 
and 13% for pig slurry. Emission varied among trials

To enhance biogas yields, slurry may be co-
digested with a range of materials, including indus-
trial waste streams and low-degradability agricultural 
residues such as straw, grass, and deep litter (Chuen-
chart et al. 2024; Møller et al. 2022). As the biogas 
sector continues to expand globally (Abanades et al. 
2022), increasing attention is being given to sourc-
ing feedstocks that do not compete for agricultural 
land. In many regions, land scarcity and competing 
demands for food production make it more sustaina-
ble to rely on co-products and residual biomass rather 
than crops grown specifically for biogas production.
The anaerobic digestion process alters slurry by 
lowering the dry matter (DM) concentration while 
increasing pH (Pedersen & Hafner 2023). A decrease 
in dry matter concentration reduces the ammonia 
emission potential when slurry or digestate is applied 
to the field (Häni et  al. 2016; Misselbrook et  al. 
2005), presumably due to improved infiltration. How-
ever, an increase in pH raises the emission potential 
by increasing the concentration of the volatile free 
ammonia species (Sommer & Husted 1995a). Over -
all, these two effects have been found to approxi-
mately counteract each other when slurry is digested 
without co-substrates, that is, digested slurry and the 
corresponding undigested slurry tend to exhibit simi-
lar ammonia emission after field application on aver -
age (Pedersen & Hafner 2023).
If co-digestion of slurry with recalcitrant bio-
masses, such as straw and deep litter, results in higher 
dry matter concentrations than in undigested slurry, 
ammonia emission after field application is likely 
to increase (Pedersen & Hafner 2023). Romio et  al. 
(2024) reported that changes in co-substrates led to a 
52% increase in the dry matter concentration of Dan-
ish digestates and an average increase in digestate 
pH in Denmark from 7.87 to 8.04 during the period 
2015–2023. This combination of higher dry matter 
and pH results in very high emission potentials (Ped-
ersen & Hafner 2023), thereby increasing environ-
mental risk.
In addition to dry matter and pH, several other fac-
tors affect ammonia emission following field appli-
cation of digestate or slurry. These include weather 
conditions, soil properties, and application method 
(Hafner et  al. 2025; van der Weerden et  al. 2021). 
There are increasing international efforts to quantify 
differences both between and within different slurry 
categories in order to improve emission predictions 
and inventories at farm and regional scales. However, 
data on digestate variables and associated emission 
remain limited, despite substantial and growing use 
of digestates as a fertilizer. Variation in key digestate 
variables, such as dry matter and pH, is considerable 
(Romio et  al. 2024), making emission predictions 
uncertain, particularly for slurry-based digestates 
with high dry matter content.
The physical characteristics of slurry or digestate 
are crucial for the emission process, as they inter -
act with soil properties and application methods and 
determine the infiltration of slurry or digestate into 
the soil and exposed surface area after application. 
Greater infiltration is generally associated with a 
lower emission (Häni et al. 2016; Misselbrook et al. 
2005). The relationship between the exposed surface 
area and emission is more complex; however, all else 
being equal, a lower exposed surface area is assumed 
to reduce emission (Misselbrook et  al. 2005; Webb 
et al. 2010). In most studies, dry matter concentration 
is the only measured physical characteristic of slurry 
or digestate (Bittman et  al. 2005; Chantigny et  al. 
2009; Sanz-Cobena et  al. 2019). Other parameters 
describing the physical characteristics and behavior 
of slurry and digestates are rarely included, and thus 
their importance as explanatory variables remains 
unknown. These parameters include apparent viscos-
ity and particle size distribution. The viscosity of a 
liquid describes its resistance to flow and is defined 
as the ratio between shear stress (force per unit area) 
and shear rate (velocity gradient) (Schneider & Ger -
ber 2020). It is hypothesized that viscosity and par -
ticle size distribution influence slurry and digestate 
infiltration into soil and the spreading of slurry and 
digestate on the soil surface.
Low-emission treatment and application tech-
niques available for untreated slurry can also be 
applied to digestates (Lamolinara et al. 2022). Diges-
tate can be injected at shallow or deep depths, which 
substantially reduces ammonia emission (Hafner 
et al. 2025; Webb et al. 2010); however, injection is 
not a viable option in growing cereal crops due to 
the risk of crop damage. Treatments that can effec-
tively lower ammonia emission following surface 
application of digestates or slurry include acidifica-
tion (Fangueiro et al. 2015) and solid–liquid separa-
tion (Pedersen et al. 2022). However, both techniques 
present challenges for digestates with high dry matter 
concentration. Effective pH reduction of digestates

typically require large amounts of acid (Nyord et al. 
2021), increasing the risk of foaming, and complicat-
ing pumping and field application (J. Pedersen, per -

associated with a substantial cost. Solid–liquid sepa-
ration can reduce emission, but separation efficiency 
and ammonia emission reductions are highly variable 
(Pedersen et  al. 2022; Romio et  al. 2024). In many 
cases, the dry matter concentration is not sufficiently 
reduced to achieve substantial ammonia emission 
reductions (Pedersen et al., 2026). The overall effect 
of separation on ammonia emission also depends 
on ammonia losses from the resulting solid fraction, 
which are often neglected when reporting separation 
effects.
For farmers receiving digestate as fertilizer, knowl-
edge of product quality is crucial to ensure appropri-
ate field management and to assess whether further 
treatment is warranted from an environmental or 
economic perspective (Epper et  al. 2025). However, 
data on ammonia emission following field application 
of digestates remain limited, particularly for diges-
tates with high dry matter concentration (Pedersen 
& Hafner 2023). The present study aimed to improve 
understanding of the relationship between digestate 
characteristics and ammonia emission following field 
application. A total of nine digestates were character -
ized using a comprehensive set of established but sel-
dom measured physical and rheological parameters. 
Ammonia emission was measured from untreated 
slurry and digestate using a dynamic chamber sys-
tem. The use of a small-plot dynamic flux chamber 
measurement approach facilitated evaluation of 
the following hypotheses by supporting replication 
and sufficient precision for statistical comparisons 
between treatments.
This study hypothesized that seldom measured 
physical descriptors, such as digestate viscosity and 
particle size distribution, offer improved predictive 
power over, or in combination with, conventional pre-
dictors such as dry matter and pH in statistical models 
of ammonia emission. As these variables are linked 
to slurry–soil interaction processes (e.g. infiltration 
and surface residence time), they may better explain 
observed differences between slurry types, including 
between undigested slurries and digestates.
Materials and methods
A total of five field trials were conducted in 2023 and 
2024 to quantify differences in ammonia emission 
after field application of digestate and pig and cattle 
slurry (Table 1). Digestates and slurries were applied 
by manual band spreading. Digestates from nine dif-
ferent biogas plants were used, along with cattle and 
pig slurry from three different commercial farms for 
each slurry type, giving a total of 15 different sources 
(i.e., plant or farm identity). All trials included diges-
tate from the biogas plant at Campus Viborg, Aarhus 
University, Denmark (digestate A), which served as 
a reference. Ammonia emission was measured using 
a system of dynamic flux chambers (Pedersen et  al. 
2024a, b).

Chamber measurements modify the environment 
of the emitting surface compared with ambient con-
ditions by altering air flow, precipitation, and solar 
radiation. Therefore, dynamic flux chamber meas-
urements were used to assess relative differences 
between treatments rather than to estimate absolute 
in-situ emission (Hafner et al. 2024; Sommer & Mis-
selbrook 2015).
Conditions during the trial
All the experiments were conducted in the same field 
at Campus Viborg, Aarhus University, Denmark 
(56.49316° N, 9.56128° E). The flat field had a loamy 
sand texture (9% clay, 24% silt, 65% sand), and winter 
wheat had been sown in the preceding autumn in both 
experimental years. The wheat was cut prior to all tri-
als to minimize crop effects on emission (Table 1).
On the day of sample application, a top-soil sample 
was taken for measurement of 1:1 water pH, and three 
undisturbed soil cores (100  cm3) were taken from the 
topsoil layer (top 5 cm) to determine dry bulk density 
and gravimetric soil water content (measured by dry -
ing at 105 °C) (APHA 2017). Each trial required an 
area of approximately 20 m × 20 m, and soil homo-
geneity was assumed within this area. A new section 
of the field was used for each trial. Soil properties and 
wheat height are presented in Table 1.
Ambient air temperature at 2 m height was logged 
continuously at a nearby weather station (< 0.7  km 
distance) at 1 h intervals (Table 1 and Fig. S1).
Digestate and slurry characteristics and their 
application
Digestates from a total of nine biogas plants were 
used in the trials. Animal slurry was the primary 
feedstock for all plants, although slurry type and 
addition of other substrates varied. Digestates A, B, 
E, F, G, H, and I originated from plants using straw 
as a co-substrate, with higher amounts in B, E, F, and 
G than in the others, although exact quantities were 
unknown. Digestate C and D did not include straw 
in the feedstock. Reactor temperatures were approxi-
mately 52 °C for all plants. Three cattle and three pig 
slurry samples were collected from six different com-
mercial farms in Denmark. All digestates and slurries 
were collected 2–4 weeks prior to application and 
stored outdoors under a roof in closed buckets until 
application.
The target total ammoniacal nitrogen (TAN) appli-
cation rate was 60 kg  ha−1 for all digestates and slur -
ries. The target volumetric application rates (m3  ha−1) 
were calculated based on the TAN concentrations 
determined photometrically 1–2 days prior to applica-
tion in a well-mixed sub-sample using a commercial 
kit (Spectroquant Test kit No. 1.00683.0001, Merck 
KgaA, Darmstadt, Germany). Subsamples were col-
lected in the field for analysis. Actual TAN applica-
tion rates, calculated from the applied volume and 
TAN concentration determined subsequently in labo-
ratory analysis of field subsamples ranged between 44 
and 73 kg TAN  ha−1 (Table S1).
In the laboratory, digestate and slurry samples 
were analysed gravimetrically for dry matter (DM %) 
and volatile solids (VS %) concentrations by heating 
at 105  °C and calcination at 550  °C (APHA 2017). 
TAN was measured as described above and total 
nitrogen was analysed using a low-field nuclear mag-
netic resonance sensor (Jensen et al. 2021). pH meas-
urements (Knick Portavo 902 pH meter) were con-
ducted in the laboratory (Table S1) and at the start of 
the application trials.
Apparent viscosity was measured in triplicate with 
a rotational viscometer (Brookfield DV2T, AME-
TEK Brookfield, Middleboro, USA). Approximately 
600 mL of sample at 20 ± 3 °C was used. Cylindrical 
spindles (61 LV, 66 LV-C, 67 LV-C, and 64 LV) were 
selected according to the apparent viscosity range 
of each sample. Apparent viscosity was measured 
for one minute at different shear rates (proportional 
to the spindle rotational speed), increasing from 1 to 
44  s−1. As recommended by the manufacturer, viscos-
ity values measured at torques lower than 10% were 
discarded.

Particle size distribution was quantified in dupli-
cate by a combination of gravimetric and laser dif-
fraction methods, since laser diffraction required 
particles < 2  mm. Approximately 30  g of sample 
was sieved through a 2  mm mesh and washed with 
approximately 500  ml deionized water. The retained 
material was dried at 105 °C for 24 h and weighed to 
determine the fraction of dry matter > 2 mm relative 
to total wet sample weight. The filtrate was analysed 
by laser diffraction (Malvern Mastersizer 2000, Mal-
vern Panalytical, Malvern, United Kingdom) using 
the Fraunhofer scattering model after dispersion with 
water. From this, the percentage of particles < 1 mm 
among those < 2 mm was determined.
Digestate and slurry were applied manually to the 
soil surface with a watering can fitted with a hose to 
mimic trailing hose application. Previous compari-
sons have shown that variability in cumulative emis-
sion is lower with manual than machine application 
(Pedersen et  al. 2024a); therefore, manual applica-
tion was used to reduce replication requirements and 
allow inclusion of more treatments per trial. The pre-
determined volume of digestate or slurry was evenly 
distributed in two narrow bands within the area cov -
ered by each dynamic flux chamber.
Ammonia emission
Ammonia emission was quantified for 150  h follow -
ing application using 16 dynamic flux chambers con-
nected to a cavity ring-down spectrometer (CRDS) 
(model G2103, Picarro Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) 
to measure ammonia concentrations in inlet and out-
let air streams. Detailed system description and vali-
dation are provided in Pedersen et  al. (2024a). The 
CRDS instrument has been evaluated for potential 
interferences of other gases in an agricultural envi-
ronment by Kamp et  al. (2019) who found that any 
interferences were negligible.
Each chamber consisted of an open-bottom opaque 
polyethylene cylinder (700  mm diameter, 392  mm 
height). Air entered via three inlet pipes (1 m height, 
80  mm diameter), positioned evenly on the top. A 
plywood deflector plate positioned 92 mm below the 
top ensured even airflow distribution (Figs.  S2 and 
S3). Air was drawn from the chamber centre by a 
fan. An iris diaphragm controlled the volumetric air 
exchange rate (AER, min−1), which was maintained at 
15  min−1 throughout all trials.
Air was drawn from each chamber and from three 
background ports positioned at the air inlet of three 
of the chambers, at 1.5  L  min−1 through a heated 
and insulated polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) tub-
ing (6.35  mm OD, 4.76  mm ID) to the CRDS 
instrument. Each sample position was measured for 
8 min per cycle. The CRDS measured the ammonia 
concentrations every 2  s. The mean ammonia con-
centration over the final 30 s of each cycle was used 
for flux calculations, as this period has been found 
to yield stable readings (Pedersen et al. 2024a).
Ammonia flux ( F , mg N  m−2  h−1) was calculated 
from the background-corrected ammonia concentra-
tion ( C , mg  N  m−3), airflow through the chamber 
( q , m3  h−1), and the area of soil surface covered by 
the chamber (A, m2) (Eq. 2 ).
The relative flux (frac. applied TAN  h−1) was 
calculated by dividing flux (mg  N  m−2  h−1) by the 
TAN application rate (mg  N  m−2). Cumulative 
emission was calculated using the trapezoidal rule, 
which approximates the area under the emission-
versus-time curve by summing the areas of trap-
ezoids formed between consecutive measurement 
points (Simmons 1996). To avoid bias, no detec-
tion limit was applied to the data, as recommended 
by Helsel (2010), therefore, some negative values 
occurred in the absence of significant emission.
Three replicates per treatment (digestate or 
slurry source) were used in each trial. Ammonia 
concentrations were recorded approximately every 
2.5 h per chamber. During trial 1 instrument mal-
function caused data loss between 48.1 h and 69.6 h 
after application. No gap filling was applied for this

Slurry exposed surface area measurements
Exposed surface area (ESA), defined as the percent-
age of soil covered by digestate or slurry, was deter -
mined using an imaging method. The dark chamber 
setup used is described in detail in Pedersen et  al. 
(2021a) and the updated imaging method used to 
quantify the exposed surface area is described in 
detail in Pedersen et al. (2024b). Digestate or slurry 
was mixed with fluorescent dye (Acid Yellow 7, 
20 g  L−1), applied manually as described above, and 
immediately covered with a dark chamber illuminated 
by ultraviolet light. A digital image was captured, 
and exposed surface area was quantified using image 
analysis. Threshold values are reported in Table S2.
Statistics
Linear mixed-effects models were used to assess and 
quantify differences among slurry types and correla-
tion between chemical and physical slurry variables 
and emission. The unit of analysis was the field trial × 
slurry source combination. The response variable was 
total cumulative emission over 150 h or 8 h, taken as 
the mean of results from 3 field plots (dynamic cham-
bers) for each slurry source in each trial. Slurry prop-
erties were based on the mean of 2 or 3 subsamples.
As slurry variables were observed rather than 
experimentally manipulated, and correlations among 
predictors were high in some cases, it was not possi-
ble to uniquely attribute variability to individual pre-
dictors. A primary subset of predictor variables was 
therefore selected for the modeling work, including 
known drivers of emission (slurry pH and dry mat-
ter concentration) and potential new explanatory vari-
ables (viscosity parameters and particle size distribu-
tion), while keeping the number of variables small 
enough to make hypothesis testing plausible and limit 
spurious results related to correlation among predic-
tor variables.
The following predictor variables were initially 
considered in the linear models: trial and source 
of digestate (i.e., plant or farm identity) as ran-
dom effects, and slurry type (digestate, cattle, or 
pig), exposed surface area, pH, dry matter, particles 
< 1  mm (% particles in fraction < 2  mm), particles 
> 2 mm (% wet sample), m (viscosity parameter), and 
K (viscosity parameter) as fixed effects. The trial var -
iable was expected to cover application timing effects 
due to intrinsically accounting for temperature and 
soil and crop conditions.
Simple bivariate correlation was used to select 
secondary variable subsets from the 10 that were con-
sidered (see Fig.  S4 for scatterplots and Pearson’s r 
values). Most of the variables were highly correlated 
with dry matter (the absolute value of Pearson’s r was 
0.69–0.90 for exposed surface area, log10K , m , and 
particles > 2 mm vs. dry matter concentration), mak -
ing it impossible to quantify unique relationships to 
emission in models that included dry matter and oth-
ers. Instead, slurry type and the following combina-
tions of fixed effects were considered:
A. Slurry type,
B. pH, dry matter, and particles < 1 mm,
C. pH, exposed surface area, particles < 1 mm, and 
particles > 2 mm,
D. pH, log10(K ) , particles < 1 mm,
E. pH, m , and particles < 1 mm, and
F. pH, particles < 1 mm, and particles > 2 mm.
The slurry type model A was included both to 
quantify observed differences among slurry types 
and provide a reference for the evaluation of mod-
els B–F, based on quantitative predictors. The full 
models were simplified by removing predictors that 
were unimportant (p > 0.1 from likelihood ratio test) 
or impossible to estimate (the random slurry source 
effect was always given a variance of zero). Names 
of the resulting simplified models in the following 
sections match the letters given above for the starting 
subset. Additionally, the importance of residual slurry 
type differences in emission not related to physical 
and chemical properties was evaluated by compar -
ing these models to an analogous set with slurry type 
included as a predictor (models B+ through F +). 
Names for models for 8 h total emission have an “8” 
suffix.
Linear mixed-effects models were fit with the 
lmer() function from the lme4 package (v1.1.37) 
(Bates et al. 2015) in R (R Core Team 2025). Hypoth-
esis tests on relationships between individual pre-
dictor variables and emission were assessed using a 
likelihood ratio test (LRT) with the drop1() or anova() 
function. Models were compared using the Akaike 
information criterion (AIC), with differences < 2 
considered trivial and 4–7 substantial (Anderson & 
Burnham 2004). Marginal and conditional R2 values

Results
Emission of ammonia was highest immediately after 
application of digestate or slurry, with large varia-
tions in flux rate and variation within measurements 
from the same digestate or slurry (Fig. 1 and Table 2). 
Diurnal emission patterns were observed for most 
digestates throughout the measuring period (150  h) 
(Fig. S5).
Total ammonia emission was strongly correlated 
with slurry type (p < 0.001 for overall term from 
likelihood ratio test for the mixed-effects model A). 
Emission was highest from digestate, with an over -
all estimate of 41% of applied TAN from model A 
(Table 3). There was evidence of correlation within 
slurry sources; emission from the same source was 
more similar across trials than emission from dif-
ferent sources, with an estimated standard deviation 
among sources (2.3% of applied TAN) similar to that 
among trials (2.8%) (Table 3).
Slurry characteristics together explained a large 
part of the observed variation in total 150 h emission 
within and between slurry types. Five models with 
a single physical parameter and pH were explored 
(Table 4). They all performed similarly, although clear 
evidence of a correlation with emission was lack -
ing for the exposed surface area and log10(K ) models 
from the LRT tests (Table 4). The estimated pH effect 
was very similar for all models, around 0.2  per pH 
unit, (20% of applied TAN per pH unit). Most mod-
els showed the expected sign of the apparent relation-
ship: total 150  h emission was positively correlated 
with dry matter, particles < 1 mm, and log10(K ) , but 
emission was unexpectedly negatively correlated with 
exposed surface area. Most of these models estimated

The average pH of the digestates in the present 
study (8.0) was similar to literature values (8.0; 
Pedersen & Hafner 2023), the Danish average (7.9; 
Adamsen & Hafner 2021), and a recent Danish study 
(8.0; Romio et  al. 2024). The pH of the cattle and 
pig slurries was likewise close to Danish averages 
(7.0 and 7.2, respectively; Adamsen & Hafner 2021). 
Overall, the slurries (including digestates) covered a 
wide pH range (Table  2), enabling assessment of pH 
effects on ammonia emission.
Consistent with Schneider and Gerber (2020) and 
Kariyama et  al. (2018), digestate and slurry viscos-
ity parameters varied substantially (Table  2). Viscos-
ity parameters for digestates covered the full range 
observed for pig and cattle slurries (Figs. 3 and S4).
Cattle slurry exhibited high K (viscosity coef-
ficient) and low m (non-Newtonian liquid, low flow 
behaviour index) values, whereas most pig slurry 
samples showed the opposite pattern, possibly reflect-
ing lower dry matter concentrations. The viscosity 
parameter K generally increases with dry matter con-
centration (Fig.  S4), consistent with increased parti-
cle resistance to flow (Langley-Randall et  al. 2024). 
However, substantial variability in K persisted at high 
dry matter concentrations (Table  2), indicating that 
dry matter alone does not fully explain flow behav -
ior and that additional factors, such as particle size 
distribution and inter-particle interactions also play 
a significant role. The parameter m decreased with 
increasing dry matter (Table  2 and Fig. S4), indicat-
ing stronger shear-thinning behavior and that higher 
dry matter was associated with less Newtonian behav-
ior. At low shear rates, particles have higher flow 
resistance until alignment reduces resistance at higher 
shear rates (Liu et  al. 2019). Samples with low dry 
matter showed less pronounced shear thinning behav-
ior and higher m values, reflecting more Newtonian 
behavior, implying that their viscosity is less sensitive 
to shear rate and therefore more uniform under vary -
ing flow conditions.
Predictors of ammonia emission
Analysis of emission and slurry variables strongly 
suggest that slurry pH and physical variables exert 
a major influence on ammonia emission (Table  4, 
Figs.  2 and 3). The pH effect is unambiguous, pre-
sent as a clear positive correlation between total 
150  h emission and pH in all models where it was 
assessed. This is consistent with the equilibrium 
shift towards free ammonia at higher pH (Som-
mer & Husted 1995a, 1995b). The effect of physi-
cal variables was less distinct. Previous field (e.g., 
Andersson et  al. 2023; Chantigny et  al. 2009; Frost 
et al. 1990) and laboratory (e.g., Sommer et al. 2006; 
Thompson et al. 1990) studies as well as analysis of 
compiled data (Hafner et  al., 2018) have shown that

for ammonia emission. Infiltration, however, depends 
on the interaction between slurry and soil properties, 
and all trials in the present study were conducted on a 
single soil type. The relative importance of dry mat-
ter and other physical slurry characteristics for infil-
tration, and hence for ammonia emission, may there-
fore differ on soils with contrasting texture, structure, 
or moisture content. Furthermore, the correlation 
between physical characters and emission breaks 
down for 8 h emission, suggesting that the physical 
properties are not as important for the initial emis-
sion rate, which is consistent with an effect mediated 
through infiltration. In contrast, a pH effect was clear 
even for 8 h emission.
Exposed surface area
Unexpectedly, emission was negatively correlated 
with exposed surface area (Table  2 and Fig.  3). A 
likely explanation is that high dry matter reduced 
both surface area (Fig.  S4) and soil infiltration, 
increasing the volume of slurry remaining on the 
soil surface. Ammonia at the surface may be more 
susceptible to volatilization than ammonia infiltrated 
into soil, where mass transfer may be impeded or 
ammonia retained (Webb et  al. 2010). This mecha-
nism may outweigh effects of exposed slurry–air 
interface area. In some studies, a lower exposed sur -
face area has been correlated to a lower cumulative 
emission (McCollough et al. 2022; Misselbrook et al. 
2002; Smith et  al. 2000). However, other studies or 
trials did not find this relationship (Misselbrook et al. 
2005; Pedersen et al. 2021a, b; Salazar et al. 2014). 
A further complication arises from the surface area 
measurement method. The present method quantified 
a two-dimensional projection of slurry at the surface; 
actual exposed surface area may vary with micro-
topography despite identical projected two-dimen-
sional area. The findings in the present study under -
line that the correlation between exposed surface area 
as projected to a two-dimensional soil surface and 
cumulative emission is not straightforward. With an 
unexpected negative correlation and less predictive 
power than dry matter, exposed surface area is a poor 
predictor of ammonia emission after application.
Digestate versus untreated slurry
Emission differences between digestate and untreated 
slurry likely reflect the previously described offset-
ting effects of anaerobic digestion on dry matter and 
pH, together with the elevated dry matter of some 
digestates relative to the undigested slurries used here 
(Pedersen & Hafner 2023). In the current study, emis-
sion was highest from digestate and lowest from pig 
slurry (Table  3). The clear evidence that pH and dry 
matter (or related physical variables) affect emission 
from the present work and previous studies suggest 
that all emission differences between digestate and 
undigested slurry can be understood and even pre-
dicted based on slurry variables. This framework is 
partially consistent with the ALFAM2 model (Hafner 
et  al. 2025) which includes pH and dry matter con-
centration effects but also lower emission from pig 
slurry compared to all other slurry types, such as 
cattle slurry or digestate. In contrast, in the present 
work it was digestate that was different from the other 
slurry types, and there was no evidence of a differ -
ence between cattle and pig slurry after adjusting for 
pH and dry matter concentration. Possible reasons 
for the discrepancy include nonlinear responses (the 
ALFAM2 model is nonlinear and the true relation-
ships between emission and pH or dry matter almost 
certainly are as well). But the large change in the 
apparent pH response when slurry type was included 
in the models is indicative of confounding between 
slurry variables and types. This implies substantial 
uncertainty in statistical model parameter estimates 
when slurry type is included with pH and dry matter 
as predictors; the digestate difference is likely spuri-
ous. Beyond these average differences between slurry 
types, additional variability arose among individual 
trials and digestate sources, discussed further below.
Sources of variability
Besides digestate A, included in all the trials, addi-
tional biogas plant sources supplied slurry for trials 
in both years. Digestate variables varied substan-
tially within source (Table  2), influencing emission 
differences. As an example, digestate C had similar 
dry matter concentration in trials 2 and 4, whereas

digestates A and G had lower dry matter in trial 
4 than trial 2, accompanied by lower emission 
(Table 2). This highlights the importance of consider-
ing the variables of the digestate at the time of appli-
cation, instead of assuming average values over long 
periods for the same digestate source. These sources 
of variability are shown clearly in the slurry type 
model A (Table 3); emission was related to both trial 
and source (i.e., biogas plant or farm identity), but the 
residual term standard deviation was nearly twice as 
large as those effects. The trial contribution may be 
related to soil conditions at the time of application, 
such as water content, as well as crop density. Fur -
thermore, varying temperatures would inevitably also 
contribute to variation.
Diurnal emission patterns
Diurnal emission patterns were observed for diges-
tates and cattle slurry, and for pig slurry with high 
dry matter in trial 3, even as late as seven days after 
slurry application (Figs. 1 and S5). It is proposed that 
the persistent diurnal pattern and emission observed 
after the initial days was due to the high dry mat-
ter concentrations of most of the digestates and cat-
tle slurry, leaving more digestate or slurry at the soil 
surface to be subjected to temperature oscillations 
influencing emission. This is consistent with previ-
ous studies showing that digestates and slurries with 
high dry matter content sustained prolonged emis-
sions with pronounced diurnal variability, whereas 
low dry matter pig slurry typically exhibits low emis-
sion rates and lacks a clear diurnal pattern (Anders-
son et al. 2023; Chantigny et al. 2009; Sanz-Cobena 
et al. 2019).
Conclusions
Although digestates exhibited higher emission than 
cattle and pig slurry, and emission variation within 
slurry types was associated with unique sources, these 
differences were largely accounted for by variation in 
pH and dry matter. This suggests that slurry type per 
se is not a primary driver of emission. Although rhe-
ological variables and particle size distribution may 
more directly reflect the physical mechanisms driv -
ing emission, their correlation with dry matter con-
centration and only marginal improvements in model 
performance suggest limited insight or practical 
improvements over dry matter as a predictor. These 
findings support the continued measurement and use 
of pH and dry matter concentration as key predictors 
in emission models and inventories, while indicating 
limited added value of routine viscosity characteriza-
tion under field conditions. Broader datasets across 
soils, climates, and application techniques are needed 
to reduce parameter uncertainty. Improved integration 
of routinely measured slurry variables into decision-
support tools may enhance mitigation strategies.
