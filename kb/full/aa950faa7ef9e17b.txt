Long‑term exposure to ambient air pollution and an increased risk 
of hyperuricemia
Su Hwan Cho1,2 · Hyun Jin Kim3 · Soontae Kim4 · Yoon‑Hee Kang5 · Jae Moon Yun1,2 · Jin Ho Park1,2 
Received: 31 March 2026 / Revised: 24 August 2026 / Accepted: 28 August 2026 
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract
Objectives Evidence suggests that ambient air pollution contributes to metabolic dysregulation, including hyperuricemia. 
However, previous studies were limited to specific groups or regions with relatively high air pollutant concentrations. This 
study examined the association between long-term exposure to ambient air pollutants and hyperuricemia in a general popu-
lation exposed to relatively low pollution concentrations.
Method This cross-sectional study analyzed 20,117 adults who underwent health screenings in the Republic of Korea 
(2015–2019). Annual mean concentrations of PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, and CO were estimated at residential addresses using 
the Community Multiscale Air Quality model. Hyperuricemia was defined as serum uric acid level > 7.0 mg/dL (males) 
or > 6.0 mg/dL (females). Multivariable logistic regression estimated odds ratios (ORs) per interquartile range (IQR) increase 
in pollutant exposure, adjusting for confounders including estimated glomerular filtration rate.
Results Among 20,117 participants, 3,078 (15.3%) had hyperuricemia. All five pollutants were significantly associated 
with hyperuricemia. The ORs (95% CIs) per IQR increase were 1.06 (1.01–1.10) for PM2.5, 1.15 (1.08–1.21) for PM10, 1.07 
(1.00–1.14) for NO2, 1.13 (1.07–1.19) for SO2, and 1.06 (1.01–1.11) for CO. Associations were stronger among current 
smokers, older adults, and individuals with dyslipidemia or central obesity (all P for interaction < 0.05).
Conclusions Long-term exposure to air pollutants was significantly associated with hyperuricemia, independent of renal 
function and even at low concentrations. These findings suggest that air pollution is an important environmental risk factor 
for metabolic disease, emphasizing the need for stricter regulations to protect vulnerable populations.
Key Points
• Long-term exposure to major ambient air pollutants is significantly associated with an increased risk of hyperuricemia, even at relatively 
low concentrations.
• The association between air pollution and hyperuricemia persists independent of renal function, suggesting a potential role in systemic 
metabolic dysregulation.
• Vulnerable populations, including older adults, current smokers, and individuals with metabolic conditions, exhibit stronger associations 
between air pollution exposure and hyperuricemia.

Introduction
Recent epidemiological studies indicated that the prevalence 
of gout and hyperuricemia has been rapidly increasing glob-
ally, imposing a substantial socioeconomic burden on health-
care systems [1]. Hyperuricemia was primarily considered a 
precursor to gout and kidney stones [2]. However, emerging 
epidemiological evidence has expanded its clinical signifi -
cance, identifying it as an independent risk factor for various 
cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, including hyperten-
sion, atherosclerosis, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and 
type 2 diabetes [3 –5]. Given its increasing prevalence and 
systemic health implications, identifying modifiable risk fac-
tors for hyperuricemia is important.
The etiology of hyperuricemia is multifactorial and 
involves genetic predispositions, dietary habits, and lifestyle 
factors. Ambient air pollution has recently attracted atten-
tion as a potential environmental determinant of metabolic 
dysregulation. Air pollutants, particularly particulate matter 
 (PM2.5 and PM10), induce systemic inflammation and oxida-
tive stress, which are key pathological pathways that may 
link environmental exposure to altered purine metabolism 
and elevated uric acid levels [6–9].
However, previous epidemiological studies examining this 
association have two notable limitations. First is regarding 
generalizability: most previous studies were restricted to spe-
cific occupational groups (e.g., traffic police officers or gov-
ernment employees) or narrow demographic segments (e.g., 
older adults), making it difficult to extrapolate the findings to 
the general population [10–13]. Second is regarding exposure 
intensity: the earlier studies were predominantly conducted in 
regions with high average air pollution concentrations. Con-
sequently, the metabolic risks associated with exposure to 
relatively low concentrations, which are typical in many other 
developed nations, remain largely underexplored, creating a 
knowledge gap regarding potential low-level toxicity.
To address this gap, this study used a representative sam-
ple of the general population exposed to a wide range of 
air pollution levels, including relatively low concentrations. 
This study aimed to determine the independent association 
between long-term exposure to five major air pollutants 
 (PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, and CO) and serum uric acid levels 
or hyperuricemia after adjusting for potential confounders, 
including renal function.
Methods
Study population
This study included adults aged ≥ 19 years who underwent 
a general health screening program at the Health Promotion 
Center of Seoul National University Hospital between 
2015 and 2019. The screening program comprised anthro-
pometric measurements, laboratory tests, major cancer 
screening, and surveys designed to obtain health-related 
information. Of the 21,575 participants, those with miss-
ing data on key variables were excluded. Additionally, par-
ticipants were excluded if they had a self-reported history 
of CKD or an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) 
of < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. The eGFR was calculated using 
the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collabo-
ration (CKD-EPI) creatinine equation [14]. Furthermore, 
participants receiving treatment for hyperuricemia or gout 
were excluded from the study. Consequently, 20,117 par -
ticipants were included in the final analysis (Fig.  1). The 
study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review 
Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. 
H-2202–073–1299). The requirement for informed con-
sent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the 
study. All procedures were performed in accordance with 
the Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments.
Air pollution exposure
Individual exposure to air pollutants, including particulate 
matter 2.5 (PM2.5), particulate matter 10 (PM10), sulfur 
dioxide (SO2), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and carbon monox-
ide (CO), was estimated using a comprehensive modeling 
approach that integrated observational data with the Com-
munity Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) modeling system 
(version 4.7.1) [ 15]. To address the spatial limitations 
of ground-based monitoring stations (Air Korea, https:// 
www. airko rea. or. kr/ web), this study employed the CMAQ 
model with a 9-km grid resolution to estimate pollution 
levels across the entire country. This modeling system 
was coupled with the Weather Research and Forecasting 
(WRF, version 3.9) model for meteorological fields and the 
Sparse Matrix Operator Kernel Emissions (SMOKE, ver -
sion 3.1) system for emission processing. The accuracy of 
the modeled concentrations was validated against ground 
measurements from the Air Korea Network, as detailed 
in previous studies [16, 17]. Daily average concentrations 
were aggregated for the 250 administrative districts and 
matched to each participant’s residential address. Regard-
ing long-term exposure, each pollutant’s annual mean 
concentration was calculated for the year preceding the 
participant’s health-screening date (1-year lag).
Definition of hyperuricemia and covariates
Hyperuricemia was defined as a serum uric acid 
level > 7.0 mg/dL in males and > 6.0 mg/dL in females, 
reflecting sex-specific differences in urate metabolism [18].

Information regarding medical history and health-related 
behaviors, including smoking, alcohol consumption, and 
physical activity, was obtained using self-administered 
questionnaires. Smoking status was categorized as “never 
smoked,” “previous smoker,” and “current smoker.” Alco-
hol consumption was classified as “non-drinking,” “light-to-
moderate drinking,” and “heavy drinking.” Heavy drinking 
was defined as consuming ≥ 15 drinks/week or ≥ 5 drinks on 
a single occasion for males and ≥ 8 drinks/week or ≥ 4 drinks 
on a single occasion for females [19]. Adequate physical 
activity was defined as performing at least 150 min of mod-
erate-intensity activity, 75 min of vigorous-intensity activity, 
or an equivalent combination thereof per week [20]. Comor-
bidities, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dys-
lipidemia, were defined based on self-reported medication 
use or the following diagnostic criteria: systolic blood pres-
sure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg for 
hypertension; fasting blood glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL or hemo-
globin A1c ≥ 6.5% for diabetes; and low-density lipoprotein 
(LDL) cholesterol ≥ 130 mg/dL for dyslipidemia.
Statistical analysis
Baseline characteristics were compared between participants 
with and without hyperuricemia using the Student’s t-test for 
continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical 
variables. The data were presented as means (standard devia-
tion [SD]) for continuous variables and numbers (percentages) 
for categorical variables. Pearson’s correlation analysis was 
performed to examine the association between air pollutants.
Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed 
to examine the association between air pollutant exposure 
(per IQR increase) and serum uric acid levels, treated as 
a continuous variable. Potential confounders were adjusted 
using three sequential models. Model 1 was adjusted for age 
and sex. Model 2 was adjusted for body mass index (BMI), 
waist circumference (WC), and lifestyle factors (smoking 
status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity). Model 3 
was adjusted for eGFR and comorbidities, including hyper-
tension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. In these models, sex, 
smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, 
and comorbidities were considered categorical variables, 
whereas age, WC, BMI, and eGFR were considered con-
tinuous variables.
Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression analyses 
were conducted to determine the ORs and 95% confidence 
intervals (CIs) for hyperuricemia associated with an IQR 
increase in air pollutant exposure, using the same adjustment 
models (Models 1, 2, and 3). The 1-year average exposure 
concentrations were used to assess the effects of long-term 
exposure.

To examine potential effect of modification by covariates, 
this study tested for interactions between air pollutants and 
key covariates in a fully adjusted model (Model 3). This 
was achieved by introducing a multiplicative interaction 
term into the regression model. Statistical significance of 
the interaction was assessed based on the interaction term’s 
p-value. Regarding continuous covariates (e.g., age and WC) 
that exhibited significant interactions, this study calculated 
and plotted the marginal effects of air pollutants across the 
range of the covariate. This visualization allowed interpreta-
tion of how the magnitude of the association changed as the 
covariate value increased. For categorical covariates (e.g., 
smoking status and comorbidities), stratified analyses were 
conducted to estimate the association within each subgroup. 
Heterogeneity of the effects between subgroups was evalu-
ated using the interaction p-value. The results of these strati-
fied analyses were visualized using a forest plot to facilitate 
comparison of effect sizes across subgroups.
All statistical analyses were performed using Stata soft-
ware (MP, version 18.0; StataCorp, College Station, TX, 
USA) and R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Sta-
tistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance 
was defined as a two-sided p-value of < 0.05.
Results
Study population’s baseline characteristics
Of the 20,117 participants, 3,078 (15.3%) had hyperurice -
mia. Compared with the non-hyperuricemia group, partici-
pants with hyperuricemia were younger and more likely to 
be male. The hyperuricemia group exhibited significantly 
less favorable metabolic profiles, characterized by higher 
BMI, WC, and blood pressure, as well as elevated levels of 
total cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL cholesterol, along 
with lower HDL cholesterol. (p < 0.01). Regarding lifestyle 
factors and comorbidities, the hyperuricemia group exhib-
ited higher rates of current smoking and heavy alcohol con-
sumption and a greater prevalence of hypertension and dys-
lipidemia. Additionally, eGFR was significantly lower in the 
hyperuricemia group. Regarding air pollutant exposure, the 
hyperuricemia group had significantly higher annual average 
concentrations of all pollutants (Table 1).
Air pollutants’ distribution and correlations
Table 2 presents descriptive statistics for the annual aver -
age concentrations of air pollutants to which participants 
were exposed. The mean concentrations (SD) of PM2.5, 
 PM10, NO2, SO2, and CO were 24.55 (2.49) μg/m 3, 44.90 
(5.25) μg/m3, 27.04 (8.80) ppb, 4.26 (0.90) ppb, and 507.77 
(70.78) ppb, respectively. The IQRs were 2.62 μg/m 3 for 
 PM2.5, 6.97 μg/m3 for PM10, 13.75 ppb for NO2, 1.10 ppb for 
 SO2, and 83.71 ppb for CO. Supplementary Fig. 1 presents 
the Pearson correlation coefficients for the five air pollutants. 
Strong positive correlations were observed between PM2.5 
and PM10 (r = 0.79) and between NO2 and CO (r = 0.85).
Air pollutants’ association with serum uric acid 
levels and hyperuricemia
The multivariable linear regression analysis (Table  3) indi-
cated that serum uric acid levels were positively associated 
with increased air pollutant concentrations. In the crude and 
minimally adjusted model (Model 1), significant positive 
associations were observed only for PM10 and SO2. How-
ever, after adjustment for potential confounders, including 
lifestyle factors and comorbidities (Model 3), the associa -
tions between PM2.5 and NO2 became statistically signif-
icant. Consequently, in the fully adjusted model, an IQR 
increase in PM2.5 (β = 0.025, p = 0.002), PM10 (β = 0.063, 
p < 0.001), NO2 (β = 0.024, p = 0.047), and SO2 (β = 0.056, 
p < 0.001) was significantly associated with elevated serum 
uric acid levels.
Table 4 presents the ORs for hyperuricemia associated 
with an IQR increase in 1-year air pollutant concentrations. 
In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), significant positive 
associations were observed for all five air pollutants. Spe-
cifically, the ORs for an IQR increase in exposure were 1.06 
(95% CI: 1.01–1.10) for PM2.5, 1.15 (95% CI: 1.08–1.21) 
for PM10, 1.07 (95% CI: 1.00–1.14) for NO2, 1.13 (95% CI: 
1.07–1.19) for SO2, and 1.06 (95% CI: 1.01–1.11) for CO in 
the fully adjusted model.
Subgroup analyses and effect modification
Significant interactions were identified between air pol-
lutants and several covariates in the multivariable logis-
tic regression models. Among the categorical covariates, 
current smoking significantly modified the association 
between PM2.5 and hyperuricemia, with a stronger associa-
tion observed in current smokers (Fig.  2). For PM10, sig-
nificant interactions were observed among current smoking 
and dyslipidemia, with the odds of hyperuricemia notably 
higher among current smokers and participants with dyslipi-
demia than among their counterparts. Among the continuous 
covariates, age exhibited significant interactions with NO2 
and SO2. The positive associations between these pollutants 
and hyperuricemia tended to increase with age. Additionally, 
a significant interaction was observed between WC and NO2, 
indicating that the odds ratio for hyperuricemia associated 
with NO2 exposure increased with higher WC (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 2).

Discussion
This large-scale cross-sectional study demonstrated signifi-
cant positive associations between long-term exposure to air 
pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, and CO) and elevated 
serum uric acid levels and hyperuricemia. These associa-
tions remained robust after adjustment for a comprehensive 
set of confounding factors, including lifestyle habits and 
metabolic comorbidities. The findings suggest that ambi-
ent air pollution is an independent environmental risk factor 
for hyperuricemia and contributes to variations in uric acid 
homeostasis.
The biological plausibility of these findings is supported 
by several potential nonrenal metabolic mechanisms, pre-
dominantly involving systemic inflammation and oxidative 
stress. Upon exposure to inhaled pollutants, airway epithe-
lial cells overproduce mucosal uric acid through xanthine 
dehydrogenase upregulation [21]. While initially protective, 
excessive uric acid acts as a damage-associated molecular 
pattern that triggers local pulmonary inflammation [22]. 
This surplus uric acid is then rapidly absorbed into the sys-
temic bloodstream via the highly vascularized alveolar net-
work. This localized inflammation also induces the produc-
tion of reactive oxygen species, causing oxidative damage 
to DNA and RNA. Beyond accelerated purine catabolism 
from the degradation of damaged nucleotides, air pollution 
directly perturbs purine metabolism within liver tissues 
[23]. Specifically, pollutant-induced systemic inflammation 
and oxidative stress amplify hepatic purine metabolism by 
promoting the immunometabolic upregulation of xanthine 
oxidoreductase—the key enzyme in uric acid synthesis—via 
proinflammatory cytokines, thereby accelerating uric acid 
overproduction [23, 24]. Furthermore, exposure to air pollut-
ants induces hepatic and systemic insulin resistance, which 
overactivates the pentose phosphate pathway—an alternative 
intracellular energy route—ultimately accelerating de novo 
purine synthesis and elevating uric acid levels. In addition, 
this exposure increases the levels of precursor amino acids, 
such as glutamine and glycine, further contributing to uric 
acid accumulation. This indicates that air pollutants actively 
interfere with both amino acid and nucleic acid metabolism, 
thereby driving uric acid production [25, 26].
These metabolic disruptions and subsequent elevated uric 
acid levels have direct clinical implications for gout develop-
ment. Recent epidemiological and experimental evidence 
highlights that both short-term acute spikes and long-term 
chronic exposure to ambient air pollutants are significantly 
associated with an increased incidence of gout flares [27, 
28]. The mechanistic transition from asymptomatic hyper -
uricemia to acute gout flares involves the direct effects of 
air pollutants on the innate immune system [29]. Airborne 
particulate matter, upon phagocytosis by macrophages, acts 
synergistically with deposited monosodium urate crystals 
to strongly activate the NLRP3 inflammasome [29]. Acti-
vation of this inflammasome triggers the robust secretion 
of proinflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1β [29].

Therefore, air pollution exacerbates the risk of clinical gout 
not merely through the gradual elevation of serum uric acid 
but also by actively priming the macrophage NLRP3 inflam-
masome and amplifying the innate inflammatory response 
to urate crystals.
The subgroup analyses revealed that the adverse effects 
of air pollution were more pronounced among vulner -
able populations. Specifically, current smokers exhibited a 
stronger association between PM exposure and hyperurice-
mia. Cigarette smoking impairs mucociliary clearance and 
induces chronic inflammation, which may act synergistically 
with air pollutants to amplify systemic oxidative stress [30]. 
Similarly, participants with dyslipidemia exhibited increased 
susceptibility to the hyperuricemic effects of PM10. Dyslipi-
demia is associated with endothelial dysfunction and a pro-
inflammatory state. Exposure to air pollutants may further 
exacerbate lipid peroxidation and inflammatory responses 
[31]. Regarding continuous variables, the risk of hyperurice-
mia associated with NO2 and SO2 increased with age. This 
may be the result of immunosenescence and a decline in 
antioxidant defense mechanisms among older adults, making 
them more susceptible to environmental oxidative insults 
[32]. Additionally, the interaction between NO2 and waist 
circumference suggested that central obesity, characterized 
by chronic low-grade inflammation, exacerbates the meta-
bolic disruption caused by air pollution [33].
A key strength of this study is the use of a representa-
tive sample of the general population, which distinguishes 
it from previous studies. Most previous longitudinal studies 
on air pollution and hyperuricemia included specific occu-
pational groups or demographic segments, with limited gen-
eralizability. For example, Tang et al. focused exclusively 
on traffic police officers, a group with high occupational 
exposure to vehicle exhaust fumes, and their sample was 
predominantly composed of males [12, 13]. Conversely, 
Duan et al.’s study, which was conducted among govern-
ment employees, included a predominantly female sam-
ple, thereby limiting sex balance [10]. Similarly, Hu et al. 
analyzed data from the CHARLS cohort, which predomi -
nantly included middle-aged and older adults, limiting the 
applicability of their findings to younger populations [11]. 
Conversely, the present study included participants from a 
general health-screening cohort with a wide range of age 
groups, a balanced sex ratio, and diverse occupational back-
grounds. This minimized selection biases related to specific 
occupations, sex ratio, and age-related physiological condi-
tions, thereby enhancing the findings’ generalizability to the 
broader public.
Another distinctive feature of this study was the dem-
onstration of adverse health effects at relatively low con-
centrations of specific air pollutants compared with those 
reported in previous studies conducted in highly polluted 
regions, such as China [10– 13]. For example, the mean 
annual concentration of PM2.5 in this study was 24.55 μg/
m3, which is considerably lower than the levels reported in 
the aforementioned Chinese cohorts (39.35–57.92 μg/m 3). 
However, this study observed that even a small increment 
of 1 IQR (2.62 μg/m3) in air pollutant exposure was signifi-
cantly associated with increased uric acid levels. This find-
ing supports the hypothesis that there is no safe threshold for 
air pollution exposure and emphasizes that even moderate 
to low levels of air pollution can pose a significant public 
health burden from metabolic diseases [34].
Furthermore, this study provides crucial insights into 
the mechanisms linking air pollution to hyperuricemia by 
demonstrating that this association is independent of renal 
function. Previous studies established that air pollution is a 
risk factor for CKD and reduced eGFR, both of which can 
secondarily lead to uric acid retention [ 35, 36]. However, 
although this study adjusted for eGFR in the regression mod-
els, the association between air pollutants and hyperuricemia 
remained statistically significant. This suggests that elevated 
uric acid levels are not solely a consequence of impaired 
renal excretion. Instead, air pollution could directly stimu-
late the overproduction of uric acid via oxidative stress and 
metabolic disruption, independent of its nephrotoxic effects 
[6–9].
This study had several limitations. First, exposure estima-
tions based solely on residential addresses may not have fully 
captured individual exposure variations arising from daily 
mobility or indoor air quality. Future studies should incor -
porate detailed information on daily mobility patterns and 
specific locations where participants spend significant time 
(e.g., workplaces or schools). By accounting for these time-
weighted locations rather than relying exclusively on static 
residential addresses, researchers can achieve a more pre-
cise and dynamic estimation of personal exposure. Second, 
despite adjustments for individual covariates, unmeasured 
socioeconomic factors influencing residential choices may 
have introduced residual confounding regarding regional 
pollution levels. To mitigate this, subsequent research 
should integrate comprehensive socioeconomic indicators, 
such as area-level deprivation indices or household income 
data. Third, this study lacked detailed data on dietary intake 
(e.g., purine-rich foods), which is a direct determinant of 
serum uric acid levels. Ideally, future investigations should 
employ nutritional logs to rigorously control for dietary 
confounders. Fourth, our analysis did not adjust for severe 
clinical outcomes, such as a history of cardiovascular events 
or neurodegenerative disorders (e.g., Alzheimer's disease). 
Specific diagnostic data for neurodegenerative disorders 
were unavailable in our general health screening cohort. 
Additionally, we could not adequately evaluate the history 
of cardiovascular events due to data limitations, including 
a high non-response rate on self-reported questionnaires, 
potential inaccuracies in self-reported diagnoses, and the 
Clinical Rheumatology 
relatively low prevalence of these severe conditions among 
an asymptomatic population undergoing routine health 
screenings. Therefore, we were unable to explore the rela-
tionship between hyperuricemia, air pollution, and these 
severe neurological and cardiovascular outcomes. Finally, 
owing to the cross-sectional design, the temporal sequence 
could not be determined, limiting this study’s ability to infer 
a causal relationship between air pollution and hyperurice-
mia. Large-scale prospective cohort studies with repeated 
measurements of both air pollution and uric acid levels are 
needed to elucidate the causal nature of this association.
In conclusion, this study provides robust evidence that 
long-term exposure to ambient air pollutants, including 
 PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, and CO, is significantly associated 
with an increased risk of hyperuricemia. Notably, these asso-
ciations persisted independently of renal function, suggest-
ing that air pollution may directly disrupt uric acid homeo-
stasis, potentially through oxidative stress and systemic 
inflammation pathways. Furthermore, significant associa-
tions were observed even at relatively low concentrations 
of air pollutants, with stronger associations found among 
vulnerable subgroups, such as smokers, older adults, and 
individuals with dyslipidemia. These findings emphasize 
the lack of a safe threshold of air pollution exposure and its 
implications for metabolic health. Therefore, stricter envi-
ronmental regulations and targeted public health strategies 
are essential to mitigate the burden of air pollution-related 
metabolic diseases, including hyperuricemia and cardiovas-
cular diseases, among the general population.
