Abstract: Constructed wetlands (CWs) are effective and sustainable engineered systems for
domestic and/or municipal wastewater treatments. In the last half-century, CWs have
become common solutions for small communities in remote mountainous areas.
However, cold climate in mountain environment often causes a significant reduction of
the pollutants removal performance compared to warmer environments. Therefore, in
the present study, we aim to help build consensus on the best design practices for
CWs in mountain environments.  A meta-analysis of the scientific literature focusing on
the CWs in cold climate was conducted. Meanwhile, several monitoring activities
(chemical analysis on wastewater and treated water and analysis of changes in the
plant communities) were carried out on 6 CWs observing the different conditions
between immediately after the construction and 3-7 years later. The results showed an
encouraging agreement between scientific studies and observations from the
monitored case studies. Lack of plan space, low temperatures and supply meteoric
inflows prompted the engineers, in the case studies, to design systems composed of
septic tank and horizontal subsurface bed. Such a solution showed a good treatment
efficiency (removal rates were more than 60% for most pollutants) in a harsh
mountainous environment. A significant difference was evident in the choice of plants.
Although the common reeds ( Phragmites australis ) remain an excellent solution, in
plain and hilly areas, planting of tufted hairgrass ( Deschampsia cespitosa ) ensures

Abstract 18 
Constructed wetlands (CWs) are effective and sustainable engineered systems for domestic and/or 19 
municipal wastewater treatments. In the last half -century, CWs have become common solutions 20 
for small communities in remote mountainous areas. However, cold climate in mountain 21 
environment often causes a significant reduction of the pollutants removal performance 22 
compared to warmer environments. Therefore, in the present study, we aim to help build 23 
consensus on the best design practices for CWs in mountain environments. A meta -analysis of the 24 
scientific literature focusing on the CWs in cold climate was conducted. Meanwhile, several 25 
monitoring activities (chemical analysis on wastewater and treated water and analysis of changes 26 
in the plant communities) were carried out on 6 CWs observing the different conditions between 27 
immediately after the construction and 3-7 years later. The results showed an encouraging 28 
agreement between scientific studies and observations from the monitored case studies. Lack of 29 
plan space, low temperatures and supply meteoric inflows prompted the engineers, in the case 30 
studies, to design systems composed of septic tank and horizontal subsurface bed. Such a solution 31 
showed a good treatment effic iency (removal rates were more than 60% for most pollutants) in a 32 
harsh mountainous environment. A significant difference was evident in the choice of plants. 33 
Although the common reeds ( Phragmites australis) remain an excellent solution, in plain and hilly 34 
areas, planting of tufted hairgrass ( Deschampsia cespitosa) ensures similar average performance 35 
but is more suitable for the mountain ecosystems. In conclusion , the present study proposes 36 
technical and engineering recommendations and a sort of ecological design to increase 37 
wastewater treatment efficiency and adapt the systems to a natural and cold environment. 38

Introduction 41 
Wastewater treatment is an extensively explored practice that constantly requests methodological 42 
and technological developments for enhancing the efficiency in removing contaminants, reducing 43 
the costs, and for improving environmental sustainability. Const ructed Wetlands (CW) are a 44 
suitable technique, widely adopted for their cost -effective, energy-efficiency, and environmental -45 
friendly impact on treating various types of wastewaters (municipal -domestic, industrial, 46 
agricultural runoff, landfill leachate, polluted rivers, etc.) (Stefanakis and Tsihrintzis 2012; Flores et 47 
al. 2019) . CWs are an integrated ecosystem that remove efficiently several pollutants such as 48 
3 
 
biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), chemical oxygen demand (COD), total phosphorus (TP), and 49 
total nitrogen (TN), by a synergic process, involving plant uptake, microbe assimilation, and 50 
substrate adsorption (Wu et al. 2015; Jafarinejad and Jiang 2019) . In addition, more than 25 years 51 
of experience showed that CWs assure lower cost, fewer operation and maintenance 52 
requirements than the traditional ones (Cooper 1999) . Although these solutions bring evident 53 
advantages, the research is still ongoing, especially in remote mountainous areas (Schwitzguébel 54 
et al. 2011; Wang et al. 2017) . Significant problems a re related to i) the construction work where 55 
large and flat surface space and unrestricted accessibility are often missing, and ii) to the cold 56 
temperatures that reduce the pollution removal efficiency of the system (Varma et al. 2021) . 57 
Many studies agree on how pollutants removal efficiencies are higher in summer than in winter 58 
(Kadlec et al. 2000; Tunçsiper 2009) and how the best nutrient removal rate occurs at a 59 
temperature of 30°C (Akpor et al. 2013) . Low temperatures negatively affect phytodegradation, 60 
nitrification, denitrification, microbial degradation, and adsorption (Ji et al. 2020; Varma et al. 61 
2021). Although some authors proposed solutions to overcome the impact of low temperature on 62 
pollutants removal, the limits of CWs in cold environment are evident and design and plant species 63 
selection remain a challenge (Yan and Xu 2014; Wang et al. 2017). 64 
The designers could act over many parameters: hydrological and hydraulic characteristics of the 65 
system (subsurface or open water surface flow), flow pat h (horizontal or vertical), medium (grain 66 
size and additional filters), and type of plants (emergent, submerged, or free -floating) (Zhang et al. 67 
2017). Three main types of phytoremediation beds can be found in literature: horizontal 68 
subsurface flow (HSF), vertical subsurface flow (VSF), and free-water surface (FWS-CW). In HSF and 69 
FWS-CW the bed is horizontal, dug in the ground at 0.5 -1m-depth, whit in the first a wastewater 70 
flow that always is embedded, while in the second a free dispersion of water on the surface. In 71 
VSF, instead, vertical tanks are filled with the medium and vegetated, and the wastewater is 72 
inputted on the surface and moves vertically to the outlet. 73 
In remote and cold mountainous areas, the selection of hydrological and hydraulic features and 74 
direction of flow is often subject to external conditions, such as topography and accessibility, thus 75 
the plant species begin a crucial design parameter (Mustafa et al. 2009; Brisson and Chazarenc 76 
2009; Vymazal 2011a; Jain et al. 2020) . In particular, the roots of plants play an indispensable role 77 
in the subsoil producing organic matter that is the energy source for heterotrophic activity by 78 
microorganisms and fungi, in their turn, responsible for transforming and decomposing the 79 
4 
 
contaminants (Brix et al. 2007; Kadlec and Wallace 2009). Meanwhile, the plants cover the surface 80 
offering insulation and keeping substrate -free in colder periods (Vymazal 2011b). In addition, the 81 
right choice of plants is key to maintaining the integrity of the landscape and en hance the 82 
ecological and environmental value of a wetland ecosystem (Celewicz-Gołdyn and Kuczyńska -83 
Kippen 2017) . Stems, roots, and leavers serve as a substrate for microbes and bacteria, and a 84 
shelter for invertebrates and vertebrates (Rejmankova 2011). 85 
In this context, despite the scientific communities making efforts to investigate the impact of 86 
different plant species on pollutants removal, few are the studies focusing on the selection of 87 
plant species that allow to balance the treatment performance and the ecological aspects in 88 
remote and cold climate areas (Põldvere et al. 2009; Cicero Fernandez et al. 2019) . Exploring the 89 
experiences in high mountains is a necessity to address the design of new efficient systems. 90 
In the present study, the main purpos e is to provide some recommendations derived from a 91 
detailed literature review and a mid -term monitoring of some case studies in mountainous areas 92 
of the Alps, at low temperature. For this purposed, we organised the work into three main 93 
chapters and objectives: 94 
 A meta-analysis of the wide literature on CW applications in cold climates to investigate which 95 
typology, medium, plant species and precautions can improve the performance in wastewater 96 
treatment. 97 
 A field monitoring that focuses on the removal rate over time of 6 CWs built with different 98 
designs and plant species. 99 
 The plant community evolution– the only design parameter that evolves over time – within the 100 
6 studied CWs through the means of ecological criteria. 101 
Furthermore, the present study provides an experience for an ecological design of new CW 102 
systems that, as well as being efficient, have to be properly adapted to a natural and precious 103 
mountainous landscape as in protected areas. 104

Material and methods 120 
Meta-analysis 121 
The study provides a meta -analysis of the recent scientific publication focused o n the use of CWs 122 
for wastewater treatment in mountainous areas in cold climate. The selection includes all the 123 
studies conducted on sites with an average annual temperature of less than 10°C ( and an average 124 
temperature in winter less than -5°C) and covers a wide range of developments in this field. In 125 
particular, the meta -analysis focuses on several factors such as the CW typologies (horizontal 126 
subsurface flow HSF, vertical subsurface flow VSF and free -water surface FWS-CW), the substrates 127 
used as a medium, the plant species, and the capacity to treat different pollutants. 128

Inlet and outlet samples of domestic wastewaters were collected from inspection wells after the 131

case studies (Aviolo, Cevo, and Tonolini). Two replicates sampling were conducted in Tonilini and 133

1.1.2 Vegetation analysis 139 
Floristic-vegetational data were collected in the 6 areas occupied by the CWs, immediately after 140 
the construction (t 0) and after several years of operation (t x, from 2 to 7 years later). Some 141 
sampling plots of 2 -16 m2 were defined on the CW beds of each study area (Aviolo: 3; Tonolini: 3; 142 
Gnutti: 3; Cevo: 2; Lozio: 3; Grevo: 3) where the plants were planted during the construction. The 143 
plant species (tracheophytes) of each sampling plot were collected and identified in the months of 144 
July for some consecutive years (2019 -2022) using “Flora d’Italia” dichotomous keys (Pignatti, 145 
1982; Pignatti et al., 2017) and the percentage of coverage was estimated for each of them. 146 
1.1.3 Ecological indicators 147 
Six floristic -ecological indicators proposed by Landolt et al. (2010) were used to evaluate the 148 
environmental requirements of the plant species found in scientific literature and detected in the 149 
sampling areas: soil moisture (F), humus content (H), light needs (L), nutrients in the soil (N), soil 150 
reaction (R), and temperature (T). The values of these indexes range from 1 to 5 ( Table 2). From 151 
the floristic -vegetational data collected, the mean values of each indicator were calculated by 152 
averaging the species values weighted on their abundance (percentage of coverage). Such indexes 153 
were used to compare the ecological differences and the evolution trajectory between the plant 154 
communities stabilized immediately after the CW construction and approximately 5-years later. 155

Results 157 
Meta-analysis 158 
The selected studies are 77 that investigated the removal performance of CWs in cold clima tes 159 
located in 27 different countries (Supplementary Materials – Table A). The meta -analysis focused 160 
on 4 different CW types: HSF, VSF and two hybrid systems, HSF+VSF and VSF+HSF. In fact, 161 
subsurface systems are more suitable for treating wastewater at low temperature because the 162 
heat loss is lower under the ground (Liang et al. 2020), whereas the free water surface systems are 163 
heavily affected by adverse conditions such as water icing and plant withering (Kadlec and Wallace 164 
2009). 165 
1.1.4 CWs typologies 166

Discussion 277 
Design key parameters from literature to experience 278 
The criteria for CW design and operation include the selection of site, substrate, wastewater type, 279 
plant species, hydraulic loading rate, hydraulic retention time, water depth, operation mood and 280 
maintenance procedures (Kadlec and Wallace 2009; Akratos et al. 2009) . In cold mountainous 281 
regions, the choice of the design key parameters should be done considering both the effects of 282 
the low temperature on the wastewater treatment performance (on phytodegradatio n, 283 
nitrification, denitrification, microbial degradation, and adsorption of the pollutants) and the 284 
impacts of the planted plants on the ecological landscape (including their ability to survive in a 285 
specific environment) (Varma et al. 2021). 286 
11 
 
1.1.10 Typology 287 
This study conducted a detailed review that identifies the VSF+HSF systems as the best solution in 288 
terms of the reduction of water pollutants. However, this typology of CW requests wide and plain 289 
spaces that are rarely available. For these reasons, the mos t adopted solution was the 290 
combination between a Imhoff tank (or septic tank) and HSF. The first element settles the solids 291 
on the bottom, while the wastewater treatment is conducted through the horizontal subsurface 292 
flow. The subsurface flow is more suita ble in cold climates as their wastewater treatment process 293 
is under the ground where heat loss is less (Liang et al. 2020) . Conversely, all those systems 294 
including surface flow are strongly affected by wastewater disruption due to water icing and plant 295 
withering (Kadlec and Wallace 2009). For these reasons, the typology composed of septic tank and 296 
HSF is the most adaptable in cold mountain areas, as shown by the performance of our case 297 
studies. Both septic tank and HSF bed have to be designed in function of PE: Many authors 298 
recommend following standard designing criteria: 3-5 m2 PE-1, gravel-based with a mean grain size 299 
<10 mm, with a slope of beds of 1 -2 % and mean depth of beds of 0.7 m (Masi et al. 2000). These 300 
designing parameters guarantee the best results in the removal of organic load, suspended solids, 301 
hygienic load, irrespective of the variations in hydraulic load, the characteristics of water treated 302 
and of seasonal temperature changes. 303 
1.1.11 Medium 304 
A design key parameter is cer tainly the medium that usually is selected in function of its hydraulic 305 
permeability and the capacity of absorbing different pollutants. Poor conductivity causes an 306 
increase of risk of clogging, whereas poor abortion by substrate means low long -term remova l 307 
performance (Wang et al. 2010) . In this context, this study underlined the good performance in 308 
removing pollutants provided by sand and fine gravel. However, these media increased the risk of 309 
clogging the first part of the CW bed where the wastewater flow is concentrated. For this reason, a 310 
reliable expedient is to fill the initial and outlet zones of the CW bed with coarse gravel to favour 311 
the wastewater flow. Moreover, it is evident how sand and gravel are poor candidates for long -312 
term phosphorus and ni trogen storage; thus, the designers prefer to add other media 313 
supplements. For example, at low temperatures, the performance could be improved mixing sand 314 
and/or gravel with other materials such as zeolite, lightweight aggregates and carbonate that 315 
reduce all the main pollutants. Another interesting media supplement was the biochar: recent 316 
studies showed good performance in removing nitrates (Boehm et al. 2020) and was considered 317 
an effective alternative technology for its low cost. However, under low -temperature conditions, 318 
12 
 
some authors noticed a decrease of microbial activity and as consequence nitrogen removal 319 
efficiency (Zhang et al. 2021). In all our systems, the choice fell on a 50 -100 mm gravel with zeolite 320 
supplement; at an elevation higher than 190 0 m asl the designers placed an additional layer of 321 
natural soil above the substrate. 322 
1.1.12 Plant species 323 
A challenge in designing CWs is to select plant species or better plant communities that can 324 
improve the pollutants removal rate. The most common plant use d in CWs for wastewater 325 
treatment were P. australis, Typha spp., and Scirpus spp. (Reed and Brown 1992). Undoubtedly, P. 326 
australis is largely recognized as the “best plant” for treating wastewater and groundwater 327 
contaminated by organic pollutants (Fester 2012), even in a low -temperature environment. P. 328 
australis has a positive effect in removing organics, ammonia, phosphorus, and fecal coliforms, 329 
and in reducing heavy metal and per -polyfluoroalkyl substances concentrations (Schierup and 330 
Larsen 1981; Vymaz al and Březinová 2016; Ferrario et al. 2022) . This choice is confirmed also in 331 
our cast studies ( Figure 2). In Cevo and Lozio (800 -1100 m als), 5 years after the construction, P. 332 
australis has benefited from a good brightness and wet condition, remaining the dominant species 333 
with a plant cover of around 80%. Nevertheless, P. australis, used in a cold climate, is probably 334 
near close range to species ascension limit (Varma et al. 2021) . In fact, P. australis (and Typha 335 
spp.) often turns yellow when the temperature fell below 5°C and completely withe rs when the 336 
temperature reached the freezing point (Yan and Xu 2014) . In addition, P. australis generally 337 
suffered a shaded environment (L=4) and an excessive presence of humus on the ground (H=3). 338 
This fact was confirmed in Grevo, where the CW bed was deg raded over time. The reasons were: 339 
(i) dense foliage of three chestnut trees that covered the CW bed; (ii) a clogging of the inlet zone 340 
due to the dense root network of the P. australis and the concentrated inlet wastewater flow; and 341 
(iii) the presence of wild animals such as boars ( Sus scrofa). P. australis cannot be the optimum 342 
solution for high -altitude locations and/or for protected natural areas. As a potential alternative, 343 
the first systems were covered by a shallower soil layer with meadow species, c ollected in 344 
proximity. The monitoring conducted in the present study underlined how D. cespitosa was the 345 
dominant species inside the conditions of the HSF beds. D. cespitosa is a common species of the 346 
Alps, widespread in high -altitude meadows, marshes, and moorland (Davy 1980) . Such species 347 
grows colonizes on soils with impeded drainage, low oxygen concentration, a wide range of pH 348 
(3.7-8.3) and poor nutrients (and also heavy metals) (Cox and Hutchinson 1979) . Moreover, it has 349 
little sensitivity to atmosphe ric humidity, wind-exposure and a longer period of cold temperature 350 
13 
 
(even producing more seed) (Davy 1982). As a tufted hairgrass plant, it was able to grow in a wide 351 
range of light intensities, also acclimatising to shade. In terms of water pollutants tre atment, D. 352 
cespitosa tolerates high level of nitrogen and phosphorus especially ammonia and provides high 353 
capacity in nutrient fixation. 354 
Problems, maintenance and possible solutions 355 
The literature overview and the monitoring of some case studies showed some 356 
problems/solutions for the design of CWs in cold climate regions. This evidence can be resumed in 357 
the following points. 358 
 Improving the wastewater treatment performance. Some recommendations are: (i) control the 359 
runoff flow and stormwater towards the sy stem (directly or diluting the wastewater supply) 360 
that strongly reduces the hydraulic retention time; (ii) if there is empty space, designing a VSF 361 
upstream to HSF to promote nitrification and aerobic processes (Merlin et al. 2002; Mantovi et 362 
al. 2003; Je nssen et al. 2005; Sayadi et al. 2012) ; (iii) sizing the CW beds at least 6 m 2 PE-1 to 363 
reduce the impacts of external factors (Buchberger and Shaw 1995; Werker et al. 2002; Brix 364 
and Arias 2005; Tunçsiper et al. 2015). 365 
 Sizing properly the CW bed . Some recom mendations are: (i) the wastewater has to be pre -366 
treated into a sedimentation tank (minimum volume 2 m 3 for a single household with up to 5 367 
PE) (Brix and Arias 2005) ; (ii) the minimum length of the root -zone system is 10 m (Brix and 368 
Arias 2005; Vymazal 2007); (iii) the bottom slope should be around 1% from inlet to outlet; (iv) 369 
guarantee a minimum bed depth of 0.60 m at the inlet side of the bed and deeper towards the 370 
outlet; (iv) install a tight membrane (minimum 0.5 mm thickness) of polyvinyl chloride or 371 
polyester reinforced with glass fibers to avoid the infiltration below; (v) protect the membrane 372 
with sand or a geotextile layer; and (vi) the substrate should be uniform sand and fine gravel 373 
0.5-8 mm. 374 
 Selecting the most adapt plant species . This study emphasized the importance of an ecological 375 
design showing the potentialities of P. australis (as expected) and D. cespitosa in function of 376 
the environment where the CW has to be constructed. P. australis guarantees good 377 
performance especially at an altitude less than 1300 m a.l.s., making some few precautions: (i) 378 
after planting, a periodic eradication of invasive species is recommended for the first 3 years 379 
until complete colonization of the bed with a medium -high plant density (or plant cover) 380 
(Budelsky and Galatowitsch 2000); (ii) maintain a water level of 5 -10 cm under the ground to 381 
14 
 
favor the plant growth; (iii) avoid the presence of excessive level of humus content in the 382 
shallower soil layer; and (iv) install a fence against wild animals (e.g. boars). Al ternatively, for 383 
locations at higher elevations and cold climates, D. cespitosa is a good solution. Some 384 
recommendations are: (i) plant at the beginning of the growing season (end of the dormant 385 
period); (ii) maintain the water level of 5 -10 cm under the g round to favor the plant growth; 386 
(iii) prevent stagnation in filtration bed that can degrade the plants, and (iv) ensure a good 387 
brightness removing or thinning the trees canopy, if necessary. 388 
 Preventing clogging. Some recommendations: (i) install an oil separator (degreaser) to prevent 389 
flocculation, viscous layer and bulking and decrease solid waste floating (Perle et al. 1995; 390 
Vidal et al. 2000; Cammarota and Freire 2006) ; (ii) spread the wastewater over the entire 391 
width of the filtration bed; (iii) design inspection wells, in the proximity of the drainage pipes, 392 
accessible to air pumps/metal stick/brushes to allow de -clogging actions. If the system allows 393 
it (present a difference in ground level or it is raised compared to ground level) leave 394 
horizontal access to the pipe. If this is not possible, provide the drainage pipe with inspection 395 
manholes to insert an air pressure pump; (iv) design inlet and outlet zones as a transverse 396 
trench filled with stones ensuring that no wastewater is exposed to the atmosph ere; and (v) 397 
use coarse and lightweight material (such as plastic media) for the inlet part of filtration bed to 398 
get easier the cleaning and replacing if necessary (Tatoulis et al. 2017). 399 
Conclusions 400 
In the last decades, CWs have become increasingly common solutions for wastewater treatment 401 
of small communities in remote mountainous areas. Although they enhanced good performance 402 
in pollutants removal rate and had low impact on landscape. However, in co ld climates, low 403 
temperatures do not promote plant growth and reduced the removal performances of many 404 
pollutants. The literature review showed that the combination VSF+HSF filling the beds with sand 405 
and fine gravel guarantee a good performance also in col d climates. The plant that was largely 406 
used is P. australis in the previous studies. The monitoring results partially reinforced the outcome 407 
of the meta -analysis, observing a good treatment efficiency in the monitored sites also in harsh 408 
mountainous enviro nments (average concentration reductions were BOD=61%, COD=42%, 409 
NH4+=76%, PO 43=61%). The lack of available space forced the engineers to design systems 410 
composed of septic tank and horizontal subsurface beds. The substrate that balances the 411 
requested dimensioning size of the bed and the performance is the fine gravel with a moderate 412 
15 
 
percentage of zeolite that favours pollutant abatement. Moreover, a significant result was 413 
provided by the floristic -vegetational monitoring and the ecological indicators that sh owed as P. 414 
australis remains an excellent solution for most warm environments, but where the CW has to be 415 
integrated into natural and, sometimes, protected areas, planting D. cespitosa assurances similar 416 
removal performance and provides more eco -compatibility. Thus, D. cespitosa is a suitable 417 
phytoremediation species for the mountain environments of the Alps. In addition, the present 418 
study reinforced the importance of an ecological assessment of the plant communities, already 419 
present in the areas and able t o colonise the empty space, that has to be inserted in the design 420 
process as a sort of ecological design of the CW. Finally, combining literature and experience is 421 
possible to provide technical, engineering, and botanical measures to improve the design and 422 
maintenance of the CWs, to increase the wastewater treatment efficiency and exploit the multi -423 
functionality of these areas. 424
