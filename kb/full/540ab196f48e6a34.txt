Effect of organic and inorganic fertilizers on the 
growth, yield, nutrient uptake and economics in rice 
(Oryza sativa L.) 
 
Gautam Rana, Mandvi Srivastava and Chandan Singh 
 
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/26174693.2026.v10.i9Sl.9945 
 
Abstract 
The present investigation entitled Effect of Organic and Inorganic Fertilizers on the Growth, Yield, 
Nutrient Uptake and Economics in Rice ( Oryza sativa L.) was conducted at the Agriculture and 
Development Centre, Sakatpura, Vikrant University, Gwalior (Madhya Pradesh ) was during the winter 
of Kharif season of 2025 -26. The experiment was laid out into Randomized Block Design (RBD) with 
3 replications and following treatments viz., T₁ = Control, T₂ = 100% RDF (NPK), T₃ = Vermicompost 
@ 5.0 t ha⁻¹, T₄ = FYM @ 10.0 t ha⁻¹ T₅ = Compost @ 10.0 t ha⁻¹, T₆ = Poultry manure @ 5.0 t ha⁻¹, T₇ 
= 75% RDF + Vermicompost @ 5.0 t ha⁻¹ T₈ = 75% RDF + FYM @ 10.0 t ha⁻¹, T₉ = 75% RDF + 
Compost @ 10.0 t ha⁻¹ , T₁₀ = 75% RDF + Poultry manure @ 5.0 t ha⁻¹ and T₁₁ = 100% RDF + 
Vermicompost @ 5.0 t ha⁻¹. The result of study revealed that T₁₁ proved to be the most effective for 
improving crop performance. It recorded the highest plant height (137.2 cm at harvest), maximum 
tillers (15.1 hill⁻¹), highest Leaf Area Index (5.42) and dry matter accumulation (80.5 g hill⁻¹), 
indicating superior vegetative growth and biomass production. T₁₁ also produced the best yield 
attributes, with 2 8.2 cm panicle length, 3.95 g panicle weight, 26.8 g 1000 -grain weight and 346 
panicles m⁻², resulting from better nutrient availability and efficient assimilate translocation. Although 
the highest grain yield was obtained with T₈ (60.8 q ha⁻¹), it was clo sely followed by T₁₁ (59.3 q ha⁻¹) 
and T₉ (58.4 q ha⁻¹). T₁₁ also recorded the highest nutrient uptake (N 96.4, P 28.3, K 111.2 and total 
235.9 kg ha⁻¹) and the maximum benefit ratio (2.52), demonstrating its economic superiority. 
 
Keywords: Rice, inorganic fertilizer organic fertilizer and nutrient uptake 
 
Introduction 
Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is one of the most important cereal crops and serves as the staple food 
for more than half of the world's population. It occupies a pivotal position in global food 
security and plays a significant role in the socio -economic development of many develop ing 
countries, particularly in Asia . India is the second -largest producer of rice after China and 
accounts for a substantial share of global rice production. Rice contributes significantly to 
national food grain production and provides livelihood opportuni ties to millions of farmers 
across diverse agro-ecological regions. The increasing population and shrinking agricultural 
land resources have created immense pressure on agricultural systems to produce more food 
from limited land. To meet the growing demand for rice, farmers have increasingly relied on 
intensive cultivation practices involving high -yielding varieties, irrigation, and chemical 
fertilizers. Among these inputs, fertilizers play a crucial role in enhancing crop productivity 
by supplying essential nutrients required for plant growth and development (Dobermann and 
Fairhurst, 2000) [2, 3]. Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium are considered primary nutrients 
for rice cultivation and directly influence vegetative growth, tillering, grain formation, an d 
yield. The Green Revolution significantly increased rice productivity through extensive use 
of inorganic fertilizers. However, continuous and indiscriminate application of chemical 
fertilizers has led to several adverse effects, including nutrient imbala nce, decline in soil 
organic matter, deterioration of soil physical properties, reduced microbial activity, and 
environmental pollution (Ladha et al ., 2003) [6]. Long -term dependence on inorganic 
fertilizers alone often results in decreased fertilizer use efficiency and declining crop 
response, thereby threatening the sustainability of rice production systems. Organic fertilizers 
have gained considerable attention in recent years due to their beneficial effects on soil 
health and crop productivity.

Organic nutrient sources such as farmyard manure (FYM), 
compost, vermicompost, poultry manure, green manure, and 
crop residues contribute essential macro and micronutrients 
while improving soil structure and biological activity 
(Bhattacharyya et al ., 2015) [1]. Organic manures increase 
soil organic carbon content, improve water -holding 
capacity, enhance cation exchange capacity, and stimulate 
beneficial microbial populations r esponsible for nutrient 
cycling. I norganic fertilizers are concentrated sources of 
essential plant nutrients such as nitrogen (N), phosphorus 
(P), and potassium (K), which are readily available for crop 
uptake. In rice cultivation, the application of inorganic 
fertilizers promotes rapid vegetative growth, higher tillering, 
and increased grain yield. Commonly used inorganic 
fertilizers include urea, diammonium phosphate (DAP), and 
muriate of potash (MOP). Proper and balanced application 
of these fertilizers enhances nutrient -use efficiency a nd 
improves nutrient uptake by rice plants. However, excessive 
or imbalanced use may lead to soil degradation, nutrient 
losses, and environmental pollution. The application of 
organic fertilizers improves soil physical, chemical, and 
biological properties and promotes long -term sustainability 
of agricultural systems. Moreover, organic amendments 
facilitate slow and continuous nutrient release, thereby 
reducing nutrient losses through leaching and volatilization 
(Palm et al., 2001) [9, 10]. Several researche rs have reported 
that incorporation of organic manures significantly improves 
root growth, nutrient availability, and overall crop 
performance in rice -based cropping sys tems. Despite their 
numerous benefits, organic fertilizers alone may not 
adequately meet the nutrient requirements of high -yielding 
rice varieties due to their relatively low nutrient 
concentration and slow nutrient release patterns (Manna et 
al., 2005) [8]. Consequently, sole dependence on organic 
sources may result in lower yields during t he initial years of 
adoption. In contrast, inorganic fertilizers provide nutrients 
in readily available forms and support immediate crop 
growth and development. Therefore, the integrated use of 
organic and inorganic nutrient sources has emerged as a 
promising approach for achieving sustainable crop 
production and maintaining soil fertility (Mahajan et al ., 
2008) [7]. Integrated Nutrient Management (INM) involves 
the combined use of chemical fertilizers, organic manures, 
and biofertilizers to optimize nutrie nt availability, improve 
nutrient use efficiency, and sustain agricultural productivity. 
The concept of INM has gained importance because it 
balances immediate crop nutrient requirements with long -
term soil fertility maintenance . Studies have shown that 
integrated nutrient management enhances soil organic 
matter content, improves nutrient availability, and increases 
fertilizer use efficiency compared to the sole application of 
either or ganic or inorganic fertilizers . Growth parameters 
such as plant height, number of tillers, leaf area index, dry 
matter accumulation, and root development are significantly 
influenced by nutrient management practices. Adequate 
nutrient supply throughout the crop growth period promotes 
photosynthesis, cell division, and biomass accumulation, 
resulting in improved cr op growth and yield attributes . The 
combined application of organic and inorganic fertilizers 
has been reported to improve rice growth by ensuring a 
balanced supply of nutrients and enhancing soil conditions 
favourable for root prolife ration and nutrient absorption . 
Organic manures improve nutrient retention and microbial 
activity, whereas inorganic fertilizers provide readily 
available nutrients to plants. Their combined use often 
results in greater uptake of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, 
and micronutrients compa red to individual applications . 
Enhanced nutrient uptake not only improves productivity 
but also minimizes nutrient losses and environmental 
contamination. The economic feasibility of nutrient 
management prac tices is an important consideration for 
farmers. Although organic manures improve soil fertility 
and sustainability, their collection, processing, 
transportation, and application may involve additional costs 
and labor requirements. Inorganic fertilizers, w hile 
providing quick yield responses, have become increasingly 
expensive due to rising produ ction and transportation costs . 
Therefore, evaluating the economics of different fertilizer 
combinations is essential for identifying nutrient 
management practices that maximize net returns, gross 
returns, and benefit -cost ratio while maintaining long -term 
productivity. In recent years, increasing concerns regarding 
environmental degradation, soil fertility decline, and 
sustainable agricultural development have empha sized the 
need for balanced nutrient management strategies. 
Integrated application of organic and inorganic fertilizers 
has been recognized as an effective approach for improving 
rice growth, yield, nutrient uptake, soil fer tility, and farm 
profitability. However, the response of rice to different 
nutrient sources varies with soil type, climatic conditions, 
crop variety, and management practices. Therefore, 
location-specific studies are necessary to identify suitable 
nutrient management practices for sustai nable rice 
production. 
 
Methods and Materials 
The present experiment was conducted at the Agriculture 
Research and Development Centre, Sakatpura, Vikrant 
University, Gwalior (Madhya Pradesh ) was during the 
Kharif season of 2025 -26. The experiment was laid out into 
Randomized Block Design (RBD) with 3 replications and 
following treatments viz., T₁ = Control T₂ = 100% RDF 
(NPK), T₃ = Vermicompost @ 5.0 t ha⁻¹, T₄ = FYM @ 10.0 
t ha⁻¹ T₅ = Compost @ 10.0 t ha⁻¹ T₆ = Poultry manure @ 
5.0 t ha⁻¹, T₇ = 75% RDF + Vermicompost @ 5.0 t ha⁻¹ T₈ = 
75% RDF + FYM @ 10.0 t ha⁻¹ , T₉ = 75% RDF + Compost 
@ 10.0 t ha⁻¹ , T₁₀ = 75% RDF + Poultry manure @ 5.0 t 
ha⁻¹ and T₁₁ = 100% RDF + Vermicompost @ 5.0 t ha⁻¹. 
The experiment was conducted using rice ( Oryza sativa L.) 
as the test crop. The variety selected for the study was Pusa 
Basmati-1, which is a popular aromatic rice variety known 
for its grain quality and adaptability under suitable growing 
conditions. The crop was grown during the rainy (Kharif) 
season of 20 25. Rice seedlings were transplanted following 
a spacing of Row -to-row spacing : 20 cm, Plant to plant 
spacing10 cm and the observations were recorded on 
growth, yield attributes, yield, nutrient uptake, and 
economic parameters 
 
Results and Discussion 
Plant Height 
Plant height was significantly influenced by treatments. The 
highest height was recorded in T₁₁ (51.2 cm), followed by 
T₇, T₁₀ and T₈, while the lowest was in T₁ (38.2 cm). The 
superior performance of T₁₁ was attributed to better nutrient 
availability, especially nitrogen, which enhanced 
photosynthesis, cell elongation and early vegetative growth. 
Similar findings were reported by Yoshida (1981) [12],

Dobermann and Fairhurst (2000) [2, 3] , Fageria (2007) [4], 
Peng et al. (1999) [11] and IRRI (2013) [5]. 
Plant height ranged from 67.8 to 86.1 cm, with the 
maximum under T₁₁ (86.1 cm) and the minimum under T₁ 
(67.8 cm). Better nutrient uptake and sustained vegetative 
growth under T₁₁ promoted stem elongation and biomass 
accumulation, whereas poor nutrien t availability reduced 
growth in T₁. These results agree with Yoshida (1981) [12], 
Fageria (2007) [4], Dobermann and Fairhurst (2000) [2, 3] , 
Peng et al. (1999) [11] and IRRI (2013) [5]. 
Plant height varied from 101.6 to 129.5 cm, with T₁₁ (129.5 
cm) recor ding the highest value and T₁ (101.6 cm) the 
lowest. Continuous nutrient availability and efficient 
photosynthesis under T₁₁ resulted in greater biomass and 
plant vigour. These findings are in agreement with Yoshida 
(1981) [12], Dobermann and Fairhurst (20 00) [2, 3] , Fageria 
(2007) [4] and Peng et al. (1999) [11]. 
Plant height ranged from 109.8 to 137.2 cm, with the tallest 
plants under T₁₁ (137.2 cm) and the shortest under T₁ (109.8 
cm). Sustained nutrient uptake and efficient assimilate 
translocation unde r T₁₁ promoted greater plant height and 
biomass. Similar observations were reported by Yoshida 
(1981) [12], Fageria (2007) [4], Dobermann and Fairhurst 
(2000) [2, 3] and Peng et al. (1999) [11]. 
 
Number of Tillers hill⁻¹ 
The number of tillers ranged from 3.8 to 6.5 hill⁻¹, with the 
highest under T₁₁ (6.5 hill⁻¹) and the lowest under T₁ (3.8 
hill⁻¹). Better crop establishment and nitrogen availability 
under T₁₁ promoted early tiller formation. Similar results 
were reported by Yoshida (1981) [12], Dobermann and 
Fairhurst (2000) [2, 3] and Fageria (2007) [4]. 
Tiller number varied from 6.4 to 11.8 hill⁻¹, with the 
maximum under T₁₁ (11.8 hill⁻¹) and the minimum under T₁ 
(6.4 hill⁻¹). Balanced nutrient supply and improved 
photosynthesis enhanced tillering under T₁₁. These findings 
agree with Peng et al . (1999) [11], Fageria (2007) [4] and 
Dobermann and Fairhurst (2000) [2, 3]. 
Productive tillers ranged from 7.9 to 14.0 hill⁻¹, with T₁₁ 
(14.0 hill⁻¹) recording the highest and T₁ (7.9 hill⁻¹) the 
lowest values. Improved nutrient uptake and better tiller 
survival under T₁₁ resulted in higher productive tillers. 
Similar findings were reported by Yoshida (1981) [12], Peng 
et al. (1999) [11] and Fageria (2007) [4].The final number of 
tillers ranged from 8.6 to 15.1 hill⁻¹, with the highest under 
T₁₁ (15.1 hill⁻¹) and the lowest under T₁ (8.6 hill⁻¹). Better 
nutrient management and assi milate partitioning under T₁₁ 
maintained more productive tillers, contributing to higher 
yield potential. These findings are supported by Dobermann 
and Fairhurst (2000) [2, 3], Fageria (2007) [4] and Peng et al. 
(1999) [11]. 
 
Leaf Area Index (LAI) 
Leaf Area Index (LAI) is a key physiological parameter that 
reflects the photosynthetic capacity of the crop canopy and 
its ability to intercept solar radiation. In the present study, 
LAI at 90 DAT showed significant variation among 
treatments, ranging from 3.15 to 5.42, with the highest value 
recorded under T₁₁ (5.42) and the lowest under T₁ (3.15). 
The higher LAI under T₁₁ may be attributed to enhanced leaf 
development, increased tillering and better nutrient 
availability, particularly nitrogen, which plays a crucial role 
in chlorophyll synthesis and leaf expansion. A higher LAI 
indicates a greater photosynthetic surface area, leading to 
improved dry matter production and assimilate 
accumulation. In contrast, lower LAI under T₁ may be due 
to reduced vegetative growth and poor canopy development. 
Similar findings were reported by Fageria (2007) [4], who 
observed that improved nitrogen nutrition significantly 
increases leaf area development in rice. Yoshida (1981) [12] 
also reported that higher LAI enhances radiat ion 
interception and biomass production, ultimately improving 
yield potential. 
 
Dry Matter Accumulation 
Dry matter accumulation is an important indicator of overall 
crop growth and reflects the efficiency of photosynthesis 
and assimilate partitioning. In t he present study, dry matter 
accumulation at harvest ranged from 52.6 g to 80.5 g hill⁻¹, 
with the highest value under T₁₁ (80.5 g) and the lowest 
under T₁ (52.6 g). 
The increased dry matter accumulation under T₁₁ may be 
due to higher LAI, better nutrient uptake and improved 
physiological efficiency. Enhanced photosynthesis and 
translocation of assimilates from source to sink resulted in 
greater biomass production. Lower dry matter under T₁ may 
be associated with limited vegetative growth and reduced 
photosynthetic efficiency. 
These results are in agreement with Peng et al. (1999) [11], 
who reported that higher biomass production in rice is 
associated with improved canopy development and nutrient 
availability. Dobermann and Fairhurst (2000) [2, 3] also 
emphasized that balanced nutrient management enhances 
dry matter production and crop growth efficiency.

Yield attributes directly determine the final productivity of 
rice. In the present study, all yield components showed 
significant variation among treatments. The highest values 
were recorded under T₁₁, indicating its superiority in 
enhancing reproductive development. Panicle length ranged 
from 21.4-28.2 cm, Panicle weight ranged from 2.68-3.95 g, 
1000-grain weight ranged from 21.9 -26.8 g, Panicles m⁻² 
ranged from 245-346 
The superior performance of T₁₁ may be attributed to better 
nutrient uptake and enhanced photosynthetic activity during 
the reproductive phase, leading to improved grain formation 
and translocation of assimilates to developing panicles. A 
higher number of panicles m⁻² indicates better tiller 
productivity, while increased grain weight reflects efficient 
grain filling. 
Similar observations were reported by Fageria (2007) [4], 
who stated that yield attributes in rice are strongly 
influenced by nutrient management and crop vigour. 
Yoshida (1981) [12] also reported that improved source -sink 
balance enhances panicle development and grain filling. 
 
Yield (Grain Yield, Straw Yield and Harvest Index) 
Grain yield is the ultimate expression of crop performance 
and depends on the combined effect of growth and yield 
attributes. In the present study, grain yield ranged from 38.6 
to 60.8 q ha⁻¹, with the highest yield recorded under T₈ (60.8 
q ha⁻¹), followed closely by T₁₁ (59.3 q ha⁻¹) and T₉ (58.4 q 
ha⁻¹). 
Straw yield ranged from 56.2 to 79.8 q ha⁻¹, while harvest 
index varied from 40.7% to 43.2%, with maximum values 
under high-performing treatments.

The higher grain yield under T₈ and T₁₁ may be attributed to 
improved vegetative growth, higher LAI, better dry matter 
accumulation and superior yield attributes. Efficient 
partitioning of assimilates to grains during the reproductive 
stage resulted in higher productivit y. Lower yield under T₂ 
may be due to poor crop growth and reduced sink 
development. 
These findings are consistent with Peng et al . (1999) [11], 
who reported that grain yield in rice is strongly associated 
with biomass production and harvest index. Doberma nn and 
Fairhurst (2000) [2, 3] also emphasized that integrated 
nutrient management improves yield components and final 
productivity. 
 
Nutrient Uptake 
Nutrient uptake (N, P, K and total) increased significantly 
with improved treatments. The highest uptake was recorded 
under T₁₁, with nitrogen (96.4 kg ha⁻¹), phosphorus (28.3 kg 
ha⁻¹), potassium (111.2 kg ha⁻¹) and total uptake (235.9 kg 
ha⁻¹). 
The increased nutrient uptake under T₁₁ may be due to 
improved root growth, higher biomass production and better 
soil-plant nutrient interaction. Enhanced uptake of nutrients 
supports physiological processes such as photosynthesis, 
enzyme activity and assimilate production. 
Fageria (2007) [4] reported that nutrient uptake efficiency is 
directly related to root growth and soil fertility management. 
Dobermann and Fairhurst (2000) [2, 3] also observed that 
balanced fertilization improves nutrient absorption and crop 
productivity. 
 
Economics 
Economic analysis showed clear variation among 
treatments. The benefit ratio ranged from 0.95 to 2.52, with 
the highest value recorded under T₁₁ (2.52), followed by T₁₀ 
(2.41) and T₉ (2.30). The lowest B ratio was observed under 
T₁ (0.95). The higher econo mic returns under T₁₁ may be 
due to increased grain yield and higher gross returns, which 
outweighed the cost of cultivation. Treatments with better 
agronomic performance generally resulted in higher 
profitability. Similar findings were reported by Kumar et al. 
(2019), who observed that improved crop management 
practices significantly enhance net returns and B ratio in rice 
cultivation. Fageria (2007) [4] also emphasized that yield 
improvement directly contributes to economic profitability 
in rice-based systems.

Conclusion 
The present investigation revealed that the different 
treatments significantly influenced the growth, yield, 
nutrient uptake and economics of rice. Among all the 
treatments, T₁₁ proved to be the most effective for 
improving crop performance. It recorded th e highest plant 
height (137.2 cm at harvest), maximum tillers (15.1 hill⁻¹), 
highest Leaf Area Index (5.42) and dry matter accumulation 
(80.5 g hill⁻¹), indicating superior vegetative growth and 
biomass production.

T₁₁ also produced the best yield attribut es, with 28.2 cm 
panicle length, 3.95 g panicle weight, 26.8 g 1000 -grain 
weight and 346 panicles m⁻², resulting from better nutrient 
availability and efficient assimilate translocation. Although 
the highest grain yield was obtained with T₈ (60.8 q ha⁻¹), it 
was closely followed by T₁₁ (59.3 q ha⁻¹) and T₉ (58.4 q 
ha⁻¹). T₁₁ also recorded the highest nutrient uptake (N 96.4, 
P 28.3, K 111.2 and total 235.9 kg ha⁻¹) and the maximum 
benefit ratio (2.52), demonstrating its economic superiority. 
Overall, the study concluded that T₁₁ was the most efficient 
treatment for achieving higher growth, nutrient uptake, yield 
attributes and profitability, while T₈ produced the maximum 
grain yield. Therefore, T₁₁ can be recommended as the most 
suitable treatment for sust ainable and profitable rice 
production under the experimental conditions.
