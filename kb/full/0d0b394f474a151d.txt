Compliance and non‑compliance patterns among listed and non‑listed 
ship recycling facilities: a comparative assessment of 22 facilities 
evaluated for the EU List

Camila Aguiar Diniz1

Abstract
Ship recycling plays a central role in the circular economy of the maritime industry, yet significant disparities persist in safety, 
environmental performance, and regulatory compliance across global Ship Recycling Facilities (SRFs). This study analyzes 
22 official inspection reports conducted by DNV for the European Commission under the EU Ship Recycling Regulation 
(EU SRR 1257/2013), comparing listed and non-listed facilities. Using a structured qualitative framework, the findings 
suggest that differences between listed and non-listed facilities are reflected not only in overall compliance levels but also in 
the nature and distribution of non-conformities across regulatory dimensions. While most facilities showed relatively high 
levels of conformity in infrastructure-related requirements, more pronounced differences between the groups were observed 
in environmental monitoring, institutional governance, traceability systems, and intertidal zone control. Non-listed SRFs 
showed recurring deficiencies in environmental monitoring, documentation, emergency preparedness, and hazardous waste 
management, whereas listed facilities generally showed stronger governance and risk-management capabilities despite minor 
shortcomings. A sensitivity analysis excluding facilities employing dismantling methods prohibited under the EU SRR identi-
fied two non-listed facilities with performance profiles comparable to several listed SRFs, indicating that structural eligibil-
ity alone does not account for all observed differences between the groups. The results further suggest that environmental 
verification, traceability, and institutional governance are among the dimensions in which recurrent differences between the 
two groups were observed. These findings provide new insights into patterns associated with different listing outcomes and 
highlight areas that may support the modernization and regulatory alignment of ship recycling facilities.

contexts marked by weak regulatory frameworks, ship recy-

recover strategically valuable metal resources.
Historically, decisions regarding whether to extend a ves-
sel’s operating life or direct it to recycling have traditionally 
reflected economic considerations such as fluctuations in 
maritime transport markets, the availability of new vessels, 
and maintenance costs (Cairns 2014). Since the 1990 s, the 
sector has exhibited a markedly asymmetric geography: 
while most ships are operated by owners in the Global 
North, most dismantling activities occur in the Global South, 
particularly in India, Bangladesh, and Pakistan (Dewan and 
Sibilia 2024). India has accounted for a substantial share 
of the global volume of recycled vessels and employs tens 
of thousands of workers (Deshpande et al. 2013), whereas 
Bangladesh has become a strategic hub due to its low labor 
costs and strong domestic steel demand (Abdullah et al. 
2023). Shipbreaking yards in these regions also generate 
substantial quantities of e-waste owing to the presence of 
electronic equipment and components containing heavy met-
als and other hazardous substances on board vessels (Roy 
et al. 2022).
Despite the economic relevance of these centers, the liter-
ature consistently highlights persistent negative externalities, 
environmental contamination, exposure to toxic substances, 
and unsafe dismantling practices, particularly in beaching 
operations (Rahman et al. 2018). Although several develop-
ing countries benefit from low-cost steel supplied by the 
ship recycling industry (Kaiser 2008), increasing interna-
tional pressure since the late 1990 s has been accompanied 
by reforms aimed at ensuring minimum safety and environ-
mental standards (Rousmaniere and Raj 2007). In this con-
text, multilateral regulatory frameworks such as the Basel 
Convention established principles governing the transbound-
ary movement of hazardous wastes, requiring disposal only 
in facilities capable of ensuring “environmentally sound 
management” (Alter 2000; Moen 2008). The adoption of 
the Hong Kong International Convention for the Safe and 
Environmentally Sound Recycling of Ships (HKC 2009) rep-
resented further development in the international regulatory 
framework by outlining global guidelines for safe and envi-
ronmentally responsible ship dismantling, despite ongoing 
criticism regarding the permissibility of dismantling opera -
tions conducted with limited infrastructure (Ahmed 2020).
Even with these regulatory advances, ship recycling 
within Europe remains limited due to high operating costs 
and stringent environmental requirements (European Union, 
2023). To regulate the recycling of EU-flagged vessels and 
strengthen environmental and safety requirements, the Euro-
pean Union adopted the EU Ship Recycling Regulation (EU 
SRR No. 1257/2013), which established the European List 
of Ship Recycling Facilities (EU List), establishing that EU-
flagged ships may be recycled only at facilities included in 
the European List (European Commission, 2013). Inclusion 
in the EU List requires detailed technical inspection reports 
and independent audits evaluating infrastructure, envi-
ronmental conditions, hazardous waste control, and legal 
compliance (Solakivi et al. 2021). At present, the classi-
fication society DNV has conducted assessments of SRFs 
located outside the EU on behalf of the European Commis-
sion. Despite the regulatory importance of this mechanism, 
the scientific literature lacks systematic analyses of these 
reports, particularly concerning patterns observed between 
listed and non-listed facilities, the application of assessment 
criteria, and transparency in the decision-making process.
Given these gaps, this study addresses the following 
research question: Which technical, environmental, opera-
tional, and regulatory patterns documented in inspection 
reports are associated with different listing outcomes for 
SRFs evaluated for inclusion in the EU List?
To answer this question, the article conducts a compara-
tive analysis of inspection reports submitted by SRFs dur -
ing the EU List application process, aiming to identify: (i) 
the technical and regulatory dimensions that differentiate 
listed and non-listed facilities; (ii) potential differences in 
the application and documentation of assessment criteria; 
and (iii) implications for the global governance of ship recy-
cling. By establishing comparable analytical parameters, this 
study contributes to a better understanding of the patterns 
observed across EU List assessments and contributes to 
international debates on minimum environmental and safety 
standards for sustainable ship recycling. The findings also 
provide a basis for discussing regulatory compliance in ship 
recycling as involving institutional and managerial capabili-
ties in addition to technical requirements.
Theoretical background
Dismantling methods and operational implications
Understanding dismantling methods is essential for exam -
ining both operational practices and the risks associated 
with ship recycling. The literature identifies four main 
approaches: dry dock, alongside, landing, and beaching, each 
differing in terms of infrastructure requirements, operational 
Environmental Science and Pollution Research 
costs, degree of mechanization, and environmental control 
(Akter and Arifuzzaman 2023). These methods are unevenly 
distributed across the globe, reflecting economic and regula-
tory asymmetries between the Global North and the Global 
South.
– Dry dock: this approach has historically been adopted in 
Europe and has become increasingly prevalent in China 
(Solakivi et al. 2021). It involves conducting dismantling 
operations within controlled and contained areas, thereby 
reducing the potential for pollutant releases into the sur-
rounding environment (Xu et al. 2025). Compared with 
less contained dismantling practices, the use of dry docks 
provides more controlled operating conditions and may 
contribute to lower occupational accident risks (Steuer 
et al. 2021).
– Floating or alongside: this method is commonly adopted 
in China, the European Union, and the United States 
of America (Tola et al. 2023). It enables dismantling 
operations to be conducted under more controlled con-
ditions using cranes, thereby reducing the risk of pol-
lutant releases and accidental spills compared with the 
conventional beaching method (Sant’ Ana et al. 2026). 
Dismantling generally begins with the upper sections of 
the vessel and proceeds from top to bottom (Schøyen 
et al. 2017).
– Landing: this method is commonly used in Turkey and 
may offer advantages in terms of occupational safety and 
environmental control (Tola et al. 2023). In this method, 
ships are pushed or pulled onto a concrete slipway, which 
facilitates spill containment and subsequent clean-up 
operations. Typically, a mobile crane operating from the 
shore is used to remove sections of the hull and machin-
ery components from the vessel. The relatively small 
tidal range and improved access to the hull and working 
area may provide operational, safety, and environmen -
tal advantages over the conventional beaching method 
(ElMenshawy et al. 2024).
– Beaching: according to Kakar et al. (2021), ships are 
beached during high tide and subsequently dismantled 
manually at low tide. The dismantled materials are then 
cut into smaller sections and segregated in storage yards 
located along the beach. As dismantling operations 
are conducted directly along the shoreline, hazardous 
substances and wastes from end-of-life ships, includ-
ing asbestos, oily residues, and toxic metals, may leach 
into or be released directly into the marine environment. 
Beaching remains widely used in parts of South Asia, 
particularly in Bangladesh, India, and Pakistan, where 
its prevalence has been associated with lower operating 
costs, limited infrastructure, and historically less strin-
gent regulatory conditions (Mathew 2022).
Examining the dismantling methods adopted by SRFs is 
important for interpreting differences in structural eligibility 
and compliance patterns under EU regulations. Although 
permitted under the Hong Kong Convention, beaching is 
not considered compatible with the requirements applicable 
to the recycling of EU-flagged vessels under the European 
Ship Recycling Regulation (European Commission, 2013) 
particularly because of concerns related to environmental 
containment, worker safety, and operational control (Dewan 
and Sibilia 2024). Accordingly, dismantling methods con-
stitute an important dimension of structural eligibility when 
comparing listed and non-listed SRFs and are among the 
aspects in which differences between listed and non-listed 
facilities were observed across the inspection reports.
Health, safety, and environmental (HSE) conditions
Ship dismantling activities in several developing-country 
contexts are associated with substantial risks to occupa-
tional health and environmental integrity (Rahman et al. 
2018; Rousmaniere and Raj 2007). Workers frequently 
face long working hours, low wages, and insufficient train-
ing, often without adequate personal protective equipment 
(Sonak et al. 2008). The handling of hazardous substances, 
such as asbestos, PCBs, heavy metals, and hydrocarbons, has 
also been reported in contexts where adequate containment 
structures or formalized waste-disposal systems are limited 
or absent (Misra 2019).
Empirical studies conducted in Bangladesh and other 
South Asian countries have reported significant contamina-
tion of marine sediments, coastal waters, and locally culti-
vated food products, raising concerns regarding potential 
public health impacts (Deshpande et al. 2012; Hasan et al. 
2020). Complementary evidence from Chinese shipbreaking 
yards suggests that inadequate training procedures and limi-
tations in management systems are associated with higher 
occupational accident rates (Zhou et al. 2023b).
Despite the breadth of documented hazards, relatively 
few studies examine how HSE-related concerns are incor -
porated into the technical criteria applied by the European 
Union when assessing SRFs for inclusion in the European 
List. Requirements related to hazardous waste management, 
emergency preparedness, International Organization for 
Standardization (ISO) certifications, and exposure control 
constitute important components of the EU List assessment 
framework (European Union, 2016). Discrepancies between 
practices commonly reported in Global South facilities and 
the regulatory requirements applied under the EU frame-
work are frequently observed in EU inspection reports and 
are among the areas in which differences between listed 
and non-listed facilities are observed. Commonly reported

non-conformities include the lack of impermeable work -
ing surfaces, inadequate procedures for handling hazardous 
waste, insufficient formal training, weak effluent contain -
ment systems, and the absence of continuous environmental 
monitoring.
Although multilateral instruments such as the Basel Con-
vention and the Hong Kong Convention establish norma-
tive principles for environmentally sound ship recycling, 
their implementation remains uneven across jurisdictions 
(Ahmed 2020; Moen 2008). As a result, balancing economic 
viability, environmental protection, and occupational safety 
continues to be one of the central challenges shaping the 
global ship recycling sector (Glinski 2022; Khan et al. 2012; 
Schulz et al. 2025).
Efforts to improve infrastructure and management 
systems
In recent years, several SRFs in India and Bangladesh have 
undertaken substantial investments to upgrade infrastruc-
ture, management systems, and certification processes, 
reflecting efforts to improve alignment with international 
standards (Mehtaj et al. 2024). These initiatives include the 
construction of impermeable cutting surfaces, the installa -
tion of controlled drainage and containment systems, the 
implementation of formal hazardous-waste management 
plans, and the adoption of mechanized handling equipment 
aimed at reducing occupational risks (Dipto et al. 2023).
Parallel to these infrastructural upgrades, several SRFs 
have sought internationally recognized certifications, such 
as ISO14001, ISO 9001, and ISO 45001, and have obtained 
Statements of Compliance issued by classification socie -
ties, including DNV, ClassNK, and RINA, in relation to 
the requirements of the Hong Kong Convention (Gunbeyaz 
et al. 2019). In India, particularly in Alang, these develop-
ments have included the modernization of dismantling areas, 
with paved working surfaces and hazardous-waste treatment 
facilities. Similarly, in Bangladesh, some yards have begun 
replacing manual cutting operations with mechanized pro-
cesses, representing incremental efforts toward safer and 
more controlled working conditions (Schulz et al. 2025).
Although these developments demonstrate ongoing efforts 
to improve ship recycling practices, their broader implica-
tions for the global ship recycling market remain uncertain. 
On the one hand, the upgrades may enhance the international 
competitiveness of certain SRFs by offering safer operational 
environments while maintaining comparatively lower labour 
and infrastructure costs. On the other hand, gaps remain in 
relation to several requirements of EU SRR No. 1257/2013, 
particularly those concerning containment, traceability, and 
governance requirements applicable to the EU List assessment 
process. These remaining gaps are observed within a broader 
context of structural and economic constraints that may pose 
challenges to achieving full compliance.
Taken together, these investments suggest a gradual 
trend toward greater alignment with international regula-
tory requirements, indicating that some SRFs in South Asia 
may, in the long term, move closer to meeting the opera-
tional and environmental standards established under the 
EU regulatory framework. However, structural challenges, 
including limited capital availability, governance limitations, 
and infrastructural deficits, may continue to pose challenges 
to achieving full alignment (Dipto et al. 2023).
Methodology
Qualitative research methods are widely used to generate 
in-depth understanding of complex phenomena, particularly 
when processes, decisions, and interactions require contex-
tual interpretation (Lim 2024). Qualitative designs may be 
guided by a conceptual framework, suggesting a predomi -
nantly deductive orientation, or may be driven by patterns 
identified within the data (Mihas 2023). Such approaches 
are particularly useful in naturalistic settings where human 
interactions, institutional behaviours, and contextual dynam-
ics are relevant to the interpretation of observed phenomena 
(Delgado Hito and Romero García, 2021).
In this study, an archival research strategy was employed 
for qualitative data collection. Archival methods encompass 
a wide range of pre-existing documents and records, which 
can serve as valuable sources of evidence for researchers 
examining formal procedures, institutional practices, and 
regulatory processes (Lim 2024). The use of archival docu-
ment analysis was considered appropriate given the nature of 
the phenomenon under investigation, an international regula-
tory process based on technical audits, and the need to exam-
ine formal evidence produced by independent classification 
societies. These sources support the identification of patterns 
associated with different EU List outcomes, the examination 
of criteria documented in the evaluations, and the interpre-
tation of differences observed across the assessed facilities, 
which are relevant to addressing the research question.
Data collection
Data were collected on 01 July 2025 from official inspection 
reports published by the European Commission. The final 
dataset consisted of 22 inspection reports, corresponding to 
22 SRFs, each documenting technical, environmental, and 
operational requirements considered in the assessment of 
SRFs located outside the European Union. These reports

were prepared by DNV as part of on-site assessments con-
ducted on behalf of the European Commission under EU 
SRR No. 1257/2013. Although the analysis relied on inspec-
tion reports prepared by a single classification society, the 
comparative approach focused on cross-case patterns and 
recurring assessment criteria, while the potential limitations 
associated with a single-source dataset were acknowledged.
During the data screening process, an inconsistency was 
identified in the document Final Report Second Inspection 
Request 003 V2. The downloaded file did not correspond to the 
Turkish facility LEYAL DEMTAŞ GEMI SÖKÜM SANAYİ 
VE TİCARET A.Ş. but instead referred to an Indian SRF. As 
the correct version of the document could not be retrieved from 
the available source, the corresponding facility was excluded 
from the comparative analysis, and this issue was acknowl-
edged as a methodological limitation. This decision was made 
to avoid introducing information that did not correspond to the 
facility under assessment into the dataset.

Comparative analysis and parameter
A comparative assessment of listed and non-listed SRFs 
was conducted across the predefined analytical categories. 
The assessment was carried out by the authors based on the 
inspection reports available for each facility, applying the 
same final set of analytical parameters across all cases. The 
classifications were reviewed and discussed by the research-
ers to promote consistency in the interpretation of the docu-
mentary evidence.
The “not validated” (NV) classification was assigned by 
the researchers when the information required to assess a spe-
cific subcategory was absent, insufficient, unclear, or could 
not be reliably verified in the publicly available facility report. 
Each report was systematically reviewed, and whenever suf-
ficient evidence was available, the corresponding subcategory 
assessment was classified as compliant (C), non-compliant 
(NC), or partially compliant (PC). NV therefore represents 
insufficient evidence for verification rather than a confirmed 
compliance status and should not be interpreted as equivalent 
to NC. In the context of the EU List assessment process, in 
which documentary evidence is used to verify compliance 
with applicable requirements, such documentary gaps were 
considered relevant to the comparative analysis.
For this reason, NV was retained as a separate classifi-
cation in the quantitative assessment and included in the 
overall distribution of classifications, so that C, PC, NC, and 
NV classifications collectively represent 100% of the sub-
category-level observations. NV percentages were therefore 
interpreted as reflecting limitations in the completeness and 
verifiability of the available evidence rather than confirmed 
operational non-compliance.
For each analytical category and facility group, the abso-
lute frequencies of compliant (C), non-compliant (NC), 
partially compliant (PC), and not validated (NV) classifi-
cations were aggregated across the corresponding subcat-
egories. For each subcategory, NI represents the number 
of facilities evaluated, while ΣNI represents the sum of the 
facilities evaluated across all subcategories within the cor -
responding analytical category and facility group. Thus, 
ΣNI corresponds to the total number of subcategory-level 
observations included in the calculation. Percentage values 
were subsequently calculated as the proportion of each clas-
sification relative to this total. The formulas used for these

Research limitations
This study is subject to limitations associated with its exclu-
sive reliance on publicly available documents. These include 
(i) the lack of access to non-public documentation from the 
European Commission; (ii) gaps or redacted information in 
reports concerning non-listed SRFs; (iii) variations in struc-
ture and level of detail across documents; and (iv) occasional 
inconsistencies, such as the previously described document 
inconsistency. Nevertheless, the available documentation 
provided a consistent basis for examining recurring patterns 
across the assessed listed and non-listed facilities.

Results
Among the 22 SRFs included in the comparative assess -
ment, 12 (54.5%) were included in the European List and 10 
(45.5%) were non-listed, as presented in Table 2. Variations 
in the distribution of C, PC, NC, and NV classifications were 
observed across the analytical categories and between the 
two groups.
Figure  1 summarizes the distribution of classifications 
across the two groups of SRFs. Considering all analytical 
categories, compliant classifications accounted for 90.26% of 
the aggregated subcategory-level observations among listed 
facilities and 82.59% among non-listed facilities. Examina-
tion at the category level showed that the magnitude of the 
differences between listed and non-listed facilities varied 
across the assessed dimensions.
Geographical differences were also observed in the distribu-
tion of the assessed facilities. Listed SRFs were predominantly 
located in Turkey, the United Kingdom (UK), and the United 
States of America (USA), whereas non-listed SRFs were con-
centrated mainly in India and Turkey. Across the listed facili-
ties, the inspection reports documented varying degrees of 
mechanization, paved and controlled working areas, and ISO 
certifications. Among non-listed facilities, beaching-based dis-
mantling methods were more frequently documented, while 
differences were also observed in environmental monitoring, 
HSE practices, and performance indicators.
Figure 1 presents the distribution of classifications across 
the analytical categories for listed and non-listed facilities. 
Figure 1a presents the proportions of C classifications, while 
Fig. 1b, c, and d present the corresponding proportions of 
NC, PC, and NV classifications, respectively. In all pan-
els, the blue bars represent the classification proportions 
for listed facilities, whereas the grey bars represent those 
for non-listed facilities. The category-level comparison 
showed that the magnitude and distribution of differences 
varied across the assessed dimensions. Non-listed facilities 
show higher percentages of NC, PC, or NV classifications 
in specific categories, particularly Health, Safety and Envi-
ronmental Performance, Indicators and Monitoring, and 
Hazardous Waste and Materials Management. NV classifi-
cations, however, should be interpreted as limitations in the 
available documentary evidence rather than as confirmed 
non-compliance.
Figure 2 presents the distribution of dismantling methods 
documented among the assessed SRFs. Beaching/intertidal 
dismantling was the most frequently documented approach 
(36.4%), followed by ramp or slipway landing (31.8%). Com-
bined methods accounted for 18.2% of the assessed facilities, 
while dry-dock and floating dismantling represented 9.1% 
and 4.5%, respectively.
Comparison by EU List status showed differences in the 
distribution of dismantling methods among the assessed facili-
ties (Table 2 and Fig. 2). Among the 12 listed facilities, ramp/
slipway-based, floating, and combined dismantling approaches 
were more frequently documented, while dry-dock dismantling 
was documented for both assessed UK facilities. Among the 
10 non-listed facilities, beaching or intertidal dismantling was 
more frequently documented, particularly in India, where all 
six assessed facilities employed some form of intertidal land-
ing, stranding, grounding, or beaching. A similar pattern was 
observed among the assessed Turkish facilities: listed Turk-
ish facilities primarily employed ramp/slipway or combined 
floating and land-based approaches, whereas beaching was 
documented for two of the four non-listed Turkish facilities.
These findings suggest that infrastructure-supported dis-
mantling methods were more frequently documented among 
the listed facilities assessed in this study. Nevertheless, the 
observed differences should not be interpreted as evidence that 
dismantling method alone explains the EU List status of the 
assessed facilities, as the EU assessment framework encom-
passes multiple technical, environmental, safety, and opera-
tional requirements. Dry-dock, floating, and infrastructure-
supported land-based operations may provide greater potential 
for the containment of wastes and effluents and for the manage-
ment of occupational safety risks, although the higher capi-
tal requirements associated with these approaches may limit 
their adoption in some ship-recycling contexts (Devault et al. 
2017). These operational characteristics are relevant to the EU 
regulatory framework, which establishes requirements con-
cerning effluent containment, impermeable working surfaces, 
hazardous-material segregation and management, and adequate 
mechanical infrastructure (European Union, 2023).

Management and Organizational Structure (MOS)
This category encompasses three subcategories: recycling 
facility plan; functional organizational chart and allocation 
of responsibilities; and ISO certifications. In this category, 
differences in the distribution of classifications between 
listed and non-listed facilities were more apparent (Fig.  1). 
The aggregated MOS subcategory classifications showed a 
compliant classification rate of 97.22% for listed facilities 
and 83.33% for non-listed facilities, while 13.33% of the 
classifications for non-listed facilities were categorized as 
NV (Fig. 1a and d).
The recurring shortcomings observed in non-listed facili-
ties relate to the absence, incomplete availability, or limited 
verifiability of the recycling facility plan, shortcomings 
related to the functional organizational chart and allocation 
of responsibilities, and insufficient evidence to verify the 
reported ISO certifications, such as ISO 9001, ISO 14001, 
ISO 45001, and ISO 30000.
Several cases exemplify these issues. The SRF evaluated 
in Application 07 (India) presented partially redacted docu-
mentation, insufficient evidence to verify the organizational 
structure, and insufficient evidence to verify ISO certifica-
tions. In the case of two Turkish facilities (Applications 49 
and 51), different classification outcomes were observed 
regarding formal certification. For Application 49, the avail-
able documentation was insufficient to verify formal certifi-
cation, resulting in an NV classification. In contrast, Appli-
cation 51 was classified as NC because the documented 
evidence indicated that the required formal certification was 
not in place.
Health, Safety and Environmental Performance 
(HSE)
The HSE category comprised the following subcategories: 
emergency response plan; personal protective equipment; 
soil environmental monitoring systems; water environ-
mental monitoring systems; air environmental monitoring 
systems; noise safety monitoring systems; intertidal zone 
control; regular medical surveillance; and training monitor-
ing and documentation. As shown in Fig. 1, C classifications 
accounted for 88.89% of the aggregated HSE subcategory-
level observations among listed facilities and 63.33% among 
non-listed facilities. PC classifications accounted for 11.11% 
and 21.11%, respectively, while NC classifications were not 
observed among listed facilities but accounted for 15.56% 
of the observations among non-listed facilities.
A facility-level examination of the underlying classifica-
tions showed that five listed SRFs received C classifications 
across all assessed HSE subcategories (Applications 26, 32, 
34, 42, and 14). The remaining seven listed facilities presented 
at least one PC classification, particularly in relation to soil 
environmental monitoring. Among the non-listed SRFs, only 
Applications 40 and 56 received C classifications across all 
assessed HSE subcategories, whereas the remaining eight pre-
sented one or more NC, PC, or NV classifications. Application 
07 illustrates the different classifications observed across the 
HSE subcategories. Soil and noise monitoring, as well as inter-
tidal zone control, were classified as non-compliant, while air 
monitoring and regular medical surveillance were classified as 
partially compliant. In contrast, the emergency response plan, 
personal protective equipment, water monitoring, and training 
monitoring and documentation were classified as compliant.
Recycling Operations (RO)
The RO category comprised four subcategories: ship recep-
tion process; Ship Recycling Plan (SRP); cutting and safety 
procedures; and access and movement control. Based on the 
inspection reports, the ship reception process was generally 
organized into two main stages. The first stage, preparation 
and pre-arrival documentation, involved contract analysis, 
verification of acceptance criteria, review of the Inventory of 
Hazardous Materials (IHM), preparation or updating of the 
SRP, verification of flag documentation, and pre-cleaning 
procedures. The second stage, arrival and securing of the 
Environmental Science and Pollution Research 
vessel, included safe berthing, initial safety inspections, and 
authorization to initiate dismantling activities.
As shown in Fig.  1, C classifications accounted for 
100% of the aggregated RO subcategory-level observations 
among listed facilities and 92.5% among non-listed facili-
ties. Among non-listed facilities, NC and NV classifications 
accounted for 2.5% and 5.0% of the aggregated RO subcat-
egory-level observations, respectively. At the facility level, 
the ship reception process received a C classification for all 
assessed SRFs, irrespective of EU List status.
Greater variation was observed in the access and move-
ment control subcategory. Some SRFs employed access-
control systems that included continuous surveillance, cam-
eras, identification cards, and, in some cases, QR-code-based 
management systems. Other facilities relied on simpler 
access-control procedures, such as manual sign-in sheets. 
At the Turkish facility Dortel Gemi Sokum, the inspection 
report considered this procedure insufficient because of its 
susceptibility to manipulation and limited traceability.
Hazardous Waste and Materials Management 
(HWM)
The HWM category comprised the following subcategories: 
treatment of hazardous waste; treatment of non-hazardous 
waste; documentation for waste traceability; temporary 
storage; and transport oversight and tracking. As shown in 
Fig. 1, C classifications accounted for 86.67% and 84.00% of 
the aggregated HWM subcategory-level observations among 
listed and non-listed facilities, respectively, while NC clas-
sifications accounted for 13.33% and 16.00%.
The qualitative examination of the inspection reports 
provided additional context for these classification patterns. 
Among listed SRFs, the final treatment of hazardous and 
non-hazardous waste was frequently outsourced to third-
party operators, while on-site waste-management activities 
were primarily related to temporary storage and material 
segregation.
Outsourced waste treatment was documented among 
both listed and non-listed facilities and was therefore not 
restricted to either group in the assessed sample. Differences 
were instead observed in the documentation and verifiability 
of waste flows, destinations, traceability mechanisms, and 
arrangements with external operators. Among non-listed 
facilities, documented shortcomings were more frequently 
related to incomplete waste-flow records and limited evi-
dence regarding waste destinations and subsequent treatment 
arrangements.
Indicators and Monitoring (IM)
The IM category comprised three subcategories: record of 
accidents, incidents, and occupational diseases; progress 
reports (internal or client-mandated); and performance indi-
cators (KPIs). As shown in Fig.  1, the IM category exhib-
ited a mixed distribution of C, NC, PC, and NV classifica-
tions across listed and non-listed facilities. C classifications 
accounted for 61.11% of the aggregated IM subcategory-
level observations among listed facilities and 66.67% among 
non-listed facilities. NC classifications accounted for 30.56% 
and 20.00%, respectively, while PC classifications repre-
sented 5.56% and 13.33%. NV classifications accounted for 
2.78% of the observations among listed facilities, whereas no 
NV classifications were recorded among non-listed facilities. 
Unlike the patterns observed in some other analytical cate-
gories, the aggregated IM results did not show a consistently 
higher proportion of C classifications or a lower proportion 
of NC classifications among listed facilities.
At the facility level, several listed SRFs did not present 
fully structured KPI systems or formalized progress-report-
ing mechanisms, with some inspection reports documenting 
practices such as near-miss reporting and brief safety meet-
ings rather than comprehensive KPI and progress-reporting 
systems. In other cases, safety and performance management 
frameworks were documented, but the available evidence 
lacked sufficient standardization or detail to support verifica-
tion of the corresponding parameters.
Among non-listed SRFs, some monitoring systems were 
reported as being under implementation, with the inspection 
reports documenting limitations related to the definition of 
objectives, assignment of responsibilities, and integration 
of monitoring information into operational processes. These 
facility-level observations are consistent with the mixed 
aggregated pattern shown in Fig. 1, in which both listed and 
non-listed facilities presented limitations across different IM 
subcategories. Overall, the IM category did not show a con-
sistent pattern of more favourable classifications among listed 
facilities. Instead, the observed results reflected variation in 
the degree of formalization and documentation of monitoring 
and performance-management practices across the assessed 
facilities. More broadly, the direction and magnitude of the 
differences between listed and non-listed facilities varied 
across the analytical categories, with more pronounced dif-
ferences observed in dimensions such as HSE and MOS.
Heat map
A heat map was developed to visually summarize the clas-
sification patterns across the assessed SRFs (Fig.  3). The 
figure shows the distribution of C, PC, NC, and NV clas-
sifications across the analytical categories (LAC, PI, MOS, 
HSE, RO, HWM, and IM), allowing visual comparison of 
patterns across listed and non-listed facilities. The classifi-
cation patterns shown in Fig.  3 were not restricted to tech-
nical and infrastructure-related dimensions but were also 
observed across governance, environmental monitoring, 
 Environmental Science and Pollution Research
documentation, and control-related subcategories. How -
ever, the direction and magnitude of these differences var -
ied across categories, and limitations in IM-related param-
eters were observed in both listed and non-listed facilities. 
Accordingly, the heat map should be interpreted as a visual 
synthesis of the classification patterns documented across 
the assessed SRFs rather than as evidence that any individual 
criterion independently explains EU List status.
The heat map analysis (Fig.  3) shows differences in the 
distribution of conformities and non-conformities between 
listed and non-listed facilities. Although each SRF is 
assessed individually, geographical differences in the dis -
tribution of conformities and non-conformities are apparent 
across the assessed facilities, suggesting potential variation 
across national and institutional contexts.
Notably, deficiencies in IM are distributed across both 
listed and non-listed facilities, suggesting that this category 
does not show a consistent pattern of differentiation between 
listed and non-listed facilities when considered in isolation. 
Instead, IM-related deficiencies are also observed among 
listed facilities that demonstrate stronger performance in 
other categories such as environmental governance, trace-
ability systems, and operational risk control. This pattern 
suggests that IM should be interpreted in conjunction with 
other dimensions rather than as an isolated indicator of dif-
ferences between the two groups.
The distribution of non-conformities across the heat map 
indicates that some categories differentiate the two groups. 
Persistent deficiencies in environmental monitoring, inter -
tidal zone control, and governance systems were more 
frequently observed among non-listed facilities, whereas 
isolated shortcomings in KPI systems or reporting struc-
tures were observed in both groups and therefore did not 
consistently differentiate listed from non-listed facilities. 
These results suggest that the magnitude and consistency of 
the observed differences vary across analytical categories.
The results show that facilities included in the EU List 
did not necessarily exhibit complete conformity across all 
assessed dimensions. Rather, listed facilities could present 
deficiencies in some dimensions while maintaining stronger 
evidence of continuous management and verification of 
operational and environmental risks.
Within the assessed sample, non-listed SRFs were pri -
marily located in India and Turkey and exhibited non-
conformities across several assessed dimensions, includ -
ing HSE, HWM, and IM (Hsuan and Parisi 2020; Ignacio 
Alcaide et al. 2017). These facilities were frequently clas-
sified as “NV”, reflecting documentary gaps or insufficient 
verifiable evidence in the available reports.
These limitations were observed not only in relation to 
physical infrastructure but also in environmental govern -
ance, traceability mechanisms, and risk management prac-
tices, consistent with previous findings (Patra et al. 2023; 
Tola et al. 2024). However, gaps remained in several areas 
relevant to EU assessment requirements.
In the specific case of the assessed Indian facilities, recent 
improvements were observed, including the installation 
of impermeable flooring, expansion of cutting areas, and 
engagement of accredited laboratories (Devaux and Nicolaï, 
2020; Ignacio Alcaide et al. 2017). Significant gaps persist

in environmental monitoring, training management, opera-
tional documentation, and institutional capacity to integrate 
indicators into decision-making. Overall, the inspection

sistencies, and insufficient verifiable evidence across these

In contrast, Turkish facilities included in the European 
List exhibited a different performance profile. Despite 
residual weaknesses, particularly in environmental aspects

The assessed SRFs located in the UK and USA exhib-

Although occasional shortcomings in monitoring indica-
tors were observed, these facilities were characterized by 
consolidated infrastructure, established safety mechanisms, 
reliable traceability systems, and consistent evidence of legal 
compliance. Taken together, despite a high compliance rate 
of 86.4% across the aggregated subcategory classifications, 
the results show that differences between listed and non-
listed facilities are not uniformly distributed across the ana-
lytical categories. Non-conformities are concentrated in a 
small group of categories (HSE, HWM, and IM), although 
their frequency and capacity to differentiate listed and non-
listed facilities vary across these categories.
The integrated analysis indicates that differences between 
listed and non-listed SRFs are not fully captured by average 
performance and are also reflected in institutional systems 
related to traceability, pollution control, and continuous, 
verifiable risk management. In summary, while infrastruc-
ture-related differences remain relevant to the assessment, 
environmental governance is also among the dimensions in 
which differences between listed and non-listed facilities 
were observed.
Sensitivity analysis excluding structurally 
prohibited dismantling methods
A sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding facili-
ties employing dismantling methods prohibited under EU 
SRR No. 1257/2013. The results showed that some of the 
differences initially observed between listed and non-listed 
facilities were reduced after these facilities were excluded 
from the analysis. Notably, Applications 40 and 56, which 
were not included in the European List, exhibited compli -
ance profiles broadly comparable to those of several listed 
SRFs across the remaining assessed dimensions once the 
dismantling-method criterion was excluded from the anal-
ysis. These results suggest that the observed differences 
between listed and non-listed facilities cannot be interpreted 
solely based on cumulative compliance performance or gov-
ernance-related dimensions.
After the exclusion of facilities employing structurally 
prohibited dismantling methods, differences in environmen-
tal monitoring, traceability, governance, and risk-control 
systems nevertheless remained evident between the two 
groups. Thus, the sensitivity analysis indicates that the 
observed assessment patterns encompass both dismantling-
method requirements and differences across governance-
related dimensions, without implying that any single dimen-
sion independently determines listing outcomes.
Discussion
The results suggest that EU List outcomes reflect different pat-
terns of conformity and non-conformity across the assessed 
dimensions, with persistent failures in environmental monitor-
ing and governance more frequently observed among non-listed 
facilities, despite relatively high overall compliance levels. This 
research indicates that differences between listed and non-listed 
SRFs are not fully captured by the total number of fulfilled 
requirements but are also reflected in the distribution of non-
conformities, particularly within the categories of governance, 
environmental monitoring, and safety systems. This conclusion 
aligns with previous research showing that international regula-
tory frameworks, such as the EU SRR No. 1257/2013, assess 
not only the physical infrastructure of facilities but also their 
institutional capacity to manage risks in a systematic, docu-
mented, and verifiable manner (European Union, 2023).
The analysis further suggests that environmental govern-
ance is one of the dimensions associated with differences 
between listing outcomes. The assessed SRFs located in 
India and Turkey exhibited notable documentary deficien-
cies, including the absence of functional organizational 
charts, incomplete ISO certifications, and inconsistencies 
in Ship Recycling Facility Plans (SRFPs). The literature 
emphasizes that institutional maturity is essential for ensur-
ing occupational safety and full traceability of hazardous 
waste management (Misra 2019; Rahman et al. 2018).
This research also raises important questions regarding 
the growing dependence of contemporary regulatory systems 
on documentary evidence as a proxy for operational safety 
and environmental performance. Although listed facilities 
generally demonstrated stronger governance structures, cer-
tification systems, and traceability mechanisms, the results 
do not necessarily imply that documented compliance fully 
reflects the functioning of industrial operations. As argued 
 Environmental Science and Pollution Research
by Dekker (2014) in Bureaucratization of Safety, complex 
management systems may progressively prioritize the produc-
tion of auditable documentation over the effective manage-
ment of operational risks, creating a potential gap between 
formal compliance and practical safety performance. In this 
respect, the stronger performance observed among facili-
ties located in the UK and USA may partially reflect their 
longer-standing institutional experience in managing complex 
bureaucratic and certification systems rather than exclusively 
superior operational conditions. This finding suggests that the 
current regulatory trend toward increasingly documentation-
intensive auditing frameworks should be critically examined, 
particularly in highly dynamic industrial settings such as ship 
recycling, where operational realities may not always be fully 
captured through formal records alone.
Nevertheless, the study does not suggest that documentation 
systems are unnecessary. Rather, the findings indicate that doc-
umentary compliance should be interpreted as one component 
of a broader institutional capability framework that must also 
include operational verification, environmental performance 
consistency, and adaptive risk management capacity.
A second key finding concerns environmental monitoring, 
where differences between listed and non-listed facilities were 
observed. Widespread failures in soil, water, air, and noise 
monitoring were observed among non-listed SRFs. This pat-
tern is consistent with studies showing that even when structural 
improvements are implemented in Alang and Chittagong, the 
monitoring of critical environmental parameters remains incom-
plete or inconsistent (Hasan et al. 2020; Schulz et al. 2025). In 
the present analysis, the absence or insufficiency of environ-
mental data was frequently observed among non-listed facilities, 
including facilities with otherwise reasonable infrastructure.
Another notable finding concerns the management of the 
intertidal zone. Among the assessed facilities, SRFs primar-
ily employing beaching or landing methods in tropical coastal 
regions exhibited higher numbers of non-conformities, par-
ticularly those related to spill control, metallic debris, and oily 
runoff. The environmental vulnerability of the intertidal zone is 
well documented in the literature, which identifies this area as 
particularly susceptible to severe and persistent environmental 
impacts (Devault et al. 2017; Dewan and Sibilia 2024). Research 
conducted in Bangladesh, for instance, highlights chronic con-
tamination of sediments and coastal biota by heavy metals (Cu, 
Pb, Cd), organotin compounds, and microcontaminants, with 
impacts that are difficult to mitigate without adequate physical 
containment (Rahman et al. 2018; Roy et al. 2022).
Additionally, the results show that infrastructure, while 
relevant, does not clearly differentiate listed and non-listed 
facilities when considered in isolation. High levels of con -
formity in “Design and Infrastructure” across both groups 
indicate that physical improvements have become wide-
spread, a trend already observed in recent studies on the 
gradual modernization of shipyards in Turkey and India 
(Abdullah et al. 2023; Akter and Arifuzzaman 2023). Nev -
ertheless, the results indicate that such improvements may 
coexist with shortcomings in governance and in the capacity 
to demonstrate sustained compliance over time.
The results suggest an apparent paradox: several listed 
SRFs also exhibit shortcomings in IM. However, the qualita-
tive analysis suggests that these deficiencies follow different 
patterns across the two groups. In listed facilities, monitor -
ing activities tend to be varied but substantive, with records 
of near misses, internal audits, and corrective procedures. 
In non-listed facilities, by contrast, monitoring is incipi-
ent, fragmented, and disconnected from decision-making 
processes, a pattern consistent with literature documenting 
systemic monitoring deficiencies in SRFs across the Global 
South (Glinski 2022; Khan et al. 2012).
Thus, differences between listed and non-listed facilities 
are not fully explained by cumulative compliance across all 
evaluated categories but are also reflected in the distribution 
of non-conformities across specific dimensions. The heat map 
analysis revealed that several facilities included in the EU List 
still exhibited isolated non-conformities, particularly in IM, 
HWM, and selected HSE parameters, indicating that certain 
deficiencies may coexist with inclusion in the EU List, includ-
ing in facilities that also present robust governance, environ-
mental control, and traceability systems. In contrast, persistent 
shortcomings in environmental monitoring, intertidal zone con-
trol, operational traceability, and institutional governance were 
more frequently observed among non-listed facilities. These 
patterns highlight differences between the groups in the docu-
mented evidence of continuous management of operational 
and environmental conditions. Furthermore, the sensitivity 
analysis excluding facilities operating under structurally pro-
hibited dismantling methods showed that differences between 
the two groups remained evident in governance-related dimen-
sions, suggesting that the observed assessment patterns extend 
beyond differences related solely to dismantling infrastructure 
and encompass institutional capacity, auditability, and verifiable 
risk management systems.
Taken together, these findings contribute to the literature 
by providing empirical evidence of differences between listed 
and non-listed facilities in the systemic coherence of govern-
ance and environmental control mechanisms, rather than in any 
single parameter. While confirming earlier insights, the study 
contributes new evidence by showing how these differences 
manifest in inspection reports, thereby addressing a gap that 
has received limited attention in previous research.
Conclusions
This study sought to identify patterns associated with dif-
ferent listing outcomes among SRFs evaluated for inclusion 
in the European List. The results suggest that differences 
Environmental Science and Pollution Research 
between listed and non-listed facilities are not fully cap-
tured by the total number of requirements met but are also 
reflected in the nature and distribution of non-conformities 
across specific assessed dimensions. Facilities that failed to 
provide consistent evidence of soil, water, air, and noise mon-
itoring, or that exhibited documentary gaps in management 
plans, showed limitations in demonstrating compliance with 
the requirements considered in the EU assessment process, 
despite physical improvements implemented in recent years.
The analysis also highlights the intertidal zone as one of 
the dimensions associated with differences in listing out-
comes. Methods such as beaching, which are prevalent in 
India and other parts of South Asia, present challenges for 
effective containment and the prevention of recurrent leak -
age conditions that may pose challenges to meeting the envi-
ronmental protection requirements established under the EU 
SRR. In contrast, the assessed facilities located in Turkey, 
the UK and USA generally showed more consistent integra-
tion of infrastructure, management practices, and monitor -
ing systems, even when minor limitations in indicators or 
performance reporting persisted.
An additional contribution of this study emerges from the 
sensitivity analysis excluding facilities employing disman-
tling methods prohibited under the EU SRR No. 1257/2013. 
Under this scenario, Applications 40 and 56 displayed per -
formance patterns broadly comparable to those of several 
listed facilities across the remaining assessed dimensions, 
suggesting that their non-listing outcomes cannot be inter -
preted solely based on overall compliance performance. 
This finding further indicates that structural eligibility cri-
teria alone do not explain all observed differences between 
listed and non-listed facilities, as differences in governance 
capability, environmental monitoring, traceability, and risk-
control systems remained evident between the two groups. 
Overall, differences between listed and non-listed facilities 
were observed not only in infrastructure and operational 
practices but also in specific dimensions related to environ-
mentally sound ship recycling.
Thus, the observed differences between listed and non-
listed SRFs are reflected not only in compliance across 
individual categories but also in governance, environmental 
evidence, and operational control. The central conclusion 
of this study is that environmentally sound ship recycling is 
associated with institutional systems capable of supporting 
consistency, verifiability, and continuous improvement. This 
perspective extends beyond physical compliance and con -
tributes to a broader understanding of the institutional and 
operational characteristics associated with environmentally 
sound and socially responsible ship recycling. By examin-
ing differences in governance, environmental monitoring, 
documentation, and operational control between listed 
and non-listed facilities, this study contributes to a clearer 
understanding of the institutional dimensions of environ-
mentally sound ship recycling and highlights the recurrent 
differences in governance observed between the two groups.
