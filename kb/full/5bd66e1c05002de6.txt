Abstract
In response to escalating global water scarcity, reducing water losses in urban drinking water services has become crucial 
for enhancing eco-efficiency. This study introduces an innovative methodological approach to benchmark water leak -
age and its associated operational-cost adjustment in the water sector. The approach employs inverse Data Envelopment 
Analysis (DEA) through reverse optimization. A case study of Chile’s water industry—marked by prolonged drought but 
maintaining stable leakage rates—was conducted to demonstrate the methodology’s value. The findings show that 6 out of 
27 assessed water companies cannot reduce water leakage without affecting their eco-efficiency. However, for the remain -
ing 21 companies, an average potential leakage reduction of 20.51% is achievable, with reductions ranging from 8.76 to 
81.55%. The economic impact of these reductions varies significantly, from US$0.27 to US$38.32 per cubic meter saved. 
These results highlight the economic complexity of reducing leakage, emphasizing the importance of balanced policies 
that address water affordability alongside eco-efficiency goals.

Nations ( 2024) highlighted that between 2002 and 2021, 
droughts impacted over 1.4 billion people, forecasting that 
by 2050, between 1.7 and 2.4 billion people in urban areas 
might face water scarcity. Addressing this growing water 
crisis requires a multifaceted approach. A critical compo -
nent in urban drinking water services is the reduction of 
water losses (Zang 2023). Annually, global water loss totals 
approximately 126 billion cubic meters, valued at around 
US$ 39 billion (Liemberger and Wyatt 2019). Water leakage 
not only results in the wastage of water resources but also 
has significant economic repercussions and poses public 
health risks due to potential contamination (Fox et al. 2016). 
Moreover, the energy expended in treating and distributing 
leaked water contributes to environmental burdens, high -
lighting the interconnected nature of water management 
with broader environmental issues (Jernigan 2024). Given 
these complexities, implementing effective leakage reduc -
tion strategies is crucial for sustainable urban water manage-
ment, underscoring the need for comprehensive solutions 
that address both the immediate challenges of water loss 
and the long-term sustainability of water resources (Pokhrel 
et al. 2023).

Within the context of sustainable urban drinking water 
supply, the assessment of water companies’ eco-efficiency 
has emerged as a valuable tool for both regulators and utili-
ties (Goh and See 2021). Eco-efficiency evaluations involve 
the estimation of a synthetic indicator for each water com -
pany, integrating various single indicators that typically 
cover economic, operational, and quality of service dimen -
sions (Cetrulo et al. 2020). Consequently, eco-efficiency 
offers a comprehensive view of water companies’ perfor -
mance, providing objective data that aids policymakers 
in designing incentives and policies aimed at promoting 
improvements across economic, environmental, and ser -
vice quality aspects of water company operations (Cetrulo 
et al. 2019). Given the critical importance of water leakage 
in the context of sustainable water services, several studies 
have incorporated the volume of water loss as an undesir -
able output in the eco-efficiency evaluation of water compa-
nies (Kumar 2010; Maziotis et al. 2023; Ravelojaona et al. 
2026). This approach positions water loss as a key variable 
to be minimized to enhance the overall performance and 
sustainability of water utilities.
Empirical studies assessing the eco-efficiency of water 
companies integrating water leakage used various models 
of the Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) method. DEA is 
a non-parametric technique capable of managing multiple 
inputs and outputs without necessitating a predefined func -
tional form for the relationships between these variables 
or requiring any a priori weighting scheme for them (Du 
et al. 2023). This method is particularly valuable because 
it focuses on individual observations, providing insights 
relative to specific units under evaluation rather than gen -
eralizing to population averages (lo Storto 2022). Molinos-
Senante et al. (2022) and Sala-Garrido et al. (2022) utilized 
a non-radial DEA approach to assess the eco-efficiency of a 
sample of 21 Chilean water companies over the period 2007–
2018. They focused on potential savings in water losses if 
utilities were eco-efficient. lo Storto (2022) employed a DEA 
metafrontier approach to assess the eco-efficiency of 71 Ital-
ian water utilities, accommodating heterogeneities among 
utilities based on their locations. Similarly, Li et al. ( 2021) 
and Du et al. ( 2023) applied this methodological approach 
to estimate changes in the eco-efficiency of Chinese water 
utilities. Walker et al. (2021) also examined changes in eco-
efficiency over time but uniquely integrated the reduction of 
water leakage as a desirable output in their model.
Prior research has effectively incorporated water leak -
age into the eco-efficiency assessments of water companies, 
enabling an evaluation of how this critical factor influences 
their overall performance (Ablanedo-Rosas et al. 2020). 
These studies have significantly contributed to highlight 
the pivotal role of water leakage management in enhanc -
ing eco-efficiency. However, two fundamental questions 
remain largely unexplored: What is the lowest level of water 
leakage that each water company can achieve while main -
taining its current level of eco-efficiency? and what are the 
economic implications, specifically in terms of increased 
operational costs, associated with efforts to reduce water 
leakage within each water company?. To reply both ques -
tions, we have inspired in the methodological approach pro-
posed by Mocholi-Arce et al. ( 2024) who use inverse DEA 
to estimate the additional operational costs associated with 
predefined improvements in water and sanitation service 
continuity. Building on that framework, the present study 
develops a leakage-specific application in which the leakage 
benchmark is determined endogenously rather than imposed 
through predefined scenarios.
The main aim of this study is to propose and apply an 
inverse DEA framework to estimate utility-specific, frontier-
consistent leakage benchmarks and their associated opera -
tional-cost adjustments while maintaining eco-efficiency 
scoreTo demonstrate the value of the proposed methodol -
ogy, an empirical analysis was conducted on a sample of 27 
water companies in Chile, representing a range of current 
water leakage levels. This research extends the application 
of inverse DEA to a leakage-specific, endogenous-target 
benchmarking problem. It thereby extends previous studies 
that have focused on evaluating the impact of water leakage 
on water company performance. From a policy standpoint, 
the findings of this research offer valuable insights for water 
regulators and managers by providing a quantitative assess-
ment of how reducing water leakage affects operational 
costs, which could influence water tariffs and affordability. 
Understanding the economics of water loss reduction is cru-
cial for setting specific targets at the water company level, 
helping to achieve a balance between service quality and 
operational costs in water services.
Materials and methods
Methodological framework for estimating the 
minimum water leakage and its impact on 
operational costs
Building on previous research that used DEA to estimate the 
eco-efficiency of water companies, including water leakage, 
this study proposes a framework based on Inverse DEA to 
determine the minimum feasible water leakage for each 
water company analysed. This framework also estimates 
the operational costs required to achieve this target while 
maintaining current levels of eco-efficiency. Specifically, 
we propose the use of an inverse DEA model framed as a 
design problem. This model functions by designing prices, 
incentives, or mechanisms that guide agent decisions to be

optimal within the calculated parameters (Gupta and Zhang 
2023). This approach ensures that all solutions generated by 
the optimization are feasible and actionable for water com -
panies. Moreover, Inverse DEA, employing reverse optimi-
zation, identifies the necessary inputs and/or outputs to meet 
predetermined efficiency targets (Emrouznejad et al. 2023). 
This methodological approach, therefore, enables the esti -
mation of both the minimum feasible water leakage and its 
corresponding impact on operational costs for each assessed 
water company.
The proposed methodological framework consists of two 
primary stages, defined as follows:
Stage 1: Assessment of the current eco-efficiency of 
water companies.
At this phase, the eco-efficiency of water companies was 
evaluated, including specifically in current water leakage 
levels. Following past research (Walker et al. 2021; Li et al. 
2021; Du et al. 2023), the analysis utilized the DEA method. 
This non-parametric approach is capable of accommodating 
different returns to scale such as constant returns to scale 
(CRS), variable returns to scale (VRS), non-increasing 
returns to scale (NIRS), and non-decreasing returns to scale 
(NDRS). Previous studies (Maziotis et al. 2023; Sala-Gar-
rido et al. 2022) have demonstrated that water companies in 
Chile typically function under VRS conditions. An input-
oriented VRS DEA model was employed because opera -
tional costs are considered a controllable input, whereas 
increases in the desirable outputs are generally beyond the 
direct control of water companies. In the empirical appli -
cation, operational cost is the only input contracted by the 
radial efficiency factor. Water leakage is incorporated as an 
undesirable output through a non-increase constraint which 
involves that the reference combination must exhibit a leak-
age level no greater than that observed for the evaluated 
company.

Stage 2: Estimation of a frontier-consistent leakage 
benchmark and its associated operational-cost adjustment.
This stage identifies, for each evaluated water utility, the 
lowest leakage benchmark represented within the estimated 
DEA technology while maintaining the observed desirable 
outputs and the Stage 1 eco-efficiency score. It subsequently 
estimates the operational-cost adjustment associated with 
reaching that benchmark under the inverse DEA constraints. 
The resulting value is a relative, sample-based benchmark 
and not an estimate of the physical or engineering minimum 
leakage of the utility’s distribution network.
To accomplish this, following to Mocholi-Arce et 
al. ( 2024), an inverse DEA approach was utilized. This 
approach involves an inverse optimization problem, which 
not only estimates a feasible solution for reducing water 
leakage but also calculates the necessary increase in opera -
tional costs (inputs) to realize this reduction (Emrouznejad 
et al. 2023).

Sample of water companies and data selection
The case study analyzed in this study focused on the Chil -
ean water industry, which serves 6,058,085 households, 
accounting for approximately 16,215,864 individuals. 
Urban drinking water coverage in Chile reaches 99.9%, 
while sanitation coverage stands at 97.5% (SISS, 2023). 
There is considerable variation in the size of water compa -
nies within Chile; the largest serves 2,160,340 households, 
while the smallest serves fewer than 1,000. Given the wide 
disparity in company sizes and the statistical data available, 
this study includes the largest 27 water companies based 
on customer numbers. These 27 companies provide water 
and sanitation services to 5,903,863 households, which rep-
resents 97.45% of all urban customers. Operating across 
Chile’s 16 administrative regions, these water utilities col -
lectively provide a representative sample for analyzing the 
Chilean water industry.
The analysis of water leakage levels among Chilean 
water companies is detailed in Fig. 1, which depicts the evo-
lution of the average percentage of water leakage over the 
past decade. The data show that the average water leakage 
rate for Chilean water companies has remained relatively 
stable, fluctuating between 23.7 and 25.0%. This stability 
corresponds to an estimated annual loss of 270 to 300 mil -
lion cubic meters of water. According to 2023 figures, the 
average monthly water consumption per customer account 
is 16.8 m³ (SISS, 2023). Consequently, the annual water lost 
is approximately equivalent to the annual consumption of 
1.4 million people. It is important to highlight that Chile 
has experienced severe drought conditions over the past 15 
years (2012–2023), significantly reducing water availability 
from both groundwater and surface sources (Bobadilla et 
al. 2024). This scarcity has compelled water companies to 
invest to secure raw water sources, with approximately US$ 
424 million spent in 2023 alone (SISS, 2023). Despite these 
investments, the rate of drinking water network replace -
ment has remained below 0.5% annually for the last decade 
(SISS, 2023), involving that a complete network replace -
ment would take over 200 years. This is particularly con -
cerning in a seismically active country like Chile, which 
recorded 6900 earthquakes in 2023 with magnitudes of 3 
or higher (CSN 2023), potentially impacting the integrity 
of water networks. In this context, it is crucial to assess the 
potential reductions in water leakage and their effects on the 
operational costs of water companies.

international past research on this topic (Cetrulo et al. 2019;

and serves approximately 190,000 customers. Its low score 
is consistent with its comparatively high operational costs,

Research findings on the eco-efficiency of Chilean water 
companies vary significantly depending on the method -
ological approaches and variables used in the assessments. 
Molinos-Senante et al. (2022) estimated an average eco-effi-
ciency score of 0.714 for a sample of 21 water companies 
in Chile, using the same inputs and outputs as this study, 
which provides consistency in the results. On the other 
hand, studies by Sala-Garrido et al. ( 2022, 2023) incorpo-
rated unplanned water supply interruptions alongside water 
leakage as undesirable outputs, resulting in considerably 
higher average eco-efficiency scores of 0.961 and 0.986, 
respectively. These findings suggest that including addi -
tional variables can significantly alter the outcomes. This 
disparity highlights how different analytical techniques can 
yield diverse estimations of eco-efficiency within the same 
industry.
Minimum level of water leakage for each water 
company
By solving Model (3), the minimum feasible water leak -
age for each water company was calculated, along with the 
potential percentage reduction based on their current levels 
of water loss (Fig. 3). It was found that 6 out of the 27 eval-
uated water companies (22.2%) cannot reduce their water 
loss while maintaining the volume of drinking water sup -
plied and their current level of eco-efficiency. Notably, one 
of these six companies is the largest water provider in San -
tiago, the capital city of Chile. The other companies where 
water leakage reduction is not feasible are WC5, WC9, 
WC13, WC14, and WC15.
It should be noted that an eco-efficiency score of 1 does 
not imply that every individual input or undesirable output 
has reached its minimum feasible value. In the input-ori -
ented DEA model, a score of 1 indicates that operational 
costs cannot be proportionally and simultaneously reduced 
while maintaining the observed desirable outputs. The 
inverse DEA model addresses a different question because 
it searches for an alternative feasible combination in which 
leakage is reduced and operational costs are allowed to 
increase, while the desirable outputs and the initial eco-
efficiency score remain unchanged. Consequently, an eco-
efficient water company may still move to another point on 
the VRS frontier characterized by lower leakage but higher 
operational costs. This explains the leakage-reduction 
potential identified for WC10, WC22, WC26, and WC27. In 
contrast, for WC1, WC5, WC9, WC13, WC14, and WC15, 
Model (3) identifies no alternative feasible point with lower 
leakage within the production possibility set estimated from 
the sample. Their current leakage levels therefore represent 
the minimum feasible levels under the model constraints.

efficiency scores. Stage 1 measures the proportional reduc -
tion in operational costs subject to non-increasing leakage, 
whereas Stage 2 explicitly determines the maximum fea -
sible reduction in leakage while maintaining the initial 
eco-efficiency level. The results therefore demonstrate that 
cost-oriented eco-efficiency and leakage-reduction poten -
tial represent related but distinct dimensions of company 
performance.
At the water industry level, aggregating the data from 
the 27 water companies evaluated, reveals that the current 
annual water loss amounts to 292,081,000 m 3. The analy -
sis estimates that the minimum feasible water loss could be 
reduced to 244,435,000 m3/year. This represents a potential 
avoidance of 47,646,000 m 3 of water lost annually. Given 
that the average monthly water consumption per customer 
account is 16.8 m³ (SISS, 2023), the estimated reduction 
in water leakage is equivalent to the annual average water 
consumption of approximately 236,000 customer accounts. 
This comparison is provided only to illustrate the magnitude 
of the estimated leakage reduction.
The current average percentage of water loss among 
the assessed Chilean water companies is 20.29% (Fig. 4). 
This rate exceeds the maximum threshold of 15% set by the 
urban water regulator for the purpose of determining water 
tariffs (Molinos-Senante and Donoso 2016). However, the 
average estimated minimum feasible water leakage stands 
at 13.02%, evidencing that, on average, the Chilean water 
industry could adhere to the current regulatory standards. 
Despite this average, Fig. 4 highlights significant dispari -
ties in both the current and minimum feasible percentages 
of water leakage among the companies. Currently, water 
leakage rates range from 4.51 to 37.22%. The company 
with the poorest performance has the potential to reduce its 
water leakage to 23.75%. The Superintendencia de Servi -
cios Sanitarios, following its Agenda 2030 adopted in 2019, 
launched a specific initiative aimed at reducing water leak -
age (SISS, 2019). As part of this initiative, water companies 
and the regulatory body have agreed upon a memorandum of 
understanding which sets a target for all water companies to 
reduce water leakage to below 25% (SISS, 2023). The find-
ings from this study demonstrate that achieving this target 
is feasible and can be accomplished without compromising 
the eco-efficiency of the water companies, as the estimated 
minimum feasible water leakage for all companies is below 
the 25% threshold.

The United Nations ( 2010) has stipulated that the cost 
for water and sanitation services should not exceed 5% of 
a household’s average income to remain financially afford -
able. Therefore, before translating leakage benchmarks into 
regulatory targets, their potential tariff and affordability 
implications should be evaluated through a separate regu -
latory and economic assessment. Such an assessment is 
beyond the scope of the present DEA framework.
In Chile, the potable water subsidy benefited over 764,770 
families, accounting for 12% of the urban customers. Par -
ticularly in the Aysen region, 34.9% of customers received 
this subsidy (SISS, 2023). The potable water subsidy in 
Chile is a direct subsidy targeted at consumers, designed 
to ensure access to a basic water supply and sewage ser -
vices for lower-income families (SISS, 2023). This context 
highlights the importance of considering affordability when 
leakage-reduction policies are translated into expenditure or 
tariff decisions. However, the present analysis does not esti-
mate tariff changes or changes in the number of households 
requiring subsidies. These effects would require a separate 
affordability and regulatory analysis.
The following issues should be considering when inter -
preting results of this study. First, DEA constructs a rela -
tive production possibility set from the observed utilities. 
The estimated leakage benchmarks therefore depend on the 
composition of the sample, the 2023 cross-sectional data, 
the variables selected, and the orientation of the models. 
Second, the analysis does not incorporate some structural 
and operating conditions such as network length and age, 
pipe material and diameter, pressure regimes, etc. that can 
materially affect leakage and the resources required to 
reduce it. The omission of these variables means that the 
model cannot distinguish fully between differences arising 
from management performance and those arising from het -
erogeneous operating environments. Third, the operational-
cost input is an aggregate annual value. It does not isolate 
expenditure specifically related to leakage management and 
does not include the capital expenditure or asset-renewal 
requirements that may be necessary to attain a lower leak -
age level. The estimated cost adjustments are therefore 
benchmark implications rather than engineering cost func -
tions or investment budgets.
Conclusions
In a global context where water scarcity is intensifying, 
reducing water losses in urban drinking water services is 
increasingly critical. Previous research has shown that water 
leakage levels significantly influence the eco-efficiency of 
water companies. This study introduces a methodologi -
cal approach based on inverse DEA to quantify the mini -
mum feasible level of water losses and assess its impact on 
operational costs at the water company level. This approach 
provides information on the operational-cost adjustments 
associated, within the inverse DEA framework, with lower 
leakage benchmarks. These operational-cost implica -
tions may be relevant for subsequent assessments of tariffs 
and affordability, although such effects are not estimated 
directly in the present study.To demonstrate the value of 
the proposed methodological approach, a case study of the 
Chilean water industry was conducted. This serves as a par-
adigmatic example, as despite the country’s prolonged and 
severe drought, the level of water leakage in water compa -
nies has remained stable over the past decade. The results 
reveal that 6 out of 27 water companies cannot reduce 
water losses without compromising their eco-efficiency. 
However, for the remaining 21 companies, the average esti-
mated reduction in water leakage is 20.51%, with a range 
from 8.76 to 81.55%. This reduction in water leakage has 
significant implications for operational costs, which were 
quantified between US$0.27 and US$38.32 per cubic meter 
of water saved. This highlights the challenge of balancing 
operational efficiency and cost-effectiveness with broader 
concerns about equitable access to water.
From a policy perspective, the proposed methodological 
approach offers valuable guidance for water regulators in 
setting realistic, data-driven leakage reduction targets. By 
identifying feasible reduction levels tailored to each water 
company, this approach promotes targeted, rather than uni -
form, policies that account for the unique circumstances and 
operational capacities of individual companies. The signifi-
cant variation in cost per cubic meter saved underscores the 
importance of a thorough economic assessment when estab-
lishing leakage reduction targets.
Looking beyond the immediate case study, adoption of 
this framework by water regulators carries three longer-
term implications. First, it would allow uniform, sector-
wide leakage thresholds such as the < 25% target currently 
agreed between Chilean water companies and the regula -
tor to be progressively replaced or complemented by com -
pany-specific targets that are consistent with each utility’s 
eco-efficiency frontier, reducing the risk of imposing infea-
sible or disproportionately costly obligations on individual 
companies. Second, because the estimated cost of leak -
age reduction varies widely across companies, recurrent

application of the model could inform the phased design 
of water tariffs and of the potable water subsidy scheme, 
so that affordability is assessed jointly with each leakage-
reduction target rather than after the fact. Third, given that 
network replacement in Chile has proceeded at below 0.5% 
per year over the past decade, repeated application of the 
model over successive regulatory periods, alongside infra -
structure renewal investment, would allow regulators and 
companies to track how each company’s minimum feasible 
leakage evolves as networks are renewed and as drought 
conditions persist. Future research should test the dynamic 
extension of this framework using multi-year panel data, 
and examine its applicability to other water-scarce countries 
facing a comparable trade-off between eco-efficiency and 
leakage reduction.
