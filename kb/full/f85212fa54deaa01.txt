Water quality and eutrophication status of the Zarivar Wetland (Iran)

Abstract
Wetlands are critically impacted by a variety of pollutants, primarily due to their position at the terminus of both surface and 
subsurface water flows. This study aims to assess the eutrophication status of Zarivar Wetland, situated in Kurdistan Prov -
ince, Iran. To evaluate the eutrophication levels, the research employed the Iranian Water Quality Index for Surface Water 
Resources-Conventional Parameters (IRWQISC) and the Carlson’s Trophic State Index (TSI) during two sampling periods in 
July and September 2021. The findings indicate that in July, the wetland exhibited an upper-mesotrophic condition, with an 
average Carlson index value of 52.2, while in September, it transitioned to both upper-mesotrophic and eutrophic conditions, 
reflected by an average Carlson index value of 60.17. The zoned map of the eutrophication index identified domestic sewage 
from Marivan city as a significant contributor to elevated eutrophication levels in the southeastern region. Additionally, runoff 
from irrigated agricultural lands, orchards, and domestic wastewater from surrounding villages contributed to heightened 
eutrophication in the northeastern and eastern areas of the wetland. A comparative analysis of water quality between July and 
September 2021 revealed IRWQISC values ranging from 3.57 to 9.72 in July and from 2.63 to 4.72 in September, categorizing 
them as relatively good and good, respectively. To safeguard Zarivar Wetland, it is recommended to implement measures 
that prevent livestock waste discharge into the wetland, manage urban and rural sewage systems, control agricultural runoff, 
and optimize fertilizer application practices.

Introduction
Worldwide, the water quality of aquatic ecosystems is 
declining, and ecological functions are degrading (Ding 
et al. 2020). Increasing water demand, population growth, 
and environmental degradation have drawn attention to 
wetlands restoration (Krimsky et al. 2021). The wetland 
water quality is affected by many factors, including point 
source pollution (i.e., domestic sewage and industrial dis-
charges, etc.) and non-point source pollution (i.e., agri-
cultural land runoff, atmospheric deposition of pollutants, 
etc.) (Qiu et al. 2018; Zohrabiet al. 2024). The area of 
wetlands in the world is decreasing due to drought, socio-
economic development, and land-use changes in the areas 
around wetlands (Hu et al. 2017). Where wetlands are not 
directly destroyed by land-use change, external factors like 
diffuse pollution can reduce wetland water quality that, in 
turn, can disrupt ecosystem services and functions (Zeng 
et al. 2015; Alghanimi et al. 2024). A scientific method to 
assess water quality and to identify potential risk sources 
is necessary to achieve ecological and environmental man -
agement (Huo et al. 2018; Ortiz-Reyes and Anex 2018; 
Shahradnia et al. 2021). The Iran water quality index 
(IRWQI) is an emerging national index in Iran to evaluate 
the quality of water resources. The IRWQI is determined 
based on the parameters of fecal coliform, biological oxy -
gen demand (BOD5), chemical oxygen demand (COD), 
turbidity, total hardness, electrical conductivity (EC), 
dissolved oxygen, nitrate, ammonium, phosphate, and pH 
(Shahsavar et al. 2021). This index has been used as case 
studies in different regions of Iran (Baghapour et al. 2017; 
Hamedi et al. 2015; Nayyeri and Zandi 2015; Sadeghi 
et al. 2015; Baghapour et al. 2017; Shahsavar et al. 2021).
Eutrophication poses a significant threat to aquatic eco-
systems and has garnered global attention (Du et al. 2019). 
Consequently, classifying wetland ecosystems based on 
their eutrophication status is crucial. The preservation 
of habitats for living organisms is essential for effective 
resource management, particularly through the applica-
tion of advanced information technologies (Naumoski and 
Mitreski 2010). The causes of eutrophication are multifac-
eted, involving a range of ecological, social, and economic 
factors (Alvarez et al. 2017). Notably, sources such as sew-
age, agricultural runoff, animal waste, and fertilizers are 
recognized as primary contributors to increased eutrophi-
cation in surface waters (Jani et al. 2020; Lusk et al. 2020). 
For decades, excessive nutrient presence in aquatic eco-
systems has been viewed as a global challenge due to 
its consequences related to eutrophication (Huang et al. 
2020). Over-fertilization can result in nutrient leaching, 
particularly phosphorus and nitrogen, from agricultural 
lands into waterways (Kiani et al. 2021). Diffuse pollution 
from nitrogen and phosphorus remains the leading cause 
of eutrophication (Beusen et al. 2016). The rising nutri -
ent concentrations in aquatic ecosystems driven by socio-
economic development and intensified human activities 
exacerbate eutrophication, which is a critical global envi-
ronmental issue that threatens the functional integrity of 
aquatic ecosystems (Wu et al. 2021; Lin et al. 2021; Field-
ing et al. 2020). Since 1945, indices and models based 
on determining the eutrophication state of aquatic ecosys-
tems have been designed by many researchers in the world. 
Several single-parameter and multiparameter indices have 
been developed for the eutrophication classification of 
lakes and wetlands (Shannon and Brezonik 1972; Cunha 
et al. 2013). The most well-known index of them is the 
trophic state index (TSI), which was proposed for temper -
ate lakes by Carlson (1977) by considering the empirical 
relations among chlorophyll A, total phosphorus, and Sec-
chi disk depth (Cunha et al. 2013). This index has often 
been used by researchers and government institutions 
to indirectly estimate algal biomasses by indicating the 
degree of eutrophication of aquatic systems (Cunha et al. 
2013). The trophic state index (TSI) provides significant 
management value and essential guidance for lakes with 
limited data or data constraints (Lin et al. 2020; Bomfim 
et al. 2019; Stamou et al. 2019). The TSI is determined 
by analyzing nutrient levels and Secchi disk depth, which 
serve as indicators of a lake's nutrient status and biological 
processes (Zhang et al. 2018).
The eutrophication state has been evaluated in the 
Three-Gorges Reservoir (China) (Xu et al. 2010) using the 
Carlson Index. It showed that after the reservoir became 
operational, its condition was oligotrophic (TSI (S) < 40) 
in most months, although both the TSI (TP) and TSI (TN) 
values were higher than the critical eutrophic state value 
(TSI (S) > 50). In Korea, Mamun and An (2017) worked 
with 182 agricultural reservoirs and showed the severe 
phosphorus limitation of these reservoirs. Phosphorus is 
a crucial nutrient that regulates phytoplankton growth. 
This relationship was confirmed through an analysis of the 
indices, demonstrating that TSI (CHL)—TSI (SD) is less 
than TSI (CHL)—TSI (TP). Dar et al. (2021) in the Brari 
Nambal urban wetland in Srinagar showed that decreased 
wetland area, along with a drop in morphometric charac-
teristics, shallow depth, and higher trophic status, indi-
cate anthropogenic pressures using the Carlson TSI (value 
between 74 and 87) that threaten this wetland’s ecological 
integrity. There are other indices, such as the Lamparelli 
TSI, used to characterize the eutrophication status of wet-
lands. Sarkar et al. (2020) analyzed the eutrophication sta-
tus of the floodplain wetlands of eastern India using the 
Carlson TSI and Lamparelli TSI. They revealed that the 
TSI scores had a similar pattern of changes with rainfall 
and water temperature for both methods. In summary, the

In Iran, the Zarivar Wetland is one of the largest sources 
of fresh water. This wetland lies very close to the Marivan 
city and has been exposed to destruction from natural and 
human factors. The relevant environmental features and 
economic and social values of this wetland are noticeable 
from different perspectives, especially in water pollution 
terms. Very few studies have focused on assessing the water 
quality of this wetland to evaluate its suitability for vari-
ous uses and to identify potential issues. For example, the 
findings of Ostad-Ali-Askari (2022) showed that the Zarivar 
international Wetland is heavily polluted with heavy met-
als that pose significant ecological risks to its environment 
and human health. In the study of Elahi et al. (2022), the 
ecological risk of toxic heavy metals was evaluated in the 
surface sediments of the Zarivar Wetland. The result of risk 
index values classified the wetland as "very highly polluted" 
during the rainy season and "considerably polluted" during 
the dry season. The study of Jamshidi and Naderi (2023) 
showed that agricultural activities, such as irrigation and 
land-use change upstream, are the main sources of nitro-
gen and phosphorus pollution. They suggested that a 50% 
reduction in fertilizers, animal manures, and irrigation water 
over a decade can potentially reduce 85% of the nitrogen 
and 25% of the phosphorus pollution loads per ton of agri-
cultural product. The objectives of the present study were 
to: (1) assess the extent of the eutrophication status of the 
Zarivar Wetland; (2) analyze the water quality of the Zarivar 
Wetland; and (3) evaluate the main factors that cause high 
eutrophication.
Materials and methods
Study area and sampling
The Zarivar Wetland have been designated as a Wetland of 
International Importance under the Ramsar Convention. The 
livelihood of many residents on the edge of the wetland also 
depends directly or indirectly from it. This wetland is located 
in the Kurdistan province of Iran, and at between 46○ 06′ 
11″ and 46○ 09′ 16″ east longitude and between 35○ 30′ 
53″ and 35○ 35′ 12″ north latitude (north of the folded belt 
of Zagros). The average height of the Zarivar Wetland is 
1285 m above sea level. This wetland was formed due to the 
severe erosion of the geological formations in this region 
(Khoram and Hoshmand 2012). In climate terms, the aver -
age minimum and maximum temperatures in this wetland 
are 5 and 20.1 °C, respectively. The average annual rainfall is 
approximately 900 mm (Anonymous 2016). Figure 1 shows 
the land-use types in the different parts of the Zarivar Wet-
land watershed in 2020. A map of land uses and land covers 
in the catchment area of the Zarivar Wetland was prepared 
using images with high spatial resolution of Google Earth 
Pro and visual interpretation method.
The selection of water sampling stations was carried out 
systematically to ensure coverage of the entire wetland area. 
It is worth noting that the peripheral areas of the wetland 
were not accessible for sampling due to dense vegetation and 
high concentration of aquatic plants. Sample water was col-
lected from 13 stations in July and September 2021. Samples 
were transferred to the laboratory at 4 °C on ice and were 
left in the refrigerator until the start of the analysis. Steriliza-
tion of sampling containers, their transportation to, and stor-
age in the laboratory were done according to the instructions 
in the Standard Method Book, 2017 edition.
Analytical methods and indices
Temperature, EC, pH, and DO parameters were measured 
using an HQ30d Portable device, model HACH. Turbidity 
was measured by a turbidity meter (aqualytic model) at the 
sampling site. Other parameters were transferred to the labo-
ratory for measurements. In the laboratory, the amounts of 
nitrate (4500-NO3-E. Cadmium Reduction Method), total 
phosphate (4500 P D, Stannous Chloride Method), total 
nitrogen (Persulfate Digestion Method), BOD (BODTrak 
II™ HACH), COD (COD 5220 C. Closed Reflux, Titrimet-
ric Method), chlorophyll A (10200 H, Spectrophotometric 
Determination of Chlorophyll), ammonium (4500-NH3 
B&C), and total Coliform (Standard Total Coliform Fer -
mentation Technique 9221 B) were estimated. The quality 
assurance (QA) of the physicochemical parameters were 
checked by the spiking method (Adding a known quantity of 
a specific substance to a sample to assess the performance of 
an analytical method). The average recoveries for parameters 
ranged from 94 to 105%. Analytical process precision was 
evaluated by relative standard deviation (RSD). To fulfill 
this aim, one water sample was analyzed in three replicates. 
The RSD values that were obtained for parameters ranged 
from 0.41% to 6%.
In addition, 86 questionnaires were completed by inter -
viewing the farmers around the wetland with questions about 
individual social characteristics, economic characteristics, 
cultivation conditions of agricultural and horticultural crops, 
and animal husbandry. The estimated amount of fertilizer 
used in the basin has been fundamentally based on: the anal-
ysis done from questionnaires and interviews with farmers; 
the amount of fertilizer used in the agricultural sector of the 
wetland research area; and the area where crops are grown.

Results and discussion
Water quality of the Zarivar Wetland based 
on the IRWQISC
Forest cover 49.1%, wetlands 23.9%, farm lands 22.6%, 
orchards 2.67%, man-made lands 1.67%, and bare lands 
cover 0.06% of the total area of this Zarivar Wetland 
watershed.
The summary statistics of water properties and param-
eters are listed in Table  1. Biological oxygen demand 
(BOD) ranged from 0.7 to 1.7, with a mean of 1.02 in 
July and one of 0.17 in September. This parameter is used 
to determine the pollution potential of the environment 
and the absorption capacity of the receiving environment 
based on living organisms’ ability to consume dissolved 
oxygen when they are released to the receiving environ-
ment (Anonymous 2013). BOD is defined as the amount 
of oxygen required for microorganisms to dissolve organic 
matter in an aqueous environment under aerobic condi -
tions. Another relevant parameter to determine the level 
of both water and wastewater pollution is chemical oxy -
gen demand (COD) (Mutlu et al. 2013b). The comparison 
of the mean value of this parameter showed that COD 
had almost doubled in September (14 mg/l) compared to 
July (7.13 mg/l). The high COD concentration in summer 
months may result from the entry of more pollutants from 
agricultural lands. It may also be due to the presence of 
many microorganisms in the river (Mirsaeedghazi 2018; 
Wu et al. 2019). The rapid rise in the COD concentration 
in summer months is caused by the accumulation of many 
factors, such as high temperature, low DO concentration, 
intensive agricultural production, eutrophication, and the 
presence of wastewater. It is very difficult to identify the 
main source of pollutions (Kumar et al. 2019; Uddin et al. 
2017; Mishra et al. 2017). However, a concentration of 
more than 25 mg/l COD in waters indicates pollution, 
while values above 50 mg/l denote severe pollution and 
possible toxicity to aquatic organisms (Güler 1997). The 
COD values in the Zarivar Wetland were less than 25 mg/l 
in both the aforementioned months.
The presence of fecal coliform in aquatic environ-
ments may indicate that water has been polluted. The 
World Health Organization sets a limit of less than 
1000 CFU/100 ml of fecal coliforms, which is considered 
safe for wastewater. In the Zarivar Wetland, the amount 
of fecal coliform was significantly smaller and decreased 
in September (87.29 MPN/100 ml) compared to July 
(518 MPN/100 ml) (Table  4). Ali and Qamar (2013) 
documented elevated coliform counts during the summer 
months in the Rawal watershed, attributing this increase 
to intensified runoff and human activities. However, the 
results of the current study reveal fluctuations in contami-
nation severity, influenced by the sampling periods and 
potentially local environmental conditions. Temperature 
and solar radiation are considered the most important 
abiotic factors (Kagalou et al. 2002; Xu et al. 2002) that 
affect the mortality rate caused by fecal coliform. Water 
temperature changes are caused by seasonal temperature 
changes (Mutlu et al. 2013a). Burkhardt et al. (2000) found 
in their study that sunlight or temperature accounted for 
99% of the reduction in fecal coliform levels in estuarine 
waters of Alabama and on Rhode Island. In the present 
study, as the mean water temperature in the sampled sta-
tions was 28.4 °C in July and 26.7 °C in September, one 
of the reasons for the smaller amount of fecal coliforms in 
September compared to July can be attributed to the drop 
in water temperature.
Dissolved oxygen is an essential compound for aquatic 
life and is also necessary for biochemical oxidation. In fresh-
water, there must be at least 5 mg/l of dissolved oxygen to 
sustain aquatic life (Atay and Pulatsü, 2000). The value of 
this parameter was between 9.3 and 18.7 mg/l in July and 
between 7.8 and 7.23 mg/l in September (Table  4). These 
values show that there was minimal dissolved oxygen in all 
the samples. EC is also very important for aquatic products. 
When conductivity passes the level of 100 μS/cm, pollution 
capacity increases (Verep et al. 2005) which, according to 
the results of the EC values in this study, it was more than 
100 μS/cm in both months. Both temperature and water 
salinity increased after taking measurements in summer.

water is 6.5–9. This pH range supports the growth and sur -
vival of a diverse array of aquatic plants, fish, and other 
organisms that thrive in wetland environments. EPA guide-
lines state that pH levels beyond this range can negatively

poorer water quality. According to the results, the range of 
pH values of the water samples in both months fell within the 
standard range. In general, changes in water quality param-
eters can significantly impact the IRWQISC and water qual-
ity. For example, high BOD and COD levels indicate a high 
degree of organic pollution, which can negatively impact 
water quality (Fallah et al. 2021). Similarly, higher EC val-
ues indicate increased levels of dissolved solids (Fallah et al. 
2021), while rising total hardness values depict higher levels 
of soluble minerals (Umar et al. 2021). These changes can 
lead to worse water quality and low IRWQISC value. Fur -
thermore, low dissolved oxygen levels can diminish water 
quality (Fallah et al. 2021). The presence of fecal bacteria 
is indicated by high fecal coliform levels, while increased 
levels of ammonium suggest rising levels of organic matter 
and nutrient pollution (Bravo de Guenni et al. 2024). High 
nitrate and phosphate levels also indicate marked nutrient 
pollution, which is dangerous for aquatic life and degrades 
water quality (Bravo de Guenni et al. 2024). Additionally, 
higher turbidity levels can reduce water clarity and quality 
by increasing the amount of suspended solids (Umar et al. 
2021).
Based on the IDW maps (Fig.  2), high IRWQISC values 
(better water quality) were observed in the northwestern, 
southeastern, southwestern, and then the northeastern, 
parts of the wetland in July, and in the southwestern and 
northwestern parts in September. The IRWQISC in these 2 
months showed that the of IRWQISC values range in July 
was between 57.3 and 72.9 and in the relatively good condi-
tion, and between 63.2 and 72.4 and in the good condition in 
September. Water quality was lower in the northeastern part 
of the wetland in July and in the western part of the wetland 
in September. The water quality parameters from the present 
study indicate that the low water quality in the northeastern 
part of the wetland in July was primarily due to elevated 
levels of nitrate and fecal coliform. In September, the decline 
in water quality was attributed to high nitrate levels and low 
dissolved oxygen compared to other seasons. The results of 
the present study are in agreement with the findings of Ebra-
himpour et al. (2011), in their study reported that most of the 
polluting sources in the north and east are non-point sources, 
such as agricultural activities and domestic sewage. Given 
the fact that a large amount of sediment enters the wetland 
from the north every year, there is more suspended matter 
in that area that causes the visibility depth of the Sashi disk 
to decrease. Polluting substances are also brought in from 
all around the wetland by seasonal waterways, which are 
effective in accelerating eutrification and advancing reeds. 
The Zarivar Wetland had worse water quality in the western 
part in July due to the high levels of ammonium, BOD5 and 
fecal coliform, and the low dissolved oxygen level compared 
to other seasons. In September, this was due to high EC, total 
hardness, fecal coliform and phosphate compared to other 
seasons, and the western part had low water quality. When

attributed to sewage from the residential areas of the villages 
without a sewage network and to the use of chemical fertiliz-

The findings of this study contribute to the expanding 
body of knowledge regarding wetland eutrophication. Spe-
cifically, the trophic state index (TSI) values observed in 
Zarivar Wetland, which transitioned from mesotrophic to 
eutrophic conditions, are comparable to those reported by 
Jarosiewicz et al. (2011) in their assessment of Pomera-
nian lakes. They identified total phosphorus as a primary 
determinant of trophic state, underscoring the significance 
of nutrient management in mitigating eutrophication. This 
observation is supported by our results, which indicate a 
strong correlation between total phosphorus levels and 
elevated TSI values during the study period. Moreover, the 
seasonal increase in TSI observed in this study—most pro-
nounced in September—mirrors patterns observed in Lake 
Simbi, as described by Opiyo et al. (2019), where seasonal 
nutrient inflows from agricultural and urban sources exac-
erbated eutrophic conditions. These findings highlight the 
impact of external nutrient loading on wetland ecosystems 
and reveal common challenges across various geographic 
contexts. Additionally, the spatial distribution of eutrophica-
tion within Zarivar Wetland, primarily driven by agricultural 
runoff and untreated domestic sewage, aligns with global 
observations. The studies on Pomeranian lakes similarly 
identified agricultural practices and urban waste as signifi-
cant contributors to nutrient enrichment, further corroborat-
ing the drivers identified in this research. Overall, this study 
provides valuable insights into the eutrophication status of 
Zarivar Wetland through the application of the IRWQISC 
and Carlson’s Trophic State Index. However, certain limita-
tions warrant consideration. The analysis was confined to 
two specific sampling periods, which restricts the ability 
to comprehensively capture seasonal variations. While the 
indices employed are robust for assessing eutrophication, 
incorporating additional factors such as soil and wind ero-
sion, sediment deposition, and detailed nutrient fluxes could 
enhance the accuracy of future evaluations. Caution should 
be exercised when generalizing these findings to other wet-
lands due to significant variability in local conditions, pol-
lution sources, and management practices. Future research 
should focus on year-round sampling, advanced modeling 
techniques, and broader spatial coverage to achieve a more 
comprehensive understanding of eutrophication dynamics 
and their underlying drivers. Addressing regional challenges 
related to agricultural drainage and wastewater management 
is crucial for preserving wetland ecosystems like Zarivar 
Wetland and preventing further eutrophication. Expanding 
future research efforts to include a broader temporal scope 
or cross-ecosystem comparative analyses could further refine 
management practices aimed at mitigating nutrient loading.
Conclusion
This study evaluated the eutrophication status of Zarivar 
Wetland utilizing trophic state index (TSI) indices and spa-
tial mapping techniques. The findings indicated that the 
wetland was in an upper-mesotrophic state in the summer 
of July 2021, with an average Carlson index of 52.2, and 
transitioned to both upper-mesotrophic and eutrophic con-
ditions by September (early autumn) 2021, reflected in an 
average Carlson index of 60.17. Spatial analyses identified 
significant pollution sources, including agricultural runoff, 
domestic sewage from adjacent villages, and excessive live-
stock grazing, which contributed to increased soil erosion 
and nutrient loading. Elevated total nitrogen levels at sam-
pling stations 12 and 13 were associated with the intensive 
application of chemical fertilizers in surrounding agricul-
tural areas. To maintain the ecological balance of the wet-
land, effective management strategies are essential, includ-
ing controlling agricultural drainage, reducing livestock 
waste, and regulating fertilizer application. However, this 
study has certain limitations, such as the absence of data on 
soil and wind erosion and its impact on nutrient dynamics, 
as well as a lack of seasonal variability beyond the months 
studied. Future research should investigate these factors to 
provide a more comprehensive understanding of eutrophi-
cation processes. By addressing these gaps, more effective 
strategies for the restoration and management of this wetland 
can be developed.
