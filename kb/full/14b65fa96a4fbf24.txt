A BSTRACT
Drip fertigation combines the application of irrigation water and soluble fertilizers through a drip sys-
tem, facilitating the supply of water and nutrients directly to the crop root zone in accordance with crop
requirements. Its ability to provide nutrients in small, frequent and localized applications allows closer
synchronization between nutrient supply, crop demand and soil moisture conditions. The increasing de-
mand for efficient use of water and fertilizers has positioned drip fertigation as an important component
of precision and sustainable crop production. This review summarizes the current understanding of drip
fertigation with particular emphasis on different crops. The review examines the influence of fertigation
on crop growth, yield and yield components, produce quality, nutrient uptake and nutrient-use effi-
ciency, water-use efficiency, and nutrient distribution in the root zone. Published evidence indicates that
precise and frequent application of water and nutrients can improve synchronisation between nutrient
supply and crop demand, thereby enhancing nutrient uptake and productivity while reducing unneces-
sary water and fertilizer losses. A meta-analysis reported that drip fertigation increased crop yield, water
productivity and nitrogen-use efficiency by 12.0, 26.4 and 34.3%, respectively, compared with conven-
tional irrigation and fertilizer practices. Vegetable-focused evidence also indicates improvements in
yield, water-use efficiency, nitrogen productivity and quality, although responses vary with crop type,
irrigation amount, nitrogen rate, soil conditions and cultivation environment. The review further dis-
cusses fertigation scheduling, fertilizer concentration, application frequency, root-zone nutrient dynam-
ics, economic considerations and constraints such as initial investment, emitter clogging and improper
nutrient management. Overall, drip fertigation provides an effective pathway for integrating water and
nutrient management and offers considerable potential for improving productivity, resource-use effi-
ciency and sustainability in crop production.
Keywords: Drip fertigation, Nutrient-use efficiency, Nutrient management, Water-use efficiency, Crop
productivity, Root-zone nutrient dynamics, Sustainable agriculture
1. Introduction
Availability of Freshwater is a prime requirement for the agriculture sector, but increasing demand for food
production, domestic use and the industrial sector has intensified the need for efficient irrigation (Verma et
al., 2025) [1]. Agriculture accounts for a very large share of freshwater use in India and emphasises the
growing relevance of micro-irrigation, irrigation scheduling and resource conservation practices. Between
2009–10 and 2019–20, the reported area under micro-irrigation increased from 9.39 to 15.91 Mha, indicating
a substantial shift toward water-efficient technologies, although the area remains small relative to total irri-
gated land. (Shi et al., 2022) [2].
Nutrient losses like leaching, runoff, volatilization, denitrification and other soil processes further complicate
agricultural resource management. Losses are particularly important when fertilizer application is poorly
synchronized with crop demand or when large quantities are exposed to irrigation or rainfall. Fertigation ad-
dresses these limitations by delivering nutrients with irrigation water, allowing the timing, frequency and
concentration of nutrient supply to be adjusted to crop growth and soil conditions (Parihar et al., 2019) [3].
The scientific basis of fertigation lies in matching nutrient supply with water distribution. Instead of apply-
ing a large fertilizer dose at one time, nutrients can be supplied in smaller quantities and delivered repeatedly
through the irrigation system. This ensures nutrients are readily available in the root zone, and the major ad-
vantage of fertigation is synchronisation. Nutrient availability can be ensured in accordance with crop phe-
nological stage, while irrigation can be adjusted to soil moisture demand. When these two streams are coor-
dinated, nutrient concentration in the active root zone can be maintained closer to the level required for plant
uptake.

2. Concept and principle of fertigation
Fertigation is the controlled application of fertilizer through irrigation
water (Hagin et al., 2002) [5]. In practical systems, fertilizer is dis-
solved in irrigation water and injected into the pressurised irrigation
network so that the nutrient solution reaches the crop through emitters
or sprinklers. The process links three management decisions on how
much nutrient is required, when it is required, and where it should be
delivered (Sandal et al., 2015) [6].
Fertigation enables precise, timely application of soluble fertilisers
according to crop demand and growth stage. When combined with
drip irrigation, it improves water and nutrient-use efficiency, enhances
crop productivity and reduces nutrient losses through leaching.
Frequent application of small fertilizer doses is particularly beneficial
in light-textured soils, where nutrients are prone to movement below
the root zone (Ashrafi et al., 2020) [7].
3. Fertigation system and major components
Fertigation equipment is designed to deliver fertilizers accurately
along with irrigation water to crops. The core components include:
Water source and pumping unit
The system begins with a well or water source that supplies irrigation
water. A pump lifts and pressurises the water so it can flow through
the filtration, fertigation and distribution network. A bypass valve is
provided to regulate or divert water flow around the pumping unit
when required.
Sand separator/hydro-cyclone
The sand separator or hydro-cyclone removes heavier particles such
as sand and grit from the irrigation water before it enters the filtration
system. This protects downstream components and reduces the possi-
bility of clogging.
Filtration unit
The sand filter removes relatively fine suspended particles and or-
ganic matter, while the screen filter provides additional filtration be-
fore water enters the distribution network. The backwash valve allows
accumulated impurities to be removed from the sand filter.
Pressure gauge and pressure control
The pressure gauge is used to monitor the operating pressure of the ir-
rigation system. Maintaining appropriate pressure is essential for
achieving uniform water and fertilizer distribution through the
drippers.
Fertilizer injection unit
A Venturi injector is used for injecting soluble fertilizer into the irri-
gation water. The pressure difference created across the Venturi
causes the fertilizer solution to be drawn into the irrigation pipeline.
This allows nutrients to be transported with irrigation water directly
to the crop root zone.
Non-return valve (NRV)
Non-return valves (NRVs) prevent the reverse flow of water or fertil-
izer solution. They protect the pump and prevent contaminated or fer-
tiliser-enriched water from flowing back toward the water source.
Mainline
The mainline is the primary pipeline that carries filtered and ferti-
gated water from the filtration or fertilizer injection unit to different
parts of the field. It forms the main pathway for water distribution.
Sub-main line
The sub-main line receives water from the mainline and distributes it
to individual field sections or groups of laterals. Ball valves are used
to control the flow to different sections, allowing individual areas to
be irrigated separately.
Polytube/laterals
The polytube or lateral lines are smaller pipes laid along the crop
rows. They transport the water–fertilizer mixture close to individual
plants root zone. Several laterals can be connected to a single sub-
main line.
Drippers/emitters
Drippers or emitters are the final delivery points of the system. They
release water and dissolved nutrients at a controlled and low dis-
charge rate near the plant root zone. This minimizes water losses and
improves the efficiency of nutrient application.
Flush valves and end stops
Flush valves are provided at the ends of laterals and other pipeline
sections to remove accumulated sediments and impurities. End stops
close the ends of lateral lines and help maintain proper water pressure
within the system.
Air valve
The air valve releases trapped air from the pipeline and allows air to
enter when necessary during system drainage. Proper air management
helps prevent pressure fluctuations and pipeline-related problems.
Ball valves
Ball valves regulate the flow of water to different sections of the field.
They allow the operator to open or close individual irrigation zones
according to crop and field requirements.

4.1 Quantitative fertigation
A measured amount of fertilizer is injected into the irrigation system
in the time of each water application. Injection may be initiated and
controlled automatically as well as manually. This method is mostly
used in soil.
4.2 Proportional fertigation
Proportional fertigation maintains a defined ratio between fertilizer
solution and irrigation water. It is particularly useful where a rela-
tively constant nutrient concentration is required in the irrigation
stream. This method is commonly used in the soilless media and
sandy soils.
4.3 Split application
Splitting the total fertilizer requirement into multiple applications is a
central fertigation strategy. Khanna et al. (2018) [11] divided fertilizer
application into eight equal splits in maize and reported placement
close to the plant base, helping to minimize leaching and improve nu-
trient utilization.

appropriate approach is one that delivers the required nutrient dose

tion systems because it determines the rate, concentration and unifor-
mity with which dissolved nutrients are introduced into irrigation wa-
ter. The major fertilizer injection methods used in pressurized irriga-
tion systems include Venturi injectors, pressure-differential fertilizer
tanks, and pump-based injection systems (Fan et al., 2017; Omary et
al., 2020) [12, 13].

6. Emitters, filtration and distribution uniformity
Emitters determine how irrigation water and dissolved nutrients are
released to the crop. Different types of emitters: inline emitters, on-
line emitters, pressure-compensating emitters, non-pressure-compen-
sating emitters, adjustable-flow emitters, flag emitters and microtubes
(Zhangzhong et al., 2015) [15].
1. Inline (inbuilt) emitters
Inline emitters are manufactured or inserted inside the dripline at pre-
determined intervals and are therefore suitable where plants are ar-
ranged in regular rows and uniform spacing is required. They are
widely used in vegetable and row-crop production because the emitter
spacing can be selected according to crop geometry. Their perfor-
mance depends on operating pressure, emitter design and the hy-
draulic characteristics of the lateral. (Source: Sokol et al., 2022) [16].
2. Online emitters
Online emitters are installed externally on a plain lateral, allowing the
emitter position to be selected according to individual plant locations.
This makes them particularly useful for orchards, plantations and
crops with irregular plant spacing. Their flexibility also allows the
number and location of emitters to be modified according to changing
crop water requirements.
3. Pressure-compensating (PC) emitters
Pressure-compensating emitters are designed to maintain a relatively
constant discharge despite variations in operating pressure. A flexible
diaphragm or regulating mechanism adjusts the flow through the
emitter, thereby improving discharge uniformity along the lateral.
Consequently, PC emitters are particularly useful in long laterals,
sloping or uneven fields, and systems where pressure variation is sig-
nificant. Recent research confirms that PC emitters can improve irri-
gation uniformity, although their compensation mechanism can be af-
fected by clogging and deterioration under adverse water-quality
conditions.
4. Non-pressure-compensating emitters
In non-pressure-compensating emitters, discharge varies with the
pressure available at the emitter. Therefore, emitters located at differ-
ent positions along a lateral may have different discharge rates when
pressure losses occur. They are generally simpler in construction and
can be economical for short laterals and relatively level fields, where
pressure variation is limited. Research on emitter hydraulics has
demonstrated the importance of the pressure–discharge relationship in
determining the uniformity of non-PC emitters.
5. Adjustable-flow emitters
Adjustable-flow emitters allow the discharge rate to be modified man-
ually according to the water requirement of individual plants. This
characteristic provides flexibility where plants have different water
requirements or where irrigation needs change during crop develop-
ment. Such emitters can also be adjusted or closed when irrigation is
not required at a particular location. Their suitability is therefore
greater in systems requiring individualized water management.
6. Flag emitters
Flag emitters are a type of online emitter designed to facilitate flush-
ing and cleaning of the flow path. Their removable or accessible de-
sign can make maintenance easier where clogging is a concern. They
can be useful in orchards, nurseries and other applications where indi-
vidual emitters are installed according to plant position. However,
their performance still depends on irrigation-water quality, filtration
and regular maintenance because all drip emitters have relatively
small flow passages that are susceptible to clogging.
7. Microtubes
Microtubes are simple small-diameter tubes used to deliver water or
fertigation solution directly to individual plants. Their discharge can
be regulated by changing the tube diameter, length and hydraulic
characteristics. Microtube systems are useful where a simple and in-
expensive point-delivery arrangement is required and can be inte-
grated with lateral lines or other emitter arrangements. Their hy-
draulic performance, however, needs to be properly designed to ob-
tain the desired discharge and uniformity.
Operational implication
Regular filter cleaning, monitoring of emitter discharge, maintenance
of appropriate pressure and periodic system flushing are essential.
Where water quality is poor, filtration and chemical treatment should
be selected according to the nature of suspended and dissolved
contaminants.

7. Fertilizer selection for fertigation
Fertilizers used in fertigation should dissolve rapidly and completely
under field conditions. It should contain minimal insoluble material
and remain chemically compatible with other fertilizers and irrigation
water. Fertilizers with high nutrient concentration, rapid dissolution,
low residue, and rapidly available to plants can be used for
fertigation.

8. Fertilizer compatibility and precipitation
Chemical compatibility is one of the most important safeguards
against emitter clogging. Precipitation may occur when incompatible
fertilizer salts react in concentrated stock solutions or in irrigation wa-
ter. Avoid mixing calcium nitrate with phosphate or sulphate fertiliz-
ers, magnesium sulphate with mono- or diammonium phosphate, and
phosphoric acid with sulphates of iron, zinc, copper and manganese.
8.1 Compatibility management
In fertigation systems, fertilizer compatibility management is essen-
tial to prevent precipitation, clogging of filters and emitters, and un-
even nutrient distribution. Fertilizers that are chemically incompatible
should be prepared in separate stock solutions, and their compatibility
should be tested using the actual irrigation water before field applica-
tion. The pH and chemical composition of irrigation water, particu-
larly the concentrations of calcium, magnesium, bicarbonate and iron,

9. Fertigation scheduling
Fertigation scheduling refers to determining the appropriate quantity,
concentration, timing and frequency of nutrient application according
to crop and field requirements. Proper scheduling improves nutrient-
use efficiency, minimizes nutrient losses and ensures that nutrients are
supplied according to crop demand. The major factors influencing
fertigation scheduling include crop growth stage, site-specific nutrient
requirements, irrigation requirements, irrigation-water pH and electri-
cal conductivity (EC). Crop stage determines the changing nutrient
demand throughout the growing period, while site-specific soil and
crop requirements help determine the appropriate nutrient dose.
Irrigation requirements influence the timing and duration of fertiga-
tion, whereas pH and EC are important for maintaining nutrient avail-
ability and avoiding excessive salt accumulation in the root zone.
Therefore, integrating these factors is essential for achieving efficient
and uniform nutrient application under precision fertigation (Singh et
al., 2026) [18]. Top of Form Bottom of Form Fertigation Scheduling
provides flexibility in both nutrient quantity and frequency.
Fertigation events may be weekly, fortnightly or monthly depending
on crop and system, but high-frequency applications are often useful
where soluble nutrients are vulnerable to leaching (Thompson et al.,
2003) [19].

10. Nutrient distribution in the root zone
The spatial distribution of nutrients depends on the interaction be-
tween fertilizer movement and the irrigation wetting pattern.
Conventional soil application followed by surface irrigation can pro-
duce uneven nutrient movement, whereas drip irrigation concentrates
water near the root zone. Drip fertigation adds dissolved nutrients di-
rectly to this wetted region and therefore offers greater control over
placement and timing. (Thomas et al., 2024) [24]. Soil fertiliser appli-
cation with traditional irrigation water: nutrient distribution is influ-
enced by runoff, infiltration, deep percolation and soil heterogeneity.
Soil fertilizer application with drip irrigation: water placement is pre-
cise, but nutrient movement still depends on soil redistribution and
fertilizer dissolution. Drip fertigation: water and soluble nutrients are
delivered together, allowing frequent and localised nutrient supply.
Drip fertigation is the most efficient approach for precision farming
and resource conservation
In sandy or coarse-textured soils, nutrient movement can be rapid be-
cause of high infiltration and relatively low nutrient retention capac-
ity. Frequent small fertigation doses can therefore reduce the amount
of soluble nutrient exposed to downward movement at any single
time, provided irrigation is not excessive.
11. Boosting crop growth through drip fertigation
Fertigation has developed as an effective nutrition management ap-
proach that enhances plant growth and development by precisely and
timely applying water-soluble nutrients through irrigation water.
Fertigation improves nutrient availability and uptake by allowing for
real-time adjustment of nutrient concentrations and ratios based on
crop growth stages and environmental conditions, thereby improving
key growth attributes such as plant height, root development, shoot
elongation, leaf area, branching, and overall plant vigour.
Several studies have reported improved growth of vegetable crops un-
der fertigation. Nikzad et al. (2020) [25] observed maximum plant
height and plant spread with 100% RDF applied in 12 equal splits at
5-day intervals in cabbage with fertigation compared with conven-
tional soil fertilization. In chilli, fertigation with 180:120:180 kg N:
P2O5:K2O ha−1 using water-soluble fertilizers resulted in higher plant
height (104.27 cm), number of branches plant−1 (16.71) and leaf area
plant−1 (89.44 dm2) than conventional fertilizer application (Reddy,
2017) [26]. In tomato, fertigation with 75% K and 100% N and P pro-
duced the highest plant height (77.6 cm), root length (17.2 cm) and
plant dry weight (107.2 g) among the treatments studied (Reddy et
al., 2022) [27]. Similarly, Savitha et al. (2010) [28] reported that apply-
ing 75% RDF through fertigation resulted in greater plant height,
number of leaves plant−1 and root length in both big onion and aggre-
gatum onion compared with soil application. Gupta et al. (2010) [29]
found that fertigation with 60% of the recommended NPK dose pro-
duced the highest plant height (132.4 cm), primary branches plant−1
(8.20) and number of nodes on the main stem (29.2) in tomato com-
pared with higher fertigation levels and soil-applied fertiliser. In broc-
coli, application of the recommended fertilizer dose (120:60:60 kg N:
P2O5:K2O ha−1) through fertigation significantly improved plant
height and number of leaves plant−1 under Tarai conditions of
Uttarakhand (Debbarma et al., 2021) [30]. Overall, these findings indi-
cate that drip fertigation can enhance vegetative growth by improving
the timing, availability and uptake of nutrients, with the magnitude of
response depending on crop, nutrient dose and fertigation schedule.
12. Fertigation for higher crop productivity
Drip fertigation improves yield attributes by maintaining favourable
soil moisture and nutrient availability within the active root zone,
thereby supporting efficient nutrient uptake and assimilate partition-
ing towards reproductive organs. Several studies have reported signif-
icant improvements in yield attributes of different crops under drip
fertigation. Rajalingam et al. (2022) [31] reported that fertigation with
150:100:150 kg N: P2O5:K2O ha−1 produced the highest fruit yield of
5.53 kg plant−1 and 37.11 t ha−1 compared with conventional fertil-
izer application. In chilli, fertigation increased dry chilli yield by
27.87–52.40% over furrow irrigation combined with conventional fer-
tilizer application (Reddy et al., 2016). Similarly, application of
125:100:125 kg N: P2O5:K2O ha−1 through fertigation increased
chilli yield by 14.84–61.55% compared with the control treatments
(Nair et al., 2023) [32]. Patil et al. (2015) [33] recorded the highest
capsicum fruit yield under drip irrigation at 100% evaporative replen-
ishment combined with 75% of the recommended nitrogen dose. In
chilli, fertigation with 263:60:345 kg N: P2O5:K2O ha−1 resulted in
the highest fruit weight compared with the conventional fertilizer rec-
ommendation of 75:40:25 kg N: P2O5:K2O ha−1 (Das et al., 2026)
[34]. Anjum et al. (2022) [35] also reported the maximum cucumber
yield of 66.71 t ha−1 with fertigation at 80% of the recommended fer-
tilizer dose. These findings indicate that appropriate fertigation levels
can improve yield attributes such as fruit weight, fruit yield per plant
and total yield, although the optimum nutrient dose varies with crop,
growing conditions and fertigation schedule. Basavaraju et al. (2014)
evaluated fertigation levels in coconut over several seasons. The
pooled nut yield increased from 68.31 nuts palm−1 in the unfertilized
control to 95.26 nuts palm−1 under 100% NPK through drip fertiga-
tion. The 75% treatment produced 87.41 nuts palm−1, while 100%
NPK through soil application produced 87.57 nuts palm−1.
13. Effect of fertigation on nutrient use efficiency
Nutrient-use efficiency (NUE) describes how effectively absorbed or
applied nutrients are converted into biomass or economic yield.
Fertigation can improve NUE by increasing the temporal and spatial
coincidence between nutrient availability and plant demand. Nutrient-
use efficiency (NUE) reflects the effectiveness with which applied
nutrients are utilized by crops. Drip fertigation can improve NUE by
supplying nutrients in small, frequent and localized applications,
thereby maintaining nutrient availability in the active root zone and
reducing losses. Gupta et al. (2015) [36] reported that drip irrigation at
80% ET combined with 60% RDF through fertigation recorded fertil-
izer-use efficiencies of 10.9, 18.3 and 27.4 q kg−1 N, P and K, respec-
tively. In chilli, Singh et al. (2017) [37] found that the highest nutrient-
use efficiency was obtained with 50% RDF through fertigation com-
bined with two foliar sprays of 1% urea phosphate, indicating that ef-
ficient nutrient application can improve fertilizer utilization even at a
reduced fertilizer dose. Similarly, Sandal et al. (2015) [6] evaluated
50, 100, 150 and 200% RDF through fertigation in broccoli and re-
ported that the 50% RDF fertigation treatment recorded high nutrient-
use efficiency, with savings of 50% fertilizer and 20–60% water com-
pared with conventional fertilization while maintaining comparable
yield levels. These findings demonstrate that higher fertilizer applica-
tion does not necessarily increase nutrient-use efficiency; rather, pre-
cise nutrient placement, suitable fertilizer rates and synchronization
with crop demand are important for maximizing fertilizer utilization.
14. Enhancing water-use efficiency through drip
fertigation

water use. In fertigation systems, WUE is influenced not only by irri-
gation quantity but also by the uniformity and timing of water and nu-
trient delivery. Kumari et al. (2014) [38] reported the highest WUE of
5.24 q ha−1 cm−1 with water-soluble fertilizer at 70% RDF under drip
irrigation. The study also reported 33.94% water saving with drip fer-
tigation compared with conventional irrigation. In tomato, Sharma

15. Fertigation under poly house, hydroponics and
soilless system
The results showed that 100% recommended fertilizer through ferti-
gation produced the best performance in cucumber under polyhouse
conditions, with productivity reaching up to 720 q ha−1. Protected
cultivation provides an environment in which water, nutrients and mi-
croclimate can be controlled more closely, making fertigation particu-
larly valuable (Kumar, 2020) [42]. Hydroponics grows plants without
soil using mineral nutrient solutions. The nutrient film technique
(NFT), deep-water culture, wick systems, ebb-and-flow systems and
aeroponics are major hydroponic approaches. (Naresh et al., 2024)
[43].
Anusree et al. (2025) [44] reported superior tomato growth and yield
under an ebb-and-flow system, while Shtaya et al. (2022) [45] found
NFT to have the highest WUE among the tested lettuce systems.
These findings show that fertigation principles extend beyond field
drip systems into controlled-environment agriculture. The potential
reductions in fertilizer requirement when nutrient-containing re-
claimed water is used, but emphasizes the need for adequate treat-
ment. Advanced treatment and disinfection may be required to control
contaminants before agricultural use. Wastewater fertigation requires
monitoring of nutrient loading, salinity, sodium, pathogens, heavy
metals and emerging contaminants. Its sustainability therefore de-
pends on treatment quality, crop selection, application rate and regula-
tory compliance.
16. Challenges, environmental risks and future
research
16.1 Emitter clogging
Clogging can be physical, chemical or biological. Suspended particles
may cause physical blockage, precipitation of dissolved minerals may
produce chemical deposits, and microbial growth can produce biolog-
ical blockage. It was reported to be around 37% biological clogging,
whereas physical clogging is 31% and chemical clogging is 22% (Shi
et al., 2022) [2].
16.2 Salinity and leaching
Repeated fertilizer application can increase salt concentration in the
root zone if irrigation is insufficient to maintain an appropriate salt
balance. Conversely, excessive irrigation can move nitrate and other
mobile nutrients below the root zone. Effective fertigation therefore
requires simultaneous control of fertilizer concentration and irrigation
volume.
16.3 Investment and skill requirements
Initial investment in pumps, filters, injectors, valves, automation and
drip infrastructure may constrain adoption among smallholders.
Maintenance, calibration and water-quality management also require
technical skills.
16.4 Research priorities
Develop crop and soil-specific fertigation schedules based on
real-time crop demand.
Integrate soil moisture, EC, pH and nutrient sensors with auto-
mated fertigation.
Develop decision-support systems for sandy and other high-
leaching soils.
Improve fertilizer compatibility protocols for locally available
irrigation waters.
Quantify long-term nitrate movement and greenhouse-gas
implications.
Evaluate economic returns for small and marginal farmers.
Standardize safe protocols for treated wastewater fertigation.
17. Conclusion
Drip fertigation provides an integrated and precise approach to water
and nutrient management by delivering soluble fertilizers with irriga-
tion water at controlled rates, frequencies and locations. The reviewed
studies demonstrate its potential to improve crop growth, yield, nutri-
ent-use efficiency, water-use efficiency and overall input-use effi-
ciency across different crops and production systems. Its major ad-
vantage is the synchronization of nutrient supply with crop demand
while maintaining favourable moisture and nutrient conditions in the
active root zone. However, fertigation is not automatically efficient
and its performance depends on proper irrigation scheduling, fertilizer
selection and compatibility, water quality, filtration, injector calibra-
tion, pressure control and system maintenance. Emitter clogging,
salinity, nitrate leaching, high initial investment and technical require-
ments remain important limitations. Future development should focus
on sensor-based and automated fertigation, crop- and soil-specific nu-
trient scheduling, real-time monitoring of soil moisture, EC and pH,
and site-specific decision-support systems. Thus, when properly de-
signed and managed, drip fertigation can serve as a key technology
for precision agriculture, resource conservation and sustainable inten-
sification of crop production.
