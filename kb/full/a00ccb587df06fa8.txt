Abstract 
Rapid growth in food production, livestock farming, and urban consumption has increased organic -
waste generation while simultaneously intensifying demand for affordable protein and lipid sources for 
animal feeds. Black soldier fly larvae ( Hermetia illucens ) provide a bioconversion route in which 
heterogeneous organic residues can be transformed into nutrient -dense biomass; however, treating all 
organic wastes as equivalent substrates overlooks major differences in protein, carbohydrate, lipid, 
fibre, moisture, mineral content, and biodegradability. This study evaluates how substrate -specific 
nutritional characteristics influ ence larval growth, biomass conversion, nutrient accumulation, and 
feed-ingredient quality. Organic wastes representing contrasting nutrient profiles are compared under 
standardized rearing conditions using larval weight gain, development rate, survival, b iomass yield, 
substrate reduction, and feed-conversion indicators. Proximate and mineral analyses determine whether 
substrate-dependent growth responses correspond with changes in larval crude protein, lipid, fibre, ash, 
and mineral composition. Integratin g substrate composition with biological and nutritional responses 
identifies which waste characteristics favour rapid biomass production and which promote nutritionally 
superior larvae. The study therefore establishes a substrate -to-larva-to-feed relations hip for selecting 
organic waste streams that simultaneously improve waste bioconversion efficiency and produce black 
soldier fly biomass suitable for sustainable animal-feed formulation. 
 
Keywords: Hermetia illucens , substrate composition , bioconversion eff iciency, larval biomass , 
nutritional quality, alternative animal feed 
 
1. Introduction 
1.1 Organic Waste Generation and the Search for Sustainable Feed Resources 
The increasing generation of organic waste from households, food -processing industries, 
agricultural activities, markets, and livestock production presents a growing environmental 
and resource -management challenge. Large quantities of biodegradable materials are 
disposed of through landfilling, uncontrolled dumping, or other pathways that fai l to recover 
their residual nutritional value [1]. Such practices contribute to greenhouse -gas emissions, 
odour generation, leachate formation, and inefficient use of nutrients that could potentially be 
returned to productive systems [2]. At the same time, livestock and aquaculture production 
remain highly dependent on conventional feed ingredients, particularly soybean meal and 
fishmeal, whose production is associated with land -use pressure, fluctuating prices, 
competition with human food systems, and conc erns regarding long -term supply 
sustainability [3]. These interconnected challenges have increased interest in circular 
bioeconomy strategies capable of transforming organic residues into useful feed resources. 
Among emerging biological conversion approach es, insect -based systems offer particular 
potential because insects can utilize nutrient -containing organic materials and convert them 
into concentrated animal biomass suitable for further evaluation as feed ingredients [4]. 
 
1.2 Black Soldier Fly Larvae as Biological Waste-Converting Organisms 
Black Soldier Fly Larvae (BSFL), Hermetia illucens, have attracted considerable attention as 
biological agents for converting organic residues into potentially valuable biomass.

The larvae exhibit rapid growth, high f eeding activity, and 
the capacity to develop on diverse substrates, including food 
waste, agricultural residues, livestock manure, and agro -
industrial by -products [5]. During larval development, 
nutrients obtained from these materials are incorporated into 
biomass containing appreciable concentrations of protein 
and lipids, thereby connecting waste reduction with nutrient 
recovery. This capacity makes BSFL particularly relevant to 
circular production systems in which organic materials are 
redirected from di sposal towards resource generation [6]. 
However, successful larval production cannot be assumed 
merely because a waste material is biodegradable. Growth, 
survival, development, and biomass composition vary 
substantially according to the nutritional and 
physicochemical characteristics of the rearing substrate. 
 
1.3 Substrate Composition as a Determinant of Larval 
Performance and Nutritional Quality 
Substrate quality represents a major determinant of both 
BSFL productivity and the nutritional characteristics o f 
harvested larvae. Differences in protein, carbohydrate, lipid, 
fibre, mineral content, moisture, and nutrient accessibility 
can alter larval feeding behaviour, survival, developmental 
rate, weight gain, biomass yield, and bioconversion 
efficiency [7]. Nu trient balance is particularly important 
because substrates with high concentrations of a single 
nutrient may not necessarily provide the combinations 
required for efficient larval development. High fibre content 
or poorly accessible nutrients may constrai n utilization, 
whereas inappropriate moisture conditions can affect 
feeding activity and substrate aeration. Importantly, 
substrate composition can also influence larval body 
composition, including protein, lipid, ash, and other 
nutritionally relevant frac tions [8]. Consequently, the 
substrate producing the greatest biomass may not 
necessarily generate larvae with the most desirable nutrient 
profile for feed formulation. Evaluation of substrate 
suitability therefore requires simultaneous consideration of 
biological productivity, waste -conversion efficiency, and 
nutritional quality rather than reliance on larval weight 
alone. 
 
1.4 Study Rationale, Objectives, and Hypotheses 
Against this background, this study investigates the 
influence of selected organic waste substrates on the growth 
performance, nutrient composition, and potential feed value 
of BSFL. It is hypothesized that differences among 
substrates will significantly influence larval growth and 
survival, substrate utilization and bioconversion efficiency , 
and the accumulation of crude protein, lipid, ash, fibre, and 
other nutritionally relevant constituents [9]. The study further 
evaluates whether substrates supporting superior growth 
also produce larvae with nutritional characteristics 
favourable for fee d-ingredient applications. By integrating 
growth, conversion, and compositional indicators within a 
common experimental framework, the study seeks to 
identify substrate-dependent trade-offs and determine which 
organic waste materials provide the strongest combination 
of efficient bioconversion, larval productivity, and 
nutritional potential for subsequent feed applications. 
 
2. Materials and Methods 
2.1 Experimental Design and Organic Waste Substrate 
Selection 
A completely randomized experimental design was adopted 
to determine the influence of contrasting organic waste 
substrates on the growth and nutritional characteristics of 
Black Soldier Fly Larvae (BSFL). Completely randomized 
designs provide a suitable basis for comparing dietary or 
substrate treatmen ts where experimental units are 
maintained under broadly homogeneous environmental 
conditions [8]. Four substrate treatments were evaluated: 
Fruit and Vegetable Waste (FVW), Mixed Food Waste 
(FW), Poultry Manure (PM), and a wheat -bran-based 
Reference Diet (RD). Each treatment consisted of three 
independent replicates containing 200 larvae, and the 
feeding experiment was conducted for 14 days. Organic 
waste streams can differ considerably in nutrient density, 
moisture, fibre content, and digestibility, makin g substrate 
selection an important determinant of BSFL production 
performance [6]. Fruit and vegetable waste represented 
predominantly plant -derived residues, while mixed food 
waste provided a heterogeneous combination of cooked 
food materials. Poultry man ure represented a nitrogen and 
mineral-rich livestock residue, whereas the reference diet 
provided a standardized benchmark. Experimental 
containers were randomly positioned within the rearing 
environment to minimize systematic spatial effects.

2.2 Substrate Collection, Preparation, and 
Standardization 
Organic waste materials were collected from representative 
local waste -generation sources and process ed under 
standardized conditions before larval introduction. 
Appropriate preprocessing of heterogeneous organic 
residues is important for reducing physical variability and 
improving accessibility of the substrate to feeding larvae [10]. 
Non-biodegradable contaminants, including plastics, metals, 
stones, and packaging materials, were manually removed

before experimental preparation. Fruit and vegetable 
residues and mixed food waste were chopped into smaller 
particles and homogenized to minimize variation in particle 
size and nutrient distribution among replicates. Poultry 
manure was thoroughly mixed before allocation to the 
experimental containers. Moisture was adjusted to 
approximately 65 -70% where necessary because excessive 
dryness or water saturation can restrict larval feeding, 
aeration, and substrate utilization [7]. Equal quantities of 
prepared substrate were subsequently allocated to each 
experimental unit on a dry-matter basis. Treatment materials 
were processed separately to prevent cross -contamination. 
Standardization of moisture, particle size, substrate quantity, 
storage, and handling reduced methodological variability 
and strengthened attribution of observed biological 
differences to substrate treatment. 
 
2.3 Larval Rearing and Experimental Conditions 
Black soldier fly larvae were obtained from an established 
Hermetia illucens colony and synchronized according to age 
before allocation to the experimental treatments. Larval age 
is an important consideration because feeding intensity, 
nutrient requirements, and biomass accumulation change 
considerably during BSFL development [9]. Six-day-old 
larvae of comparable size were randomly distributed among 
ventilated plastic rearing containers at a stocking density of 
200 larvae per experimental unit. Stocking density was 
maintained consistently across treatments because 
differences in la rval density can alter competition for 
substrate and consequently influence individual and total 
biomass production [12]. All experimental units were 
maintained at approximately 28 ±2 °C and 65 -75% relative 
humidity under a 12 h light and 12 h dark regime . 
Environmental conditions were maintained as consistently 
as possible because temperature and substrate conditions 
influence BSFL development and feeding activity [6]. 
Substrate availability and moisture were monitored 
throughout the 14 -day cultivation pe riod. At harvest, larvae 
were separated from residual substrate by sieving, cleaned, 
counted, weighed, and prepared for subsequent 
compositional analysis.

2.6 Proximate and Nutritional Composition Analysis 
Following harvest, representative larvae from each replicate 
were cleaned and subjected to a short fasting period to 
reduce interference from residual material within the 
digestive tract. Sample preparation before compositional 
analysis is important for obtaining comparable estimates of 
the intrinsic nutritional composition of insect biomass [15]. 
Larvae were subsequently dried to constant mass and 
homogenized be fore laboratory analysis. Moisture content 
was determined from mass loss during oven drying, while 
ash content was measured following controlled incineration 
in a muffle furnace. Standard proximate-analysis procedures 
provide established approaches for cha racterizing the 
principal nutritional fractions of feed materials [12]. Crude 
protein was determined from measured nitrogen 
concentration using an appropriate nitrogen -to-protein 
conversion factor. Crude lipid was quantified by solvent 
extraction, while cr ude fibre was determined following 
sequential acid and alkaline digestion. Where required, 
carbohydrate content was estimated by difference. Nutrient 
concentrations were reported primarily on a dry-matter basis 
because this permits more meaningful comparis on among 
larvae containing different amounts of water [9]. Replicate 
measurements were used to characterize substrate -
dependent variation in harvested larval nutritional quality. 
 
2.7 Statistical Analysis 
Each rearing container was treated as an independent 
experimental unit, providing three biological replicates for 
each substrate treatment. Experimental-unit independence is 
important for avoiding artificial inflation of sample size 
when multiple organisms are maintained within the same 
rearing container [13]. Data were summarized as 
mean±standard deviation and analyzed using R statistical 
software. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro -Wilk 
test, while homogeneity of variance was examined before 
parametric treatment comparisons. Treatment effects on 
larval growth, survival, harvested biomass, substrate 
reduction, bioconversion efficiency, and nutrient 
composition were evaluated using one -way analysis of 
variance. Where significant differences were detected, 
Tukey's honestly significant difference test w as applied for 
post-hoc comparisons [15]. Statistical significance was 
established at p < 0.05. Pearson correlation analysis was 
subsequently used to examine associations among substrate 
characteristics, larval growth, bioconversion efficiency, and 
nutrient composition [11]. This integrated analysis provided 
the statistical basis for determining whether differences in 
substrate quality were associated with corresponding 
changes in biological performance and feed -related 
nutritional characteristics. 
 
3. Results 
3.1 Influence of Organic Waste Substrate on Larval 
Growth and Survival 
Initial larval weights were comparable across treatments, 
ranging from 17.8 ±0.6 to 18.2 ±0.5 mg per larva, with no 
significant treatment effect at the beginning of the 
experiment (p = 0.684). This initial comparability reduced 
the likelihood that subsequent differences resulted from pre -
existing variation in larval size. By day 14, substrate type 
produced a significant effect on final larval weight ( p < 
0.001). Larvae reared on mix ed Food Waste (FW) attained 
the highest final weight of 189.6 ±6.8 mg, followed by the 
Reference Diet (RD) at 176.3±5.9 mg, poultry manure (PM) 
at 151.7±6.4 mg, and Fruit and Vegetable Waste (FVW) at 
137.5±5.6 mg. Differences in nutrient availabilit y among 
organic substrates can generate substantial variation in 
BSFL biomass accumulation [16]. 
The same treatment pattern was evident for weight gain and 
specific growth rate. FW produced a mean weight gain of 
171.5±6.6 mg and an SGR of 16.78 ±0.25% d ay⁻¹, 
significantly exceeding FVW and PM. Total harvested 
biomass reached 35.1 ±1.2 g per replicate under FW

3.2 Substrate Reduction and Bioconversion Performance 
Substrate utilization also varied significantly among 
treatments. The greatest substrate reduction was recorded 
for FVW at 72.6 ±2.4%, followed by FW at 68.8 ±2.1%, RD 
at 63.5 ±1.9%, and PM at 57.4 ±2.6% ( p < 0.001). The 
integrated association pattern in Figure 3a shows that 
substrate reduction and biomass -related responses did not 
follow identical relationships across the measured 
characteristics. High reduction of fruit and vegetable 
residues was therefore not accompanied by the highest 
larval biomass production. This distinction is relevant 
because substrate reduction measures disappearance of 
material rather than the proportion of consumed nutrients 
incorporated into insect biomass [18]. 
FW instead demonstrated the highest bioconversion 
efficiency, r eaching 18.7±0.9%, compared with 16.9 ±0.8% 
for RD, 13.6 ±0.7% for PM, and 12.4 ±0.6% for FVW. Its 
feed conversion ratio was correspondingly lower at 
2.74±0.16, indicating more efficient transformation of 
consumed substrate into larval biomass. The multivariate 
separation of the substrate treatments further illustrates the 
association of FW with favourable biomass and 
bioconversion responses (Figure 3b). Efficient BSFL 
production depends on nutrient conversion as well as 
organic-matter reduction [13]. Conversely, the negative 
relationship between substrate crude fibre and bioconversion 
efficiency (Figure 3d) indicates that increasing fibre content 
was associated with reduced conversion performance ( r = 
−0.79, p = 0.020). Consequently, although FVW ach ieved 
the greatest waste reduction, FW provided the strongest 
overall balance between substrate utilization and 
recoverable biomass production. 
 
3.3 Effects of Substrate Type on Larval Nutrient 
Composition 
Marked differences in harvested larval composition 
accompanied the observed growth responses. Crude protein 
ranged from 38.6 ±1.3% DM in larvae reared on FVW to 
46.8±1.5% DM in larvae produced on PM. Larvae reared on 
FW contained 44.7 ±1.2% crude protein, while those 
receiving RD contained 42.3±1.4%. The positive association 
between substrate crude protein and larval crude protein is 
evident in Figure 3c ( r = 0.81, p = 0.015), indicating that 
protein-rich substrates were generally associated with 
greater protein concentrations in harvested larvae. The 
broader correlation structure presented in Figure 3a further 
demonstrates the relationship between substrate nutritional 
characteristics and larval nutrient accumulation. The 
relatively high protein concentration associated with PM 
was therefore consisten t with its higher substrate crude -
protein content, although this treatment did not generate the 
greatest biomass. Substrate composition can influence larval 
nutrient accumulation without producing proportional 
effects on growth [17].

DM, followed by RD at 27.8 ±1.3%, FVW at 24.2 ±1.2%,

during BSFL development [21]. The multivariate distribution 
in Figure 3b shows FW positioned in association with 
substrate lipid and carbohydrate characteristics, whereas

separation am ong substrate treatments. Larval ash was

3.4 Relationships between Substrate Characteristics, 
Growth, and Nutrient Accumulation 
Correlation analysis provided further evidence that substrate 
characteristics were associated diff erently with growth and 
larval composition. Substrate crude protein was positively 
correlated with larval crude protein ( r = 0.81, p = 0.015), 
supporting an association between dietary nitrogen 
availability and protein accumulation in harvested biomass. 
Similar substrate-dependent variation in BSFL composition 
has been reported when larvae are reared on nutritionally 
contrasting organic materials [19]. Substrate lipid content 
was positively associated with larval lipid concentration (r = 
0.86, p = 0.006) an d final larval weight ( r = 0.74, p = 
0.036). 
In contrast, substrate crude fibre was negatively correlated 
with final larval weight ( r = −0.76, p = 0.029) and 
bioconversion efficiency (r = −0.79, p = 0.020). Fibrous and 
less readily digestible fractions can limit the accessibility of 
nutrients required for rapid larval biomass production [15]. 
Substrate moisture showed a moderate positive association 
with substrate reduction but a weaker relationship with 
biomass production, further demonstrating that rapid 
disappearance of substrate was not equivalent to efficient 
biomass conversion. These relationships collectively 
indicated that balanced nutrient accessibility, rather than the 
concentration of any single component, was more closely 
associated with favourable production outcomes [22].

3.5 Comparative Ranking for Feed-Ingredient Potential 
Integration of growth, conversion, and nutritional indicators 
identified FW as the strongest overall substrate for 
producing BSFL biomass with potential feed -ingredient 
value. FW generated the greatest final larval weight, 
harvested biomass, survival, and bioconversion efficiency 
while simultaneously producing larvae containing 44.7% 
crude protein and the highest lipid concentration of 31.4% 
DM. Protein and lipid are particularly relevant components 
when BSFL biomass is considered as an alternative nutrient 
source for animal-feed formulation [20]. 
PM ranked highest for crude protein but performed less 
favourably for growth, survival, bioconversion, and lipid 
accumulation. Its comparatively high larval ash 
concentration also demonstrated that high protein 
concentration alone was insufficient for identifying the most 
balanced substrate treatment. FVW achieved the greatest 
substrate reduction but generated the lowest harvested 
biomass, protein concentration, and bioconversion 
efficiency, emphasizing the distinct ion between waste -
management performance and feed-biomass production. RD 
provided consistently strong growth and nutritional 
performance but lacked the same direct organic -waste 
valorization advantage as the waste treatments. Accordingly, 
the integrated ra nking was FW > RD > PM > FVW for 
overall feed -ingredient potential. This ranking reflects 
compositional and production characteristics only and does 
not demonstrate feeding efficacy, digestibility, safety, or 
animal-performance benefits, which require dedi cated 
feeding trials before practical feed recommendations can be 
established. 
 
4. Discussion 
4.1 Substrate -Dependent Regulation of BSFL Growth 
Performance 
The marked differences in larval growth among substrates 
demonstrate that the capacity of BSFL to uti lize organic 
waste does not imply equivalent biological performance 
across waste types. Larvae reared on FW achieved the 
highest final weight, weight gain, harvested biomass, and 
survival, indicating that its comparatively balanced protein, 
lipid, and carb ohydrate composition provided readily 
accessible nutrients for tissue development. Previous studies 
have similarly demonstrated that BSFL growth is strongly 
influenced by the balance and availability of nutrients within 
the rearing medium [23]. Although PM contained the highest 
crude protein concentration, larvae reared on this substrate 
did not attain the greatest biomass, suggesting that high 
dietary protein alone was insufficient to maximize growth. 
Nutrient utilization can be constrained by mineral 
concentration, substrate structure, digestibility, and 
imbalances between available protein and energy [21]. 
The comparatively lower growth recorded for FVW may 
therefore reflect its lower protein and lipid concentrations 
together with its higher fibre content . Structural plant 
components can reduce nutrient accessibility and limit the 
amount of metabolizable substrate available for rapid larval 
development [26]. Moisture may also have contributed to 
treatment differences because excessive water can alter 
aeration and the physical feeding environment. The superior 
performance of FW consequently appears to reflect an 
advantageous combination of nutrient density, accessibility, 
moisture, and substrate structure rather than dominance of 
any single nutritional constituent. 
4.2 Nutrient Availability, Waste Reduction, and 
Bioconversion Efficiency 
The substrate -utilization results further demonstrate that 
waste reduction and biomass conversion represent related 
but distinct components of BSFL performance. FVW 
showed the greatest substrate reduction, yet produced the 
lowest bioconversion efficiency and substantially less 
harvested biomass than FW. Organic -material reduction 
during BSFL cultivation encompasses larval consumption as 
well as metabolic, microbial, and moistur e-associated 
changes and therefore cannot be interpreted entirely as 
conversion into insect tissue [24]. A considerable proportion 
of consumed nutrients may be utilized for maintenance 
metabolism and respiration rather than retained as new 
biomass. 
The low er conversion efficiency observed for FVW may 
additionally reflect its relatively high fibre concentration. 
Less digestible fractions can increase apparent substrate 
disappearance without providing an equivalent supply of 
assimilable nutrients for larval g rowth [27]. In contrast, FW 
combined high substrate reduction with the greatest 
bioconversion efficiency and lowest feed conversion ratio, 
indicating more effective recovery of substrate nutrients as 
larval biomass. Microbial transformation within organic 
substrates may also modify nutrient accessibility during 
larval feeding [22]. These findings indicate that evaluating 
waste substrates solely according to reduction percentage 
could favour materials that perform well in waste 
degradation but poorly in productive nutrient recovery. 
 
4.3 Substrate -Mediated Changes in Larval Nutrient 
Composition 
Differences in larval proximate composition demonstrate 
that substrate selection influenced not only the quantity of 
biomass produced but also its nutritional characteristics. PM 
generated larvae with the highest crude protein 
concentration, while FW produced larvae containing slightly 
less protein but substantially more lipid. The positive 
relationship between substrate and larval protein 
concentrations suggests that ni trogen availability 
contributed to protein accumulation. Nevertheless, larval 
composition cannot be regarded as a direct reproduction of 
substrate composition because BSFL actively digest, 
assimilate, transform, store, and expend nutrients during 
development [25]. 
The particularly high lipid concentration of FW -derived 
larvae may reflect the combined availability of dietary lipid 
and metabolizable energy. Excess assimilated carbohydrate 
can contribute to lipid accumulation through insect 
metabolic pathways, meaning that larval fat concentration 
may respond to overall energy availability rather than 
dietary lipid alone [28]. Conversely, the higher ash 
concentration observed in PM -derived larvae corresponded 
with the mineral -rich nature of the manure substrate . This 
result illustrates why elevated crude protein should not 
independently determine the nutritional desirability of BSFL 
biomass. 
The protein and lipid values obtained across the treatments 
were broadly consistent with the substantial nutritional 
variability reported for BSFL reared on different organic 
materials [21]. Such variation is important for feed 
formulation because a protein -oriented application may 
favour a different production substrate from one intended to 
provide both protein and dietary e nergy. The observed

protein-lipid differences therefore represent substrate -
dependent nutritional trade -offs rather than evidence of a 
universally superior nutrient profile. 
 
4.4 Integrating Growth Performance and Nutritional 
Quality 
Considered together, th e growth, bioconversion, and 
compositional findings answer an important practical 
question: the substrate maximizing one nutritional 
characteristic does not necessarily maximize overall 
production performance. PM generated the highest crude -
protein concent ration, whereas FW produced the greatest 
biomass, survival, bioconversion efficiency, and lipid 
concentration while maintaining relatively high protein 
content. Optimization of BSFL production consequently 
requires simultaneous consideration of biomass yie ld and 
nutrient quality [29]. 
The performance of FVW reinforces this conclusion. 
Despite achieving the highest substrate -reduction 
percentage, it generated comparatively low biomass and 
lower protein and lipid concentrations. Waste -management 
efficiency and feed-biomass production should therefore not 
be treated as interchangeable outcomes. Substrate selection 
should instead account for nutrient density, conversion 
efficiency, survival, biomass yield, and final nutrient 
composition according to the intended application [23]. 
Under the conditions evaluated here, FW provided the 
strongest overall compromise among these characteristics, 
while PM could remain relevant where increased larval 
protein concentration is prioritized. 
 
4.5 Implications for BSFL as a Sus tainable Feed 
Ingredient 
The combined protein and lipid concentrations observed in 
the harvested larvae support continued consideration of 
BSFL biomass as a potential ingredient in animal -feed 
systems. Insect-derived biomass has been investigated as an 
alternative or supplementary source of nutrients in poultry, 
aquaculture, and livestock diets, particularly where 
dependence on conventional protein ingredients presents 
economic or environmental challenges [30]. The present 
findings further suggest that organic residues can be selected 
strategically to influence both biomass productivity and the 
nutritional characteristics of the resulting larvae. 
This creates a direct connection between waste valorization 
and feed -resource production. Instead of treating 
biodegradable residues solely as materials requiring 
disposal, BSFL cultivation provides a pathway through 
which part of their nutrient content can be recovered in 
concentrated insect biomass [26]. Such recovery is consistent 
with circular -bioeconomy principl es that seek to maintain 
biological resources within productive systems. However, 
the present findings demonstrate nutritional potential, not 
feeding performance. Proximate composition alone cannot 
establish digestibility, nutrient bioavailability, palatab ility, 
optimal inclusion levels, or effects on animal health and 
productivity. Those outcomes require controlled feeding 
studies before BSFL produced from these substrates can be 
recommended for specific animal diets. 
 
4.6 Practical Constraints, Study Limit ations, and Future 
Research 
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting 
these findings. Organic waste composition can vary 
seasonally and geographically, meaning that the 
experimental substrates may not fully represent materials 
encountered under commercial production conditions [24]. 
The relatively short experimental period and controlled 
rearing environment also limit direct extrapolation to large -
scale systems. Proximate composition provides important 
nutritional information but does not c haracterize amino-acid 
quality, fatty -acid composition, digestibility, or nutrient 
bioavailability [28]. Furthermore, waste -derived substrates 
may contain pathogenic microorganisms, chemical 
contaminants, heavy metals, or other undesirable 
compounds whose transfer into larvae requires careful 
assessment. Regulatory restrictions may additionally 
determine which waste streams can legally be used for 
insects intended for animal feed [30]. Future studies should 
therefore combine substrate optimization with amin o-acid 
and fatty -acid profiling, microbial and contaminant 
assessment, digestibility testing, economic analysis, scale-up 
evaluation, and controlled animal -feeding trials. These 
considerations provide the necessary bridge from 
experimental nutrient recover y to safe and commercially 
viable BSFL feed production. 
 
5. Conclusion 
5.1 Principal Findings 
This study demonstrates that organic waste substrate type is 
an important determinant of BSFL growth performance, 
substrate utilization, biomass recovery, and nutritional 
composition. The findings showed that substrates supporting 
extensive waste reduction did not necessarily generate the 
greatest larval biomass or the most favourable conversion 
efficiency. Mixed food waste provided the strongest overall 
growth performance, combining high survival, biomass 
production, and efficient substrate conversion, whereas 
poultry manure favoured greater protein accumulation 
without producing equivalent growth performance. Fruit and 
vegetable waste demonstrated substantial w aste-reduction 
capacity but comparatively weaker biomass and nutrient -
production outcomes. These patterns indicate that BSFL 
performance depends on the interaction between substrate 
nutrient availability, physical characteristics, and larval 
nutrient utilization. Substrate suitability should therefore be 
evaluated using integrated biological, conversion, and 
nutritional indicators rather than any single performance 
measure. 
 
5.2 Feed-Ingredient and Waste-Valorization Significance 
The combined findings demons trate the potential for 
selected organic waste streams to be transformed into 
nutritionally valuable BSFL biomass rather than being 
treated exclusively as disposal burdens. Importantly, the 
results indicate that effective waste valorization requires 
more t han maximizing substrate disappearance. A suitable 
substrate should simultaneously support efficient organic -
matter utilization, high larval survival and biomass 
production, favourable nutrient accumulation, and consistent 
production performance. Mixed foo d waste provided the 
most balanced outcome under the experimental conditions, 
while other substrates demonstrated specific advantages that 
may be useful for different production objectives. Practical 
substrate selection must nevertheless account for local 
availability, compositional consistency, processing 
requirements, potential contaminants, microbial safety, and 
regulatory acceptability. Integrating these considerations can

strengthen the contribution of BSFL production to resource 
recovery while generat ing insect biomass wi th potential 
value as a protein and energy-containing feed ingredient. 
 
5.3 Final Perspective and Research Direction 
The findings provide a basis for optimizing BSFL 
production according to both waste -management objectives 
and desired nutritional characteristics of harvested biomass. 
Further research should determine whether the favourable 
compositional characteristics observed experimentally 
translate into practical feed value through detailed amino -
acid and fatty -acid profiling, diges tibility assessment, 
microbial and chemical safety evaluation, and controlled 
animal-feeding trials. Economic feasibility, substrate supply 
consistency, processing requirements, and performance 
under scaled production conditions should also be 
investigated before commercial recommendations are 
established. Ultimately, strategically selected organic 
residues can support an integrated pathway in which organic 
waste management enables efficient insect bioconversion, 
insect bioconversion generates nutritionally valuable 
biomass, and that biomass contributes to more sustainable 
feed production systems.
