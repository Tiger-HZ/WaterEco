 Abstract 
 Unmix Optimum (UnmixO) was developed to analyze data, such as sediment PAH data, that were resistant to existing methods of multivariate analysis. Using a geometrical approach, UnmixO uses multiple advanced nonlinear optimization algorithms to find potential sources that obey non-negativity constraints while optimally fitting the data. UnmixO does not require specific knowledge of the uncertainties in the data and will work for smaller data sets than other multivariate models. UnmixO was able to identify polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) contaminant sources contributing to sediment samples based on sample composition data with good diagnostic values. Results were compared to published EPA Chemical Mass Balance (CMB) sediment results from Lady Bird Lake (LBL) Austin, TX and 40 lakes (40LKS) across the U.S. A Chi-sum approach determined which UnmixO source profile best matched profiles used in CMB sediment studies; two coal tar (CT) sealcoat sources and a mixed combustion source contributed to the sediment PAHs. These results were consistent with CMB results for the LBL and 40LKS studies that estimated CT sealcoats contribute over 80% of PAHs to urban lakes. UnmixO results also showed that CT sealant’s contribution to sediments decreased after the City of Austin ban in 2006. 
 Keywords: Unmix Optimum, Receptor modeling, Sediments, Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon, Multivariate, Sources 1. Introduction 
 Sediment polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) sources include petroleum, wood, and coal combustion, and coal tar ( Abdel-Shafy and Mansour, 2016 ). Multiple studies have identified coal tar (CT) sealants as a significant source of PAHs in sediments coming from CT-sealed parking lots ( Mahler et al., 2005 ), dust ( Van Metre et al. 2009 ), and stormwater ( Selbig, 2009 ). EPA ( EPA, 2011 ) also conducted a controlled six-month study of three asphalt test plots that were not driven on with different surface treatments: coal tar emulsion sealcoat, asphalt emulsion sealcoat, and an unsealed control. CT sealcoat was a significant source of phenanthrene, anthracene, fluoranthene, pyrene, and total PAH (TPAH) concentrations were more than 100 times higher from CT-sealed test plots than asphalt-sealed and unsealed asphalt. In addition, PAHs volatilize following application of CT sealcoat and their abundance changes over time ( Van Metre et al. 2012 ). This composition change is largest in PAH source tracers associated with coal combustion (chrysene), oil combustion (indenopyrene), and motor vehicles (benzo[ghi]perylene). 
 High concentrations of PAHs in sediments near CT-sealed parking lots have been associated with a decrease in ecological health at sites downstream ( Scoggins et al. 2007 ). CT sealcoat exposure in newts decreased their righting ability and diminished liver enzyme activity ( Bommarito et al. 2010 ), and induced genotoxic effects in mice ( Long et al., 2016 ). Undiluted CT sealcoat runoff was also toxic to water fleas ( Ceriodaphnia dubia ) and fathead minnows ( Pimephales promelas ) ( Mahler et al. 2015 ). CT sealcoat represents a source of PAHs in indoor house dust ( Mahler et al., 2010 ) and ingestion of the dust has been found to increase excess lifetime cancer risk ( Williams et al. 2013 ). Washington and Minnesota banned the sale of coal tar pavement sealcoats in 2012 and 2014, respectively, based on reported environmental impacts and source apportionment studies ( Van Metre and Mahler, 2010 ; Crane, 2014 ). 
 The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency has developed receptor models for source apportionment and environmental forensics ( Murphy and Morrison, 2014 ) from multiple media, including air ( Hopke et al. 2016 ); wet deposition ( Keeler et al. 2006 ); water ( Yang et al., 2012 ; Li et al., 2015 ; US EPA 2015); and sediments ( Sundqvist et al., 2010 ; Assefa et al., 2014 ; Brown et al., 2015 ; Yu et al. 2015 ). Three tools have been developed: Chemical Mass Balance (CMB), Positive Matrix Factorization (PMF), and Unmix. The CMB model combines measured profiles and sample data in an uncertainty-weighted linear regression to calculate contributions from each source to a sample. PMF and Unmix are multivariate models that use different mathematical approaches to extract source profiles and contributions from the measured sample data. A key difference between these approaches is that CMB uses measured profiles of assumed sources directly in modeling, while multivariate models extract profiles from the data using mathematical models; extracted profiles are identified by comparing them to measured profiles. The minimum number of samples needed to run these models successfully is 30 to 100 for PMF and UnmixO, but just one for CMB, which also requires estimated uncertainty in source compositions and that the compositions be non-collinear. The measured, assumed profiles used for CMB may not accurately represent profiles of the actual sources impacting the samples. CMB also requires a matched set of species for all profiles (which can limit source-specific profiles) such as pine burning which can be separated using resin acids ( Rogge et al., 1998 ; Fine et al., 2002 ; Bullock et al., 2008 ). 
 The first study using CMB to evaluate sediment PAH sources found that coke ovens and motor vehicles contributed the largest fraction ( Li et al., 2003 ). CMB results are based on profiles used in the model; measuring and adding CT sealcoat profiles allowed the United States Geological Survey (USGS) to identify CT sealcoats as a major source of PAHs in urban lake sediments ( Mahler et al., 2005 ; Van Metre and Mahler, 2010 ; Van Metre and Mahler, 2014 ). CT sealcoat was also found to be a major source of sediment PAHs in Springfield, Missouri; Minneapolis/St. Paul, Minnesota; and Milwaukee ( Pavlowsky, 2013 ; Crane, 2014 ; Baldwin et al., 2016 ). Two papers by O’Reilly et al. (2012 , 2013 ) have raised questions on the CMB analyses and uncertainties in measured profiles and PAH reactivity. The USGS and Minnesota analyses ( Van Metre and Maher, 2010 ; Van Metre and Mahler, 2014 ; Crane, 2014 ) used the EPA Chemical Mass Balance 8.2 (CMB); O’Reilly et al. (2012 , 2013 ) suggested applying another approach such as EPA Unmix. O’Reilly (2012) used EPA Unmix to evaluate the 40LKS data and needed to remove phenanthrene and anthracene to find a solution. Unmix sources were identified using a correlation analysis between Unmix and measured profiles and high correlations (> 0.8) were found for many sources including fuel oil, Austin parking lot dust, coal, coal tar sealant, and highway runoff. The O’Reilly (2012 , 2013 ) and Stout and Graan (2010) emphasize that it is important to used multiple approaches in environmental forensics. 
 Sediment analyses typically have relatively small datasets (< 50) and contaminant concentrations represent an average over months or longer, which results in few samples having zero or low contaminant concentrations ( Uchimiya and Masunaga, 2007 ; Uchimiya et al., 2007 ). Averaging time, and organic volatilization ( Van Metre, 2012 ) and partitioning ( Neff et al., 2005 ) are critical considerations in sediment datasets since they decrease sample variability and increase differences between measured profiles and actual sources. A new environmental forensics data analysis tool called Unmix Optimum (UnmixO) can evaluate averaged data sets with fewer samples. The model algorithm that determines the sources is quite distinct from the one used in previous versions of Unmix. The focus of our paper is to describe the new algorithm and diagnostics, and compare results to published PAH sediment source apportionment results from CMB ( Van Metre and Mahler 2010 , 2014 ).

Methods 
 2.1. Measurement Data 
 UnmixO evaluated sediment data from both Lady Bird Lake (LBL) and the 40 Lakes (40LKS) ( Van Metre and Mahler 2010 , Van Metre and Mahler 2014 ). LBL and 40LKS studies had 12 PAHs present in measured source profiles: phenanthrene, anthracene, fluoranthene, pyrene, benzo[a]anthracene, chrysene, benzo[b]fluoranthene, benzo[k]fluoranthene, benzo[e]pyrene, benzo[a]pyrene, indenopyrene, and benzo[ghi]perylene. 2.2. CMB influential species 
 The CMB Modified Pseudo-Inverse Normalized (MPIN) matrices from the LBL and 40LKS studies were evaluated to understand the differences between the CMB and UnmixO results. The MPIN identifies which CMB-fitting species have the greatest influence on the source contribution estimates from each profile ( U.S. EPA, 2004 ; Kim and Henry, 1999 ). MPIN information was provided by USGS for two CT sealcoat-impacted samples (LBL and one 40LKS sample) and one sample not impacted by CT sealcoat. Guidance on interpreting the absolute value of the CMB MPIN output is listed in Kim and Henry (1999) . 2.3. Unmix Optimum 
 Receptor models are mathematical mixture models that solve the chemical mass balance of the species measured in a sample, and sources that contributed to the sample. Receptor models solve the chemical mass balance using data from samples that have been chemically analyzed to identify source fingerprints; this speciated dataset can be viewed as a data matrix X of dimension n by m , in which n samples and m chemical species were measured. Rows and columns of X and related matrices are indexed by i and j , respectively. The goal of receptor modeling is to identify the number of factors p , the species profile f of each factor, and the amount of mass g contributed by each factor to each individual sample that solve the chemical mass balance between measured species concentrations and factor profiles ( Equation 1 ):

where e ij is the residual for each sample/species and c ij is the modeled solution of x ij . Entire matrices are denoted by capital bold-face letters. Columns of the factor contribution matrix G may be denoted by g k and, similarly, rows of the factor profile matrix F are denoted by f k . This mass balance was calculated using both EPA Positive Matrix Factorization (PMF) 5.0 and UnmixO. The PMF solution was not well-defined, based on the number of displacement algorithm swaps ( Brown et al., 2015 ), and it has been used to evaluate sediment data in other studies (EPA PMF 1.1 - Stout and Graan, 2010 ; EPA PMF 3.0 - Assefa et al. 2014 ). The high number of swaps indicate the solution has rotational ambiguity and it may be due to the limited number of species in this analysis (12) and the low number of samples. 
 A new geometrical approach, UnmixO, utilizes multiple advance optimization approaches, always provides a feasible solution and developed new diagnostics for evaluating the solution. UnmixO seeks to minimize the area of the triangle formed by the sources (or volume of the simplex if more than three sources) while obeying non-negativity constraints on source compositions and contributions ( Figure 1 ). To accomplish this, an UnmixO user can choose one of two constrained nonlinear optimization algorithms from Matlab (MathWorks; Natick, MA). Sequential quadratic programming (sqp), the most advanced of the two algorithms available in UnmixO, was used for analyses in this report. The sqp algorithm works best for data with fewer than six sources. UnmixO’s other constrained nonlinear optimization option in is the interior-point algorithm, which is very stable and reliable for data with six or more sources and larger data sets (> 50 samples), but can miss the global minimum with three to five sources. UnmixO also has a tolerance level that controls the number and size of negative values of source contributions allowed in results. The tight setting was used here; it allows very small negative source contributions, while the loose setting allows larger ones. If the non-negativity constraints did not have some “wiggle room,” the nonlinear optimization algorithms would almost never find an initial solution from which to converge to an acceptable model. An initial starting non-negativity constraint level is selected by the user and, if a solution is found, UnmixO reduces the constraint level by a factor of two and looks for a solution. This continues until no solution is found. UnmixO then reverts to the last solution, i.e., the one that comes closest to satisfying non-negativity constraints.

 UnmixO Cartesian Plot showing red data points and sources as vertices of the triangle. UnmixO error analysis uses a Monte Carlo method for multivariate models ( Timmerman et al. 2007 ). UnmixO-predicted concentrations are used as the base case and error is added as 10, 15, or 20 percent (1-sigma error). An error of 20 percent, half of the 40% measured profile uncertainty assumed by Van Metre and Mahler (2010 , 2014 ), was selected. A new UnmixO solution was then calculated with the error-perturbed data and the results saved. This was repeated 400 times to give 400 UnmixO results that quantify uncertainty of results due to random errors in the data. Confidence intervals of the UnmixO source compositions and contributions were estimated. In addition, UnmixO profile signal-to-noise ratios were calculated as profile species concentration/sigma, where sigma is the standard deviation of Monte Carlo results. Monte Carlo methods may underestimate uncertainties (but not always) if there are one or two highly influential points in the analysis. 
 A Cartesian coordinate plot shows how UnmixO constructs a solution for a dataset with three sources ( Figure 1 ). Details of how an Unmix plot is generated (and its properties) are found in Henry (1987) and Henry and Kim (1990) . Lines in the figure can be explained by using benzo[ ghi ]perylene (BgP) as an example; this line is in the upper-left corner of the graph and any point on this line corresponds to a benzo[ ghi ]perylene value of zero. Any point on the same side of the line as the origin corresponds to a positive benzo[ ghi ]perylene value, and any point of the other side of the line corresponds to a benzo[ ghi ]perylene composition of less than zero. To have non-negative source compositions and contributions, three source points must be found that define a triangle (or, more generally, a simplex if the number of sources is greater than three) which encloses all the data points (red dots) and lies entirely inside the convex region defined by the lines where anthracene (An), benzo[ b ]fluoranthene (BbF), and indenopyrene (IP) equal zero. This area has been shaded gray in Figure 1 ; note that not all the red dots lie inside the triangle because random errors in the data allow for small violations of non-negativity constraints. Vertices of the triangle represent the pure source compositions, and the perpendicular distance from the triangle edges is used to calculate the combination of sources contributing to any given sample (see Appendix A ). Clearly, there is an infinite number of source points that can satisfy the non-negativity constraints ( Henry, 1997 ). UnmixO applies a constrained nonlinear optimization algorithm to find the sources that produce the smallest triangle meeting these constraints. 
 Although the Cartesian plot is a useful visualization for three dimensions, many UnmixO solutions have more than three or four sources; a graphic helps the user visualize and assess the multivariate model of the data. UnmixO combines a parallel coordinate plot with barycentric coordinates, which are a generalization of the ternary plot from physical sciences, to display a mixture of three things as a point inside an equilateral triangle ( Figure 2 .) Steps for developing the plot are described in Appendix A .

 UnmixO parallel coordinate plot Some of the basic properties of the UnmixO parallel coordinate plots are green lines that are the vertices of the simplex that UnmixO has fit to the data; cyan lines are data points, with one line for each point and any cyan line that goes outside the area bounded by green lines is a point with a negative source contribution. Small negative contributions are due to random errors in the data. The red line is the distance to the outer boundary of the feasible region. If any part of the green line lies outside the red line, some of the source compositions will be negative. Blue lines show the barycentric coordinates of the origin which represents a weighted mean of all the data. In general, the source with the largest mean value will explain most of the data. 
 The quality of the fit of the UnmixO model to the data can be assessed from the parallel coordinate plot. Faces of the simplex are defined by points where one source is 0 or near 0. If there are not many such points, that face will not be well-supported by the data. From Figure 2 , we see that Sources 1, 2, and 3 all have data points with a range of source contributions from near 0 to over 50 percent. This is a sign of a good fit to the data; a model in which contributions of most sources were small would be a poor fit. 
 The overall success of the UnmixO in fitting the data is gauged by the Figure of Merit (FOM), which ranges from 0 to1. FOM is the mean of two numbers: the median of the r-squared values of the species (a measure of how well UnmixO is fitting the data as a whole) and the fraction of the data points that have non-negative source contributions. Negative source contributions are often unavoidable, but there should be few such cases. Thus, if UnmixO fits all species very well and there are few negative source contributions, the FOM will be close to 1. 2.4. Chi-sum approach of UnmixO and measured profiles 
 A Chi-sum approach was used to determine which UnmixO profile had the closest match to the measured source profiles used in the CMB analyses ( Van Metre and Mahler, 2010 ): sealants (asphalt, sealant pavement dust Austin, sealant scraping Austin, sealant pavement dust six cities, sealant scraping Milwaukee, one-week-old CT-sealant scraping, sealant products, NIST coal tar), motor vehicle (diesel, gasoline vehicles, NIST diesel, tire dust, traffic average, traffic tunnel, NIST diesel, used motor oil #1, used motor oil #2), wood combustion (pine-wood soot), and coal combustion (coal average, coke oven emissions, fuel-oil combustion, power plant, residential heating). 
 The Chi-sum analysis represents the sum of the squared residuals of the observed, minus the measured, so a lower value represents a better fit. In this analysis, the UnmixO error was used to weight the observed, minus the predicted, to reduce the impact of species with high UnmixO uncertainties. It is critical to include uncertainties in any approach to match UnmixO and measured profiles since the source profile species concentration to uncertainty or signal-to-noise ratio can range from less than 1 to more than 6.

 By default, UnmixO source compositions are normalized so that the sum across all sources is the average value of the species in the data. To compare UnmixO and literature source profiles given as a percentage of total, we converted the literature profile to the same format as the UnmixO profile by multiplying by the total PAH that UnmixO apportioned to the source in question. The Chi-sum approach allows comparison of profiles that have species with large and small compositions and high and low uncertainties. The data and steps for the Chi-sum approach are shown in the Appendix with the UnmixO LBL results in Table B.1 ; Table B.2 lists measured source profile mass fractions; Table B.3 provides example Chi-sum calculations. Because the error is determined by a Monte Carlo method, errors and therefore Chi-sum values will vary slightly from run to run. Euclidean distances have also been used for comparing source profiles and principle component results, however, they do not incorporate uncertainty in the calculation ( Balwin et al., 2016 ). UnmixO source profiles were compared to a subset of profiles reported in Van Metre and Mahler (2014) : CT sealcoat pavement dust from six cities ( Mahler et al. 2005 , Van Metre et al. 2009 )), traffic tunnel air ( Li et al. 2003 ), power plant emissions ( Li et al. 2003 ), asphalt ( Boonyatumanond et al. 2007 ), fuel oil combustion ( Li et al. 1999 ), and pine-wood soot ( Schauer et al., 2001 ). The Chi-sum as used here is similar to the chi-squared statistic for the sum of squares of standard normal random variables. However, the Chi-sum is not intended to be any form of statistical test, as the underlying assumptions of the chi-square statistic, namely, normally distributed random variables is not satisfied. 3. Results and discussion 
 3.1. LBL Time-Series of Source Tracer PAHs 
 The CMB LBL MPIN results greater than 0.5 demonstrated that a few key species were influential: coal tar dust was separated using high fluoranthene, benzo[a]anthracene, and benzo[b]fluoranthene; motor vehicle was separated using fluoranthene and benzo[ghi]perylene; coal was separated using idenopyrene; oil burning was separated using indenopyrene and benzo[a]anthracene; and pine burning was separated using phenanthrene, pyrene, and benzo[b]fluoranthene. The LBL pre- and post-CT sealcoat ban data provided an opportunity to evaluate if loadings of these source tracers changed before and/or after the ban in 2006 ( Figure 3 ). Correlation coefficients between the paired high MPIN species were 0.75 or higher, indicating they all have a similar trend before and after the ban and may not represent unique sources in the LBL data set. The strong linear relationship between the PAHs, pre- and post- ban, suggest that concentration variation is mainly associated with one source type.

 UnmixO solutions were evaluated with cartesian and parallel coordinate plots; FOM, profiles; time series of normalized source contributions; observed vs. predicted Pearson correlation coefficients; and observed vs. predicted species concentrations.  3.2.1. LBL 
 UnmixO analyzed the LBL data with 42 samples and 12 PAHs, plus a sum of the PAHs (TPAH). CC site data were not included because of significantly higher TPAH concentrations (50.06 mg kg −1 ) than other samples (0.28 to 17.47 mg kg −1 ). The Unmix Numfact algorithm ( Henry et al., 1999 ; Park et al., 2000 ) determined that three sources had a signal-to-noise ratio greater than 2, and the FOM of the UnmixO 3-source solution was 0.99. Observed vs. predicted linear regression statistics were evaluated and UnmixO reproduced the measured data with a coefficient of determination (r 2 ) of 0.97 or higher. All species in Sources 1 and 2 had signal-to-noise ratios greater than 2, demonstrating that these compositions were well above the noise. Source 3 has only four species with signal-to-noise ratios above 2 and its composition is not well-determined. The Cartesian and parallel coordinate plots for the LBL analysis are shown in Appendix Figures C1 and C2 . Source 2 has the lowest uncertainty as shown by the Cartesian plot that has multiple points near the pure source. The parallel coordinate plot had a low number of negative contributions and contributions were distributed for all sources. The UnmixO profiles for LBL are shown in Table 1 and the largest TPAH concentration is associated with Source 2 (2.57 mg kg −1 ).

 3.2.2. 40 Lakes – East, Central, West Regions 
 UnmixO was used to evaluate the USGS 40LKS data ( Van Metre and Mahler, 2010 ). The number of samples for the west, central and east regions were 33, 42, and 45, respectively. Each region’s FOM diagnostics were greater than 0.99, and observed vs. predicted coefficient of determination values were greater than 0.96 for all species. Two CT sealant sources and mixed source were identified using the Chi-sum analysis in each region. UnmixO source profile and Chi-sum results for each region are presented in Appendix B Tables 6 to 11 , and Cartesian and parallel coordinate plots are presented in Appendix C Figures 2 to 8 . Cartesian plots showed that the UnmixO solution edges are well-supported in the east region; the central mixed source did not have data points near the vertex, indicating the UnmixO profile is uncertain. Also, the west region had only one point near the CTS 2 source, indicating that the UnmixO solution was highly influenced by this sample. Parallel coordinate plots for all regions had few negative source contributions, and central and west distribution of contributions were not well-distributed for the central mixed and west CTS 2 source. The two plots provide additional important qualitative information on two sources that have high uncertainties which may not show up in estimates like Monte Carlo analysis.

 The 40LKS data provided an opportunity to compare UnmixO and CMB results in regions with different sources. The CMB ( Van Metre and Mahler, 2010 ) and UnmixO CT sealcoat contributions to total PAH concentrations by Region are compared in Table 4 ; all of the 40LKS sample data were used to calculate the UnmixO average, while only a subset of the sites was evaluated by CMB ( Van Metre and Mahler, 2010 ).  The UnmixO CT sealcoat contribution was higher than CMB in the central and east regions, confirming that CT sealcoat is a significant source of PAHs. An UnmixO mixed source (motor vehicle, oil, and coal sources) also contributed 11, 18, and 24 percent of the TPAH in the east, central, and west regions, respectively. This low contribution of non-sealcoat urban sources is consistent with Pavlowsky (2013) who found the lowest total PAHs contributions from concrete parking lots. O’Reilly et al. (2012) identified Unmix sources using a correlation analysis between the Unmix and measured profiles using correlation analysis did not account for Unmix profile uncertainties. In addition, the analysis combined all the regions which is not needed for a model such as UnmixO which can analyze smaller data sets. The O’Reilly Unmix results demonstrated that multiple measured sources were correlated with the Unmix profiles and this is consistent with the results this analysis which was only able to resolve a mixed combustion source. UnmixO evaluated each region separately with all species which allowed identification of different source impacts in the Chi-sum analysis, as well as identifying asphalt sealant as a source in the west. CT sealant contributed the largest amount of TPAH in the central and east regions, and a mixed source contributed up to 18%. The west region Unmix profiles ratios were similar to asphalt and contributed 76% of the TPAH. 4. Conclusions 
 A new geometrical approach (UnmixO) was developed to evaluate sediment data. Key features of UnmixO are that it always provides a feasible solution, it can be used to evaluate small data sets (<50), and it utilizes peaks in data sets such as sediments which deposit over longer time periods. New diagnostic plots and an FOM metric were developed to evaluate the UnmixO solutions and these also provided insight into UnmixO profile uncertainties. Finally, a critical approach for determining how well UnmixO profiles matched measured profiles was developed. 
 The City of Austin’s ban of CT sealcoat in 2006 provided a unique opportunity to evaluate trends in UnmixO source contributions. The decrease in CT sealcoat contributions was similar to CMB results that have been reported; all of the influential CMB species decreased after the ban, indicating that one source was the primary contributor to PAHs. UnmixO also found that multiple CT sealcoat sources may represent aging of the sealcoat, different formulations, or transport processes such as heavy rain. Multiple sealcoat source compositions from aging may obscure the impacts of minor sources found in the reported CMB analyses due to the lack of organic species that can be used as tracers of non-sealcoat sources. CT sealant was a significant source of the TPAH in the LBL and 40LKS central and east region analyses, contributing >80% of the TPAH. The combined 40LKS analysis suggests that at least 63% of the TPAH is from CT sealant and combustion sources are contributing a maximum of 37%. Supplementary Material
