Impact of COVID‑19 pandemic on cultural ecosystem services 
from urban green spaces: a case from English Bazar Urban 
Agglomeration, Eastern India
Arijit Das1 · Manob Das1  · Swarnali Saha1 · Paulo Pereira2
Received: 16 May 2022 / Accepted: 5 April 2023 / Published online: 24 April 2023 
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2023
Abstract
Urban green space (UGS) provides multiple cultural ecosystem services (CES) and enhances people’s physical and mental 
well-being. The importance of UGS was more critical during crisis periods such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Recently, UGS 
has been under severe threats due to rapid urban expansion and climate change. Particularly, the cities in developing countries 
have experienced a dramatic loss of green spaces due to land use and land cover (LULC) change. This study aims to assess 
the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on CES perception obtained from two public UGS, i.e., Subhankar Sishu Uddyan 
(SSU) and Bandh Road (BR) in English Bazar Urban Agglomeration (EBUA), Eastern India. A survey was performed to 
assess the valuation of CES, CES obtained from UGS, and the use of CES difference during partial (unlock phases) and 
after lockdown periods. The non-parametric tests were carried out to understand the significant differences between CES 
and UGS perceived by the respondents. Explanatory factor analysis was also performed to find the association among CES 
for each UGS. The results reported substantial variations of UGS use during partial and after lockdown periods. During 
the after-lockdown period, the UGS mainly were used for social relations (51%), spending time with relatives and friends 
(25%), spending time with children (11%) in SSU and walking (25%), spending time with relatives and friends (21%), and 
physical activities (17%) in BR. During the partial lockdown period, a significant difference between SSU and BR was 
recorded from social relations and health value among all CES. After the full lockdown period, a significant difference was 
observed in recreation, social relations, health, and aesthetic value. The frequency and duration of UGS use during partial 
lockdown periods substantially increased. About 12% of the total visitor visited UGS daily after the lockdown, whereas it 
was 4% during the partial lockdown. The study’s findings will assist urban planners and policymakers in understanding the 
need for UGS to promote the well-being of the citizens.
Keywords Ecosystem services · Green spaces · COVID-19 · Well-being · Health value
Introduction
Urban green space (UGS) is highly vulnerable to rapid urban 
expansion and climate change (Bardhan et al. 2016; Xu et al. 
2019). In the last few decades, there has been an increase 
in research focused on the UGS impact on urban population 
well-being (e.g., Ma et al. 2019; Kothencz et al. 2017; Nath 
et al. 2018). Despite the UGS’s importance, the UGS are 
under severe threat due to rapid urban expansion (Arshad 
et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2019), one of the major drivers of 
UGS loss (Girma et al. 2019). In developing countries such 
as India, unplanned urban expansion is considered a severe 
threat to UGS (Kaur et al. 2021). This decreases access to 
green spaces (Arshad et al. 2020) and the ecosystem ser -
vices (ES) supplied. Due to this, several studies highlighted 
the importance of including UGS and ES in urban plan-
ning (Kabisch et al. 2015). Urban expansion in developing 
countries is growing dramatically. This process affects UGS 
tremendously in India, and ES is provided (Govindarajulu 
2014; Sundaram 2011). Although urbanization poses severe 
threats to UGS, few studies in India focused on ES supplied

by UGS (Ramaiah and Avtar 2019; Kadaverugu et al. 2021). 
More particularly, the cultural ecosystem services (CES) 
obtained from UGS remain unexplored in India.
UGS supplies habitats essential to prevent biodiversity 
loss in urban areas (Niemela 1999) that are key to supplying 
a vast number of regulating (e.g., air quality and climate reg-
ulation, noise reduction, carbon sequestration, water purifi-
cation), provisioning (e.g., food, medicinal plants, biomass), 
and cultural (e.g., recreation, landscape aesthetics) ES vital 
to urban dwellers’ well-being (Johnson et al. 2019; Giacchè 
et al. 2021; Pinto et al. 2021). Several psychological and 
physical health benefits can be obtained from UGS, such as 
reduced mental stress, depression, anxiety, physical fitness, 
and social interaction (Jabbar et al. 2021; Reyes-Riveros 
et al. 2021). Since establishing the Millennium Ecosystem 
Assessment (MEA, 2005), ES and their contributions to 
well-being have been assessed from diversified perspectives 
(e.g., quantitative and qualitative methods). CES quantifi-
cation is challenging since CES perceptions and attitudes 
vary between cultures and must consider intangible values 
(Ko and Son 2018). Since the 1990s, urban CES research 
has become popular (Hubacek and Kronenberg 2013) and 
was mainly focused on recreation and tourism (Abildtrup 
et al. 2013; de Groot et al. 2010). Very little work has been 
conducted on other CES components such as spirituality, 
aesthetic value, and cognitive development since they are 
non-physical and challenging to quantify (MEA, 2005; Ko 
and Son 2018). Thus, UGS has become one of the critical 
measures of quality of life, development of the cities, and 
sustainable development (Zhu and Xu 2021; Claudia et al. 
2022). Notably, the importance of UGS has been well recog-
nized during crisis periods such as the COVID-19 pandemic 
(Claudia et al. 2022).
In December 2019, COVID-19 originated in Wuhan 
(China), spreading rapidly worldwide (Noszczyk et al. 
2022). The World Health Organization (WHO) declared 
a global public health emergency on 11 March 2020. 
The heavily affected countries implemented lockdowns 
to restrict the spread of the disease (Lian and Feng  2022; 
Kumari and Toshniwal  2020). The public spaces, GS, 
were closed to prevent COVID-19 transmission (Benzell 
et al. 2020; Noszczyk et al. 2022; Gorzelany et al. 2022). 
In India, the lockdown was imposed on 25 March 2020, 
continuing until 31 May 2020. During this period, strict 
restrictions on access to public spaces (such as parks) 
were imposed. After 31 May 2020, unlock phases were 
implemented from 1 June 2020 and continued until 31 
March 2022). During this un-lockdown phase, many 
relaxations were provided on access to public spaces. 
The COVID-19 pandemic outbreak substantially affected 
urban life and UGS usage (Venter et al. 2020). Human 
mobility was strongly restricted (Slater et al. 2020), 
affecting people’s physical and mental health. The limi-
tations to obtaining CES from UGS were high (Richard 
and WCPO staff  2020; Reynolds and Forgione 2020). 
Access to public spaces was restricted (Rogers 2020; 
Yip and Chau 2020). Recent studies showed that UGS 
substantially reduced psychological stress (such as poor 
mental health and post-traumatic stress) that increased 
due to COVID-19 impacts (Geng et al. 2021; Slater et al. 
2020). Therefore, examining the impact of the COVID-19 
lockdown on CES supplied by UGS is essential. Thus, 
this study aims to examine the impact of COVID-19 amid 
the lockdown on CES supplied by UGS.
Several global studies recognized that the COVID-19 
lockdown affected CES supplied by UGS (Ugolini et al. 
2020; Mayen Huerta and Cafagna 2021; Berdejo‐ Espi-
nola et al. 2021). Nevertheless, in the Indian context, 
most of the study was performed on various aspects of 
CES obtained from UGS, such as benefits from urban 
parks (Basu and Nagendra  2021), UGS with their neces-
sity (Ramaiah and Avtar  2019), and UGS planning for 
climate changes mitigations (Govindarajulu 2014). How -
ever, to our knowledge, no studies were carried out to 
understand the impact of lockdown on the CES obtained 
from UGS. The outbreak of COVID-19 primarily affected 
the use, perception, and attitudes of the people (Grima 
et al. 2020; da Schio et al. 2021), and most of the study in 
India was performed on the impact of COVID-19 on air 
quality (Das et al.  2021; Naqvi et al. 2021; Sharma et al. 
2020), water quality (Arif et al.  2021), urban vulnerability 
(Mishra et al.  2020), and urban slum vulnerability (Das 
et al.  2021), but the impact of COVID-19 pandemic on 
CES obtained from UGS has been ignored and unexplored. 
Thus, these gaps in the previous research studies inspired 
us to perform a study on the impact of COVID-19 amid 
the lockdown on CES obtained from the UGS. This study 
has immense scope to understand UGS’s role in provid-
ing CES from a diversified perspective during the cri -
sis periods. Thus, this research aims to assess the impact 
of the COVID-19 pandemic on the CES during partial and 
after-lockdown periods in English Bazar Urban Agglom-
eration (EBUA), Eastern India. The specific objectives of 
this work are (1) to understand the use of UGS during 
the partial lockdown and after the complete lockdown, 
(2) to compare the use of CES during the partial and com-
plete lockdown, and (3) to find out the association CES 
during the partial and complete lockdown, respectively. 
Few research questions have been formulated to fulfill the 
objectives of the study. These research questions are as fol-
lows: (1) For what purposes were UGS used during partial 
and after-lockdown periods? (2) Is there any difference in 
CES usage between partial and after-lockdown periods? 
(3) To what extent are the CES associated?

Materials and methods
Study sites
Two UGS were selected to study COVID-19 impact on CES 
(Fig. 1). These UGS are located in the EBUA, Eastern India. 
EBUA is one of the largest urban agglomerations in West 
Bengal, with an area of 28 km2 and a population of more 
than 0.3 million (Dutta and Das 2019). EBUA comprises 
two statutory towns (English Bazar Municipality and Old 
Malda Municipality) and three census towns (Bagbari, 
Chhatianmore, and Sahapur). Recently, EBUA experienced 
rapid urban growth, and it was considered the fastest-grow-
ing agglomeration in West Bengal (Dutta and Das 2019). 
The city experienced a rapid change in vegetation cover, 
water bodies, and agricultural land due to dramatic land 
use and land cover change (Shaw and Das 2018; Dutta and 
Das 2019). The average temperature is 26 °C, and the aver-
age rainfall is around 1500 mm in EBUA (Dutta and Das 
2019). There are very few UGS in English Bazar city, such 
as parks, playgrounds, and green spaces at the city and the 
neighbourhood level. These UGS are widely used for recrea-
tion, social relations, and physical activities. Some major 
UGS are SSU, BR, Brinda Bani Maidan, Malda College 
Ground, Prasanti Uddyan, Maldar Uthon, and Mahananda 
Park. Unfortunately, no large park or GS exist in the city. 
Among all the parks, SSU and BR are the most popular and 
widely used UGS and, therefore, selected for this work. SSU 
is located in the city’s center and is a public park maintained 
by the English Bazar municipality. This park is not only used

CES studied and survey and data collection
In this study, six CES categories obtained from UGS 
were developed following the framework proposed by 
the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (MEA, 2005) and 
TEEB (2010). These six CES were categorized into 24 
sub-CES based on previous works (Barrena et al. 2014; 
Norton et al. 2012; Bieling et al.  2014) (Supplementary 
questionnaire S1; Section. 1 to 5). The CES studied are 
recreation, social relations, inspirations, aesthetic value, 
sense of place, and health value. The details of the CES 
are presented in Table  1.
Previously, to conduct the questionnaires, we tried to 
understand how much the visitors value the CES obtained 
from SSU and BR. The visitors were asked to identify the 
six most relevant.

Face-to-face interviews were performed in SSU and in 
BR (N = 215). The visitors were randomly selected from 
the UGS to evaluate the impact of COVID-19 amid the 
lockdown on the use, attitudes, and perception of CES in 
EBUA. Individual and focus group discussions (compris-
ing five to six people) with the park visitors were con-
ducted as well. In total, 104 individuals were interviewed 
in SSU and 111 in BR. The survey was performed in mid 
of July to 29 August 2021 for partial lockdown (unlock 
phases). During the survey, the respondents were also 
asked about CES use, attitudes, and perception during 
the after-lockdown periods. In SSU, the survey was con-
ducted one time, i.e., from 4 to 6 pm, and in the case of 
BR, it was carried out during the morning (6 am to 8 am) 
and the evening (4 pm to 7 pm), respectively. The survey 
was conducted during this time because it is in this time 
that visitor frequency is high. In the case of BR, this 
GS is mostly used for different purposes in the morning 
(mainly for physical activities) and evenings (for mul-
tiple reasons), such as walking and spending time with 
friends. The questionnaires were developed in English. 
However, then questions were asked in Bengali Dialect. 
The UGS has also assessed the frequency of UGS visits 
and duration spent in UGS. The details of the methodol-
ogy are presented in Fig.  2.
The respondents valued the six CES and the different sub-
categories using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from very 
important/highly agree (5) to very low important/highly dis-
agree (1). The survey was focused on the following aspects 
(Supplementary S2):
1. Socio-demographic attributes of the visitors
2. Perceived valuation of CES from UGS
3. Activities performed at UGS during partial and after 
lockdown periods
4. Duration and frequency of UGS use during partial and 
after-lockdown periods
5. Perception toward CES (subcategories), respectively

In this study, several descriptive statistics (median, quar -
tiles) were carried out. The median refers to the middle most 
value of the datasets meaning 50% of datasets are smaller 
or equal to the median, and 50% are higher or higher than 
the median. The first and third quartiles refer to the 25% 
and 75% of the datasets arranged in increasing order. In this 
study, the median and the first and third quartiles have been 
calculated based on the CES obtained from the UGS. There 
were variations in the use of UGS during partial and after

Results
UGS visitors’ profile
This study surveyed 55% of males and 45% of females from 
SSU and 61% of males and 39% of females from BR. The 
details socio-demographic profile of the respondents is 
presented in Table  2. In SSU, the majority of the visitors 
were 20–30 years old (76%), followed by 30–40 years old 
(13%), 40–50 years old(9%), and above 50 years old (2%). 
On the other hand, in BR, most of the visitors belonged to 
the age group of 20–30 years (58%), followed by 30–40 years 
(19%), 40 to 50 years (11%), and above 50 years (12%). 
The majority of the respondents had high school (class 8 to 
12) (32%) level followed by Bachelor (30.5%), elementary 
(from class 1 to 7) (23.5%), and above master (13.5%). The 
educational status of the visitors was different in the two 
UGS. In SSU, 62% of visitors were students, 8% worked 
as government employees, 14% were business people, and 
16% were domestic. In BR, 49% of visitors were students, 
21% were housewives, 19.0% were business people, and 
11.0% worked as government employees (Supplementary 
Table S1).
Except for education level, recreation was significantly 
higher in BR for gender, education level, and occupation. 
Regarding social relations, except for age, all the remaining 
variables were significantly higher in SSU than in BR. 
Inspiration and aesthetic value were significantly higher in 
BR than in SSU in all the variables. Sense of place was 
significantly higher in BR than in SSU for gender and 
occupation. Finally, the health value was significantly higher 
in BR than in SSU in all the studied variables (Table 2).

Reasons and duration of visits to UGS
During the partial lockdown, SSU was mostly used for social 
relations (37%), followed by spending time with relatives 
and friends (21%), experiencing nature and its aesthetic 
beauty (15%), and mental refreshment and relaxation (13%), 
respectively (Fig.  3). On the other hand, during a partial 
lockdown, BR was mostly used for spending time with rela-
tives and friends (29%), followed by walking (21%), physi-
cal activities (14%), fresh environment (10%), and mental 
refreshment and relaxation (8%). During the partial lock -
down, about 43% of visitors visited UGS less than 1 time in 
a month, followed by once a week (22.5%) and 1 to 4 times 
a month, respectively. Most of the visitors (32%) spent up 
to 1 h, followed by 1 to 2 h (30%), and 2 to 3 h (22%) during 
the partial lockdown.
During after lockdown periods, SSU was mostly used 
for social relations (51%), followed by spending time with 
relatives and friends (25%) and spending time with children 
(11%). In BR, UGS was mainly used for walking (25%), 
followed by spending time with relatives and friends (21%), 
physical activities (17%), and experiencing nature and its 
natural beauty (12%). Thus, from the overall result, it was 
reported that UGS was mostly used for social relations 
(27%), followed by spending time with relatives and friends 
(23%), walking (14%), and physical activities (9%) (Sup-
plementary Figs. S1 and S2). After the lockdown, most of 
the visitors visited UGS less than 1 time in a month (32%), 
followed by 1 to 4 times a month (21.5%) and 2 to 4 times 
a week (1%). However, most of the visitors visited BR 2 
to 4 times a week (28%), followed by less than 1 time in a 
month (23%) and daily (19%). After the lockdown, 47% of 
visitors spent 1 to 2 h, followed by up to 1 h (34%) and 2 to 
3 h (12%).

Multivariate analysis
Factor analysis (FA) was performed for each UGS during 
partial and after-lockdown periods. The exploratory FA 
loading for two UGS is detailed in Supplementary Tables S2 
and S3. During the partial lockdown, factor 1 was explained 
by social relations (SR5), aesthetic value (AV16), and sense 
of place (SP17 and SP20). In contrast, factor 2 was formed 
by recreation (REC3), inspirations (IN9 and IN10), aesthetic 
value (AV14), and sense of place (SP19) for SSU. Although 
six groups can be identified, the CES parameters were scat-
tered in this case. The first group, CES for SSU, includes 
SR5, SR7 (social relations), and REC4 (recreations). 
Another group was formed with IN9 (inspiration) and HV21 
(health value). The third group was composed of SR8 (social 
relation), IN12 (inspiration), and REC1 (recreation), and the 
fourth group was formed by SR6 (social relations) and SP19 
(sense of place) (Fig. 4).
In the case of BR, factor 1 was explained by social rela-

Discussion and conclusions
The study showed that COVID-19 amid lockdown substan-
tially impacted CES obtained from two UGS in an Indian 
urban agglomeration (EBUA), Eastern India. Valuation of 
CES obtained from the UGS, frequency of use, and duration 
varies between two UGS. There were substantial variations 
between the two UGS toward the valuation of CES during 
partial and after lockdown periods. Both the UGS reported 
that the most commonly mentioned CES was recreation, fol-
lowed by social relations, aesthetic value, and inspirations, 
respectively. During after-lockdown period, relatively higher 
importance was given to social relations (4.88), followed 
by recreation (4.56), aesthetic value (4.23), and inspiration 
(4.12). In SSU and from BR, relatively higher importance 
was given to aesthetic value (4.79), followed by health 
(4.77), inspiration (4.58), recreation (4.32), and social rela-
tions (3.68). In the case of the use of UGS, it was reported 
that UGS was mostly used for social relations (27%), fol-
lowed by spending time with relatives and friends (23%), 
walking (14%), and physical activities (9%). During after 
lockdown period, BR was mainly used for walking (25%), 
followed by spending time with relatives and friends (21%), 
physical activities (17%), and experiencing nature and its

In the previous literature, it was well documented that UGS 
play a significant role in enhancing mental and physical 
health (Lee et al. 2015; Astell-Burt and Feng 2019; Ugo-
lini et al. 2020; Enssle and Kabisch 2020). According to 
Annerstedt et al. ( 2012), UGS promote physical activities, 
helps recover illness faster, enhances general mental health, 
and reduces stress and mental disorders. Mainly, in densely 
populated areas like EBUA, the contributions of UGS are 
more crucial (Bateman et al. 2013; Keeler et al. 2019). The 
survey result showed that users substantially increased 
after lockdown periods due mainly to physical activities 
and enhancing mental health. In many recent studies, it 
was documented that the physical and mental health of the 
people were affected mainly by UGS during the COVID-19 
pandemic (Xie et al., 2020 ; Ugolini et al. 2020 ). Thus, the 
multi-functional activities of UGS largely affect the use of 
UGS (Kaczynski and PotwarkaLR 2008; Zwierzchowska 
et al. 2018).
From the study’s findings, it was well documented that 
UGS are used for multiple perspectives, and the use of CES 
from the UGS substantially increased after lockdown peri-
ods. Now, it is important to compare the findings of the 
study with other previous studies. Recently, several studies 
were carried out to assess the impact of the COVID-19 pan-
demic on the attitudes, use, and perception of UGS world-
wide (Berdejo‐Espinola et al. 2021; Geary et al. 2021; Pouso 
et al. 2021). For example, Beckmann-Wübbelt et al. (2021) 
performed a study in southwestern Germany. They showed 
that visits to the peri-urban forest (PUF) increased during 
the COVID-19 pandemic, and PUFs were mostly used for 
enhancing mental health (such as stress reduction). As per 
the study of Ugolini et al. (2021) in Italy, it was reported 
that UGS were important for relaxation and physical exer -
cises during the pandemic periods. Ugolini et al. (2020) also 
studied multiple countries (such as Croatia, Israel, Spain, 
Italy, Lithuania, and Slovenia). Their findings revealed that 
UGSs were mostly used for physical activities, relaxation, 
and experiencing nature, respectively. Behavioral changes 
during the COVID-19 pandemic were also reported, such as 
walking in tree-lined streets (Spain) and small urban gardens 
(Italy) and traveling outside the city to green areas (Lithu -
ania), respectively. Geng et al. (2021) studied the impact of 
the COVID-19 pandemic on global urban park visits. Their 
findings reported that the number of park visitors increased 
before 16 February 2020 (before the extreme outbreak of 
COVID-19) compared to prior to the COVID-19 pandemic 
periods. From the result of the study, it was reported that 
the frequency and duration spent in UGS increased after 
lockdown periods due to the relaxation of the restrictions. 
The frequency of visits to UGS increased by 30% after the 
lockdown compared to the partial lockdown period. During 
the partial lockdown period (in July and August 2020), there 
was a strict restriction on the use and accessibility of public 
space use, and there were limited opportunities to visit UGS 
in the city.
Policy implications
The COVID-19 pandemic allowed realizing the importance 
of UGS for the well-being of the people. It also revealed 
that the presences of UGS (like urban parks) are crucial 
to enhancing the city dwellers’ quality of life, particularly 
during crisis periods like COVID-19 amid lockdown. In 
many previous studies, it was well documented that the 
frequency of UGS uses substantially increased during the 
COVID-19 pandemic (Lopez et al. 2020; Beria and Lunkar 
2021). The role of UGS in enhancing physical and mental 
health during COVID-19 was well recognized (Lin et al. 
2019; Schipperijn et al. 2010). It denotes the importance of 
UGS during crisis periods, and these UGS were very impor-
tant for the mental and physical well-being of urban dwell-
ers (Venter et al. 2020; Kleinschroth and Kowarik 2020; 
Grima et al. 2020). In urban areas, city dwellers remained 
locked at home and spent less time outside. People became 
disconnected from social interactions and felt lonely. The 
urban dwellers became mentally depressed, and there were 
minimal opportunities for outdoor activities due to the 
lockdown. Thus, importance and dependency substantially 
increased after the lockdown periods of the COVID-19 pan-
demic (Ugolini et al. 2020; Noszczyk et al. 2022). Thus, 
from the study’s findings, UGS (parks, gardens, patches of 
green spaces, and tree-lined streets) must be promoted to 
enhance urban health and well-being. In developing coun-
ties like India, conservation and management of UGS have 
become severe challenges due to rapid unplanned and hap-
hazard urban expansions (Ramaiah and Avtar 2019; Dhyani 
et al. 2018). In the Indian context, it was well reported that 
there was a dramatic loss of green (vegetation cover) cover 
due to rapid urban expansions and conversion of green 
cover (vegetation cover) to other land use, respectively 
(Nagendra et al. 2012; Bhat et al. 2017). UGS planning and 
management is one of the great challenges to city planners 
and policymakers in fast-growing, developing cities like 
India. Previous studies exposed concerns regarding the loss 
of urban green in cities due to rapid urban expansion and

the deterioration of environmental quality in Indian cities 
(Sundaram 2011; Govindarajulu 2014). In EBUA, several 
studies were performed by Dutta and Das (2019), Shaw and 
Das (2018), and Pal and Ziaul (2017), and their findings 
reported that there was a dramatic increase in the urban 
area and rapid transformation of land use and land cover. 
According to the study of Dutta and Das (2019), from 1991 
to 2016, 17% of vegetation cover was transformed into agri-
cultural land, and 2.33% was converted into a built-up area. 
Therefore, urgent actions must be adopted to sustain urban 
environmental quality across India. In the Indian context, 
the roles of UGS were well documented in previous work 
to promote the urban environment, mitigate urban heat 
island (UHI) effects, enhance mental health well-being, 
and obtain CES from UGS (Chaturvedi et al. 2013; Raha-
man et al. 2021; Mukherjee et al. 2017). The status of UGS 
(at city and neighborhood scale) and per-capita availability 
of UGS across the cities of India were assessed to achieve 
urban sustainability and promote urban health well-being 
(Anguluri and Narayanan 2017; Singh 2018; Shekhar and 
Aryal 2019). Therefore, effective urban landscape planning 
is urgently required to promote urban green space manage-
ment at the city and neighborhood levels.
This study has immense scope to understand UGS’s 
role in providing CES and its role in crisis periods. This 
study is crucial to capture CES’s diverse perspective dur -
ing and after the partial lockdown periods. Despite this, 
a few limitations of the study can be reported. First, in 
this study, July–August 2020 and July–August 2021 were 
considered partial (unlock phases) and after-lockdown 
periods. Second, during the pandemic periods, an online 
survey was performed due to a lack of equipment, and min-
imal responses from the survey were collected to maintain 
COVID-19 protocols onsite. Thus, a sample for the study 
may not be sufficient. Third, in this study, the urban green 
space sites were selected based on the frequency of use and 
importance of UGS sites. Future researchers must consider 
the size of UGS and diverse sources of ES to understand 
visitors’ perceptions of ES better.
