Abstract
The accelerated decline of insects is driven by anthropogenic pressures such as habitat loss, pesticide use, and climate 
change. Species vulnerability varies according to their exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity to these threats. Arti -
ficial light at night (ALAN) further increases this vulnerability by disrupting nocturnal and crepuscular species, particu -
larly moths. The subfamily Arctiinae is ecologically diverse, exhibits chemical defenses and ultrasonic signaling, and 
is considered a valuable bioindicator group. Here, we investigate the relationship between ALAN exposure and habitat 
suitability for Arctiinae moths across the Neotropical region. We analyzed 17,989 occurrence records representing 3447 
Arctiinae species, integrating bioclimatic variables and nighttime light data. We quantified the spatial overlap between 
potential species distributions and ALAN, including light intensity within occupied pixels. All species showed some level 
of exposure to artificial light, with 24% having more than 50% of their potential range affected. Species with broader 
geographic ranges exhibited disproportionately higher exposure. Predicted species richness was generally higher in illu -
minated areas, except in the Pantanal, indicating that regions of high ecological suitability often coincide with urbanized 
and artificially lit environments. The risks posed by ALAN to nocturnal biodiversity highlight the need for conservation 
strategies focused on areas with low levels of light pollution. Future studies integrating spatial, functional, and behavioral 
data will be essential to better understand both direct and indirect impacts of ALAN and to prioritize the conservation of 
nocturnal species in the Neotropical region.

From glow to risk: degree of exposure of Arctiinae moths

Introduction
The accelerated decline of insects, organisms essential to 
ecological processes such as pollination, nutrient cycling, 
and the maintenance of food webs, is driven by multiple 
anthropogenic pressures (Gebremariam 2024; Grevé et al. 
2024). These include habitat loss and fragmentation result -
ing from agricultural and urban expansion, as well as pes -
ticide use (Scolozzi and Geneletti 2012). Together with 
climate change, these pressures reduce both the abundance 
and diversity of organisms (Wagner 2022), undermining 
ecosystem stability and limiting the capacity of ecosystems 
to adapt to future disturbances (Dainese et al. 2019).
Understanding the impacts of anthropogenic pressures 
across taxonomic groups requires evaluating species vul -
nerability, defined as the interaction between exposure, 
sensitivity, and adaptive capacity to specific threats (De 
Marco et al. 2018). This framework can be treated as a

spatial problem, in which exposure corresponds to the over-
lap between threats and species distributions (Dawson et al. 
2011; Foden et al. 2013). Within this context, artificial light 
at night (ALAN) represents an important anthropogenic 
stressor currently undergoing rapid global expansion. More 
than 80% of the global population lives under light-pol -
luted skies, with artificial sky brightness extending beyond 
urban areas (Falchi et al. 2016), while evidence indicates 
a continuous increase in its extent and intensity driven by 
urbanization and technological changes (Davies and Smyth 
2018). In addition, ALAN reduces the visibility of natural 
night skies (Kyba et al. 2023) and can mask biologically 
relevant light cycles, such as lunar and twilight cues (Sey -
moure et al. 2025). Together, these processes make ALAN a 
global driver of environmental change that increases species 
exposure within the vulnerability framework (De Marco et 
al. 2018).
Among the factors increasing insect vulnerability, artifi -
cial light at night (ALAN) has emerged as a major anthro -
pogenic stressor. Emitted by sources such as streetlights, 
vehicle headlights, and urban infrastructure, ALAN dis -
rupts ecosystems by altering the behavior and physiology of 
nocturnal and crepuscular species, thereby increasing their 
sensitivity to environmental change (Bará and Falchi 2023; 
Gaston et al. 2014). However, growing evidence suggests 
that ALAN can also affect diurnal species, either indirectly 
through changes in ecological interactions and resource 
availability or directly by disrupting circadian rhythms 
and behavior (Davies and Smyth 2018; Gaston and Holt 
2018). In combination with land-use change, particularly 
the conversion of natural habitats into urban and agricul -
tural landscapes (Falchi et al. 2016; Haddad et al. 2015), 
ALAN exacerbates habitat loss and fragmentation, with 
documented negative consequences for insect populations 
(Camacho et al. 2021; Owens et al. 2020).
ALAN has expanded rapidly over recent decades and now 
affects a substantial portion of the Earth's surface. Global 
assessments indicate that more than 80% of the world’s pop-
ulation lives under light-polluted skies, with artificial sky 
brightness extending far beyond urban centers (Falchi et al. 
2016). The increasing extent and intensity of nighttime illu-
mination have led ALAN to be recognized as an important 
component of global environmental change, with conse -
quences for ecological processes operating across multiple 
spatial scales (Davies and Smyth 2018). Recent evidence 
further suggests that artificial lighting continues to increase 
worldwide, contributing to a rapid decline in the visibility 
of stars and the natural darkness of night skies (Kyba 2023). 
Beyond increasing brightness, ALAN can mask biologically 
important natural light cycles, including lunar and twilight 
cues used by many organisms for orientation, movement, 
reproduction, and other ecological functions (Seymoure et 
al. 2025).
Moths are especially sensitive to ALAN and have con -
sequently become model organisms for studying the eco -
logical effects of light pollution (Boyes et al. 2020; Degen 
et al. 2024; Kawahara et al. 2018). This sensitivity is largely 
driven by their nocturnal habits and strong reliance on natu-
ral light cues for orientation and navigation. Artificial light 
profoundly influences moth behavior, often attracting indi -
viduals and promoting species aggregation in illuminated 
areas. Numerous hypotheses have been proposed to explain 
this flight-to-light behavior (Callahan 1965; Hamdorf and 
Höglund 1981; Mazokhin-Porshnyakov 1961; Robinson 
and Robinson 1950; Sotthibandhu and Baker 1979). More 
recently, experimental evidence has shown that many flying 
insects exhibit a dorsal light response, in which they orient 
their dorsal side toward the brightest light source to main -
tain stable flight. Under artificial lighting, this mechanism 
can lead to disorientation, circling behavior, and prolonged 
attraction around light sources, providing a mechanistic 
explanation for the widespread aggregation of nocturnal 
insects around artificial lights (Fabian et al. 2024).
However, attraction to artificial light varies according 
to several factors, particularly the wavelength and type of 
lighting. Short-wavelength light, especially blue and ultra -
violet spectra (370–400 nm) commonly emitted by cool 
white LEDs, causes strong sensory disruption, leading to 
disorientation, increased collision risk, and reduced sur -
vival (Cowan and Gries 2009; Donners et al. 2018; Frank 
2006). Recent evidence suggests that these effects are 
driven by wavelength-specific physiological and behavioral 
responses, with Fabian et al. (2024) demonstrating that blue-
rich light exacerbates navigational failure and energetic 
costs in moths. In contrast, longer-wavelength lighting, such 
as amber, red, or sodium-vapor lamps, exerts comparatively 
weaker effects on moth attraction and behavior (Fabušová 
et al. 2024).
Light responsiveness is further shaped by species-spe -
cific traits (Merckx and Slade 2014), as well as by body 
size and sex (Somers-Yeates et al. 2013; Van Langevelde 
et al. 2011). Larger, visually adapted individuals and males 
generally exhibit stronger attraction to artificial light (Gar -
ris and Snyder 2010). For males in particular, it remains 
unclear whether this pattern reflects heightened photorecep-
tive sensitivity or increased nocturnal activity and encounter 
rates with light sources (Garris and Snyder 2010). Owing to 
this strong phototactic response, light traps have become a 
widely used and highly effective method for surveying moth 
assemblages across multiple families (Justice et al. 2016; 
McDermott et al. 2018; Van Grunsven et al. 2014; Wölfling 
et al. 2016).

Beyond attraction alone, moth susceptibility to ALAN 
extends to disruptions of key physiological processes, such 
as diapause, even under low levels of nighttime illumina -
tion (Degen et al. 2024; Van Geffen et al. 2014). ALAN also 
alters behavioral and ecological dynamics, including repro-
duction and foraging efficiency, ultimately contributing to 
population declines and shifts in species distributions (Gas-
ton and Holt 2018; Owens et al. 2020; Sanders et al. 2021; 
Van Grunsven et al. 2020).
This study focuses on Arctiinae moths (Lepidoptera: 
Erebidae), a diverse subfamily comprising approximately 
11,000 species worldwide (Zenker et al. 2016), of which 
around 6000 occur in the Neotropical region and 1650 in 
Brazil (Ferro and Diniz 2010). Arctiinae are notable for their 
chemically mediated defenses (e.g., sequestration of pyr -
rolizidine alkaloids), aposematism, mimicry, pronounced 
sexual dimorphism (Conner and Weller 2004; Weller et 
al. 1999), and their ability to emit ultrasonic signals as a 
defense against bat predation (Barber et al. 2009). They also 
represent one of the most widely used lepidopteran groups 
in environmental bioindication (Hilty and Merenlender 
2000). Despite increasing research on the physiological and 
behavioral effects of light pollution, large-scale evaluations 
of the exposure of Arctiinae moths to artificial light at night 
(ALAN) are still limited. To address this gap, we quantified 
the degree of exposure of Arctiinae moths to ALAN across 
the Neotropical region by evaluating the overlap between 
species potential distributions and illuminated areas.
Materials and methods
Species occurrence records
Occurrence records of Arctiinae moths across the Neotropi-
cal region were obtained from online databases, including 
the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF; h t t p s : 
/ / w w w . g b i f . o r g ) and speciesLink (http://splink.cria.org.br/), 
as well as from specimens collected by the authors and data 
compiled from the scientific literature (Delfina and Teston 
2013; Ferro and Romanowski 2012; Grados et al. 2018; 
Moraes and Duarte 2015; Moreno and Ferro 2016; Nasci -
mento et al. 2016; Silva and Silva 2014; Teston and Ferro 
2016a, b, 2019, 2020).
In addition, we examined specimens deposited in Bra -
zilian scientific collections, including the Coleção Becker 
(Camacan, BA), Coleção Entomológica do Instituto Nacio-
nal de Pesquisas da Amazônia, Coleção Entomológica 
Padre Jesus Santiago Moure (Universidade Federal do 
Paraná), Coleção Entomológica da Universidade de Bra -
sília, Coleção do Laboratório de Estudos de Lepidópteros 
Neotropicais (Universidade Federal do Oeste do Pará), 
Coleção Zoológica do Maranhão, Instituto Oswaldo Cruz 
(FIOCRUZ), Museu Entomológico Ceslau Biezanko (Uni -
versidade Federal de Pelotas), Museu de História Natural 
(Universidade Estadual de Campinas), Museu Nacional 
(Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro), Museu Paraense 
Emílio Goeldi, and the Museu de Zoologia da Universidade 
de São Paulo.
A total of 17,989 occurrence records representing 3447 
Arctiinae species were compiled, distributed across 601 
geographic coordinates in 24 countries. Prior to spatial 
analyses, all scientific names were carefully reviewed to 
correct spelling errors and resolve synonymies, ensuring 
taxonomic consistency and valid species-level identifica -
tions across all data sources. Records were then subjected to 
spatial cleaning and standardization, including conversion 
of coordinates to decimal degrees using the WGS84 datum, 
removal of records located outside the Neotropical region 
or in marine environments, and elimination of duplicate 
coordinates for the same species. These procedures were 
implemented using the CoordinateCleaner package (version 
3.0.1) in R (version 4.3.1; R Core Team 2023).
When geographic coordinates were not available, 
approximate coordinates for collection localities were 
obtained using Google Maps, following Pessoa da Silva and 
De Marco (2023). At the end of this process, the final data -
set comprised 227 species with eight or more occurrence 
records (Bean et al. 2012; Pearson et al. 2007), distributed 
across 17 countries and totaling 364 unique geographic 
coordinates within the Neotropical region. This minimum 
occurrence threshold was adopted to ensure the robust -
ness of species distribution models, as both theoretical and 
empirical evidence indicate that models calibrated with few 
records tend to exhibit higher uncertainty, lower reliability, 
and reduced predictive performance (Bean et al. 2012; Pear-
son et al. 2007; Stockwell and Peterson 2002).
Environmental data
We used 19 historical bioclimatic variables related to tem -
perature and precipitation (1970–2000) obtained from 
WorldClim version 2.1 ( http://www.worldclim.org) (Fick 
and Hijmans 2017), at a spatial resolution of 5 arc-min -
utes, corresponding to grid cells of approximately 9.24 × 
9.24 km. This resolution reduces spatial autocorrelation 
among the original variables while minimizing the influ -
ence of fine-scale biotic interactions on species distribution 
estimates (Lobo et al. 2010).
Because collinearity and high dimensionality among 
environmental predictors can lead to unstable model perfor-
mance and increased uncertainty (De Marco and Nóbrega 
2018), we performed a principal component analysis (PCA) 
using the ENMTML package (version 1.0.3; Andrade et al.

Journal of Insect Conservation (2026) 30:86 
2020). The first eight principal components were retained, 
explaining approximately 96.5% of the total variance in 
the environmental dataset (Supplementary Table S1). The 
major axes primarily represented gradients associated with 
temperature, precipitation, and climatic seasonality, thereby 
summarizing the main environmental variation across the 
Neotropical region while reducing collinearity among pre -
dictor variables. (Supplementary Table S1). These compo -
nents were subsequently used to calibrate ensemble species 
distribution models, resulting in continuous maps of envi -
ronmental suitability for each species. Suitability values 
were then extracted and used as response variables in subse-
quent spatial analyses evaluating the overlap between spe -
cies’ potential distributions and artificial light at night.
Light pollution data
Light pollution data were obtained from the NOAA Defense 
Meteorological Satellite Program–Operational Linescan 
System (DMSP-OLS) Nighttime Lights dataset, available 
through the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform. This 
dataset provides records of nocturnal light emissions cap -
tured by the Operational Linescan System (OLS) sensor 
aboard Defense Meteorological Satellite Program (DMSP) 
satellites. Each DMSP-OLS image corresponds to a cloud-
free annual composite of nighttime observations acquired 
during hours of darkness. The data have a nominal spatial 
resolution of approximately 1 km at the equator, resampled 
from an original footprint of ~ 2.7 km, and pixel values are 
expressed as digital numbers (DN) ranging from 0 to 63, 
which serve as comparative indicators of light intensity. 
These digital number (DN) values represent relative radi -
ance and are widely used as proxies for artificial light inten-
sity in large-scale ecological studies. A value of 0 indicates 
the absence of detectable light, whereas higher values rep -
resent increasing levels of persistent artificial illumination; 
highly illuminated urban areas typically saturate at DN = 63 
(Bennie et al. 2014).
Data extraction and processing were conducted using the 
GEE programming interface integrated with R via the rgee 
package (Aybar et al. 2020), which enables direct access to 
and manipulation of geospatial datasets hosted on the plat -
form. The image collection was filtered for the year 2013, 
the most recent year available for DMSP-OLS data, and 
the band corresponding to stable lights ( stable_lights) was 
selected, representing persistent light sources such as urban 
public lighting. We acknowledge that the use of data from 
a single year does not capture temporal variation in light 
intensity or spatial distribution, and that changes may have 
occurred before or after 2013, potentially affecting spe -
cies records collected over multiple years. Nevertheless, 
DMSP-OLS Stable lights constitute a widely used proxy for 
persistent anthropogenic nighttime illumination and urban 
development patterns at regional and continental scales 
(Small et al. 2011; Li and Zhou 2017). Therefore, despite 
this limitation, the 2013 dataset is suitable for assessing 
general spatial associations between artificial light at night 
(ALAN) and species occurrence.
We processed the nighttime light data (in GeoTIFF for -
mat) in R using the terra package version 1.8 (Hijmans 
2021) (Supplementary Fig. S1). Initially, we reprojected 
the shapefile of the Neotropical region to match the coordi-
nate reference system (CRS) of the raster and subsequently 
rasterized using the rasterize function, ensuring com -
patibility with the species distribution models. For spatial 
standardization, the data were adjusted to the cell resolu -
tion of the models (~ 9.24 km × 9.24 km) and to the same 
geographic extent, ensuring precise overlap. Finally, we 
exported the processed rasters again as GeoTIFFs, preserv -
ing geospatial metadata for subsequent analyses. For this 
specific analysis, light pollution data were binarized to dis -
tinguish the presence versus absence of artificial illumina -
tion, with pixels > 0 considered illuminated. This approach 
was adopted to assess broad-scale spatial overlap between 
species’ potential distributions and permanent artificial light 
and does not account for variation in light intensity.
Potential species distribution models
To reduce uncertainty and increase the robustness of spe -
cies distribution model results, we used four algorithms 
for modeling: Bayesian Gaussian – GAU (Golding 2014), 
Maximum Entropy – MaxEnt (Phillips et al. 2017), Random 
Forest – RDF (Liaw and Wiener 2002), and Support Vecto-
rial Machine – SVM (Karatzoglou et al. 2004). These algo-
rithms encompass different theoretical bases for estimating 
habitat suitability and are widely used in species distribution 
modeling (Beeman et al. 2021; Sillero et al. 2021; Velazco et 
al. 2019). According to Barbet-Massin et al. (2012), the way 
pseudo-absences are distributed across geographic space 
can influence model performance. In this study, pseudo-
absences were generated using an environmental constraint 
method to allocate them in regions distinct from the envi -
ronmental space occupied by the species. This method helps 
to reduce bias in model training by avoiding areas that are 
likely suitable but unsampled. The pseudo-absence data 
were applied to all the algorithms mentioned above.
We used two data partitioning protocols. For species 
with fewer records (between 8 and 20), models were fitted 
using bootstrap. The dataset was split into training (70% 
of the records) and testing (30% of the records) subsets. 
For species with more than 20 records, the checkerboard 
method was applied. In this method, geographic space is 
divided into blocks, and occurrence data are split into two

subgroups for model fitting and evaluation (Andrade et 
al. 2020). Sørensen’s similarity index (Li and Guo 2013; 
Sørensen, 1948) was used as a measure of model reliabil -
ity. This index ranges from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 
indicating more accurate models (Araújo et al. 2018). For 
each species, the ensemble model was calculated as the 
mean suitability of models performing at or above the mean 
Sørensen index value for each algorithm (Pessoa da Silva 
and De Marco 2023).
To reduce omission (underprediction) and commission 
(overprediction) errors, the M-SDM method was applied 
a posteriori using the PRES technique, which restricts pre -
dictions to locations supported by occurrence records. This 
approach reduces the inclusion of environmentally suitable 
areas that may not be occupied due to historical, biogeo -
graphic, or dispersal constraints, thereby providing a more 
realistic representation of species distributions (Mendes et 
al. 2020). All analyses related to species distribution model-
ing were conducted using the ENMTML package version 
1.0.3 (Andrade et al. 2020) in R 4.3.1 (R Core Team 2023).
Analytical procedures
To investigate the degree of overlap between the potential 
distribution of each species and light pollution, we con -
ducted a spatial analysis in R. Initially, the raster files of 
species’ potential distributions were imported from a folder 
containing tiff files (Pessoa da Silva and De Marco 2023). 
The light pollution layer was resampled to match the same 
extent (the Neotropical region) and spatial resolution (cells 
of 9.24 × 9.24 km) as the generated models. For this specific 
analysis, light pollution data were binarized to distinguish 
the presence versus absence of artificial illumination, with 
pixels > 0 considered illuminated. This approach was used 
to assess broad-scale spatial overlap and does not account 
for variation in light intensity. For each species, the spatial 
overlap between its potential distribution and illuminated 
areas was assessed.
For the primary analysis, light pollution data were bina -
rized to distinguish illuminated from non-illuminated areas, 
with pixels > 0 classified as exposed to artificial light. This 
binary approach was used to quantify broad-scale spatial 
overlap between species’ potential distributions and ALAN, 
independent of variation in light intensity. In addition, we 
characterized species’ exposure to ALAN using continuous 
intensity values, including mean, minimum, and maximum 
light levels across each species’ distribution. The total num-
ber of occupied pixels (as a proxy for potential distribution 
area), as well as the proportion of illuminated pixels within 
each species’ range, were also calculated to describe spatial 
exposure patterns. To test whether species with larger poten-
tial distributions experience higher exposure to artificial 
light, we fitted a linear regression model relating distribu -
tion size (number of occupied pixels) to mean light inten -
sity across species distributions, treating each species as an 
independent replicate.
To assess the potential influence of light pollution on bio-
diversity patterns, the exposure of species richness to artifi-
cial light at night (ALAN) was estimated for each Brazilian 
biome (Amazon, Atlantic Forest, Cerrado, Caatinga, Pan -
tanal, and Pampa), as defined by the official classification 
and spatial boundaries provided by the Brazilian Institute 
of Geography and Statistics (IBGE), and for the entire Neo-
tropical region. This exposure was calculated as the differ -
ence between the mean species richness in illuminated and 
non-illuminated pixels. To quantify the uncertainty associ -
ated with this difference, 95% confidence intervals CI were 
estimated for light exposure. The lower and upper bounds 
were calculated by propagating the error from the CI already 
obtained for the illuminated and non-illuminated groups.
Results
All evaluated moth species showed some level of overlap 
between their potential distribution area and regions with 
artificial light. The percentage of overlap ranged from 
0.25% to nearly 89%. Fifty-four species (24%) had more 
than 50% of their predicted distribution area within arti -
ficially illuminated areas. Utetheisa ornatrix (Linnaeus 
1758) exhibited the highest overlap rate (88.7%), followed 
by Idalus carinosa (Schaus 1905) and Aclytia heber (Cra-
mer 1780) (both 80%). Approximately 18% of the species 
showed less than 10% overlap between illuminated and 
non-illuminated areas. Among these, Dysschema hilara 
(Weymer 1895) exhibited the lowest overlap, with only 
0.25% (Supplementary Table 2). A positive and significant 
relationship was observed between light pollution intensity 
and species’ distribution area (Fig. 1), indicating that more 
widely distributed species are proportionally more exposed 
to areas with high levels of artificial brightness.
We analyzed the spatial overlap between species occur -
rence points and the light pollution map in the study region. 
The highest light pollution values are primarily concen -
trated in urban areas and densely populated regions, whereas 
peripheral and less urbanized areas exhibit low levels of 
artificial illumination. Species occurrences are distributed 
heterogeneously with respect to light pollution. A signifi -
cant portion of occurrences is located in areas with low to 
moderate light pollution. However, some occurrences are 
also found in areas with high levels of artificial light (Sup -
plementary Figure S2).

minated areas was higher than in non-illuminated areas

across the Neotropical region and in five Brazilian biomes 
(Fig. 2A). However, the opposite pattern was observed in the

concentrated in the Amazon region (Fig. 2A and 3). In con-

areas (e.g., the Brazilian Atlantic Forest) and major urban 
centers (such as the capitals of Mexico, Venezuela, and 
Argentina) (Fig. 3). Overall, spatial patterns suggest that 
light pollution partially overlaps with areas of high richness, 
particularly in biomes such as the Atlantic Forest, whereas 
in regions of extremely high richness, such as central Ama-
zonia, the overlap is less intense.
Discussion
Although the effects of Artificial Light at Night (ALAN) 
on nocturnal fauna have been receiving increasing atten -
tion (Boyes et al. 2021; Fabian et al. 2024; Fabusova et al. 
2024; Hakbong et al. 2021; Kalinkat et al. 2021a, b; Mac-
Gregor et al. 2015; MacGregor et al. 2017; Owens et al. 
2018; Owens et al. 2024; Seymoure et al. 2025; Van Kop-
penhagen et al. 2025a; Van Langevelde et al. 2017), our 
study is the first to integrate large-scale species distribu -
tion modeling with ALAN exposure. All Arctiinae species 
evaluated here exhibit some exposure to artificial light at 
night within their potential distribution areas, highlighting 
the growing concern over the impacts of light pollution on 
nocturnal biodiversity (Boyes et al. 2020; Fabusova et al. 
2024; MacGregor et al. 2017; Owens et al. 2018). Although 
DMSP-OLS digital number (DN) values (0–63) represent 
relative radiance rather than direct measurements of illumi-
nance, they can be interpreted in the context of natural noc-
turnal light. Low DN values correspond to conditions close 
to natural darkness (e.g., new moon), intermediate values 
to moonlit conditions, and high values to levels of artificial 
illumination that exceed natural night-time light by several 
orders of magnitude. This interpretation is consistent with 
studies that compare artificial light to natural light sources 
or standardized sky brightness metrics (e.g., Falchi et al. 
2016; Seymoure et al. 2025), allowing ALAN to be inter -
preted as a gradient of replacement of natural night-time 
darkness conditions by artificial illumination.
Species with broader distributions were proportionally 
more exposed to ALAN, likely because their larger geo -
graphic ranges increase the probability of overlapping with 
urban centers, transportation corridors, and other human-
modified landscapes where artificial lighting is concen -
trated. This pattern indicates that exposure to artificial light 
is not restricted to narrowly distributed species and that even

widely distributed taxa may experience substantial exposure 
across portions of their range. As urban areas continue to 
expand and artificial lighting becomes increasingly perva -
sive, ALAN may represent a growing pressure on nocturnal 
biodiversity at continental scales, affecting species across a 
broad spectrum of geographic distributions (Li et al. 2022; 
Ren et al. 2023; Seto et al. 2012).
The vulnerability of a species to light pollution is 
assessed based on its degree of exposure, sensitivity, and 
capacity for adaptation or persistence under artificial light. 
In our set of moths, we found that the degree of exposure is 
high (exceeding 50% of the potential distribution area) for 
54 species. These species belong to various subtribes and 
exhibit different levels of dietary specialization, body sizes, 
types of chemical defense, and activity periods. However, 
due to the absence of studies investigating the sensitivity 
or adaptive capacity of these species to light, it is not yet 
possible to quantify their overall vulnerability to exposure 
to ALAN.
Recent evidence indicates that larger moths are more 
strongly affected by light pollution, exhibiting steeper 
declines in naturally dark regions than in already illumi -
nated urban areas (Know et al. 2024). This pattern sug -
gests that species adapted to naturally dark environments 
may be particularly sensitive to the introduction of artificial 
light, even at relatively low levels, because it represents a 
substantial alteration of natural nighttime conditions. Such 
changes can interfere with orientation, reproductive behav -
ior, and increase predation risk (Boyes et al. 2021; Slade et 
al. 2013). Consequently, although ALAN may affect a broad 
range of species, its impacts are likely to be disproportion -
ately greater for more sensitive groups within the Arctiinae.

35% higher in areas exposed to ALAN compared to non-
illuminated areas. This pattern remained consistent across

Because species richness estimates were derived from 
potential distributions generated by species distribution 
models (SDMs), which are based primarily on climatic suit-
ability, rather than from observed population responses, the 
higher richness found in illuminated areas reflects a greater 
spatial overlap between biodiversity-rich regions and areas 
exposed to artificial light, rather than a positive ecologi -
cal effect of ALAN. Consequently, the realized richness 
of Arctiinae in these areas may be substantially lower than 
predicted, as artificial light at night and other disturbances 
associated with urbanization may reduce local population 
persistence or prevent species from occupying habitats that 
are otherwise climatically suitable. Therefore, the overlap 
between high predicted species richness and illuminated 
areas should be interpreted as an indication of potential con-
servation risk, rather than evidence that these areas maintain 
equally high levels of realized biodiversity.
Since light pollution is typically associated with urban -
ization (Henderson et al. 2009; Elvidge et al. 2013; Bicken-
bach et al. 2014), species are likely to be more exposed not 
only to artificial light but also to various other disturbances 
characteristic of urban environments, such as chemical pol-
lution (e.g., CO₂, insecticides), noise pollution (Arctiinae 
detect and emit ultrasonic signals), reduced food availabil -
ity, and microclimatic changes.
Beyond light intensity and spatial extent, the ecological 
consequences of ALAN also depend strongly on its spectral 
composition and lighting characteristics. Different lighting 
technologies emit distinct wavelength distributions, result -
ing in varying levels of attraction and behavioral disruption 
in nocturnal insects. Short-wavelength-rich (blue-enriched) 
light has been reported to elicit stronger attraction in moths 
than longer-wavelength light, and may also produce stron -
ger behavioral and physiological effects in some nocturnal 
insects (Van Langevelde et al. 2011; Somers-Yeates et al. 
2013). However, responses to light colour are not necessar-
ily consistent across species or experimental contexts, and 
some studies have found limited or contrasting effects of 
wavelength on moth activity and other ecological responses. 
Thus, although spectral composition is an important com -
ponent of ALAN, its effects may depend on species-specific 
sensory and behavioural characteristics, as well as on other 
properties of the light source. Likewise, lighting design, 
including shielding, beam directionality, and light spill, 
determines how far artificial light penetrates surrounding 
natural habitats, thereby influencing the spatial extent of 
ecological disturbance (Owens et al. 2024; Van Koppenha-
gen et al. 2025a). Consequently, ALAN can disrupt orienta-
tion, movement, pheromone-mediated mate attraction, and 
ultrasonic communication, reducing reproductive success 
and altering ecological interactions such as plant–pollina -
tor networks and other trophic processes that support moth 
populations (Altermatt et al. 2009; Boyes et al. 2020; Mac-
Gregor et al. 2017; Owens et al. 2018; Van Geffen et al. 
2015a, b; Van Langevelde et al. 2017).
In the Pantanal, species richness was higher in areas with-
out ALAN than in illuminated areas, suggesting that dark 
areas may play an important role in maintaining nocturnal 
biodiversity. Although the mechanisms underlying this pat-
tern remain uncertain, the introduction of artificial lighting 
into landscapes that historically experienced low levels of 
nighttime illumination may disrupt the behavior and eco -
logical interactions of nocturnal insects. The predominantly 
open vegetation of the Pantanal may allow artificial light 
to spread over greater distances than in densely forested 
environments, potentially increasing the spatial extent of 
ALAN exposure (Kyba et al. 2015; Jechow et al. 2021). 
The Pantanal is also experiencing increasing environmen -
tal pressures, including prolonged droughts, more frequent 
and intense wildfires, and extensive land-use change, with 
approximately 45% of its original area already modified by 
human activities (Alho et al. 2011; Tomas et al. 2019).
The Amazon region, southern Mexico (Yucatán Penin -
sula), and southern Central America (Panama, Nicaragua, 
and Honduras) harbor the highest potential richness of Arc-
tiinae species. Coincidentally, these regions currently exhibit 
low levels of exposure to ALAN. However, urbanization in 
the Amazon has been rapidly advancing, transforming eco -
systems through the expansion of roads, agriculture, and 
mining activities (Fenzl et al. 2020), which may accelerate 
the spread of ALAN into previously isolated areas. There -
fore, as suggested for the Pantanal, the establishment of new 
protected areas and the preservation of existing ones would 
be highly recommended.
This study opens new perspectives by highlighting the 
impacts of ALAN on nocturnal biodiversity in the Neotropi-
cal region, emphasizing the vulnerability of Arctiinae moths. 
The overlap between areas of high species richness and arti-
ficially illuminated zones indicates significant ecological 
risks, which are likely to intensify with urban expansion and 
large-scale development projects in the Amazon and other 
biomes. Future research should expand monitoring efforts 
in under-sampled regions and integrate spatial, functional, 
and behavioral data to better understand the responses of 
moths and other nocturnal organisms to the direct and indi-
rect effects of ALAN. In addition, an important limitation
