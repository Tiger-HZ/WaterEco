Galveston Bay, the largest estuary on the Texas coast, receives industrial effluent from the Houston Ship Channel — one of the most heavily contaminated waterways in the United States — while simultaneously supporting warm-water cyanobacterial blooms and a major commercial and recreational fishery. I hypothesize that Galveston Bay represents a convergent exposure environment in which cyanobacterial neurotoxins (BMAA, DAB, AEG) and industrial contaminants (dioxins, PCBs, petrochemical compounds) accumulate in the same water column, sediment, and food web, reaching human populations through multiple simultaneous pathways: dietary ingestion of contaminated seafood, dermal and mucosal contact during recreational water use, and potential aerosolization from the bay surface. Despite extensive documentation of industrial contamination and growing evidence that cyanobacteria are seasonally present in the bay, no published study has measured BMAA, DAB, or AEG concentrations in Galveston Bay water, sediment, or commercially harvested seafood. Analogous Gulf Coast environments in South Florida have shown BMAA concentrations in marine organisms up to 7,000 μg/g — comparable to the levels originally linked to ALS/Parkinsonism-dementia complex on Guam. Texas reports an age-adjusted ALS prevalence of 3.7 per 100,000 (Mehta et al., 2024), below the national average of 4.4, but this figure likely reflects structural undercounting in a state with only 0.62 ALS specialist clinics per million population (Jones, 2026). I propose a targeted study to measure cyanobacterial neurotoxin concentrations in Galveston Bay water, sediment, and commercially harvested species, and to assess whether the combination of cyanotoxin and industrial contaminant exposure constitutes a previously uncharacterized convergent risk environment for ALS and other neurodegenerative diseases.
