<p xmlns:xsi="http://www.w3.org/2001/XMLSchema-instance" class="first" dir="auto" id="d1213161e89">Coffee producing countries need to quantify and manage emissions as part of their Nationally Appropriate Mitigating Action commitments, as do major coffee companies with Net Zero commitments. Estimates of Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions from coffee production and in-country processing present a 10-fold variation depending on the sources included, study boundaries, assumptions made and variable production practices [1]. It is possible to identify the major emissions sources, such as nitrogen fertilizer applications and management of crop residue (<a data-untrusted="" href="https://www.sustaincoffee.org/coffee-carbon-research" id="d1213161e91" target="xrefwindow">Sustainable Coffee Challenge 2026</a>). While the minimum agronomic emissions per kg of coffee increase with higher levels of nitrogen application, there is capacity for most farms to increase nitrogen-use efficiency. Emissions from coffee pulp can be considerably reduced though mixing with lower-N plant residues. The inter-study variability in non-CO<sub>2</sub> GHGs (e.g. sensitivity to N application rates) is further exacerbated following changes in Global Warming Potential estimates for both N<sub>2</sub>O and CH<sub>4</sub> depending on the source with the latter further varying by source such as mucilage and wastewater treatment. While estimating carbon stocks from agroforestry shade trees is relatively straightforward, estimating annual sequestration rates is complex without application of models for tree growth and management (e.g. Walsh et al. [2]). Just measuring tree growth is insufficient, additionality and permanence of that sequestration should be addressed. How carbon removals are estimated and allocated to coffee affects whether coffee production appears carbon neutral. By using consistent methods indications are that productive farms with moderate shade may be close to carbon neutral and generally profitable, but to become carbon sinks (to offset emissions from elsewhere in the value chain) requires greater presence of shade trees, leading to a reduction in profitability [2]. Through methodical observations arrived at by research and supported by literature it is relatively clear which areas and practices can contribute to reducing emissions or increasing sequestration, however assessing carbon neutrality or net zero with reasonable certainty remains a challenge.
