Energy management in photosynthetic organisms occurs at multiple levels, including the electron transfer and 
metabolic reactions. Salt stress imposes osmotic, ionic, energetic, and oxidative constraints on cyanobacteria, 
profoundly reshaping carbon allocation and photosynthetic performance. To maintain osmotic balance, cyanobacteria 
employ a salt-out strategy, accumulating compatible solutes such as glucosylglycerol and sucrose while maintaining 
low intracellular Na⁺ concentrations. This review provides a comprehensive perspective by synthesizing current 
knowledge on cyanobacterial overflow under salt stress, emphasizing its physiological context rather than focusing 
on a single dedicated pathway. We compare metabolite overflow responses under nitrogen limitation, high light, 
mixotrophy, and combined stresses, and discuss methodological approaches integrating targeted analytics, untargeted 
exometabolomics, energy charge measurements, and flux analysis. Collectively, energy management via metabolite 
overflow under salt stress is proposed as an emergent, condition-dependent outcome of integrated carbon, energy, 
and redox regulation.
Highlights
● Salt stress imposes energetic constraints and reshapes photosynthetic performance
● Energy management occurs at the levels of electron transfer and metabolism
● Response to salt stress is proposed for biomanufacturing via energy management
Introduction
Cyanobacteria, algae, and green plants can harvest sunlight 
and store it as chemical energy. Specifically, they capture 
sunlight energy and transfer it to the energy carriers ATP 
and NADPH in living cells (Long et al. 2015, Ort et al. 
2015, Hou et al. 2017, Nocera 2017, Shen et al. 2021, 
Hou and Allakhverdiev 2023). Photosynthetic energy is 
regulated and managed via a variety of mechanisms to 
maintain normal growth and to survive environmental

stress (Aro et al. 1993, Baker and Bowyer 1994). Energy 
management occurs at multiple levels, including at 
the level of electron transfer and at the level of metabolic 
reactions. Mechanisms at the electron transfer level have 
been investigated for decades, including the xanthophyll 
cycle, nonphotochemical quenching, cyclic and alternative 
electron transfer, etc. (Aro et al. 1993, Baker and Bowyer 
1994, Long et al. 1994, Niyogi 1999, Kramer et al. 
2003). In contrast, energy management mechanisms at 
the metabolic level are less understood. Carbon fixation 
and metabolism also play an important role in energy 
regulation. 
As biomass growth is often limited by nutrient 
limitation or other environmental conditions, a significant 
amount of organic compounds can be secreted into the 
medium in what is known as overflow metabolism. We 
have reported the overflow metabolism in mutant strains 
of cyanobacteria to study its potential in producing organic 
acids, and to identify such a metabolism as an energy 
regulation mechanism (Carrieri et al. 2012, Cano et al. 
2018).
Most living cells can only survive within a limited 
range of salinity. While life is believed to have originated 
in the saline environment of early oceans, modern 
organisms exhibit significant differences in their tolerance 
to salt (Oren 2014, Gunarathne et al. 2023). Salinity 
remains a crucial environmental factor shaping aquatic 
ecosystems and is increasingly a serious constraint in 
terrestrial environments due to the global expansion of 
soil salinization (Gunarathne et al. 2023). As a result, 
salt acclimation has garnered long-standing interest not 
only in ecology but also in biotechnology, particularly 
for improving salt tolerance in crop plants (Erdmann and 
Hagemann 2001).
Algae and cyanobacteria are central to salt-acclimation 
research. These photosynthetic microorganisms 
naturally inhabit environments with high, variable, or 
compositionally complex salinity, ranging from freshwater 
to marine and hypersaline systems. In addition to their 
ecological relevance, cyanobacteria serve as useful model 
organisms for understanding how photosynthetic cells 
respond to osmotic and ionic stress. This is due to their 
relatively simple cellular organization, short generation 
times, and well-developed genetic tools (Santos-Merino 
et al. 2021). Many characteristics of cyanobacterial 
photosynthesis, membrane composition, and signal 
transduction closely resemble those of chloroplasts in 
higher plants, making them valuable models for plant 
stress biology (Gross et al. 2012).
In cyanobacteria, an increase in external salinity 
poses two immediate and interconnected challenges. 
First, reduced water availability leads to water loss from 
the cell, resulting in a decreased cell volume and turgor 
pressure. Second, elevated concentrations of inorganic 
ions increase the passive influx of ions such as Na⁺ and Cl⁻, 
disrupting enzyme activity, membrane potential, and ionic 
homeostasis (Yang et al. 2020). Successful acclimation, 
therefore, requires a coordinated cellular response aimed at 
restoring osmotic balance and cell volume, re-establishing 
ion homeostasis through regulated transport and membrane 
adjustments, and protecting proteins, nucleic acids, and 
membranes from the combined effects of high ionic 
strength and reduced water activity (Klähn and Hagemann 
2011). In most cyanobacteria, these goals are achieved 
through a salt-out strategy, in which intracellular inorganic 
ion concentrations are kept low while osmotic adjustment 
is facilitated by the accumulation of compatible organic 
solutes. For example, in Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803, 
glucosylglycerol, and sucrose serve as major osmolytes 
(Hagemann 2011). The transcriptomics analysis revealed 
that the salt stress response is energetically costly and 
involves extensive changes in gene expression, protein 
turnover, antioxidant defenses, and central metabolism 
(Hagemann 2011, Klähn and Hagemann 2011).
Salt stress is also closely linked to photosynthetic 
function. Elevated Na + and Cl – concentrations impair 
electron transport, disrupt cellular K⁺/Na⁺ balance, 
and promote the formation of reactive oxygen species 
(ROS), ultimately damaging the photosynthetic apparatus 
(Long et al. 1994). The short-term salt stress is an acute 
shock response occurring within minutes to hours, and 
the long-term salt acclimation is a steady-state 
physiological adaptation over extended periods. This 
distinction is critical, as metabolic and photosynthetic 
responses differ substantially between these two states. 
For example, under short-term salt stress, D1 protein 
repair inhibition contributes to photoinhibition; however, 
in long-term salt-acclimated cells, D1 turnover rates can 
recover to levels comparable to non-stressed conditions. 
Since cyanobacteria depend on a continuous light-driven 
electron flow, this creates a fundamental physiological 
conflict: under salt stress, cells must allocate more 
energy towards homeostasis and protection, while 
the photosynthetic machinery becomes increasingly 
vulnerable to over-reduction and oxidative damage (Long 
et al. 1994). Understanding how cyanobacteria resolve this 
imbalance is a central question in stress physiology.
One adaptive response that has attracted attention 
is metabolite overflow, defined as the excretion of fixed 
carbon as small organic compounds when photosynthetic 
carbon input exceeds the cell's capacity for growth or stable 
storage (Hudson 2021). Rather than representing a simple 
loss of carbon, overflow metabolism is now considered 
a regulated strategy that stabilizes cellular redox state and 
energy balance under metabolic constraints. Disruption 
of major carbon storage sinks, such as glycogen, leads to 
the extracellular accumulation of metabolites, including 
pyruvate and 2-oxoglutarate. Similar excretion patterns 
can occur due to high light or nutrient imbalances (Cano 
et al. 2018). Salt stress can promote overflow through 
several mechanisms: increased ATP demand for ion 
transport and compatible solute synthesis, altered carbon 
partitioning away from biomass, and heightened oxidative 
pressure on the photosynthetic electron transport chain 
(Allakhverdiev and Murata 2008). At the same time, 
the biosynthesis of osmolytes can compete for carbon 
internally, which means salt stress may either suppress 
or enhance overflow, depending on the species, genetic 
background, nutrient availability, and light conditions. 
These opposing influences make salt-associated metabolite

overflow a complex and still incompletely understood 
phenomenon. 
Beyond its physiological significance, metabolite 
overflow under salt stress has important biotechnological 
implications. Large-scale cyanobacterial cultivation is 
often envisioned in seawater-based systems to conserve 
freshwater resources and reduce contamination risks 
(Bolay et al. 2025). However, salt acclimation imposes 
substantial energetic costs and can divert carbon away from 
productivity, while overflow represents both a challenge, 
due to carbon loss, and an opportunity, as naturally secreted 
metabolites could be redirected toward valuable products 
such as organic acids, alcohols, or polyhydroxybutyrate 
(PHB)/3-hydroxybutyrate-related compounds (Bai et al. 
2025). Achieving predictable and efficient production 
under saline conditions, therefore, requires a mechanistic 
understanding of how osmotic and ionic stress intersect 
with photosynthetic performance, carbon storage, and 
metabolite export. In addition, hypoosmotic shock 
reduction in salinity can activate mechanosensitive 
channels, resulting in the release of intracellular compatible 
solutes, a process known as ‘bacterial milking’, which has 
been explored for metabolite harvesting.
In this review, we provide perspective by synthesizing 
current knowledge on cyanobacterial energy management 
under salt stress and integrating physiological, 
methodological, and mechanistic perspectives. We first 
summarize the core processes underlying salt acclimation 
in cyanobacteria, with emphasis on osmotic adjustment, 
ion transport, and photosynthetic vulnerability. We 
then describe experimental strategies for detecting and 
quantifying overflow, including targeted analytical 
approaches and exometabolomics compatible with high-
salinity systems. Next, we compare overflow responses 
across environmental conditions and genetic backgrounds 
to identify recurring patterns associated with salinity stress. 
By integrating these perspectives, this review aims to 
provide a framework for interpreting overflow metabolism 
in natural saline environments and for guiding the rational 
design of cyanobacterial strains and cultivation strategies 
for seawater-based bioprocesses.
Physiology of cyanobacteria under salt stress
Cyanobacteria encounter a multifaceted physiological 
challenge in response to salt stress, which involves complex 
interactions among osmotic stress, ionic imbalance, and 
secondary oxidative and metabolic stresses rather than 
a singular disturbance (Erdmann and Hagemann 2001, 
Yang et al. 2020). An increase in external salinity exposes 
these microorganisms to a variety of physiological 
responses; namely, the influx of sodium (Na⁺) and chloride 
(Cl⁻) ions occurs simultaneously (Fig. 1). These stresses are 
not independent; rather, they interact in intricate ways that 
influence both the immediate cellular responses to elevated 
salinity and the long-term acclimatization processes that 
enable growth to resume in saline environments. While 
most of our in-depth knowledge arises from a relatively 
small number of extensively studied model strains, 
the fundamental physiological strategies employed to 
cope with salt stress are notably conserved across diverse 
cyanobacterial species inhabiting freshwater, brackish, 
marine, and hypersaline ecosystems.
Early salt shock responses: rapid osmotic and ionic 
imbalance: When cyanobacteria are abruptly exposed 
to elevated salinity, they undergo an immediate phase of 
osmotic shock, marked by rapid water efflux from the cell 
due to a steep osmotic gradient (Fig. 1). This loss results in 
a significant reduction in cell volume and turgor pressure. 
If this loss of turgor is not promptly compensated, it can 
destabilize membranes and compromise the structural

integrity of the cellular architecture (Pade and Hagemann 
2015). Additionally, the elevated concentrations of 
Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in the external environment facilitate 
their passive influx into the cytoplasm, driven by 
established electrochemical gradients. The uncontrolled 
accumulation of these ions poses a substantial threat 
to cellular bioenergetics and viability by disrupting 
enzyme activities, membrane potential, and intracellular 
pH. This acute phase of the salt shock occurs within 
seconds to minutes and is physiologically distinct from 
the subsequent acclimatization period. Transcriptomic 
and proteomic studies consistently indicate that numerous 
stress-responsive genes and proteins may be transiently 
induced during this initial response phase, signifying 
an emergency mobilization of cellular resources rather 
than a transition toward stable metabolic adaptation 
(Yu et al. 2024). As the duration of salt exposure increases, 
many of these early physiological alterations diminish, 
suggesting that measurements taken shortly after the onset 
of salt exposure may not accurately reflect the physiology 
of cells that have transitioned to acclimation.
Osmotic adjustment via compatible solutes: To restore 
turgor pressure while avoiding toxic intracellular ion 
concentrations, most cyanobacteria employ a salt-out 
strategy. To avoid high internal concentration of inorganic 
ions, Na+ is actively exported, while osmotic adjustment 
is achieved by synthesizing or importing compatible 
solutes, small organic molecules that can accumulate to 
high concentrations without impairing enzymatic function. 
The specific types of compatible solutes produced 
generally correspond to the level of salt tolerance in that 
species. For example, many moderately halotolerant 
species predominantly synthesize glucosylglycerol, while 
species with low salt tolerance use trehalose or sucrose, 
and halophiles use glycine betaine or glutamate (Pade et al. 
2015). The metabolic investment required for the synthesis 
of these compatible solutes is substantial, necessitating 
significant amounts of fixed carbon, adenosine 
triphosphate (ATP), and reducing power. This leads to 
a pronounced reorganization of central carbon metabolism 
during the acclimatization process (Furmaniak et al. 2017, 
Klähn et al. 2021). The diversion of metabolic resources 
toward osmolyte production disrupts the typical balance 
between growth, storage, and other metabolic obligations. 
Consequently, cyanobacteria that have acclimated to 
salt stress frequently exhibit reduced growth rates and 
distinct intracellular and extracellular metabolite profiles 
compared to unstressed cells, even after restoration of 
osmotic balance.
Ion homeostasis and membrane transport: 
The accumulation of compatible solutes is insufficient 
for survival in saline conditions due to the ongoing influx 
of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Thus, it becomes imperative for 
cyanobacteria to actively regulate ion transport to prevent 
cytotoxic concentrations of these ions from accumulating 
within the cell. Maintaining a high intracellular potassium 
(K⁺)/sodium (Na⁺) ratio is especially critical, as numerous 
enzymes, ribosomes, and pivotal metabolic processes 
are exceedingly sensitive to elevated Na⁺ concentrations 
(Roychoudhury et al. 2025). Ion homeostasis is achieved 
through the coordinated activity of various membrane 
transport systems, including Na⁺/H⁺ antiporters and other 
specific ion transporters. The energy demands of these 
systems are considerable, as the export of ions is coupled 
to proton gradients generated by both photosynthetic and 
respiratory electron transport processes. The maintenance 
energy cost of salt stress is 10× the energy cost without 
salt stress in E. coli (Schink et al. 2024). Furthermore, salt 
stress alters membrane lipid composition and transporter 
abundance, thereby influencing membrane permeability 
and transport efficiency (Roychoudhury et al. 2025). These 
findings underscore the notion that membranes serve not 
only as passive barriers but as integral regulatory platforms 
essential for acclimation to marine environments.
Effects of salt stress on membranes and the 
photosynthetic apparatus: The plasma membrane and 
thylakoid membrane system are directly affected by salt 
stress (Sudhir et al. 2005). Changes in ionic strength and 
water activity impact membrane fluidity, protein–lipid 
interactions, and the stability of large photosynthetic 
complexes. Because cyanobacteria carry out both 
photosynthesis and a significant portion of respiratory 
electron transport within the thylakoid membrane, 
perturbations at this level have system-wide consequences 
(Agrawal et al. 2015). Experimental studies show 
that salt stress disrupts electron transport in both 
Photosystem II (PSII) and Photosystem I (PSI). Altered 
K⁺/Na⁺ ratios and direct ionic effects on protein complexes 
impair electron flow, increasing susceptibility to 
photoinhibition. Importantly, salt stress does not primarily 
accelerate photodamage itself but instead inhibits 
the repair of damaged PSII by suppressing the synthesis 
of key components, particularly the D1 protein encoded 
by the psbA genes (Allakhverdiev et al. 2002, Marin 
et al. 2003, Allakhverdiev and Murata 2008). As a result, 
photoinhibition becomes more severe under illumination, 
even at moderate salt concentrations. These responses are 
not unique to salt stress but are part of a broader general 
stress response shared across multiple abiotic stress 
conditions.
Oxidative stress as a secondary consequence: 
The disruption of photosynthetic electron transport 
caused by salt stress significantly impacts cyanobacterial 
physiology by leading to an over-reduction of the electron 
transport chain. This over-reduction facilitates the increased 
production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), primarily 
at the PSII and PSI complexes. The phenomenon is 
particularly pronounced when light absorption persists at 
high levels despite reduced metabolic demand, leading to 
unchecked ROS accumulation that can adversely affect 
cellular integrity and function (Liu et al. 2021). Elevated 
concentrations of ROS are highly damaging; they can 
cause oxidative damage to vital cellular structures, such 
as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. This oxidative 
damage further undermines photosynthetic efficiency 
and disrupts membrane integrity, thereby harming

cellular health. Moreover, high ROS concentrations can 
inhibit protein synthesis by targeting essential translation 
factors, thereby exacerbating the inhibition of PSII repair 
mechanisms and intensifying photoinhibition, a state 
in which the photosynthetic apparatus is subjected to 
stress and impaired in the light-energized environment

damage, cyanobacteria have evolved a range of protective 
strategies. These mechanisms encompass the activation 
of various antioxidant enzymes that scavenge ROS, 
the accumulation of carotenoids that provide 
photoprotection, the activation of energy-dissipating 
pathways that help regulate excess excitation energy, and 
the synthesis of photoprotective proteins, all of which 
work synergistically to mitigate oxidative stress.
Two-phase acclimation and system-level reprogram-
ming: Cyanobacterial acclimation to salt stress can be 
described in terms of two distinct but interrelated phases 
(Fig. 1). The initial shock phase is marked by rapid 
physiological responses, characterized by extensive 
alterations in gene expression and metabolic pathways 
that protect the organism from immediate cellular damage. 
During this phase, there is an influx of signaling that 
triggers an array of protective measures aimed at stabilizing 
cellular function under stress. Following this initial 
response, the organism enters a longer-term adaptation 
phase, during which a narrower and more specialized suite 
of processes is sustained. This includes the synthesis of 
compatible solutes that help maintain cellular osmotic 
balance, the activation of ion transport mechanisms to 
manage toxic ion concentrations, membrane remodeling 
to enhance cellular resilience, and the fortification of 
antioxidant defenses to counteract persistent oxidative 
stress (Hagemann 2011, Klähn and Hagemann 2011, Kim 
et al. 2016, Yang et al. 2020). Comprehensive system-
level analyses have revealed that acclimating to saline 
environments necessitates a global reallocation of cellular 
resources. In response to increasing energy demands for 
ion pumping and repair processes, carbon flux is redirected 
away from biomass production toward maintenance and 
protective functions. This strategic reallocation implies that 
photosynthetic output must be meticulously controlled to 
prevent a redox imbalance that could compromise cellular 
integrity. Thus, the cell's metabolic landscape undergoes 
significant restructuring under saline conditions, reflecting 
the organism's acute adaptability to environmental 
stressors (Erdmann and Hagemann 2001, Hagemann 2011, 
Klähn and Hagemann 2011, Yang et al. 2020).
Physiological context for energy management under 
salt stress: From a physiological standpoint, salt stress 
engenders a complex interplay between energy availability 
and biosynthetic capacity that is often untenable. 
The photosystems in cyanobacteria may continue to 
capture light energy effectively; however, growth and 
macromolecular synthesis are constrained by osmotic, 
ionic, and oxidative challenges. Under these adverse 
conditions, the synthesis of compatible solutes, which 
serve as protective osmotic agents, starts to compete 
with other carbon sinks within the cell. Concurrently, 
the associated maintenance energy costs rise sharply, 
leading to a resource balance precariously adjusted. 
The overflow of pyruvate and 2-oxoglutarate observed 
in glycogen mutants during N-starvation is abolished by 
adding salt stress because the synthesis of glucosylglycerol 
replaces the sink (Díaz-Troya et al. 2020).
In response to the physiological challenges posed by 
salt stress, cyanobacteria may redirect excess fixed carbon 
into storage polymers, a strategy that helps preserve some 
nutrients. In certain instances, they may also excrete 
low-molecular-mass metabolites into the surrounding 
environment to manage internal stress. The extent to 
which salt stress facilitates or inhibits this metabolite 
overflow depends on a delicate balance among osmolyte 
production capacity, available storage capacity, and 
overall photosynthetic activity. It is crucial to understand 
that overflow metabolism under salt stress does not arise as 
a straightforward or inevitable result of increased salinity; 
rather, it is an emergent property that reflects the intricate, 
integrated nature of cellular physiology under challenging 
environmental conditions. To date, there is limited direct 
evidence demonstrating metabolite excretion under 
steady-state salt-acclimated conditions (Carrieri et al. 
2010, Díaz-Troya et al. 2020); therefore, this option should 
be considered a hypothesis requiring further investigation.
Mechanisms of cyanobacterial energy 
management 
The phenomenon of energy management, including 
metabolite overflow, in cyanobacteria can best be 
interpreted as a complex interplay of physiological, 
metabolic, and regulatory mechanisms, rather than 
a simplistic linear pathway (Chi et al. 2019). As shown 
in Fig. 2, current research suggests that the mechanisms 
facilitating overflow can be categorized into three 
conceptual layers: (a) the driving forces that promote 
overflow, (b) the metabolic branch points at which excess 
carbon is generated, and (c) the pathways that enable 
metabolites to be extruded into the extracellular space.

face significant challenges that impose a substantial

as stress defense and cellular maintenance, specifically 
ion transport mechanisms, membrane repair processes, 
antioxidant defense systems, and the production of

diverted resources, processes such as protein synthesis, 
cell growth, and cellular division commonly slow down. 
Interestingly, while light harvesting and carbon fixation 
can persist, the overall anabolic activity is hindered. 
Under these conditions, anabolic sinks, such as biomass 
formation and macromolecular synthesis, become limited, 
leading to an accumulation of fixed carbon within central 
metabolic intermediates. This accumulation consequently 
increases the likelihood that carbon will be diverted from 
intracellular metabolic pathways, ultimately leading to 
the export of overflow metabolites from the cell (Hudson 
2021). Alternatively, even salt-stressed cells do not 
excrete organic compounds under steady-state conditions, 
because they build up a high intracellular carbon sink, 
the compatible solute pool; hence, excess organic 
carbon should be used to maintain this pool according to 
the growth rate. While this mechanism is plausible based 
on current metabolic understanding, direct experimental 
validation under salt stress conditions remains limited. 
Energy and redox balance: Another significant aspect 
of overflow metabolism is its role in maintaining 
energy and redox balance amidst metabolic imbalances. 
The process of exporting reduced carbon compounds can 
help mitigate elevated levels of intracellular metabolites 
while also aiding in the dissipation of excess ATP and 
reduced equivalents generated during photosynthesis 
(Hudson 2021). This phenomenon has been prominently 
demonstrated in studies involving glycogen-deficient 
cyanobacterial mutants, in which the excretion of organic 
acids is tightly correlated with changes in cellular energy 
charge, necessitating compensatory adjustments in 
photosynthetic activity. Under salt stress, this balance can 
be altered in multiple ways. Increased ATP consumption 
from processes such as ion pumping and the synthesis 
of compatible solutes heightens the risk of oxidative 
stress, especially under illuminated conditions (Jassal 
et al. 2025). Furthermore, the inherent capacity for internal 
carbon storage sinks might be affected or even temporarily 
saturated. Collectively, these factors foster an environment 
in which overflow metabolism emerges as a strategic 
approach to stabilize cellular energy and maintain redox 
homeostasis. Consistent with this view, mutant-based 
studies by Cano et al. (2018) that disrupt carbon–nitrogen 
balance, such as glycogen-deficient strains, demonstrate 
distinct overflow phenotypes, highlighting the central role 
of metabolic regulation in controlling metabolite excretion.
Intersection of stress signaling and carbon regulation: 
The acclimatization of cyanobacteria to high salinity 
is a highly coordinated process governed by intricate 
regulatory networks that blend environmental sensing with 
metabolic control mechanisms. Salt-responsive histidine 
kinases, alongside global transcriptional regulators, 
regulate expression patterns encompassing not only 
stress defense mechanisms but also key central metabolic 
pathways (Chaffai et al. 2024). Central regulatory systems 
that interlink carbon and nitrogen metabolism respond 
to fluctuations in the concentrations of intracellular 
metabolites such as ATP, ADP, and 2-oxoglutarate 
(Forchhammer and Selim 2020). This integration of 
regulatory networks suggests that overflow metabolism 
may function as an actively modulated response rather 
than a purely passive outcome of metabolite accumulation. 
In this context, overflow metabolism reflects 
an orchestrated redistribution of carbon flux catalyzed 
by stress signaling networks that prioritize cellular survival 
at the expense of growth (Hudson 2021). The recent work 
by Alford et al. analyzed overflow metabolisms in different 
mutants with affected C/N regulation (Alford et al. 2025).
Metabolic branch points
Pyruvate as a central metabolic node: Pyruvate plays 
a pivotal role in the metabolic network of cyanobacteria, 
serving as a central hub at the convergence of glycolysis, 
Calvin cycle-derived carbon metabolism, and acetyl-CoA 
formation. When the pathways responsible for downstream 
utilization become saturated or limited, pyruvate contents 
can increase significantly. This excess pyruvate may 
be excreted directly or rerouted to fermentative or 
overflow pathways, which may be native to the organism 
or introduced through metabolic engineering efforts 
(Pathania and Srivastava 2025). Experimental evidence 
strongly supports pyruvate as a major branching point for 
overflow metabolism. For example, Cano et al. (2018) 
demonstrated that a glycogen-deficient Synechocystis sp. 
PCC 6803 mutant (ΔglgC) accumulated high intracellular 
energy charge and released significant amounts of 
pyruvate and 2-oxoglutarate into the extracellular 
medium, particularly under conditions of elevated light 
input (Foyer 2018). These results indicated that when 
the primary carbon storage pathway is impaired, central 
metabolites such as pyruvate become key outlets for 
excess photosynthetic carbon and energy. Numerous 
investigations of organic acid production in cyanobacteria 
have consistently underscored the importance of pyruvate 
as a critical branching point from which a variety of 
overflow products can be synthesized (Pathania and 
Srivastava 2025). In addition to organic acids, several 
amino acids derived from pyruvate may also contribute to 
overflow, particularly under nitrogen-limited conditions 
when the glycogen sink is absent.
Metabolite 2-oxoglutarate as a carbon-nitrogen signal: 
Metabolite 2-oxoglutarate (2-OG) serves dual functions in 
cyanobacterial systems, acting as both a crucial metabolic 
intermediate and a signaling molecule that links carbon 
status to nitrogen availability. Under nitrogen limitation, 
2-OG tends to accumulate and is particularly prone to 
excretion in strains with impaired carbon storage (Cano 
et al. 2018, Esteves-Ferreira et al. 2018). Its intracellular 
concentrations are finely tuned by nitrogen assimilation

PII-dependent signaling networks. The dynamics of 
2-OG are likely to be affected by salt stress, even when 
sufficient external nitrogen is present. Variations in energy 
status, redox balance, and nitrogen-assimilation efficiency 
can induce a state resembling functional nitrogen stress, 
leading to enhanced accumulation of 2-OG and its 
potential release as an overflow metabolite (Cano et al.

input exceeded cellular assimilation capacity, indicating 
that energy imbalance alone can trigger overflow 
metabolism. The experimental evidence for 2-oxoglutarate 
accumulation is best demonstrated in studies where cells 
are shifted from nitrate to ammonium conditions.
Carbon storage vs. export: glycogen, PHB, and 
compatible solutes: Cyanobacteria possess multiple 
internal carbon sinks that can buffer excess fixed carbon, 
including glycogen, polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) in certain 
species or conditions, and compatible solutes such as 
glucosylglycerol and sucrose, which are utilized during 
salt acclimation (Lucius 2024). Among these, glycogen 
represents a major storage sink that generally suppresses 
overflow when functioning efficiently. Salt stress increases 
the demand for compatible solutes, drawing carbon into 
osmolyte synthesis. A useful working hypothesis is 
that if the capacity or regulation of compatible solute 
synthesis is limiting or delayed, particularly during early 
phases of salt shock, fixed carbon may transiently exceed 
the buffering capacity of internal sinks and spill into 
overflow metabolites such as organic acids or other 
exudates, based on the experimental data from rice 
(Owen 1987). This hypothesis is not a reliable support 
for this scenario in salt-shocked cyanobacteria and may 
be tested using time-resolved exometabolomics combined 
with measurements of osmolyte pathway enzyme 
expression and abundance, including key enzymes such as 
glucosylglycerol-phosphate synthase (Hagemann 2011). 
This hypothesis remains to be validated specifically in 
cyanobacteria under salt stress.
Export routes: Overflow metabolism requires not only 
the production of excess metabolites but also their release 
into the extracellular environment. Several non-mutually 
exclusive routes may contribute to this process.
One possibility is transport via specific or low-
specificity membrane transporters. Some organic acids and 
sugars are known to be exported by dedicated transport 
systems, and salt acclimation is accompanied by changes 
in transporter expression profiles, suggesting a role for 
regulated export mechanisms. Alternatively, changes in 
membrane permeability under salt and oxidative stress 
may facilitate passive leakage. Salt-induced alterations in 
membrane composition, combined with ROS-mediated 
damage, can increase membrane permeability and modify 
efflux behavior, thereby contributing to the accumulation 
of extracellular metabolites. A third, species-dependent 
mechanism involves the release of outer membrane 
vesicles, which can contribute to extracellular pools 
of metabolites and small molecules, even if not 
always classified as classical overflow metabolism. 
Mechanosensitive channels likely play a critical role in 
mediating metabolite release during osmotic transitions, 
particularly during hypoosmotic shock.
A major unresolved challenge in the field is 
distinguishing between regulated export and damage-
associated leakage. Addressing this question requires 
an integrated experimental approach that combines 
exometabolomics with viability assays, membrane 
integrity measurements, and time-resolved stress markers 
(Pinu et al. 2018). Such strategies are essential for 
determining whether overflow under salt stress represents 
an adaptive, regulated process or a byproduct of cellular 
damage or, most likely, a continuum between the two. Cano 
et al. (2018) addressed this question using live/dead cell 
staining. In that example, live cells were found to overflow 
metabolites (Cano et al. 2018). Additionally, Kirsch et al. 
(2017) reported the role of the glucosylglycerol-degrading 
enzyme in acclimation to fluctuating salinities.
Overflow metabolism under different conditions
Overflow metabolism in cyanobacteria is not restricted to 
a single environmental trigger but emerges across 
a range of conditions that create imbalances among carbon 
fixation, energy input, and biosynthetic capacity. Studies 
across model cyanobacteria have shown that nutrient 
availability, light intensity, carbon source, and salinity all 
influence whether fixed carbon is retained internally or 
released into the extracellular environment.
Carbon–nutrient imbalance: nitrogen limitation and 
glycogen as a primary carbon sink: Nitrogen starvation 
is one of the best-characterized conditions that promote 
overflow-like phenotypes in cyanobacteria. Under 
nitrogen deprivation, photosynthetic carbon fixation can 
continue for some time, while growth and protein synthesis 
slow down due to limited nitrogen availability. Many 
cyanobacteria respond by diverting excess fixed carbon 
into glycogen, which serves as a primary storage sink and 
helps stabilize cellular energy balance. When glycogen 
synthesis is disrupted, however, this buffering capacity 
is lost, and carbon is forced into alternative pathways, 
including extracellular excretion.
A landmark study demonstrated that glycogen synthesis 
and metabolite overflow jointly contribute to energy 
balancing in Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803. Glycogen-
deficient strains excreted organic acids, particularly 
pyruvate and 2-oxoglutarate, under nitrogen starvation 
and, notably, even under nitrogen-replete conditions 
when exposed to high light, provided that the energy 
input exceeded a critical threshold (Gründel et al. 2012). 
Complementary work in Synechococcus elongatus PCC 
7942 further examined the factors controlling pyruvate 
excretion in glycogen storage mutants, highlighting 
that overflow depends strongly on the growth regime,

light intensity, and nutrient status, rather than nitrogen 
availability alone (Benson et al. 2016). More recent 
studies have continued to refine this picture, emphasizing 
the central role of glycogen as a metabolic buffer that 
suppresses overflow under balanced conditions. Together, 
these findings establish glycogen as a primary carbon sink 
that limits overflow. When glycogen capacity is reduced 
or eliminated, overflow metabolism becomes a prominent 
alternative route for excess fixed carbon.
Recently, Cano et al. (2018) suggested that additional 
carbon sinks can also participate in cellular energy 
management. Manipulation of glycogen and sucrose 
synthesis pathways has been shown to influence 
photosynthetic productivity, indicating that these pathways 
may function as ATP-consuming cycles that help balance 
photosynthetic energy input with metabolic demand 
(Carrieri et al. 2012, Cano et al. 2018). Reducing flux 
through glycogen or sucrose cycling can redirect energy 
toward growth, demonstrating that these storage pathways 
compete with biomass production for photosynthetically 
derived energy (Cantrell et al. 2023). Energy buffering 
in cyanobacteria is not limited to carbon metabolism. 
Polyphosphate metabolism has also been implicated 
in energy management. Polyphosphate synthesis and 
degradation can dissipate ATP, providing an additional 
regulatory layer for maintaining energy homeostasis. 
Deletion of the ppk gene, encoding polyphosphate kinase, 
has been shown to increase ATP contents and enhance 
photosynthetic productivity, suggesting that polyphosphate 
cycling normally acts as an energy-consuming buffer that 
stabilizes intracellular energy balance (Sebesta et al. 2024).

High light and energy-driven overflow: High light 
intensity can also trigger overflow metabolism in 
the absence of nutrient starvation. Under such conditions, 
photosynthetic electron flow and ATP generation increase, 
while downstream anabolic sinks may become saturated. 
In glycogen-deficient backgrounds, highlight alone is 
sufficient to induce organic acid excretion, supporting 
the concept of energy overflow driven by excess light 
input (Zhang et al. 2021). These observations suggest 
a threshold behavior, whereby cyanobacteria export 
metabolites once internal mechanisms for dissipating 
excess energy and reducing power are exceeded. Notably, 
the comprehensive work of the Ducat group demonstrated 
that engineering sucrose export creates an external 
carbon sink with multiple consequences, enhancing 
photosynthetic activities and metabolic output (Santos-
Merino et al. 2021).
Mixotrophy and external carbon supply: Mixotrophic 
growth, such as supplementation with glucose or other 
organic carbon sources, further alters carbon flux and 
redox balance. Under these conditions, carbon enters 
the metabolic network through both CO 2 fixation and 
the uptake of external substrates. Several studies report 
enhanced secretion or excretion phenotypes in mutants or 
under specific nutrient regimes during mixotrophic growth 
(Zhang et al. 2021). The unifying principle remains 
consistent: when total carbon influx exceeds the cell's 
capacity for assimilation, storage, or balanced growth, 
overflow pathways become energetically favorable.
Salt stress as a potential multifactor driver of overflow: 
Salt stress represents a particularly complex driver of 
energy management because it simultaneously increases 
maintenance energy demand and constrains biosynthetic 
capacity. Ion pumping and the synthesis of compatible 
solutes substantially increase ATP consumption, while 
osmotic and ionic stress can slow growth and impair 
protein synthesis. Under these conditions, photosynthetic 
activity may persist intermittently, creating mismatches 
between carbon fixation and utilization that favor 
the export of metabolites.
Salt also reshapes carbon allocation by competing with 
internal sinks. Carbon is redirected toward compatible 
solutes such as glucosylglycerol or sucrose, which can 
suppress organic acid overflow in some cases. However, 
if osmolyte synthesis is limited by regulatory bottlenecks 
or temporal delays, particularly during the early stages 
of salt shock, fixed carbon may transiently accumulate 
and spill into extracellular metabolites. Additionally, salt 
stress can intensify photoinhibition by inhibiting the repair 
of PSII and increasing reactive oxygen species, further 
destabilizing the redox balance and promoting the need 
for alternative dissipation routes. System-level analyses 
reinforce this view. Transcriptomic and metabolomic 
studies reveal that salt acclimation entails a dynamic, 
time-dependent reorganization of metabolic networks, 
particularly shortly after salt exposure, encompassing 
pathways for the synthesis of compatible solutes and 
broader central metabolism. Such reorganization can 
generate transient overshoots in metabolite pools, thereby 
contributing to extracellular release.
Importantly, overflow under salt stress should not be 
equated with a single metabolite signature. Depending on 
species and growth conditions, the exometabolome may 
include organic acids, sugars, osmolytes, amino acids, 
and other small metabolites. Salt-associated overflow is 
therefore best viewed as a spectrum of exudation behaviors 
rather than a uniform response.
Sodium stress cycling represents a powerful 
environmental strategy to enhance carbon flux toward 
fermentative products in cyanobacteria. Carrieri et al. 
(2010) demonstrated that growth under high salinity 
induces substantial accumulation of soluble carbohydrates 
(up to ~50% of dry mass), while subsequent exposure to 
hypoionic conditions accelerates carbohydrate catabolism 
and significantly increases fermentation product 
yields, including ethanol, acetate, and formate. This is

the possibility of salt stress as a potential multifactor driver 
of overflow in cyanobacteria.
Engineering perspective: overflow as a platform for 
bioproduction: From a biotechnological standpoint, 
overflow metabolism is increasingly framed as 
an opportunity rather than a liability (Fig. 3). If 
cyanobacteria naturally export carbon under imbalanced 
conditions, metabolic engineering can redirect this 
flux toward the production of desired products. Recent 
reviews on cyanobacterial organic acid production 
describe strategies to channel pyruvate and related branch 
points into lactate, acetate, acetoin, and other value-
added compounds (Angermayr et al. 2015). In principle, 
salt stress could serve as a controllable lever to induce 
overflow-like states; however, this approach must balance 
enhanced export against the negative impacts of salinity 
on growth and photosynthetic efficiency.
Methods to measure cyanobacterial overflow 
(under salt stress and beyond)
Salt acclimation in the model cyanobacterium 
Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803 was analyzed by integrating 
transcriptomic, proteomics, and metabolic data (Carrieri 
et al. 2010). A dynamic reorganization occurred during 
the first hours after salt shock. The massive accumulation 
of glucosylglycerol then had a measurable impact on 
the carbon and nitrogen metabolism. The data provide 
the basis for engineering cyanobacterial salt tolerance and 
to further understand its regulation (Klähn et al. 2021).
At a conceptual level, metabolite overflow is defined 
by the accumulation of extracellular metabolites over time. 
In practice, however, accurately quantifying overflow 
in cyanobacteria, particularly under salt stress, requires 
careful experimental design to disentangle biological 
secretions from physicochemical artifacts introduced by 
saline matrices. As shown in Fig. 4, robust workflows 
therefore integrate growth and physiological measurements 
with targeted and untargeted exometabolomics and, when 
possible, complementary analyses of metabolic flux, 
energy status, and regulatory state.
Experimental design essentials, especially under salt 
stress: Appropriate controls are critical for interpreting 
extracellular metabolite accumulation. Media-only 
controls incubated under identical light and temperature 
conditions are crucial for capturing non-biological 
changes, such as salt-dependent chemical instability or 
evaporation-driven concentration effects. Cell-free filtrate

Sampling procedures must minimize post-harvest 
artifacts. Cells should be rapidly separated from 
the supernatant by centrifugation or filtration, and

intracellular metabolomics are planned. In long-term 
salt experiments, evaporation should be monitored 
carefully, as even modest volume loss can alter effective 
salinity and metabolite concentrations. Normalization is 
equally important: extracellular metabolite accumulation 
is typically normalized to optical density (OD₇₅₀), 
chlorophyll content, dry mass, or cell counts. Because 
salt stress can alter cell morphology and aggregation, 
the choice of biomass proxy should be validated for each 
condition (Ladd et al. 2021). High salinity can interfere 
with analytical workflows, particularly liquid separation 
techniques, due to ionic strength and charge effects, 
representing a significant experimental challenge.
Targeted analytical approaches for extracellular 
overflow metabolites: Targeted analytical techniques 
remain the backbone of overflow studies. High-performance 
liquid chromatography (HPLC) with refractive index 
or UV detection is commonly used to quantify organic 
acids and sugars, including pyruvate, acetate, lactate, and 
succinate, as well as compatible solutes such as sucrose or 
glucosylglycerol, in some cases. HPLC is well-suited for 
routine time-course experiments and mutant screening due 
to its robustness and accessibility.
Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry 
(LC–MS/MS) offers higher sensitivity and selectivity and 
is particularly valuable when overflow metabolites are 
present at low concentrations or span diverse chemical 
classes. LC–MS/MS enables simultaneous detection 
of organic acids, amino acids, osmolytes, and selected 
phosphorylated intermediates. Gas chromatography–mass 
spectrometry (GC–MS) is especially effective for volatile 
or derivatized compounds and is useful when expanding 
analyses beyond canonical overflow metabolites to 
include alcohols and fermentative products. Nuclear 
magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, while less 
sensitive, provides unambiguous structural identification 
and absolute quantification in complex saline matrices and 
is often used to validate findings from mass spectrometry-
based methods.
Untargeted exometabolomics: Untargeted exometabolo-
mics is increasingly employed to map the extracellular 
metabolite landscape under diverse environmental 
conditions. This approach enables the discovery of 
condition-specific exudate signatures and can reveal 
unexpected metabolites associated with stress adaptation, 
redox balancing, or osmoprotection (Ladd et al. 2021). 
Under salt stress, untargeted methods are particularly 
powerful because overflow is not restricted to central 
carbon intermediates but may include amino acids, 
osmolytes, sugars, and other stress-related metabolites. 
High-throughput untargeted workflows also facilitate 
systematic comparisons across nutrient regimes, light 
intensities, and salinity levels, providing a broader view of 
overflow phenotypes.

regulation: To move beyond descriptive measurements, 
mechanistic studies of overflow often incorporate assays

Conclusions 
Cyanobacteria advanced oxygenic photosynthesis and 
are valuable models to study molecular mechanisms of 
the photosynthetic process under different environmental 
conditions (Hudson 2021, Shen et al. 2021, Hou and 
Allakhverdiev 2023). During recent years, they have been 
increasingly considered as green cell factories for different 
biotechnological purposes (Hudson 2021). For example, 
salt stress constrains cyanobacterial growth in nature as well 
as in mass cultivation systems. The photosynthetic carbon 
fixation and cellular demand differ, leading eventually to 
the release of organic compounds to achieve equilibrium 
as an alternative energy management mechanism (Cano 
et al. 2018). Salt stress and metabolite overflow in 
cyanobacteria represent an underexplored intersection of 
physiology and metabolism. The significant contributions 
of Murata and Allakhverdiev et al. established that salt 
induces both osmotic and ionic stress on plant cells, which 
severely disrupts photosynthesis (Allakhverdiev et al. 
2002, Marin et al. 2003, Allakhverdiev and Murata 2008). 
While existing studies suggest that carbon imbalance 
under stress may promote overflow-like behaviors, direct 
evidence under salt-acclimated steady-state conditions 
remains limited. 
Future work integrating controlled physiological 
experiments with systems-level analyses will be essential 
to distinguish adaptive overflow from stress-induced 
leakage and to validate the hypotheses proposed in this 
review. 
Future progress will depend on combining controlled 
physiological experiments with systems-level analyses, 
including multi-omics integration, stable-isotope flux 
measurements, and genome-scale metabolic modeling. 
Such approaches will be essential for disentangling cause 
and consequence in salt-associated overflow and for 
identifying regulatory nodes that can be targeted through 
genetic or process engineering. Future studies should also 
consider hypoosmotic transitions, during which rapid 
solute release via mechanosensitive channels provides 
a well-established mechanism for metabolite export. 
By framing metabolite overflow as an emergent property 
of carbon imbalance under stress, this review proposed 
a conceptual foundation for interpreting cyanobacterial 
behavior in saline environments and for guiding 
the rational design of robust strains and cultivation 
strategies for sustainable bioprocessing. It is important 
to note that direct experimental evidence linking salt 
acclimation to metabolite overflow under steady-state 
conditions remains limited, and several of the connections 
discussed in this review should be considered hypotheses 
requiring further validation.
