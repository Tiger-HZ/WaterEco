Abstract
Aerosol pollution is a chronic air pollution problem that occurs every year in 
Thailand. It is mostly caused by human activities and climate conditions, and has 
severe negative impacts on both human health and the economy. This study 
investigates the relationship between aerosol pollution and various economic and 
environmental factors using the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) framework. 
Additionally, it forecasts aerosol pollution through 2050 by applying four machine 
learning approaches and comparing their accuracy and predictive performance. 
The results indicate that the Random Forest (RF) model provided the most accurate 
estimation across the train, validation, and test periods. The study found an inverted 
U-shape relationship between aerosol pollution and economic growth, consistent 
with an EKC pattern. This suggests that as Thailand’s economy expands, aerosol 
pollution is projected to decline at an average annual rate of − 0.0569% to − 0.0636% 
from 2000 to 2050, except for the Southern region (R4), which shows an increasing 
rate. Furthermore, environmental features, specifically regional characteristics (R), 
Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), rainfall, and wind speed, strongly 
influence aerosol pollution levels. Among economic features, population density 
was found to positively influence aerosol pollution. The study’s findings indicate that 
population density and GPP per capita remain primary drivers of aerosol pollution 
in high-activity regions. Consequently, although not directly analyzed in our models, 
we propose introducing green finance instruments as a strategic mechanism to 
decouple economic activity from environmental degradation.

Thailand’s insufficient attention to environmental issues has led to worsening annual 
air pollution, resulting in high economic costs. In 2019, environmental management 
agencies received just 0.4% of the national budget, or 0.05% of GDP , well below alloca -

rising from 210,603 billion Baht to 871,300 billion Baht over that period. Attavanich, [ 6]

diminished quality of life and restricted outdoor activities; decreased real estate values in

1.1 Using machine learning to predict aerosol pollution in conjunction with the EKC 
theory
Determining the relationship between aerosol pollution and economic growth remains a 
significant challenge because governments can use these findings to inform decisions on 
environmental policies and related matters in advance. Moreover, machine learning is a 
powerful tool for finding patterns in data and predicting trends with larger, more com -
plex datasets. These tools can be used to build real-time forecasting models that require 
no human input. As we mentioned before, this study uses the Environmental Kuznets 
Curve (EKC) analysis framework to find the relationship between aerosol pollution and 
economic growth. This will reveal the direction of the relationship, specifically whether 
the aerosol pollution problem follows an inverted U or a U-shaped curve as the economy 
grows. The analysis is conducted using four machine learning algorithms: Linear Regres-
sion (LM), Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS), Random Forest (RF), 
and Support Vector Regression (SVR). This is because machine learning can identify 
non-linear relationships, which can be applied alongside EKC theory as an alternative 
to econometric models. Furthermore, machine learning is highly effective at predicting 
future trends in aerosol pollution [ 30]. Moreover, this study employs popular interpre -
table machine learning methods, using Shapley Values and Partial Dependence Plots to 
interpret the model and explore the relationship [22].
The objectives of this study are: (1) to apply four currently popular machine learning 
algorithms within the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) analysis framework to find 
the relationship between aerosol pollution and economic growth, as well as other fea -
tures affecting changes in aerosol pollution in each region of Thailand; (2) to test the 
performance of these four machine learning algorithms in forecasting aerosol pollu -
tion and identifying future trends based on the specified model; and (3) to compare the 
performance of Linear Regression (LM) against other popular machine learning algo -
rithms used in this study. The research contributions of this study include using new 
machine learning algorithms to forecast aerosol pollution in Thailand and utilizing 
provincial-level satellite data (Remote sensing) collected via the Google Earth Engine 
platform, as well as secondary data from government agencies. This alternative data -
set is derived from satellite image processing rather than official Thai government data 
(e.g., aerosol pollution, wind speed, temperature, and precipitation). It also builds new 
knowledge regarding data analysis using machine learning algorithms in conjunction 
with Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) analysis as an alternative to using economet -
ric models, such as in the studies of Maneejuk et al., [ 34], Phadkantha and Yamaka [44], 
Phrakhruopatnontakitti et al. [46], Shahzad and Aruga [50], Srisaringkarn and Aruga [51, 
52]. The model developed in this study can then be applied to larger and more complex 
datasets for future environmental policy planning in Thailand. Additionally, the feature 
contributions identified here provide a guide for selecting variables in future advanced 
machine learning and econometric models. Because our machine learning approach 
indicates feature importance and forecasting rather than exact causal effects and magni -
tudes, this foundational knowledge is essential for building robust models that can yield 
deeper economic insights into the aerosol pollution issue. The novelty of this study lies

2 Literature reviews
The Kuznets Curve was originally proposed by Kuznets [ 31] to examine the relation -
ship between economic growth and income inequality. Decades later, Grossman and 
Krueger [20] extended this concept to develop the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC). 
This theory postulates a relationship between various indicators of environmental 
degradation and per capita income. In the early stages of economic growth, pollution 
emissions increase and environmental quality declines. However, beyond a specific per 
capita income threshold (which varies by environmental indicator), the trend reverses: 
at higher income levels, further economic growth leads to environmental improve -
ment. Consequently, the theory suggests that environmental impacts or per capita emis-
sions follow an inverted U-shaped relationship with per capita income [ 20, 42, 53, 54]. 
Although empirical evidence supports the EKC hypothesis for certain countries, it is 
not universally applicable to all forms of environmental degradation, income levels, or 
national contexts.
The EKC relationship in this study is categorized into five patterns: (1) an increas -
ing monotonic relationship; (2) a decreasing monotonic relationship; (3) an inverted 
U-shaped pattern; (4) a U-shaped pattern, which is in direct contrast to the EKC; and (5) 
a flat pattern, indicating that emissions are not influenced by income levels.
2.1 Air pollution policies
According to UNEP [ 59], there is no global legal framework for Ambient Air Quality 
Standards (AAQS). Similarly, a clear legal commitment to specific AAQS compatible 
with human life, health, and the natural environment does not yet exist in public inter -
national law. However, some key regional legal instruments on air quality—particularly 
in the EU—require individual signatory countries to develop relatively robust legal sys -
tems for air quality control. Currently, 66% of countries are subject to constitutional legal 
requirements that may, over time, be interpreted as requiring legally mandated accept -
able air quality. Examples of AAQS enforcement mechanisms are shown in Table 2.
 
Moreover, for the technological innovation to improve environmental and air qual -
ity Hassan et al. [ 23] indicates that most countries must invest more in R&D to drive 
technical innovation and support renewable energy, thus enabling the energy transition.

Previous research has highlighted how research spending and renewable deployment 
affect environmental degradation [ 24, 36]. However, these studies have not addressed 
the impact of energy transition and research spending on technological innovation. 
Some academics contend that more funding for R&D improves environmental quality 
and reduces emissions. For example, Fernández Fernández et al. [ 16], and Zafar et al.

At present, Thailand has to update air pollution control laws and environmental 
innovation technologies to align with the international regulation standard [ 47]. Con -
sequently, Thailand urgently needs to enact more updated air pollution control act to 
establish better air quality standards in the future.

The rationale for selecting these machine learning methods is their robust ability to 
model complex, non-linear relationships. Algorithms such as Support Vector Regres -
sion (SVR) employ kernel functions to evaluate data similarity, enabling them to identify 
underlying patterns and generate accurate non-linear predictions. Additionally, Multi -
variate Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS) and Random Forest (RF) operate as Adap -
tive Basis Function Models (ABM). Because they are non-linear in their parameters, 
these models frequently yield significantly better predictive performance than linear 
approaches. Furthermore, ABM bypasses a common limitation of kernel-based meth -
ods: the inherent difficulty of specifying an optimal kernel function for complex datas -
ets. Ultimately, these techniques build on foundational linear regression concepts while 
automatically capturing non-linear dynamics. This eliminates the need to manually engi-
neer polynomial features, resulting in a more highly accurate analytical framework [38].
2.3 Stochastic impacts by regression on population, affluence, and technology model 
(STIRPAT)
In this study, we utilize the STIRPAT model developed by York et al., [ 67] as our core 
mathematical framework. In conjunction with the EKC theory, we use this model to 
examine the relationship between aerosol pollution, economic growth, and other signifi-
cant variables. The STIRPAT equation is expressed as follows:

𝑖𝑖 𝐴𝐴𝑐𝑐
𝑖𝑖 𝑇𝑇𝑑𝑑
𝑖𝑖 𝑒𝑒𝑖𝑖 (1)
In this equation, the constant a act as the scaling factor. The variables b, c, and d rep -
resent the exponents of P , A, and T, respectively, which must be empirically estimated, 
while e denotes the error term. Specifically, P represents population, A denotes affluence 
(captured by GPPpc and GPPpc2), and T accounts for all factors influencing environmen-
tal impact beyond population and affluence (e.g., NDVI, rainfall, wind speed, daytime 
land surface temperature, and region-specific features). These variables are the changes 
in consumption and production of goods and services, and their associated environmen-
tal impacts, resulting from changes in economic variables driven by improvements in 
service efficiency [ 61, 67]. Ultimately, the STIRPAT model and EKC framework guide 
the empirical analysis and conclusions presented throughout this study.

3.2 Data
In this study, we use data from 76 provinces in Thailand from 2000 to 2023, divided 
by region: BANGKOK AND VICINITIES, CENTRAL, WESTERN, EASTERN (R1), 
NORTHEASTERN (R2), NORTHERN (R3), and SOUTHERN (R4). The data were col -
lected from both the GEE platform and Thai government agencies. Using remote sensing 
data from the GEE platform helps overcome the limitations of environmental data avail -
ability, which is otherwise restricted and does not cover all areas of Thailand. However, 
this study does not include Buengkan province due to limited data from 2000 to 2011.

3.3.5 Shapley values (SHAP)
The concept of Shapley Values is grounded in cooperative game theory. It posits that 
the feature values of an individual observation collaborate to shift the model’s prediction 
away from its expected base value. The method decomposes this total shift, distribut -
ing it among features based on their marginal contributions across all possible subsets, 
ensuring a ‘fair’ allocation. To do so, Shapley Values assess every combination of predic -
tors to determine each predictor’s impact. Focusing on feature 𝑥𝑥𝑗𝑗 , the approach will test 
the accuracy of every combination of features not including 𝑥𝑥𝑗𝑗 and then test how adding 
𝑥𝑥𝑗𝑗 to each combination improves the accuracy [11, 22]. The additivity property of Shap-
ley Values is expressed in the following equation:
 𝐷𝐷𝑆𝑆𝑆𝑆𝑆𝑆𝑆𝑆 𝐷𝑥𝑥𝐷 = 𝐸𝐸[ 𝐷𝐷𝐷 𝐷𝑥𝑥𝐷] + ∑ 𝑀𝑀
𝑗𝑗𝑚1 ∅ 𝑗𝑗 𝐷𝐷𝐷𝐷 𝑥𝑥𝐷 (14)
While 𝐸𝐸[𝐷𝐷𝐷 𝐷𝑥𝑥𝐷] is baseline expected value.
In the context of forecasting aerosol pollution, Shapley Values quantify the marginal 
contribution of predictors such as GDP per capita and regional characteristics to the 
model’s output. Specifically, a high positive Shapley Value for GDP per capita indicates 
that the observed economic features drive the model to predict higher aerosol pollution 
relative to the baseline average. This interpretation allows us to infer the extent to which 
specific economic features influence environmental outcomes.
3.3.6 Partial dependence plots (PDPs)
Partial dependence plots (PDPs) illustrate the relationship between a target response and 
a specific set of input features by marginalizing over the values of all other input features 
(known as ‘complement’ features). Intuitively, partial dependence can be interpreted as 
the expected target response as a function of these input features. Given the limits of 
human perception, the feature set must be kept small (typically one or two dimensions);

use of low-emission vehicles, implementing carbon credit mechanisms, and adopting alter-
native energy sources (KASIKORN RESEARCH CENTER, [26]). Therefore, we used 2050 as 
the forecast limit. However, since forecasting accuracy naturally decreases over such a long 
period, new data analysis should be performed continuously to ensure the forecast remains 
as accurate as possible.

5 Conclusions and policy implications
This study investigated the relationship between aerosol pollution on economic and environ-
mental features using the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) framework. It also forecasted 
aerosol pollution through 2050 to identify future trends, applying four machine learning 
approaches and comparing their predictive accuracy and performance.
The study found that the RF model provided the most accurate estimation throughout 
the train, validation, and test periods compared to other machine learning algorithms. As 
detailed in Table 11 of the Appendix, the Random Forest (RF) grid search was configured 
with a minimum terminal node (leaf) size of 1, a sample fraction of 0.8, and bootstrap sam-
pling disabled (False). Consequently, the algorithm learned from the data until each leaf con-
tained only a single observation, permitting the trees to grow to their maximum depth. The 
0.8 sample fraction split the training dataset into 80% for each individual tree, reserving the 
remaining 20% for error evaluation. Furthermore, disabling bootstrap sampling ensured that 
the algorithm did not draw duplicate data points during tree construction. Finally, the num-
ber of predictors evaluated at each split was restricted to 2, meaning the algorithm consid-
ered only two randomly chosen features to determine the optimal branching path. Because 
of these characteristics, RF can seamlessly accommodate mixed discrete and continuous

This study identifies an inverted U-shaped relationship consistent with the Environmental 
Kuznets Curve (EKC) align with [1]. By employing the Random Forest (RF) machine learn-
ing algorithm to detect these patterns, this study offers a supportive conclusion aligned with 
econometric models (e.g., Linear Regression (LM). This means that as Thailand’s economy 
grows, aerosol pollution levels will gradually decrease. According to Fig. 6c, the EKC turning 
point is at 11.492 or international $9335 (adjusted by Thailand PPP at 2024) (also confirm 
by regression analyses in Table 13 in the Appendix). Certainly, economic factors are clearly 
causes of aerosol pollution [60], including urbanization, population density, and economic 
growth [71]. However, because aerosol pollution is highly sensitive to environmental con-
ditions such as wind speed, rainfall, and topography, which can reduce aerosol or blow it 
elsewhere, some areas in Thailand experience more severe aerosol pollution than others. 
As illustrated in Fig. 5, region-specific features have the most impact on aerosol pollution 
levels. This implies that aerosol pollution is a transboundary problem that requires urgent 
regional collaboration. The central government should collaborate with local governments in 
each region to address aerosol pollution through measures such as preventing the improper 
burning of agricultural materials and controlling wildfires during the dry season. Although 
aerosol pollution is declining, the average annual rate of decline is marginal (less than 1%). 
Consequently, it can be concluded that aerosol pollution will remain a persistent issue for 
Thailand without appropriate mitigation strategies, with the potential to intensify in the 
future.
5.1 Policy implications
Recognizing RF as a robust methodological instrument for assessing the influence of aerosol 
pollution on economic growth within the EKC framework and for constructing forecasting 
models is advisable. The government may draw on the empirical insights generated by this 
study to develop economic–aerosol pollution forecasting systems or to employ the model’s 
feature contribution metrics to evaluate prospective causal impacts on economic perfor-
mance. In this analytical context, the RF model demonstrates superior efficiency and meth-
odological suitability.

Srisaringkarn and Aruga Discover Atmosphere (2026) 4:24 
Furthermore, the analyses showed that aerosol pollution exhibits a slightly downward 
trend. However, the government should plan long-term prevention measures to minimize 
the severity of future aerosol pollution and ensure sustainability. Examples include control-
ling industrial areas that cause environmental problems to minimize their impact on the 
public, such as zoning industrial areas according to wind direction and topography so that 
aerosol pollution is carried away from the public. This is because regional characteristics 
(R) and environmental condition features—specifically NDVI, wind speed, precipitation, 
and Land Surface Temperature at Daytime—have the greatest impact on aerosol pollution. 
Therefore, the government must keep these factors in mind when establishing industrial 
zones. To protect surrounding communities, these zones should be established in spa -
cious areas with adequate air circulation and sound environmental conditions. Additional 
approaches to address these region-specific factors include preventing the inappropriate 
burning of agricultural waste and improving wildfire management, as these activities con-
tribute significantly to transboundary pollution in neighboring regions. The study’s findings 
indicate that population density and GPP per capita remain primary drivers of aerosol pollu-
tion in high-activity regions. Consequently, although not directly analyzed in our models, we 
propose introducing green finance instruments as a strategic mechanism to decouple eco-
nomic activity from environmental degradation [5]. Such instruments could provide the nec-
essary capital for manufacturing industries and business firms to adopt cleaner technologies, 
thereby addressing the persistent pollution issues identified in the long-run forecast through 
2050. Moreover, support should be given to the enactment of a Climate Change Act and a 
Clean Air Act in Thailand. This serves as an integrated solution to air pollution—including 
transboundary pollution—and promotes stricter environmental legislation in the country.
5.2 Limitations of the study
This study has four limitations. Firstly, it did not include other important factors such as 
industrial density, environmental pollution control laws in Thailand, technological advance-
ment, transboundary transport, wildfire management, agricultural burning, etc. This may 
limit the scope of policy recommendations and reduce the accuracy of aerosol pollution 
forecasting. As indicated in previous studies [8], these variables directly affect variations in 
aerosol pollution across regions. However, since this study utilizes provincial-level data in 
Thailand, the aforementioned variables could not be incorporated into the analysis. Sec -
ondly, this study used provincial-level data of Thailand, which may lead to inaccuracies in 
future aerosol pollution forecasts. Therefore, using more granular data, such as at the sub-
district level, would make the forecast more accurate. Thirdly, if the scope of the study could 
be expanded, for example, by comparing with neighboring countries, it would lead to better 
policy recommendations and would also increase the number of observations used to train 
the machine learning algorithm. Fourthly, this study uses annual-average aerosol pollution 
data, which are influenced by topography and environmental conditions (such as wind speed, 
rainfall, etc.) throughout the year. Therefore, if we use seasonal data of aerosol pollution, we 
will see the apparent pattern of aerosol pollution in Thailand because we can control the 
environmental conditions that are not consistent for the whole year, but focus only on 
the timing of the severe aerosol pollution period [4, 29]. Moreover, while machine learn-
ing approaches cannot report statistically significant results for each feature used in the 
model, this limitation underscores the importance of using machine learning alongside

Srisaringkarn and Aruga Discover Atmosphere (2026) 4:24 
an econometric model (as shown in Table 13 in the Appendix) or another machine 
learning approach, such as Causal Forests.
5.3 Future studies
For the next study, researchers should focus specifically on the district or sub-district 
level, given differences in topography and environmental factors. This will make the 
study results more accurate. Furthermore, the use of spatial econometrics remains 
important, as it enables the quantification of the spatial effects of aerosol pollution. 
Based on our analysis, we recommend incorporating our findings into advanced machine 
learning (e.g., Causal Forests) and econometric models of the Thai economy and aerosol 
pollution, as they are significant drivers of aerosol pollution levels in Thailand.
