Abstract
Target-yield fertilizer prescriptions are usually validated through crop yield, but their 
link with rhizosphere biological functioning remains less understood in Alfisol based 
pulse systems. This short-term field study evaluated whether FYM adjusted STCR-IPNS 
could improve greengram productivity, nutrient uptake, enzyme activity, microbial 
response, and PCA derived soil quality index (SQI). Greengram cv. CO 8 was grown 
in a 12 treatment randomized block design comparing STCR-NPK and FYM adjusted 
STCR-IPNS at 0.8, 1.0, and 1.2 t ha − 1 yield targets with vermicompost, FYM, RDF, 
RDF + FYM, farmer practice, and control treatments. STCR-IPNS at the 1.2 t ha − 1 target 
produced the highest crop response, with plant height, LAI, and SPAD values of 
56.63 cm, 3.98, and 50.76, respectively. The treatment achieved 105.8% of the target 
yield and recorded higher response ratio, benefit: cost ratio, crude protein content, 
and crude protein yield than the other nutrient management options. Harvest 
stage N, P and K uptake peaked at 56.01, 16.96 and 41.43 kg ha − 1, respectively. 
FYM adjusted STCR-IPNS also improved microbial biomass, selected nutrient cycling 
enzyme activities, and PCA derived SQI. These results indicate that FYM adjusted 
STCR-IPNS can support greengram productivity while strengthening biological soil 
quality indicators under the tested Alfisol condition.
Keywords STCR-IPNS, Target yield prescription, FYM nutrient credit, Greengram 
rhizosphere, Alfisol, Enzyme mediated nutrient cycling, Microbial biomass, PCA derived

 1 Introduction
High yield fertilizer strategies have improved crop output, but their long-term value now 
depends on whether they can sustain enzyme-mediated nutrient cycling, microbial reg -
ulation, and soil-health function [ 22, 58]. The problem is no longer fertilizer response 
alone, but whether yield focused nutrient use can avoid disrupting nutrient balance, 
rhizosphere biotic processes, and the long-term trade-off between productivity and 
biological resilience [ 9,25,51]. Thus, fertilizer prescriptions must be judged not only by

yield attainment, but also by their capacity to maintain rhizosphere microbial structure 
and enzyme-regulated soil function [ 25, 62]. Greengram (Vigna radiata L.) is a relevant 
test crop for this question because it combines protein-rich grain production, biologi -
cal N inputs, soil organic carbon restoration, and nutrient retention in intensive systems 
[6, 38]. Integrated nutrient management (INM) partly addresses this challenge by cou -
pling mineral fertilizers with organic inputs that improve biochemical activity, microbial 
dynamics, and crop productivity [ 5, 20]. The unresolved step is whether soil-test based 
nutrient prescriptions can synchronize crop demand with microbial and enzyme-medi -
ated nutrient turnover. The soil test crop response (STCR) approach provides such pre -
cision by translating soil fertility status into target-yield fertilizer requirements instead of 
uniform fertilizer application [36, 42].
For greengram, this precision is agronomically and ecologically important. Fertilizer 
management must support yield, grain quality, N acquisition, and biologically active soil 
function at the same time [29, 38]. Recent greengram studies show that STCR integrated 
fertilization can improve yield, nutrient acquisition, nodulation, and PCA derived soil 
quality [31, 36, 44]. However, the enzyme microbial pathway behind this response is still 
not fully resolved. This gap is important because organic-mineral nutrition can sustain 
plant nutrient supply through microbially mediated nutrient release and enzyme driven 
N transformation [ 14]. Therefore, FYM derived nutrient credits in STCR-IPNS should 
be tested not only as fertilizer substitutions, but also as carbon linked inputs that influ -
ence microbial biomass, respiration, enzyme activity, and soil quality index formation 
[3, 27, 46, 57]. The novelty of this study lies in extending STCR-IPNS validation beyond 
conventional yield and nutrient uptake assessment. Previous studies on greengram have 
mainly focused on fertilizer prescription, crop yield, nutrient use efficiency, and basic 
soil fertility response. However, limited information is available on how FYM-adjusted 
STCR-IPNS regulates enzyme mediated nutrient cycling, microbial biomass, microbial 
population dynamics, respiration behaviour, and PCA derived biological soil quality in 
Alfisol based pulse systems. Therefore, this study provides an integrated crop-rhizo -
sphere evaluation by linking target yield fertilizer prescription with productivity, NPK 
acquisition, soil enzyme activity, microbial functional response, and soil quality index 
under greengram cultivation. We hypothesized that FYM-adjusted STCR-IPNS would 
improve greengram productivity by synchronizing target-yield nutrient supply with NPK 
uptake, rhizosphere enzyme activity, microbial functional response, and PCA-derived 
soil quality. This approach makes the study distinct from earlier STCR based greengram 
studies because it evaluates whether FYM nutrient credits can improve not only target 
yield achievement, but also the biological functioning of the rhizosphere. Thus, the work 
contributes a soil-health oriented validation framework for STCR-IPNS in greengram 
grown on Alfisol. The objectives were to (i) quantify green gram productivity, yield par -
titioning, grain quality, and NPK acquisition under STCR-NPK, FYM adjusted STCR-
IPNS, organic, blanket fertilizer, and farmer-practice benchmarks; (ii) determine the 
corresponding rhizosphere response in soil fertility, enzyme activity, microbial biomass, 
microbial population, respiration, and metabolic quotient; and (iii) integrate biological 
and biochemical indicators through PCA derived SQI to identify the nutrient-manage -
ment pathway most strongly linked with soil health and crop performance.

2 Materials and methods
2.1 Site-soil context and validation framework
The STCR-IPNS validation platform (Fig.  1) was established for green gram ( Vigna 
radiata L.) cv. CO 8 at Poolampatty, Vagarai block, Dindigul district, Tamil Nadu, 
India (10.557° N, 77.569° E; Fig.  2). The experiment was conducted during one green -
gram growing season and is therefore presented as a short-term field validation of FYM 
adjusted STCR-IPNS under Alfisol conditions. During crop growth, the site had a warm 
semi-arid monsoonal environment, with mean maximum and minimum temperatures 
of 34.15 and 21.5  °C, relative humidity of 71.0–95.0%, and 26.6  mm rainfall over four 
rainy days during the cropping period (Table  1). These weather conditions are reported 
to clarify the seasonal environment under which treatment responses were obtained and 
to support cautious interpretation of field-scale agronomic recommendations.

2.2 Treatment architecture and fertilizer prescription
The experiment included 12 treatments arranged in a RBD with three replications. The 
treatments included absolute control, organic only inputs, STCR-NPK, FYM adjusted 
STCR-IPNS, recommended dose of fertilizer, recommended dose of fertilizer + FYM, 
and farmers practice. Paired STCR-NPK and STCR-IPNS treatments were tested for 0.8, 
1.0, and 1.2 t ha − 1 yield targets. Fertilizer N, P₂O₅, and K₂O requirements were calcu -
lated using greengram-specific STCR prescription equations. In STCR-IPNS treatments, 
FYM derived N, P₂O₅, and K₂O credits were deducted from the corresponding inorganic 
fertilizer requirements. Organic-only, RDF, RDF + FYM, farmer’s practice (FP), and con -
trol treatments served as non-prescription benchmarks. The treatment structure was 
designed to compare fertilizer only target yield prescription with FYM-adjusted target 
yield prescription and commonly used non-prescription nutrient-management prac -
tices, thereby aligning the study with agricultural production, soil fertility management 
and soil-quality evaluation.
The target yield term (T) in the STCR prescription equations was entered in q ha − 1, 
whereas the treatment labels are expressed in t ha − 1. Thus, the 0.8, 1.0, and 1.2 t ha − 1 
targets correspond to 8, 10, and 12 q ha − 1 respectively, for fertilizer-dose calculation. 
The calculated fertilizer doses were then adjusted using the STCR validation limits. A 
maintenance dose of 50% of the blanket recommendation was applied where the cal -
culated value was lower than the maintenance level, and a maximum dose of 150% of 
the blanket recommendation was applied where the calculated value exceeded the upper 
limit. As the blanket recommendation for greengram was 25:50:25 kg N: P 2O5:K2O 
ha− 1, the maintenance dose was 13:25:13 kg ha − 1 and the maximum dose was 38:75:38 
kg ha− 1 after rounding. In STCR-IPNS treatments, FYM derived N, P , and K credits were 
deducted from the calculated inorganic fertilizer requirement before applying these 
maintenance and maximum dose limits. Therefore, the dose difference between matched 
STCR-NPK and STCR-IPNS treatments is not constant across yield targets, because 
some calculated values were adjusted to the maintenance or maximum dose limits. The 
STCR prescription equations used in this study were derived for greengram cv. CO 8 on 
Palaviduthi Alfisol using the inductive cum targeted yield approach of Ramamoorthy et 
al., [35] and the AICRP-STCR methodology. The equations were based on soil-test val -
ues, nutrient requirement, soil nutrient contribution, fertilizer nutrient contribution and 
FYM nutrient contribution.

2.4 Crop productivity, nutrient acquisition, and grain-quality measurements
Growth, physiological, and nodulation responses were assessed using plant height, leaf 
number, leaf area index, SPAD value, chlorophyll traits, photosynthetic pigments, and 
nodule number. Yield formation was quantified using pods plant − 1 , seeds pod − 1 , test 
weight, grain yield, haulm yield, total biomass, and harvest index. Nutrient acquisition 
was estimated as N, P , and K uptake in grain, haulm, and total biomass. Grain quality 
was characterized by crude protein, crude fibre, total sugars, and true protein. Crude 
protein content was calculated from grain nitrogen content using a conversion factor of 
6.25 [28]; crude fibre was determined by the acid and alkali digestion method [ 21], total 
sugars were determined by the anthrone colorimetric method [59], and true protein was 
determined by the Folin phenol method [ 26]. Prescription performance was evaluated

2.5 Rhizosphere fertility, enzyme activity, and microbial functional indicators
Rhizosphere fertility and carbon pools were assessed at vegetative, flowering, and 
harvest stages using available N, P , K, SOC, labile carbon, and water-soluble carbon. 
Enzyme-mediated nutrient cycling was characterized through urease, alkaline phos -
phatase, β-glucosidase, nitrate reductase, and dehydrogenase, representing N, P , C, and 
redox-linked biochemical processes. Urease activity was determined following Tabatabai 
and Bremner [48], alkaline phosphatase following Tabatabai and Bremner [47], dehydro-
genase following Casida et al., [ 10], β-glucosidase following Eivazi and Tabatabai [ 12], 
and Nitrate reductase activity in rhizosphere soil was determined from NO₂ ⁻-N pro -
duced during anaerobic incubation [1]. Soil was incubated under waterlogged conditions 
with KNO₃ as substrate and 2,4-dinitrophenol to inhibit further reduction of nitrite at 
25 °C for 24 h, after which the accumulated NO₂⁻-N was determined colorimetrically.
Microbial functional response was evaluated using microbial biomass carbon, micro -
bial biomass nitrogen, potentially mineralizable nitrogen, bacteria, fungi, actinomycetes, 
basal respiration, and metabolic quotient. Microbial biomass carbon was estimated by 
the chloroform fumigation-extraction method of Vance et al., [ 50], and microbial bio -
mass nitrogen following Brookes et al., [ 7]. Potentially mineralizable nitrogen was esti -
mated by short-term anaerobic incubation. Five grams of < 2 mm soil were placed in 
incubation tubes, flooded with distilled water to exclude entrapped air, sealed, and incu -
bated at 40 °C for 7 days. After incubation, 15 mL of 4 M KCl and 0.2–0.3 g MgO were 
added, and NH₄-N released during incubation was determined by steam distillation [55]. 
Bacterial, fungal, and actinomycete populations were enumerated by the serial-dilution 
plate-count method [ 34]. Soil suspensions were serially diluted using sterile distilled-
water blanks. For bacteria, 1 mL of the 10 ⁻⁶ dilution was plated on nutrient agar and 
incubated at 30 °C for 24–48 h. Actinomycetes were enumerated on Ken Knight agar 
and incubated at 30 °C for 7 days. Colony counts were expressed as colony-forming units 
per gram of soil and basal soil respiration was determined from CO₂ evolution [ 4]. One 
hundred grams of soil on an oven-dry-equivalent basis were adjusted to field-moisture 
conditions and incubated in airtight 1-L flasks containing 8 mL of 0.5 N NaOH as a CO₂ 
trap at 28 °C for approximately 20 h. After incubation, 5 mL of 3 N BaCl₂ and phenol -
phthalein indicator were added to the NaOH trap, and the unreacted alkali was titrated 
with 0.5 N H₂SO₄. Respiration was expressed as CO₂ evolved per unit soil and incuba -
tion time. Metabolic quotient was calculated as basal respiration per unit microbial bio -
mass carbon. These indicators linked STCR-IPNS nutrient prescription with nutrient 
availability, enzyme activity, microbial population dynamics, respiration balance, and 
soil-health assessment, with the treatment-wise dataset provided in Table SM2.

2.7 Statistical analysis
Treatment effects were analyzed by analysis of variance appropriate for a randomized 
block design using the AGRES statistical program (Pascal Intel Software Solutions). Sta -
tistical significance was assessed at P ≤ 0.05, and treatment means were compared using 
the critical difference (CD) at the 5% probability level, with standard error of difference 
(SEd) reported where applicable. Count and percentage-based variables were analyzed 
on their original measurement scales. PCA was used for treatment discrimination and 
SQI construction, and graphical outputs visualized treatment responses, PCA-derived 
separation, and integrated indices. PCA, indicator standardization, loading selection, 
and SQI calculation were performed using R software and Microsoft Excel.

3.4 Nutritional quality modulation under STCR-IPNS
Crude protein and true protein increased most under FYM-adjusted STCR-IPNS, giving 
the strongest grain-quality response against STCR-NPK and benchmark nutrient strate -
gies (Fig. SM5a; Table SM1e). Fibre and sugar fractions varied with treatment, showing

in greengram grain (Fig. SM5a, b). At equivalent target-yield levels, STCR-IPNS main -

tein-sugar quality response (Fig. SM5b, c).
3.5 Enzyme-microbial activation of rhizosphere fertility
Rhizosphere fertility showed a treatment and stage dependent response, with FYM 
adjusted STCR-IPNS showing clearer improvement in available N, P , and K than fertil -
izer only STCR-NPK and non-prescription benchmarks (Table SM2a). However, SOC, 
labile carbon, and water-soluble carbon showed only non-significant treatment related 
trends across crop stages. Enzyme responses generally followed the treatment pattern, 
with clearer responses in urease, nitrate reductase, alkaline phosphatase, and dehydro -
genase under STCR-IPNS (Table SM2b). β-glucosidase showed treatment related varia -
tion, but the difference between T9 and the next-best treatment was not consistently 
significant across stages. Therefore, β-glucosidase was not used as independent evidence 
of significantly enhanced carbon turnover. Microbial biomass carbon, microbial biomass 
nitrogen, and potentially mineralizable nitrogen increased most strongly under STCR-
IPNS, alongside higher bacterial, fungal, and actinomycete populations (Table SM2c; Fig. 
SM3a, b). Basal respiration increased along with microbial biomass, indicating greater 
microbial activity under integrated nutrient management (Table SM2c; Fig. SM3c, d). 
However, metabolic quotient did not show a consistent significant decline across stages. 
Therefore, it was interpreted as a stage dependent respiration indicator rather than as 
confirmed evidence of improved microbial carbon use efficiency under STCR-IPNS.

ordination distinguished STCR-IPNS from STCR-NPK, organic-only, RDF, RDF + FYM,

adjusted prescription (Fig.  6a, d). Indicator contribution and SQI ranking identified 
enzyme activity, microbial biomass, respiration balance, and nutrient availability as 
dominant SQI drivers; overall, STCR-IPNS was the pathway most closely associated 
with improved soil health and crop performance (Fig. 6b-d; Table SM2d-e).

4 Discussion
FYM adjusted STCR-IPNS improved greengram performance by linking target-yield 
nutrient prescription with canopy function, nodulation, biomass allocation, and nutrient 
acquisition [54]. The main contribution of this study is that it connects the agronomic 
performance of STCR-IPNS with enzyme and microbial indicators of soil biological 
quality. This is important because target-yield fertilizer prescriptions are usually vali -
dated through yield response alone, whereas the present study also evaluates whether 
the prescription improves rhizosphere functional processes such as nutrient-cycling 
enzyme activity, microbial biomass formation, microbial population development, and 
respiration balance. The stronger canopy growth, SPAD response, photosynthetic pig -
ment status, and nodulation indicate that soil-test-based mineral nutrient supply, when 
combined with FYM-mediated biological activation, created a rhizosphere environment 
favorable for chlorophyll formation, root activity, N fixation, and reproductive develop -
ment [43]. It should also be noted that chlorophyll a at 45 DAS was higher under T6 
than T9. Therefore, the STCR-IPNS advantage was interpreted as an overall crop perfor-
mance and soil-biological response rather than as superiority for every individual physi -
ological variable. Similar gains in plant height, leaf area index, nodulation, and yield 
attributes under integrated nutrient management in greengram have been reported by 
Kumar et al. [ 19], while Rath et al. [ 37] linked nutrient-managed greengram productiv -
ity with improved root growth, nodulation, and nutrient uptake; manure substitution 
has also been associated with higher enzyme activity, bacterial diversity, and crop yield 
[61]. The yield advantage under FYM adjusted STCR-IPNS was mainly associated with 
higher grain yield, haulm yield, and total biomass rather than a clear improvement in 
harvest partitioning. Therefore, the higher productivity of T9 should be interpreted as a 
biomass driven response supported by better nutrient supply and crop growth, not as a

direct increase in harvest index [11]. The stepwise response from 0.8 to 1.2 t ha− 1 targets 
supports the agronomic relevance of target-yield fertilizer prescription. Higher percent 
achievement, response ratio, and benefit: cost ratio further indicate the economic advan-
tage of FYM adjusted STCR-IPNS under the tested field condition. Similar gains in.
productivity, resource-use efficiency, and nutrient cycling have been reported under 
integrated nutrient systems [ 15, 40], and FYM-fertilizer integration has improved soil 
fertility and biochemical activity [ 41]. Higher N, P , and K uptake further suggests that 
STCR-IPNS improved nutrient acquisition as a stoichiometric response, with FYM 
contributing to sustained nutrient release, root-zone activity, and recovery of applied 
nutrients. This agrees with reports that manure–fertilizer combinations improve root 
growth, nutrient uptake, and yield, while organic substitution and integrated fertilization 
strengthen SOC fractions, enzyme activity, NPK balance, and nutrient recovery [ 23, 53, 
60].
The grain quality response adds another layer to this mechanism. Higher crude pro -
tein and true protein, together with changes in fibre and sugar fractions, suggest that 
STCR-IPNS altered N assimilation and grain quality response in greengram. This is rel -
evant for pulses, where yield improvement should retain protein density and carbohy -
drate balance [37, 39, 49]. The improvement in crude protein yield was therefore linked 
not only with higher grain yield, but also with improved N uptake and better physiologi -
cal performance under integrated nutrient supply.
In the rhizosphere, FYM adjusted STCR-IPNS acted as a biological nutrient-regula -
tion pathway. SOC, labile carbon, and water-soluble carbon likely supplied substrates 
for microbial biomass, enzyme production, and mineralization. However, the car -
bon-pool response should be interpreted cautiously because SOC, labile carbon, and 
water-soluble carbon showed treatment related trends but did not consistently show 
statistically significant separation across all crop stages. Therefore, these results suggest 
short-term substrate support from FYM rather than a confirmed strong improvement 
in carbon pools. The parallel increases in urease, nitrate reductase, alkaline phospha -
tase, β-glucosidase, dehydrogenase, MBC, MBN, PMN, and microbial populations indi -
cate stronger N transformation, P mobilization, C turnover, oxidative metabolism, and 
microbial functional capacity. Among these indicators, β-glucosidase was interpreted 
carefully because the difference between the best-performing treatment and the next-
best treatment was not always statistically distinct. Thus, β-glucosidase is discussed as 
part of the overall enzyme response pattern rather than as an independent conclusive 
indicator of enhanced C turnover. Increased basal respiration with reduced metabolic 
quotient further indicates improved microbial carbon-use efficiency rather than ineffi -
cient respiratory loss. However, metabolic quotient was not treated as a uniformly sig -
nificant decline across all stages. Its response is therefore discussed as a stage dependent 
respiration indicator, not as conclusive evidence of consistently improved microbial car-
bon use efficiency. Similar organic mineral effects on rhizosphere nutrients, microbial 
biomass, microbial diversity, soil quality, enzyme activity, SOC fractions, and fertility 
have been reported under combined amendments, manure substitution, FYM–fertil -
izer integration, and partial organic substitution [ 23, 41, 56]. Finally, the PCA-MDS-SQI 
framework confirmed that STCR-IPNS superiority was not driven by a single vari -
able, but by integrated changes in enzyme activity, microbial biomass, respiration bal -
ance, nutrient availability, and crop performance. The clear separation of FYM-adjusted

STCR-IPNS from STCR-NPK and non-prescription benchmarks therefore indicates 
a more functionally organized rhizosphere, consistent with soil-quality frameworks 
based on minimum data sets, integrated scoring, and microbial indicators [ 8, 13, 16, 30]. 
Although the present study showed clear treatment responses in greengram productiv -
ity, nutrient uptake, enzyme activity, microbial indicators, and PCA derived SQI, the 
results should be interpreted within the limitation of a one-season field experiment. Sea-
sonal variation in rainfall, temperature, residual nutrient dynamics, microbial activity, 
and crop response may influence the performance of STCR-IPNS under field conditions. 
Therefore, the findings should be considered as short-term validation evidence rather 
than a final long-term recommendation. Multi season and multi-location experiments 
across different Alfisols, soil fertility gradients, and pulse-based systems are required to 
confirm the stability and wider applicability of FYM adjusted STCR-IPNS.
5 Conclusion
This study shows that FYM adjusted STCR-IPNS can move greengram nutrient man -
agement beyond yield based fertilizer prescription. Under the tested Alfisol condition, 
target-yield fertilization combined with FYM supported crop productivity, NPK uptake, 
and rhizosphere biological functioning. The strongest response was recorded under the 
1.2 t ha − 1 STCR-IPNS treatment. The novelty of the study is the integration of target 
yield based STCR-IPNS with biological soil quality indicators in greengram. By com -
bining yield response, nutrient uptake, enzyme activity, microbial indicators and PCA 
derived SQI, the study provides a broader validation of STCR-IPNS than yield based 
assessment alone. The study contributes empirical evidence that biological soil qual -
ity indicators can strengthen the evaluation of STCR-IPNS beyond conventional yield 
validation. It also provides a practical framework for integrating soil test-based fertil -
izer prescription, organic nutrient inputs and rhizosphere functional indicators in pulse-
based nutrient management. For field application, however, the reliability of this strategy 
depends on site-specific soil test calibration, accurate assessment of FYM nutrient com -
position and realistic consideration of input availability and economic feasibility. Future 
research should extend this framework across contrasting Alfisols, other soil groups, 
different pulse crops and multi-season field conditions. Long-term studies should also 
examine whether the observed improvements in carbon pools, enzyme activity, micro -
bial biomass and PCA derived soil quality are sustained under repeated STCR-IPNS 
application. By connecting target yield fertilization with enzyme-mediated nutrient 
cycling and biological soil quality, FYM-adjusted STCR-IPNS offers a forward-looking 
pathway for improving pulse productivity while maintaining the functional integrity of 
soil systems.
