ORIGINAL ARTICLE
Paddock design influences soil inorganic nitrogen 
distribution in a pasture‑based sow system with poplar trees
Magdalena Ullfors · Kiril Manevski · Uffe Jørgensen · Maarit I. Mäenpää · 
Søren Ugilt Larsen · Martin Jensen · Anne Grete Kongsted
Received: 7 July 2025 / Accepted: 6 June 2026 
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract In pasture-based pig production, high soil 
inorganic nitrogen (N-inorg) amount and uneven dis-
tribution combined with low vegetation cover lead 
to nitrate leaching. Integrating trees can mitigate 
this by maintaining a vegetation ensuring nitrogen 
(N) uptake, but there is limited knowledge on how 
to implement this approach. An on-farm experiment 
was conducted using 16 single sow paddocks with 
grass-clover ley and centrally located poplar trees to 
investigate the effect of paddock design on elimina-
tion behaviour, area covered with faeces and herba-
ceous vegetation, and distribution of N-inorg. Four 
treatments were evaluated in three consecutive sow 
batches, involving 48 lactating sows, combining 1) 
tree management—cutting trees with application of 
wood chips as mulch under the trees or unmanaged 
trees; and 2) sow resource position—placing the 
hut and feeding trough on the same side or opposite 
sides of the trees. Topsoil N-inorg concentrations 
were sampled initially, after two, and after three sow 
batches. Elimination behaviour and area covered with 
faeces indicated higher N deposition within the tree 
zone compared to the grass zones, with no clear treat-
ment effect. However, N-inorg was more evenly dis-
tributed in paddocks with resources on opposite sides 
of the trees by ranging at 41.9–44.2 kg N ha⁻1 instead 
of 23.8–76.1 kg N ha⁻ 1. Higher N-inorg amount was 
associated with lower herbaceous vegetation cover. 
Tree management had no effect. These findings sug-
gest that a paddock system with centrally placed pop-
lar trees and huts/feeders on either side may enhance 
the distribution of soil N-inorg and potentially reduce 
N leaching.

Introduction
Animal production is associated with high nitrogen 
(N) surpluses (Jan et al. 2017) and thus the environ-
mental challenge of N losses. These challenges are 
further complicated by free-range production sys-
tems implemented to provide animal benefits. Surface 
N balances for free-range pig and poultry paddocks 
can easily exceed 300  kg  N  ha−1 in a year (Kong-
sted et  al. 2019; Manevski et  al. 2024; Williams 
et  al. 2000). This N surplus is mostly added by the 
animal manure and is subject to gaseous N emissions 
(ammonia (NH3) volatilisation, nitrous oxide (N2O) 
from denitrification) or nitrate (NO3
–) leaching, and 
only a small part is integrated in the soil organic mat-
ter pool. Under temperate wet climate and on sandy 
soils, such as in Denmark and parts of England, up to 
40–90% of the N surplus is leached from free-range 
pig paddocks (Eriksen 2001; Williams et  al. 2000). 
Of this, more than 200 kg N ha−1 can leach during 
one year with sows (Eriksen 2001; Kongsted et  al. 
2020). Leached NO3
– is hazardous to groundwater 
quality (Schullehner et  al. 2018) and contributes to 
the eutrophication and oxygen deficiency of surface 
waters (Davie and Quinn 2019; Meier et  al. 2019). 
Environmentally sustainable free-range pig produc-
tion therefore remains challenged, and sound meas-
ures are needed to reduce the high surplus of N.
This challenge is exacerbated by the pigs’ spatially 
concentrated elimination behaviour (Eriksen 2001; 
Salomon et al. 2007), leading to N ‘hotspots’ of about 
1,800  kg  N  ha−1, at a short distance around the hut 
and the feeding area (Salomon et  al. 2007). Herba-
ceous vegetation cover acts as an N sink, but is typi-
cally destroyed by rooting and trampling, so that large 
quantities of inorganic N remain in the soil (Eriksen 
et al. 2002). Equipping sows with nose-rings is aimed 
at maintaining the grass cover but cannot fully achieve 
it (Eriksen et al. 2006). Woody perennials, on the con-
trary, are more robust and can withstand pig damage 
once well established (Horsted et al. 2012; Jørgensen 
et al. 2018). In addition to adsorbing some N which 
would otherwise leach (Manevski et  al. 2019), trees 
benefit the pigs by providing shade and a more natural 
environment (Jakobsen 2018; Schild et al. 2018). By 
redirecting the pigs’ activity, trees might help main-
tain the grass cover and thus interactively contribute 
to soil N uptake (Horsted et al. 2012; Jørgensen et al. 
2018). Trees also take up N during the non-growing 
season (Ma et al. 2021) with their wider and deeper 
root system compared to herbaceous vegetation (For -
tier et al. 2013; Manevski et al. 2019). When integrat-
ing trees, European farmers strive to continue receiv -
ing subsidies under the Common Agricultural Policy 
of the EU (CAP). Energy tree crops must be cut down 
after a specific time (Regulation 2022/C 485/01,

2022, Point 2.4), e.g. after at most 20 years for pop-
lars in Denmark (Landbrugsstyrelsen 2022). The 
regrowing shoots provide a sheltered environment for 
the pigs and are expected to increase N demand of the 
poplar trees. The harvested material, if not used for 
energy, can be chopped and used as wood chip mulch 
(Barrett and Burke 2000; Tang et al. 2024). Such an 
organic material rich in carbon (C) can increase soil 
C/N ratio and thus reduce soil inorganic N by provid-
ing C for microbial immobilisation of N (Seneviratne 
et al. 1997; Sønsteby et al. 2004). However, the effect 
of trees and wood chip mulch on soil N distribution in 
free-range animal paddocks remains poorly studied.
The magnitude and the evenness of N distribution 
in free-range animal paddocks depend on stocking 
density and feed input (Eriksen et  al. 2006; Quin-
tern and Sundrum, 2006). A high stocking density 
and high N excretion from inefficient feeding lead to 
unevenly distributed N with ‘hotspots’ that are diffi-
cult to control. Directing the pigs’ eliminations to the 
tree area might enhance N distribution and improve 
its uptake by the trees. However, current knowledge 
on trees’ effects on pig elimination behaviour and N 
cycling within pasture paddocks is limited to a few 
studies in pigs (Horsted et  al. 2012; Jakobsen et  al. 
2018; Jørgensen et  al. 2018; Manevski et  al. 2019) 
and comparable work in poultry (Manevski et  al. 
2024) or cattle (Dahlgren et al. 1997). In Denmark, an 
experimental study indicated that poplar trees reduce 
 NO3
– leaching by 75–80% in the tree area compared 
to the grass-clover area (Manevski et al. 2019). In the 
same experiment, the sows did not strongly prefer 
to eliminate in the tree area compared to the pasture 
(Jakobsen et al. 2018). Pigs are known to place their 
eliminations in relation to the location of resources 
like the hut and the feeding trough, understood here 
as resources from the pigs’ perspective (Andersen 
et al., 2020; Salomon et al. 2007; Stolba and Wood-
Gush 1989). This explains why, in another experi-
ment, growing-finishing pigs preferred the tree area 
located between key resources (Horsted et al. 2012). 
Hence, the paddock design in terms of resource posi-
tion is relevant for the distribution of pig excreta and 
thus soil N distribution and management (Jakobsen 
et al. 2018).
The objective of this study with free-range lactat-
ing sows in paddocks with grass-clover and poplar 
trees was to evaluate the effects of novel manage-
ment practice on soil inorganic N and its spatial 
distribution. The treatments included the tree man-
agement referring to using the harvested tree biomass 
as wood chip mulch, and different resource positions. 
The connection between these treatments and soil 
inorganic N was investigated by quantifying the elim-
ination behaviour, area covered with faeces, and her -
baceous cover. Higher levels of soil inorganic N were 
expected in areas where animals eliminated, faeces 
covered the soil and herbaceous vegetation cover was 
sparse. It was hypothesized that placing the resources 
hut and feeding trough on opposite sides of the tree 
zone will distribute the inorganic N within the pad-
dock compared to placing the resources on the same 
side of trees. Additionally, harvesting and adding 
wood chips to the soil in the tree area was hypothe-
sized to reduce the inorganic N amounts in this veg-
etation zone compared to no tree management.
Materials and methods
Experimental setup and treatment description
The field experiment was conducted from April 2023 
to March 2024 on a commercial organic pig farm 
in Denmark (Fig.  1). The field consisted of sixteen 
15 × 63 m paddocks, each housing one lactating sow, 
and with poplar trees centrally placed. This results in 
a stocking density of 10.6 sows  ha−1. These paddocks 
were used to test four treatments in four replicates. 
The treatments comprised four combinations of the 
two factors: 1) tree management (T) where trees were 
either managed with cutting and placing the wood 
chips (WC) on the soil surface in the preceding year 
(TM) or trees were unmanaged (TU) and 2) resource 
position (R) where hut and feeding trough were either 
placed on the same side (RS) or opposite sides (RO) 
of the trees. The paddock area was split in two halves 
where the tree management treatment was applied 
(paddock no. 9–16, TM, Fig.  1). The resource posi-
tion treatments were randomized within half-blocks 
of the same tree management (Fig. 1).
The poplar clone OP42 (Populus maximowiczii × P. 
trichocarpa) was planted in 2011 and occupied about 
one third of the paddock area. The trees were planted 
in eight rows crossing the paddocks, with 2 m between 
rows and 3 m distance between the two middle rows. 
Within rows, the distance between trees was 2.5  m, 
i.e. a plant density of about 1,900 trees  ha−1. All the

During the experiment in 2023, three consecutive 
batches of 16 sows were each introduced about one 
week before farrowing in mid-April, end of July and 
beginning of November, and removed after 10 weeks 
of lactation (Fig. 3). One sow in batch 1, one in batch 
2 and three in batch 3 were removed from the pad-
docks by the farmers before the end of lactation. As 
such, a total of 43 sows stayed in the paddocks dur -
ing the whole lactation period. The sows were of the 
crossbreed Nordic Landrace x Yorkshire (TN70 by 
Topigs Norsvin) and in their fourth parity on aver -
age, with a range from first to seventh. They were 
equipped with nose-rings. One-strand electrified wire 
fenced the sows but not the piglets who could roam 
freely between paddocks. Each paddock contained a 
2.60 × 2.25 m farrowing hut, a sow feeding station, and 
a shared water station (Fig.  1, Fig.  4). Piglet feeding 
stations were in every second paddock. Huts were 
straw-bedded and reallocated after each batch (Fig. 4). 
On warm days, wallowing water was provided in the 
grass zone west of the trees (zone C, Fig. 1).
Historically, the farm followed a crop rotation of 
sows every second year, alternating with spring bar -
ley (Hordeum vulgare) undersown with grass-clover. 
In the rotation preceding the measurements, four sow 
batches were managed: in the first two, huts and feed-
ing troughs were placed on opposite sides of the tree 
rows; in the last two, both were positioned on the 
same side.
Data collection
Farm data
To calculate the N mass balance, collected farm data 
on inputs and outputs included the litter-wise count 
of born, eliminated and weaned piglets as well as the 
live weight of weaned piglets (weighed batch-wise) 
and eliminated piglets (estimated) within the pad-
docks. On average, the experimental sows weaned 
13.0 piglets per litter, with an average individual pig-
let weight of 29.7 kg. Straw amount and feed N (crude 
protein) content were retained from farm records. The 
sow and piglet feed amount was assumed to be the 
same for each litter according to the farm’s feeding 
curves. Mean sow live weight losses were estimated 
based on literature (Kongsted et al. 2019).

Weather data
Data on daily mean temperature (°C) and daily pre-
cipitation (mm) based on a 20 × 20 km and 10 × 10 km 
grid, respectively, for the years 2014–2024 was 
attained from the Danish national grid database pro-
vided by the Danish Meteorological Institute (DMI).
Elimination behaviour and area covered with faeces
To register sow elimination events, each sow batch was 
observed during lactation weeks 4 and 7 (Fig.  3). For 
batches 1 and 2, observations were conducted in two 
blocks: from 2.30 pm to 7.00 pm on the first day and 
from 7.00 am to 12.10 pm on the following day with half 
an hour break per block. For batch 3, both blocks were 
performed on a single day, and the observation time 
window was reduced from 7.40 am to 4.30 pm due to 
morning and evening darkness. Each of the 16 paddocks 
were observed for similar durations on each observa-
tion day. During observations, two observers on oppo-
site sides of the trees observed half of each paddock. 
They moved along paddocks in consecutive order but 
with randomisation of starting point and walking direc-
tion. The observers systematically switched sides after 
observing all paddocks once. To provide the time to walk 
from paddock 1–16 and reverse, a break of 10 min was 
included. By continuous sampling, urination and def-
ecation events were registered at paddock level, includ-
ing the sow’s position according to the three vegetation 
zones (Fig. 4). In both week 4 and 7 of the first sow 
batch, observers spent one sampling interval of 2 min 
at each sow and paddock before moving to the adjacent 
one. For the following batches 2 and 3, the interval was 
prolonged to 4 min to disturb the sows less with two pad-
docks observed per interval. Leafless trees during batch 3 
observations allowed one person to perform the observa-
tions alone. In total, each paddock was observed for 3.7 
h, resulting from 52, 104, and 108 min per sow in batches 
1, 2, and 3, respectively. Only one sow in batch 3 was 
removed before observations. At the end of each batch, 
the percentage of area covered with faeces was visually 
estimated in 1 × 1 m grids in paddocks 5–12. The faeces 
of sows and piglets could not be distinguished.
Herbaceous cover
From April 2023 and monthly until the final sow 
batch, herbaceous cover (%), including grass, clover 
and weed, was visually estimated in all 16 paddocks 
across three subzones within each of the grass zones 
A and C (Fig. 4).
Soil inorganic nitrogen and texture
Within each of the 16 paddocks, soil samples (at least 
200  g) were taken with augers from 0–20  cm depth 
in vegetation zones A, B, and C using 18, 15, and 18 
sampling points, respectively (Fig.  4). Soil inorganic 
N was determined from samples taken at each point in 
March 2023 (before sow introduction; date 1), Octo-
ber 2023 (after batch 2; date 2) and March 2024 (after 
sow batch 3; date 3). Samples were pooled by veg-
etation zone per paddock resulting in 48 samples per 
date. These were cooled to − 18  °C and dried. Con-
centrations of NO3
– and ammonium (NH4
+) in DM 
were determined spectrophotometrically after extrac-
tion with 2  M KCl and summed to represent total 
inorganic N (N-inorg). Concentrations (mg kg DM−1) 
were converted to kg N  ha−1 assuming a bulk density 
of 1.4 g  cm−3 for Danish soils presented by Rubaek 
and Sørensen (2011). For soil texture determination, 
one sample was taken at each point in March 2023 
(Fig.  3), then pooled by vegetation zone and treat-
ment, resulting in 12 samples in total. Soil particle 
size was determined by soil sieving and hydrometer 
measurements after dispersion in 0.002  M sodium 
pyrophosphate (Gee and Bauder, 1986). Total N and 
C concentrations were determined after air drying at 
60 °C and sieving (> 2 mm) by the DUMAS dry com-
bustion using a Vario Max CN Element Analyzer.
Nitrogen mass balance
The excreted N (ex animal) was calculated as the 
difference between feed input and sow weight loss 
on the one hand and piglet weight gain on the other 
hand. By including straw for bedding, atmospheric 
deposition (Ellermann et  al. 2024) and biological N 
fixation (Jakobsen et  al. 2015) as further inputs, the 
surface N balance was calculated for the year with 
sows (April 2023 to March 2024).

zone effects

Correlation analysis between area covered with 
faeces, herbaceous cover and soil inorganic N
In order to aggregate the repeated measures of area 
covered with faeces and herbaceous cover at paddock 
level, paddock was included as a main effect in the 
generalized linear mixed models described above. 
The LS-means estimated at paddock-level were used 
to investigate the association with the soil inorganic N 
measured at paddock level.
The relationship between area covered with faeces 
and soil inorganic N was explored by running Spear -
man’s correlations (n = 48) between N concentrations 
 (NH4
+-N, NH3
–-N, and N-inorg) at soil sampling 
dates 2 and 3, and LS means of area covered with fae-
ces. This was done for the area covered with faeces 
at the end of sow batches 2 and 3, which occurred 21 
and 34 days before soil sampling, and for sow batches 
1 and 2, which occurred 113/117 and 154 days before 
soil sampling.
Additionally, the Spearman’s correlation with soil 
inorganic N was tested for herbaceous cover model 
estimated as LS means for different days before the 
soil sampling date (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 14, 28, 42, 56 
days) for the grass zones A and C.
Results
Site conditions
In 2023, the mean daily temperature approximated 
the 10-years average of 9.1  °C (9.2  °C in 2023 and 
9.6 °C in 2024). Similarly, the annual precipitation of 
1,250 and 1,218 mm in 2023 and 2024, respectively, 
was close to the 10-years average of 1,302 mm. The 
highest daily precipitation occurred in September 
2023 (Fig. 5).
The topsoil (0–20 cm) texture was classified before 
pig introduction as coarse sand, following Danish soil 
classification (JB1; Breuning-Madsen et  al. 2001) 
with 4.7% clay on average (SD = 0.3%). Samples con-
tained 2.0% total C (SD = 0.3%) and 0.14% total N 
(SD < 0.1%) on average, which were higher in the tree 
zone compared to the grass zones by factor 1.3. The 
C/N ratio averaged 13.7 (SD = 0.3).
Surface nitrogen balance
The total feed input was 490  kg  N  ha−1 for sows and 
219 kg N  ha−1 for piglets. Weaned, dead and otherwise 
lost piglets contributed 343, 9 and 12 kg N  ha−1 to pig-
let weight gain, respectively. Including a sow weight 
loss of 28  kg  N  ha−1, 373  kg  N  ha−1 was excreted. 
Including the further inputs of straw bedding, atmos-
pheric deposition and biological fixation of 29, 11 and 
22 kg N  ha−1, respectively, results in a surface N bal-
ance of 435 kg N  ha−1 (Appendix 3, Table 4).
Sow elimination behaviour
The count of total eliminations (n = 141) observed 
at three sow batches, in all sixteen paddocks and in 
three vegetation zones was influenced by the vegeta-
tion zone only (χ 2 = 12.6, df = 2, p = 0.002) and not 
by tree management (χ 2 = 0.1, df = 1, p = 0.715) nor 
resource position (χ 2 < 0.1, df = 1, p = 0.963) or any

of the interactions between them (p > 0.2703). Elimi-
nations were observed more often in the tree zone B 
(31 times) than in the grass zones A (8  times) and

of the observed urination events occurred in the tree

Discussion
Nitrogen deposition by free-range pigs
The average 2.3 urinations and 3.0 defaecations per 
sow per observation day found in this study were 
slightly lower than those reported by Jakobsen et  al. 
(2018), who recorded an average of 2.8 urinations and 
3.5 defaecations per lactating sow per day in compara-
ble paddocks with two rows of poplar trees at one end 
and with comparable total daily observation time. This 
lower frequency persists despite starting observations 
earlier in the morning (7 am in batch 1 and 2, and 
7.40 am in batch 3) to capture expected early morning 
eliminations close to the hut, as suggested by Jakob-
sen et  al. (2018). When comparing to 24-h record-
ings of indoor lactating sows, which showed 3.1–3.4 
urinations and 1.5–4.5 defaecations per sow per day 
depending on the stage of lactation and feeding strat-
egy (Andersen and Pedersen 2011; Tabeling et  al. 
2003), the frequencies observed in this study suggest 
that some eliminations, possibly those occurring in the 
very early morning and during the night, were missed.
Total sow elimination counts were higher in the 
tree zone (approx. one third of the paddock area) than 
in the grass zones. Urination and defecation followed a 
similar pattern. This indicates that sows used the trees 
differently than grass, either for activity in general or 
eliminations specifically. Compared to Jakobsen et al. 
(2018), which observed 7% of urinations and 19% of 
defaecations in the tree zone covering 18% of the pad-
dock area, locating the trees in the centre of the pad-
dock seems to have increased the proportion of urine 
and faeces deposited in the tree zone. However, piglet 
behaviour was not observed, although their elimina-
tions might also influence the soil N hotspots.
The total counts of observed sow elimination 
behaviour were relatively low, which limited the abil-
ity to detect treatment effects. Not only longer obser -
vation time windows but also more observation days 
would enhance the robustness of the data. For future 
studies, it is important to consider the development 
of alternative indicators of sow elimination behav -
iour, as direct observations are time-consuming, and 
video observations are challenged by the presence of 
trees. Higher count observations would also allow the 
differentiation of urinations and defaecations, which 
is highly relevant to short-term N availability, since 
about 70% of N excreted by lactating sows is con-
tained in the urine (Dourmad et al. 1999).
The visual scoring of faeces covered area only par-
tially supports the findings from the sow behavioural 
observations. This may be partly explained by inclu-
sion of piglet faeces in the visual scoring although 
piglet elimination site preferences are in general 
expected to reflect that of sows, except during the first 
few days in life (Andersen et  al., 2020). Also, when 
considering faeces hotspots, defined as more than 50% 
cover within the observed 1 × 1  m, these were most 
often (90% of hotspots) located within the tree zone. 
Additionally, LS means of all area covered with faeces 
recordings show that the covered area was larger in 
the tree zone compared to grass zones, especially after 
batches 1 and 3, but only in unmanaged trees (TU; 
Fig. 6). This difference between tree zones with and 
without management might be a result of high precipi-
tation a few days before these two sampling occasions. 
Field reports from the data collector suggested that 
rainfall washed faeces between WC. Therefore, area 
covered with faeces under managed trees (TM) might 
have been underestimated compared to more accurate 
estimates for the soil surface under unmanaged trees 
(TU) and in grass zones. Additionally, in October 
2023, after batch 2, leaf litter covered the soil surface 
in tree zones, which hampered the estimation. Hence, 
it can be assumed that the paddocks with unmanaged 
trees (TU) without autumn leaf litter (after batch 1 and 
3) represent the most robust observations. All in all, 
these factors impeding observations explain the low 
correlation with soil inorganic N. Additionally, the 
organic faeces N must be mineralized before it can 
influence soil inorganic N.

Nitrogen uptake by herbaceous vegetation
The strongly decreasing herbaceous cover in autumn 
with sows present (Fig. 7) suggests that equipping the 
sows with nose-rings does not suffice to inhibit vegeta-
tion destruction, as reported before (Eriksen et al. 2006). 
Soil inorganic N and herbaceous cover were strongly 
correlated, with the strongest association observed when 
herbaceous cover was estimated on the same day as soil 
sampling, rather than from 3 days to 6 weeks prior. This 
likely reflects the absorption of inorganic N by the cur-
rent vegetation, with potential nitrogen losses through 
leaching increasing as the time interval between veg-
etation estimation and soil sampling widens, especially 
under conditions of sufficient precipitation.
Soil inorganic nitrogen in farrowing paddocks
The NO3
− amount of 14.1 kg N  ha−1 on average before 
pig introduction was comparable to 14–16  kg  N  ha−1 
found for comparable paddocks (Eriksen et  al. 2002; 
Kongsted et  al. 2020). After two and three batches of 
lactating sows, respectively, inorganic N amounts were 
33.7 and 44.7 kg N  ha−1 across treatments and zones, 
with peaks of 87.1 and 108.6 kg N  ha−1. These N lev-
els are much lower than amounts reported from other 
pig systems without (Eriksen et al. 2002) or with trees 
integrated and with corresponding surface N balances 
(Jørgensen et  al. 2018), where hotspots reached up to 
500 kg N  ha−1 (Jakobsen et al. 2018). This could be due 
to deviations from the current experiment setup such as 
larger sampling depth (0–40 cm in Eriksen et al. (2002); 
0–50  cm in Jakobsen et  al. (2018)), smaller sampling 
distance without pooling which increases variation 
(1–4 m in Jakobsen et al. (2018)), examination of grow-
ing pigs without nose-rings instead of lactating sows 
with nose-rings (Jørgensen et al. 2018). Another factor 
decreasing inorganic N in the topsoil is the high rain-
fall in August and late September 2023, causing high 
percolation and thereby downward transport of NO3
−. 
Finally, as the piglets were able to roam freely, similar 
to the conditions described by Jakobsen et al. (2018), a 
proportion of their excretions may have been deposited 
outside the experimental area and thereby not reflected 
in the soil N levels within the experimental paddocks.
The findings of initially higher NO3
−-N and NH4
+ 
concentrations under trees compared to grass-clover 
ley (Fig.  8) were likely connected to leaf litter. The-
vathasan and Gordon (1997) found that 50 kg N  ha−1 
 year−1 were contributed by leaf litter collected at 
0–2.5 m distance to rows of 7 m high poplar trees in 
an alley-cropping system, which supposedly increased 
nitrification rates. This contribution fits the current 
finding that total C and N concentration in the tree 
zone were higher by factor 1.3 compared to the grass 
zones. Additionally, an effect of former pig presence 
is possible, since the average of 21.3 kg N  ha−1 (inor-
ganic N) is higher than 17.0  kg  N  ha−1 observed in 
the depth 0–50 cm under trees without pigs in March 
in Jakobsen et  al. (2018). Another influence is the 
harvest of spring barley every two years in the grass 
zones, which is estimated to remove 65  kg  N  ha−1, 
including straw, each time (Kongsted et  al. 2020), 
whereas no biomass was removed from the tree area.
Differences in soil inorganic N between vegetation 
zones after the presence of sows interact with treat-
ment effects. In addition to influences of the vegeta-
tion on animal behaviour (compare Sect.  "Nitrogen 
deposition by free-range pigs"), poplars affect soil 
N differently than pasture plants. Poplar trees have 
higher fine root biomass at the depth 40–60 cm (For -
tier et al. 2013), can accumulate more N (Fortier et al. 
2015, 2010), and their N uptake remains higher dur -
ing the non-growing season compared to herbaceous 
vegetation (Ma et  al. 2021). However, near the soil 
surface (0–20 cm), the fine root biomass is higher in 
herbaceous vegetation (Fortier et al. 2013).
Effect of resource position on nitrogen cycling
A higher soil inorganic N amount in the tree zone 
of RO was anticipated when the hut was located on 
its opposite side (RO) compared to on the same side 
(RS). Indeed, locating the hut on the opposite side of 
the trees, in zone C, at first moved the zones of high 
soil inorganic N from zone A with the feeding station 
to zone B (trees) and C (grass east). Later, it equalled 
out the zone differences with soil inorganic N levels 
in zone A with the feeding station remaining high. 
This aligns with earlier findings of high inorganic N 
found close to feeding areas (Jakobsen et  al. 2018) 
which can be caused by feed waste. However, feed 
waste is expected to be low due to the farmer’s effort 
to reduce it, such as a lid on the feeding trough. The 
two resources being placed on the same side clearly 
increased N accumulation on this side, zone A, so 
that N availability will exceed plant demand and is 
likely to be leached. Overall, the results indicate that

Vol.: (0123456789)
separating the resources hut and feeding trough across 
the tree zone may contribute to a more even distribu-
tion of inorganic N within the paddock.
This effect of resource position was not evident in 
elimination behaviour or in the area covered with fae-
ces (Sect.  "Nitrogen deposition by free-range pigs"). 
Most eliminations and faecal hotspots were observed 
in the tree zone, but this did not elevate soil inorganic 
N above the levels found in the grass zones. This may 
partly be explained by N exceeding the immobilisation 
capacity and being lost from the topsoil during winter. 
At moderate soil temperatures, poplar trees with most 
of their fine roots in the depth 0–20  cm (Fortier et  al. 
2013) could have absorbed some inorganic N even dur-
ing the non-growing season (Ma et al. 2021). However, 
in coarse sandy soil, any remaining soluble N in the form 
of NO3
– is likely to be leached from the topsoil due to 
the high rainfall typical of Danish winters (Eriksen 2001; 
Eriksen et al. 2002).
Soil organic N amount in the grass zones was influ-
enced by the resource position and strongly nega-
tively correlated to herbaceous cover, which showed 
the opposite reaction to the resource position. In the 
grass zone with the feed and hut (zone A), herbaceous 
cover in June and September in 2023 was increased 
by 12–22 percentage points by moving the hut to the 
other side of the trees (zone C), where no difference 
was observed. The preserved vegetation in zone A 
thus contributed to reducing available N amount in the 
soil, as it was observed earlier by Eriksen et al. (2006), 
supposedly by N uptake and restrained N mineralisa-
tion due to evaporation lowering soil moisture.
Overall, the importance of resource position 
aligns with earlier findings that regular moving of 
resources supports a more even distribution of nutri-
ents (Eriksen and Kristensen 2001; Jakobsen et  al. 
2018). Apparently, the location of the hut determined 
the location of the highest soil inorganic N amount, 
which was partly mediated by the herbaceous cover.
Effect of tree management on nitrogen cycling and 
herbaceous cover
By combining tree cutting and WC surface mulching, 
the tree management could influence the N dynamics 
in several ways. Tree cutting might change the micro-
environment regarding air temperature, humidity, and 
wind. Regrowth of shoots might amplify N demand, 
although reduced evaporation might limit the uptake. 
Applying WC as surface mulch adds organic C, might 
reduce and balance the topsoil temperature, and helps 
to retain soil moisture, which are factors influencing 
N net mineralisation (Yansheng et al. 2020).
However, in this sampling of 16 paddocks with 
non-randomized location of tree management treat-
ments, no significant influence on soil inorganic N 
was observed. Tendencies showed that managing the 
trees might increase N-inorg amount across zones 
with no indication for increased N demand of man-
aged trees. This could be connected to rust (Melamp-
sora sp.) having infected 90% of leaves of the man-
aged trees at the end of the growing season in 2023.
Furthermore, the expected effect of WC in reducing 
soil inorganic N through microbial immobilisation (Yan-
sheng et  al. 2020) was not evident at the scale of this 
study. In contrast, Sønsteby et al. (2004) observed up to 
25% reduction in soil NO3
–-N using spruce bark mulch, 
at high N fertilisation level (120 kg N ha–1), likely due to 
its higher C/N ratio of 135. In comparison, WC (pecan) 
with a C/N ratio of 30, similar to the current study’s ratio 
of 33, did not immobilise N even when incorporated 
(Tahboub et al. 2007), suggesting this ratio is insufficient 
to trigger additional N for decomposition. This and the 
small particle size explain why applying hardwood saw-
dust with a C/N ratio of about 500 reduced net minerali-
sation in Averett et al. (2004). Regarding the size of WC, 
the results from a pot experiment suggested that WC 
smaller than 1.6 mm promoted stronger N immobilisa-
tion and soil C stabilisation within 8 months, compared 
to WC up to 12.7  cm (Gao et  al. 2024). Hence, it is 
likely that the decomposition of the relatively large WC 
used in this experiment (1–5 cm) was too slow to signifi-
cantly reduce the high soil N levels. This is supported by 
the low topsoil C/N ratio of 14 measured in March 2023 
one year after application of 20  t  organic C  ha–1 from 
WC with an estimated C/N ratio of 33. Overall, the lack 
of incorporation, the relatively large size, and the low 
C/N ratio of the WC limited the C decomposition and 
thus N immobilisation.
Tree management increased herbaceous cover in 
both grass zones by 12 percentage points in January 
2024. The higher herbaceous cover under tree man-
agement may be linked to shading effects, though 
this influence should be minimal in grass zone A due 
to its position in the south-east of the trees. Interest-
ingly, the variation in herbaceous cover during win-
ter when the tree canopies are leafless suggests other 
factors may also be at play. The higher herbaceous

cover under tree management is more likely a result of 
changes in foraging behaviour, since pigs are known 
to change their diet seasonally. Rooting activity peaks 
in autumn and winter (Calosi et  al. 2024), and WC 
being an attractive rooting material (Jensen and Ped-
ersen 2007) may help reduce vegetation destruction. 
Bornett et al. (2003) demonstrated that vegetation can 
be protected by providing a designated rooting area 
with mushroom compost. In the present study, piglets, 
unlike sows, were not nose-ringed and remained in the 
paddock until reaching approximately 20 kg, suggest-
ing they contributed substantially to rooting activity.
The tree management treatment represented a man-
agement strategy feasible for farmers. The selling of 
WC for energy purposes is missed out, but the rela-
tively small-sized paddock areas are not always profit-
able to be harvested for selling anyway. However, the 
resulting confoundment of tree cutting and WC mulch-
ing did not allow their separate evaluation, which is a 
suggestion for future research. In addition, the effect 
of smaller-sized WC incorporated into the soil instead 
of surface mulching should be investigated.
Conclusion
This on-farm experiment evaluated a novel sow pad-
dock design incorporating centrally located poplar trees 
in a pasture-based system. As hypothesized, separating 
key resources by placing the hut and feeding trough on 
opposite sides of the trees altered the spatial distribution 
of soil inorganic N. This arrangement resulted in a more 
even distribution of topsoil inorganic N across vegetation 
zones compared to placing resources on the same side of 
the trees. After three sow batches, inorganic N amounts 
were similar among zones in the separated-resource 
design, whereas resource location on the same side pro-
duced pronounced accumulation in the grass zone con-
taining both feed and hut. During summer and autumn, 
separating resources helped to maintain herbaceous 
cover in the zone with the feeding through. However, dif-
ferences in soil inorganic N between resource positions 
could not be fully explained by herbaceous cover, and 
elimination behaviour observations were probably too 
few to show treatment differences. Tree management in 
terms of cutting trees at 2 m height with surface applica-
tion of coarse wood chips did not influence soil inorganic 
N despite expectations of reduced N availability through 
carbon inputs and tree regrowth. Limited soil incorpora-
tion of woodchips, their size, and C/N ratio likely con-
strained these effects. Nevertheless, increased herbaceous 
cover following winter suggests that wood chips redi-
rected pig rooting behaviour away from the grass zones. 
Overall, the results demonstrate that a paddock system 
with centrally located poplar trees combined with stra-
tegic placement of the hut and feeding can improve the 
spatial distribution of soil inorganic N through pig root-
ing and elimination behaviour, thereby potentially reduc-
ing the risk of nitrate leaching in pasture-based systems.
