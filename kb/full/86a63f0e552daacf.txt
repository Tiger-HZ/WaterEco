Composting is the art of converting waste organic matter into biologically stable humic substances rich in available plant nutrients, primarily mediated through diverse microorganisms. The process is divided into two phases. Microbial activity characterizes the first phase, which results in the decomposition of agro-residues in three stages, that is, the mesophile, thermophile, and cooling stages, during which degradation of both simple and complex organic matter takes place. The second stage is humification, where the stable product is humic substances formed by the reorganization of organic matter. Due to the importance of humic substances in fertility, structure, and ecology of soil, the degree of humification is a measure of compost quality. Agro-residue-based organic manure or compost is expected to play a pivotal role in shaping sustainable agriculture and efficient waste management practices in future. However, like all biological processes, composting is also significantly affected due to the rapidly accelerating climate change (CC) and emerging contaminants (EC). The influence of such inevitable conditions over the microorganisms and process parameters involved in composting must be assessed to understand their subsequent impact on soil and crop nutrition. Currently, most of the research studies focus on the impact of composting or similar practices on CC. Similarly, compost is recognized as a mediator of spreading antibiotics, heavy metals, and antimicrobial-resistant genes into agricultural fields and the environment, facilitating their entry into the food chain. However, very little attention has been drawn to the effects of CC and EC on feedstock, process, and quality of compost. In the present chapter, we attempted to compile and discuss the research studies available on the impact of CC and EC on the quality of feedstock, composting process, and quality of the compost.
