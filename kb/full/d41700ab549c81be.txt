Impact of long-term nutrient management on soil
physico-chemical properties across the soil depth of a
Vertisol under soybean-wheat cropping
Indar Raj Naga1, BK Dixit2, BS Dwivedi3, RK Sahu4, PK Mishra5, Surbhi Jain6
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/2618060X.2026.v9.i9n.6640
A BSTRACT
Long-term nutrient management can influence soil chemical properties and carbon dynamics, with im-
portant implications for maintaining soil quality under continuous cropping. The present study evalu-
ated the effects of long-term fertilizer and manure application on soil pH, electrical conductivity (EC)
and soil organic carbon (SOC) across the soil profile of a Vertisol under a soybean-wheat cropping sys-
tem during 2024-25 at the AICRP-LTFE, JNKVV, Jabalpur, Madhya Pradesh, India. The long-term ex-
periment was initiated in 1972 and comprised nine nutrient-management treatments, including graded
NPK levels, nutrient omission treatments, NPK + Zn, NPK + FYM, sulphur-free NPK and an unfertil-
ized control, arranged in a randomized block design with three replications. Soil samples collected after
wheat harvest from six depths (0-15, 15-30, 30-45, 45-60, 60-75 and 75-90 cm) were analysed for pH,
EC and SOC. Soil pH remained statistically unaffected by nutrient management, with values ranging
from 7.50-7.68 at 0-15 cm to 7.60-7.78 at 75-90 cm, showing a slight increase with depth. Similarly, EC
was not significantly influenced by treatments and ranged from 0.14-0.18 dS m−1 in the surface layer to
0.17-0.22 dS m−1 at 75-90 cm, indicating no appreciable salt accumulation. In contrast, SOC showed
significant treatment variation and declined progressively with depth, ranging from 4.28-8.99 g kg−1 at
0-15 cm to 2.01-3.34 g kg−1 at 75-90 cm. The 100% NPK + FYM treatment consistently maintained the
highest SOC, whereas 100% N and control recorded the lowest values. All those, SOC was more re-
sponsive to long-term nutrient management than pH and EC, highlighting the importance of integrating
FYM with balanced mineral fertilization for sustaining soil carbon and soil quality under continuous
soybean-wheat cultivation.
Keywords: Electrical conductivity, Long-term fertilizer experiment, Soybean-wheat cropping system,
Soil organic carbon, Soil pH, Vertisol
Introduction
Soil fertility is a dynamic property governed by continuous interactions among nutrient inputs, crop removal,
organic matter turnover and changes in soil chemical conditions. Under intensive and continuous cropping,
long-term nutrient removal without adequate and balanced replenishment can progressively modify soil
chemical properties and carbon dynamics, with implications for the sustainability of soil productivity.
Therefore, evaluation of long-term nutrient management needs to extend beyond crop productivity to include
changes in key soil properties that regulate nutrient cycling, retention and availability (Sharma et al., 2022;
Datta et al., 2025) [2, 1]. Among these properties, soil pH, electrical conductivity (EC) and soil organic car-
bon (SOC) provide complementary information on soil reaction, soluble salt status and the condition of the
soil organic matter pool.
Soil pH is a fundamental property governing nutrient solubility, adsorption-desorption reactions and the
availability of essential plant nutrients. Its response to long-term fertilizer application depends on fertilizer
source, application rate, nutrient balance, cropping history and the buffering capacity of the soil. These fac-
tors are particularly important in Vertisols, which constitute an important agricultural soil resource in the
semi-arid and sub-humid regions of India. Their high clay content, smectite-dominated mineralogy, high
cation exchange capacity and pronounced shrink-swell behavior influence nutrient retention, water move-
ment and chemical reactions within the soil profile (Zhang et al., 2022) [3]. Consequently, although Vertisols
possess considerable buffering capacity, prolonged cultivation and contrasting fertilizer regimes may gradu-
ally modify their chemical environment, particularly where nutrient replenishment is inadequate or unbal-
anced (Peng et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2020) [4, 5].
Electrical conductivity (EC) is an important indicator of the soluble salt status of soil and reflects the con-
centration of dissolved ionic constituents in the soil solution. Repeated application of mineral fertilizers in-
troduces substantial quantities of nutrient ions and may influence soil EC depending on fertilizer rate, nutri-
ent combination, crop uptake, rainfall, drainage and movement of soluble constituents through the profile.

determining whether continuous fertilizer and manure application re-

chemical environment of intensively cultivated soils. The response of
EC may also vary with depth because of differences in ion movement,

Soil organic carbon (SOC) is a major indicator of soil quality and is
closely associated with soil structure, aggregation, nutrient retention
and biological functioning. Long-term mineral fertilization can influ-
ence SOC indirectly by modifying crop productivity, root growth and
residue returns, while organic amendments such as farmyard manure
(FYM) provide an additional and direct source of carbon to the soil.
The magnitude of SOC change depends on the quantity and quality of
carbon inputs, nutrient balance, cropping intensity and the stabiliza-
tion of organic matter within the soil matrix (Kumari et al., 2024) [6].
Integration of mineral fertilizers with FYM can therefore enhance car-
bon inputs and contribute to the maintenance of soil organic matter
over the long term (Patial et al., 2022; Sahu et al., 2026) [7, 8]. Long-
term fertilizer experiments provide an appropriate framework for as-
sessing these cumulative changes because nutrient-management ef-
fects are evaluated under a relatively consistent soil, climate and crop-
ping history.
The soybean-wheat cropping system is an important production sys-
tem in central India, particularly Madhya Pradesh, where continuous
cultivation involves substantial nutrient demand and repeated removal
of nutrients through harvested biomass. Long-term differences in fer-
tilizer rate, nutrient combination and organic manure application can
consequently create contrasting trajectories of soil chemical proper-
ties and carbon status (Meena et al., 2023) [9]. Furthermore, pH, EC
and SOC are not uniformly distributed within the soil profile. Surface
soil generally receives greater inputs of crop residues and root-de-
rived organic matter, whereas the distribution of soluble constituents
and soil reaction may be influenced by downward movement, mineral
interactions and changes in organic matter with depth. Hence, assess-
ment across the soil profile is necessary to capture the cumulative ef-
fects of long-term nutrient management beyond the surface layer.
Long-term fertilizer experiments (LTFE) provide a unique opportu-
nity to quantify the cumulative and residual effects of contrasting nu-
trient-management practices under relatively consistent soil, climate
and cropping conditions over several decades (Dikr, 2023; Tiwari et
al., 2024) [10, 11]. The present investigation was therefore undertaken
to evaluate the vertical distribution of soil pH, electrical conductivity
and soil organic carbon in a Vertisol under a long-term soybean-wheat
cropping system subjected to contrasting rates and combinations of
mineral fertilizers, nutrient omission, Zn application, FYM integra-
tion and an unfertilized control. The study specifically aimed to char-
acterize depth-wise changes in soil pH, EC and SOC and assess the
long-term effects of contrasting nutrient-management practices on the
chemical environment and carbon status of the soil profile.
Materials and methods
Experimental site and design
The investigation was carried out in 2024-25 using the ongoing Long-
Term Fertilizer Experiment (LTFE) established at JNKVV, Jabalpur,
Madhya Pradesh, India (23°21′ N, 79°94′ E; 393 m above mean sea
level). The experiment began in 1972 and the soybean-wheat crop-
ping sequence has been maintained since 1993-94. The experimental
soil is a clayey Vertisol (Typic Haplustert) of the Kheri series, classi-
fied under the fine, montmorillonite, hyperthermic family.
The study comprised nine contrasting nutrient-management regimes:
50% NPK, 100% NPK, 150% NPK, 100% NPK + Zn, 100% NP,
100% N, 100% NPK + FYM, 100% NPK-S and an unfertilized con-
trol. Treatments were maintained in a randomized block design with
three replications, with each experimental plot measuring 17 × 10.8
m. The recommended fertilizer rates were 20:80:20 kg N:P:K ha−1 for
soybean and 120:80:40 kg N:P:K ha−1 for wheat. Farmyard manure
was incorporated annually at 5 t ha−1 in soybean, whereas the NPK +
Zn treatment received 20 kg ZnSO4 ha−1 in alternate wheat seasons
until 1987. For the NPK-S treatment, we supplied phosphorus using
DAP instead of SSP.
Soil sampling
After wheat harvest in 2024-25, we collected profile soil samples sep-
arately from each treatment and replication at six depths: 0-15, 15-30,
30-45, 45-60, 60-75 and 75-90 cm. Within each experimental plot, we
collected soil from multiple representative points using a screw auger
to account for spatial variability. Samples corresponding to individual
treatment × replication × depth combinations were maintained sepa-
rately for subsequent laboratory analysis. The samples were then air-
dried, manually cleared of visible roots and plant residues, gently dis-
aggregated and passed through a 2-mm sieve before determining soil
physicochemical properties.
Analysis of soil physico-chemical properties
The soil samples were analysed for soil pH, electrical conductivity
(EC) and soil organic carbon (SOC) using standard laboratory
procedures.
Soil pH
Soil pH was determined in a 1:2.5 soil-water suspension following
Piper (1966) [12]. Ten grams of air-dried soil was mixed with 25 mL
of distilled water and stirred thoroughly. After equilibration, the pH of
the suspension was measured using a calibrated digital pH meter after
standardization with buffer solutions of known pH.
Electrical conductivity
Electrical conductivity was determined in the 1:2.5 soil-water extract
following Jackson (1973) [13]. The soil-water suspension was allowed
to stand overnight and the electrical conductivity of the clear super-
natant was measured using a conductivity meter. The EC values were
expressed as dS m−1 at 25 °C.
Soil organic carbon
Soil organic carbon was determined by the Walkley et al. (1934) [14]
chromic acid wet oxidation method. Briefly, 1 g of air-dried soil was
taken in a 500-mL conical flask and a blank was run simultaneously.
Ten millilitres of 1 N potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) solution was
added, followed by 20 ml concentrated sulphuric acid (H2SO4). The
contents were mixed carefully and allowed to stand for 30 min for ox-
idation of organic carbon. Subsequently, 200 ml distilled water, 10 ml
orthophosphoric acid and 1 ml diphenylamine indicator were added.
The excess dichromate was titrated against 0.5 N ferrous ammonium
sulphate (FAS) until the colour changed from blue to green. Soil or-
ganic carbon was calculated from the difference between sample and
blank titration readings and expressed as g kg−1 soil.
Statistical analysis
The data for pH, EC and SOC were subjected to analysis of variance
(ANOVA) appropriate for the randomized block design. Treatment
means were compared using the critical difference (CD) at the 5%
probability level when the treatment effect was significant.
Results and discussion
Soil pH
Long-term nutrient management did not significantly affect soil pH at
any of the sampling depths, indicating that soil reaction remained rel-
atively stable despite more than five decades of contrasting fertilizer
and manure application. Across the treatments, soil pH ranged from
7.50-7.68 at 0-15 cm and increased slightly with depth to 7.60-7.78 at
75-90 cm (Fig 1). The 100% NPK + FYM treatment generally
recorded the lowest pH, whereas the control maintained the highest
values throughout the profile. The relatively lower pH under FYM
may be associated with the decomposition of organic materials and
the formation of organic acids; however, the magnitude of this change
was insufficient to produce a statistically significant treatment effect.

The limited response of soil pH to long-term nutrient management is
characteristic of the strong buffering capacity of Vertisols. Their high
clay content and carbonate-bearing nature can effectively moderate
changes in soil reaction arising from repeated fertilizer inputs. Similar
stability of soil pH under long-term nutrient management in black
soils was reported by Dwivedi et al. (2015) [15], while Dubey et al.
(2015) [16] observed non-significant variation in pH across depths fol-
lowing prolonged fertilizer application in Vertisol. Bairwa et al.
(2020) [17] attributed the stability of soil reaction to the clay-rich na-
ture and Tiwari et al. (2024) [11] likewise reported statistically un-
changed soil pH under continuous fertilization and manuring.
The slight increase in pH with depth, together with the absence of sig-
nificant treatment effects, suggests that the inherent buffering proper-
ties of this Vertisol were sufficient to minimize cumulative changes in
soil reaction under the long-term nutrient regimes. In contrast to SOC,
which showed clear treatment-related differences, pH remained
within a relatively narrow range throughout the 0-90 cm profile, indi-
cating greater resistance of soil reaction to long-term nutrient-man-
agement changes.

Electrical conductivity
Electrical conductivity (EC) was not significantly influenced by long-
term nutrient management at any soil depth, indicating that prolonged
fertilizer and manure application did not result in appreciable accu-
mulation of soluble salts in the soil profile. At 0-15 cm, EC ranged
from 0.14-0.18 dS m−1, while at 75-90 cm it increased slightly to
0.17-0.22 dS m−1 (Fig 2). Among the treatments, 150% NPK gener-
ally recorded the highest EC, whereas 50% NPK recorded the lowest
values.

The relatively higher EC observed under 150% NPK is consistent
with the greater quantity of fertilizer-derived ions introduced into the
soil under this treatment. Singh et al. (2015) [18] similarly reported
relatively higher EC under long-term 150% NPK, 100% NPK and
100% NPK + Zn treatments. Nevertheless, the absence of significant
treatment differences in the present study indicates that the additional
soluble ions associated with higher fertilizer inputs did not accumu-
late to a level sufficient to substantially modify the electrical conduc-
tivity of the soil.
A slight increase in EC with depth was observed across the soil pro-
file. This pattern may reflect redistribution and downward movement
of soluble constituents within the profile. Since individual soluble
ions were not quantified in the present investigation, the depth-wise
increase in EC cannot be attributed to any specific ionic component.
Importantly, the consistently low EC values indicate that the soil re-
mained under a low-salinity condition despite long-term nutrient ap-
plication. Suman et al. (2018) [19] reported similarly marginal
changes in EC under long-term inorganic fertilization, while Birla et
al. (2015) [20] highlighted the potential role of FYM in regulating soil
chemical conditions. Pradhan et al. (2024) [21] and Tiwari et al.
(2024) [11] also reported low EC and limited salt accumulation under
long-term nutrient-management practices.
Thus, the EC response indicates that even prolonged application of
contrasting fertilizer regimes, including the higher 150% NPK level,
did not produce significant salt accumulation within the 0-90 cm soil
profile. This suggests that the balance among fertilizer inputs, crop
uptake and movement of soluble constituents was sufficient to main-
tain a low-salinity soil environment under the soybean-wheat crop-
ping system.
Soil organic carbon
In contrast to pH and EC, soil organic carbon (SOC) showed a signifi-
cant response to long-term nutrient management and declined pro-
gressively with increasing soil depth. Across treatments, SOC ranged
from 4.28-8.99 g kg−1 at 0-15 cm, decreasing to 2.01-3.34 g kg−1 at
75-90 cm (Fig 3). The higher SOC concentration in surface soil re-
flects the greater contribution of crop residues, root-derived carbon
and other organic inputs to the upper soil layer. With increasing depth,
these carbon inputs and associated biological processes generally de-
crease, resulting in lower SOC concentrations. Similar depth-wise de-
clines under long-term nutrient management have been reported by
Inwati et al. (2022) [22] and Thakur et al. (2011) [23].

The 100% NPK + FYM treatment consistently recorded the highest
SOC throughout the profile, with values of 8.99, 7.29, 5.84, 5.12,
3.86 and 3.34 g kg−1 at 0-15, 15-30, 30-45, 45-60, 60-75 and 75-
90 cm, respectively. The sustained superiority of this treatment high-
lights the importance of continuous organic inputs for maintaining
soil carbon under intensive cropping. Annual FYM application di-
rectly contributes organic substrates to the soil, while balanced fertil-
ization can promote crop growth and consequently increase carbon re-
turn through roots and crop residues. Swarup et al. (2000) [24] simi-
larly reported an increase in SOC from 5.7 to 9.6 g kg−1 under 100%
NPK + FYM. Comparable enhancement of SOC under integrated nu-
trient management has been reported by Patel et al. (2018) [25],

The 150% NPK treatment also maintained relatively high SOC,
recording 8.15, 6.43, 5.45, 4.75, 3.49 and 3.01 g kg−1 across the six
depths and remaining statistically comparable with 100% NPK +
FYM at most depths. In the absence of an external organic amend-
ment, the higher SOC under 150% NPK may be related to sustained
crop productivity and greater biomass, root and residue inputs result-
ing from greater nutrient availability. Thus, mineral fertilization can
contribute indirectly to SOC maintenance by increasing plant-derived
carbon inputs. Similar responses were reported by Kushwaha et al.
(2017) [30], while Nagwanshi et al. (2023) [31] observed an increase
in SOC from 5.62 g kg−1 under sub-optimal fertilization to 8.51 g
kg−1 under super-optimal fertilization.
The balanced 100% NPK and 100% NPK + Zn treatments also main-
tained comparatively high SOC, particularly in the surface layer,
where concentrations were 7.68 and 7.73 g kg−1, respectively. Their
relatively higher SOC compared with nutrient-omission treatments
may reflect sustained crop growth and greater return of plant-derived
organic matter. Adequate nutrient availability can support biomass
production and consequently increase carbon inputs through crop
residues and roots. These observations are consistent with the broader
findings of Raghuwanshi et al. (2016) [32], Panwar et al. (2019) [33]
and Patidar et al. (2021) [34] regarding the role of balanced fertiliza-
tion in maintaining soil organic matter and nutrient cycling.
In comparison, 100% NP and 100% NPK-S recorded lower SOC,
with surface-layer values of 6.95 and 7.26 g kg−1, respectively, de-
clining to 2.54 and 2.68 g kg−1 at 75-90 cm. The lower SOC under
these treatments may be associated with long-term nutrient imbalance
and reduced crop-derived carbon inputs. In the NPK-S treatment, sul-
phur omission may have restricted efficient nutrient utilization and
biomass production, thereby reducing the return of carbon through
roots and residues. These results emphasize that long-term mainte-
nance of SOC under continuous cropping depends not only on the
quantity of fertilizer applied but also on the balance among essential
nutrients.
The lowest SOC concentrations throughout the profile were observed
under 100% N and the control. At 0-15 cm, SOC was 5.08 g kg−1 un-
der 100% N and 4.28 g kg−1 under control, declining to 2.13 and 2.01
g kg−1, respectively, at 75-90 cm. The lower SOC under N-only fertil-
ization indicates that nitrogen supply alone was insufficient to main-
tain the carbon status achieved under balanced nutrient management.
Nutrient imbalance may restrict crop productivity and biomass return,
thereby reducing replenishment of soil organic matter. The unfertil-
ized control represents the greatest degree of nutrient depletion be-
cause crops were continuously cultivated without external nutrient re-
plenishment. The resulting low SOC reflects the cumulative effect of
continuous nutrient removal combined with limited external carbon
inputs. Similar reductions in SOC under N-only and unfertilized treat-
ments were reported by Kumari et al. (2013) [35], Suman et al. (2017)
[36], Choudhury et al. (2018) [37] and Bairwa et al. (2020) [17].
The three properties exhibited contrasting sensitivity to long-term nu-
trient management. Soil pH and EC remained comparatively stable,
whereas SOC showed a strong response to long-term nutrient-man-
agement history. The persistent superiority of 100% NPK + FYM in
maintaining SOC demonstrates the value of combining organic and
mineral nutrient sources under continuous soybean-wheat cultivation.
Conversely, the narrow ranges of pH and EC across treatments indi-
cate that the inherent buffering capacity and chemical characteristics
of the Vertisol moderated substantial changes in soil reaction and sol-
uble salt status. From an LTFE perspective, these results demonstrate
that SOC provides a more sensitive indicator of long-term changes in
soil quality than pH and EC under the conditions of the present exper-
iment. The treatment contrasts observed after several decades further
highlight the importance of sustained organic inputs and balanced fer-
tilization for maintaining the functional quality of Vertisol under in-
tensive cropping.
Conclusion
Soil pH and electrical conductivity remained relatively stable across
the long-term nutrient-management treatments, whereas soil organic
carbon showed a pronounced response to nutrient-management his-
tory and soil depth. Soil pH remained within 7.50-7.78, while EC re-
mained low at 0.14-0.22 dS m−1, indicating strong buffering and no
appreciable salt accumulation in the Vertisol profile. In contrast, SOC
declined progressively with depth, ranging from 4.28-8.99 g kg−1 at
0-15 cm to 2.01-3.34 g kg−1 at 75-90 cm. The 100% NPK + FYM
treatment consistently maintained the highest SOC, whereas 100% N
and control recorded the lowest values. Overall, the findings demon-
strate that SOC was more responsive to long-term nutrient manage-
ment than pH and EC, with NPK + FYM supporting better mainte-
nance of soil carbon and soil quality under soybean-wheat
cultivation..
