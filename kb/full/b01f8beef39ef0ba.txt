Abstract
Access to safe drinking water remains a challenge in many rural coastal communities. 
This study assessed fungal contaminants and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) in 
groundwater from coastal Ondo State, Nigeria, and evaluated associated public 
health risks. Using culture-based methods and GC-MS analysis, twelve fungal isolates 
across eight genera, including Aspergillus, Fusarium, and Penicillium, known for 
mycotoxin production were detected. Among the fungi, Rhodotorula and Rhizopus 
had the highest percentage occurrence of 77.8 and 66.7% respectively. Eighteen 
PCB congeners were quantified, with individual congener concentrations ranging 
from below the detection limit to 3.90 ng/L (PCB18); the corresponding total (ΣPCB) 
concentration is reported in the main text. Low-chlorinated homologues (Di- and 
Tri-PCBs) dominated the congener profile. All PCB concentrations remained below 
the US EPA Maximum Contaminant Level of 500 ng/L for total PCBs. Corrected non-
carcinogenic health risk assessments revealed that hazard quotients for all assessed 
congeners remained below the threshold of 1.0, indicating that modelled non-
carcinogenic risks are within acceptable limits; carcinogenic risk was not evaluated 
because the required congeners were not determined. Nevertheless, the occurrence 
of fungal genera at colony counts exceeding commonly cited but non-regulatory 
reference values warrants further microbiological monitoring; fungal pathogenicity, 
toxin production and health outcomes were not directly assessed in this study. These 
findings highlight the need for integrated monitoring of microbial and chemical 
contaminants in coastal groundwater systems and further investigation to inform 
targeted interventions to protect community health.

 1 Introduction
The consumption of microbiologically contaminated water remains a major global 
public health concern, contributing significantly to endemic diseases such as cholera, 
typhoid fever, diarrhea, tuberculosis and hepatitis A [11, 23]. While bacteria, viruses and 
parasites are widely recognized as important waterborne pathogens, fungi have received 
comparatively less attention despite their increasing detection in drinking water sys -
tems. Fungi can alter organoleptic properties of water and produce mycotoxins with 
documented health effects [ 14]. These organisms, which include yeasts and filamentous 
molds, are heterotrophic and capable of producing secondary metabolites with poten -
tial toxicological significance. Their presence in drinking water has been linked to con -
tamination during pipe installation, distribution system biofilm formation and poor 
maintenance of water infrastructure. In immunocompromised individuals, exposure to 
waterborne fungi may contribute to opportunistic infections and other health complica -
tions [14].
Beyond microbial contamination, groundwater resources are increasingly threat -
ened by chemical pollutants, particularly persistent organic pollutants (POPs). These 
compounds can be introduced into aquatic systems through industrial discharge, agri -
cultural runoff and improper waste disposal practices. Among POPs, polychlorinated 
biphenyls (PCBs), dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT) and dichlorodiphenyldichlo -
roethylene (DDE) are of major concern due to their toxicity, environmental persistence 
and ability to bioaccumulate in biological systems [ 1, 27, 28]. While various POPs exist 
in the environment, this study focuses exclusively on PCBs due to their extreme persis -
tence, widespread historical use in electrical equipment in Nigeria, and high potential 
for groundwater leaching compared to other POPs that may degrade faster or sorb more 
strongly to surface soils.
The potential sources of both microbiological and chemical contaminants in the study 
area are largely linked to anthropogenic activities. Microbiological contamination pri -
marily originates from poorly constructed hand-dug wells, proximity to pit latrines, 
and surface runoff carrying animal waste. Conversely, chemical contaminants such as 
PCBs are potentially introduced through improper disposal of spent transformer oils, 
e-waste dumping, and leachate from unlined municipal landfills. Furthermore, the co-
occurrence of these contaminants warrants investigation, as microbial communities 
can influence the biodegradation and transformation of persistent organic pollutants in 
groundwater systems. These shared anthropogenic pressures create a complex contami -
nation profile in coastal aquifers.
Groundwater, which forms a critical component of the hydrological cycle within 
subsurface aquifers, serves as a major source of drinking water in many develop -
ing regions [ 33]. However, groundwater contamination is often difficult and costly to 
remediate, posing long-term risks to both environmental and human health [ 37, 38].

instance, Ayeta et al. [6] observed elevated health risks associated with groundwater use

Among chemical contaminants, PCBs remain particularly concerning due to their 
extreme toxicity and persistence. These compounds are widely distributed environmen -
tal pollutants capable of inducing mutagenic and carcinogenic effects in humans [ 8, 24, 
25, 34, 39]. Exposure to dioxin-like PCBs has been associated with disruptions in liver 
vitamin balance, hepatotoxicity and impaired detoxification mechanisms, including oxi -
dative stress, apoptotic activation and DNA damage [ 8, 25, 39]. Consequently, PCB con-
tamination of drinking groundwater represents a significant human health concern.
Recent studies have increasingly documented PCB contamination in Nigerian ground-
water. For instance, Ediagbonya et al. [ 10] assessed PCB risks in surface and groundwa -
ter across three Nigerian states, while Aralu et al. [ 5] conducted a systematic review of 
PCB levels and toxicity in Nigerian aquatic systems. Despite these existing studies, there 
remains a critical research gap regarding the integrated assessment of both microbial 
and chemical contaminants in the specific coastal aquifers of Ondo State. Therefore, this 
study aims to investigate the presence of fungi and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) in 
groundwater from the coastal region of Ondo State, Nigeria, and to evaluate the associ -
ated human health risk from continued consumption of these groundwaters.
2 Methods
2.1 Study area and hydrogeological context
The study was conducted in coastal communities of Ondo State, Nigeria, specifically 
Ese-Odo, Irele and Ilaje Local Government Areas. The study area encompasses Igbobini 
and Irele and Omin in Ese-Odo and Irele LGAs respectively, and Igbokoda in Ilaje LGA. 
According to the 2006 population census, these areas have populations of 158,256 and 
289,838 for Ese-Odo, Irele and Ilaje LGAs respectively [ 29]. The region is characterized 
by significant anthropogenic pressure, including the presence of small-scale mechanic 
workshops, e-waste recycling sites, and proximity to industrial zones involved in petro -
leum handling and electrical equipment maintenance, which serve as potential point 
sources for chemical contaminants such as PCBs.
The hydrogeological setting is characterized by shallow unconfined aquifers within the 
coastal plain sands of the Niger Delta Basin. Groundwater occurs at depths ranging from 
5 to 30 m below ground level with seasonal fluctuations influenced by monsoonal rainfall 
patterns [33]. The aquifer materials consist primarily of fine to medium-grained sands 
with intermittent clay lenses that may influence contaminant transport and attenua -
tion. Similar coastal aquifer systems in West Africa show vulnerability to anthropogenic 
contamination due to high permeability and limited natural attenuation capacity [ 6, 
17]. The dry season sampling period, December 2023 to February 2024, was selected to 
minimize dilution effects from rainfall infiltration and to assess worst-case contaminant

2.2 Sample collection and distribution
Fifty groundwater samples were collected from nine distinct locations during the dry 
season, December 2023 to February 2024. At each of the nine locations, replicate sam -
ples (5–6 per site) were collected from distinct hand-dug wells or boreholes used for 
domestic supply. These replicates represent biological replicates capturing spatial vari -
ability within each community, rather than technical replicates from a single source. 
Samples were collected using sterile 500 mL polypropylene bottles following standard

2.3 Chemicals and instruments
All chemicals used were of analytical grade. Methylene chloride (HPLC grade, Batch 
No. MC2023-09), hexane (HPLC grade, Batch No. HX2023-11), sodium hydroxide (AR 
grade, Batch No. SH2023-04), and sulfuric acid (AR grade, Batch No. SA2023-02) were 
purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. PCB standard reference materials (Batch No. PCB-
STD-2023) were obtained from AccuStandard. Potato Dextrose Agar (Hi Media, Batch 
No. PDA-HM-2023) was used for microbiological analysis.
Instrumentation included an Agilent 7820 A gas chromatograph coupled to a 5975 C 
inert mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, USA). An autoclave (Model: Prestige 
Deluxe, Make: Hawkins) was used for sterilization. Microbial incubation was performed 
in a membrane incubator (Model: MI-400, Make: Labtech). pH adjustments were moni -
tored using a digital pH meter (Model: PH-100, Make: Hanna Instruments).
2.4 Microbiological analysis
Potato Dextrose Agar (39 g, Hi Media) was dissolved in 1000 mL distilled water, steril -
ized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min at 1.1 kg/cm² pressure [ 19]. Each water sample 
was serially diluted to the 6th dilution and 1 mL aliquots were inoculated into sterile 
Petri dishes, overlaid with pre-sterilized PDA and incubated at 28 ± 2 °C for 5 days for 
fungal isolation. Pure cultures were obtained by repeated streaking on PDA. Fungal 
identification relied solely on morphological and cultural characteristics following stan -
dard mycological keys [ 14]. An experienced mycologist independently verified the mor -
phological identifications. Consequently, all ecological and health interpretations are 
restricted to the genus level. Species-level identifications presented in the data are tenta-
tive and require molecular confirmation for definitive resolution. Future studies should 
incorporate molecular methods, for example ITS sequencing, to improve taxonomic 
accuracy [37].

2.5 PCB extraction and quantification
2.5.1 Extraction protocol
PCB extraction followed liquid-liquid extraction procedures adapted from US EPA 
Method 3510 C and validated for Nigerian environmental matrices [ 10, 22]. One liter 
of water sample at neutral pH was serially extracted with methylene chloride. pH was 
adjusted using 10 N sodium hydroxide or 1:1 sulfuric acid solution as required. Labora -
tory control samples and blanks were processed alongside field samples. One milliliter 
of 200 ng/mL surrogate working solution was added to procedure blanks, LCS and all 
samples. Extracts were concentrated using Kuderna-Danish apparatus and exchanged 
into hexane (60 mL) for cleanup. The combined extract was concentrated to 1 mL final 
volume prior to GC-MS analysis [11].
2.5.2 GC-MS analysis
Analysis employed an Agilent 7820 A gas chromatograph coupled to a 5975 C inert mass 
spectrometer with an electron-impact source and triple-axis detector (Agilent Technol -
ogies). Separation used an HP-5 capillary column (30 m x 0.32 mm x 0.25 μm film thick -
ness) coated with 5% phenyl methyl siloxane. Helium carrier gas flowed at 1.2 mL/min 
constant flow with an initial pressure of 0.26 psi and an average velocity of 40.00 cm/
sec. Injection volume was 1 µL in splitless mode at 250 °C. Oven program: 110 °C hold 
1 min then ramp to 310 °C at 15 °C/min hold 3 min. The total run time for the analysis 
was 17.33 min. Solvent delay: 3 min. Mass spectrometer operated at 70 eV in EI mode 
with ion source 230 °C, quadrupole 150 °C, transfer line 300 °C. Data acquisition used 
SIM and scan modes (m/z 35–550 at 2.0 scans/sec). Calibration was performed using 
a seven-point calibration curve ranging from 10 ng/L to 500 ng/L, with a coefficient of 
determination (R²) > 0.99 for all congeners.
Although MRM parameters were requested, the analytical method employed standard 
GC-MS in electron impact (EI) mode with selected ion monitoring (SIM). Therefore, 
MRM transitions and collision energies are not applicable. Instead, Table  2 provides the 
precursor ions and target SIM ions used for quantification.
2.5.3 Quality control

10, respectively. Field blanks and spiked blanks were analyzed to assess background 
contamination. Procedure and solvent blanks contained no target compounds. Sur -
rogate recoveries ranged from 83 to 112%, meeting acceptance criteria established for 
PCB analysis in West African environmental samples [ 10, 22]. Congener-specific LOD

2.6 Data analysis
Data were organized, cleaned and checked for accuracy using IBM SPSS Statistics v28.0. 
Prior to parametric testing, normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and 
homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. Shapiro-Wilk ( p > 0.05) and 
Levene’s tests (p > 0.05) confirmed that the data met the assumptions of normality and 
homogeneity of variance, respectively. ANOVA was employed to assess spatial variation, 
followed by Tukey HSD post hoc tests for pairwise comparisons, as data met assump -
tions of normality and homogeneity of variance. Significance level was set at p < 0.05. 
Descriptive statistics (mean, interquartile range) summarized PCB concentrations across 
sites. Similar statistical approaches have been applied in groundwater quality assess -
ments in coastal Nigeria [ 9, 20]. At the location level (9 locations), non-detects (BDL) 
were most frequent for PCB52 and the Tri-PCB homologue group (5 of 9 locations, 
55.6%), and occurred at 1–2 of 9 locations (11.1–22.2%) for PCB187, PCB183, PCB180, 
PCB170, PCB206, Hepta-PCB and Octa-PCB; all other congeners had no location-level 
non-detects. Location-level, rather than full replicate-level, non-detect frequencies are 
reported here because raw replicate-by-replicate concentration data were not retained 
in a form permitting a formal censored-data sensitivity analysis; LOD/2 substitution 
followed by ANOVA/Tukey HSD was applied uniformly, and a non-parametric Krus -
kal-Wallis comparison (reported for the fungal colony-count data) was not addition -
ally performed for every PCB congener. No correction for multiple comparisons (e.g., 
Bonferroni or false-discovery-rate adjustment) was applied across the congener- and 
homologue-level ANOVA tests; given the number of comparisons, the reported p-val -
ues should be interpreted with this in mind. Statistical comparisons of fungal occurrence 
and colony counts among locations were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test due to 
non-normal distribution of the microbiological data.

2.7.2 Unit consistency and population specification
PCB concentrations measured in ng/L were explicitly converted to mg/kg by multiplying 
by 1 × 10 ⁻⁶, assuming a water density of 1 kg/L. This corrects previous unit conversion 
errors where ng/L was erroneously treated as mg/L. Risk calculations were performed 
separately for adult (70 kg) and child (15 kg) exposure scenarios. Two complementary 
risk metrics are presented: (i) Tables 7 and 8 report the maximum single-congener 
hazard quotient (HQ), which is higher for adults (0.187, PCB31) than children (0.005, 
PCB31) under the exposure parameters applied to that specific congener; and (ii) Sup -
plementary Table S4 reports the cumulative, all-congener hazard index (HI) per loca -
tion, which is consistently higher for children than adults (approximately 2.1–2.4-fold) 
at every sampling location, consistent with children’s greater ingestion rate per unit body 
weight. The cumulative HI in Supplementary Table S4 is the more appropriate metric

3 Results and discussion
3.1 Fungal contaminants in groundwater
The study revealed the presence of twelve fungal isolates belonging to eight genera in the 
assayed groundwater samples from the coastal region of Ondo State, Nigeria (Table  4). 
Rhodotorula spp. was the most frequently detected genus (77.8% of samples), followed 
by Rhizopus spp. (66.7%) and Fusarium spp. (55.6%). Aspergillus spp., a genus contain -
ing known opportunistic pathogens, was detected in 55.6% of samples. Other genera 
included Mucor spp. (44.4%), Trichophyton spp. (33.3%), Penicillium spp. (22.2%), and 
Torulopsis spp. (11.1%).
Among these isolates, Torulopsis candida and Rhodotorula rubra are yeasts while the 
six other genera are molds. This result corroborates the report of Hageskal et al. [ 14] 
that fungi obtained from drinking water include yeast but mostly a variety of filamen -
tous molds including those with potential for disease causation, allergy induction and 
toxigenic properties. Among the molds recovered were Penicillium species, Aspergillus 
species and Fusarium species which are known to produce mycotoxins such as patulin, 
aflatoxins and zearalenone even if in diluted or less concentrated amounts. Although 
these genera are known to produce mycotoxins, actual mycotoxin production was not 
measured in this study and is highly dependent on specific environmental conditions. 
Therefore, the potential health risk is inferred from the presence of these genera rather 
than confirmed mycotoxin levels. These mycotoxins and metabolites may bioaccumulate 
with continued consumption over a long period and become harmful to human health.
Quantitative analysis revealed that median fungal colony counts ranged from 15 to 
45 CFU/100 mL across the sampling locations. These counts are reported here descrip -
tively; no universally applicable WHO or Nigerian regulatory numerical limit for fun -
gal counts in drinking water was identified, and no single benchmark value is invoked 
to characterize the water as contaminated. Values represent medians with interquar -
tile ranges provided in Supplementary Table S2; n = 5–6 replicates per location. The 
Kruskal-Wallis test indicated a statistically significant difference in fungal colony counts 
among the nine locations (H(8) = 45.54, p < 0.001), confirming spatial heterogeneity in 
fungal contamination. The prevalence of Aspergillus and Fusarium species aligns with 
reports from groundwater systems in Ghana [4] but contrasts with lower detection rates 
in treated urban supplies in South Africa [ 26]. The presence of genera capable of myco -
toxin production in some environmental contexts underscores the need for mycotoxin 
screening and molecular confirmation in future assessments; pathogenicity, viability and 
toxin production were not directly tested in this study. The detection of these fungal gen-
era in untreated groundwater warrants microbiological monitoring and further investi -
gation of their potential public-health significance, consistent with the occurrence-level 
findings of Ikuesan and Ediagbonya [ 20]. The percentage occurrence of fungal genera 
in the assayed groundwater samples is presented in Fig. 2, which illustrates the domi -
nance of Rhodotorula, Rhizopus and Aspergillus species across the sampled locations

3.2 PCB congener distribution and concentrations
Table 5 shows the concentrations of polychlorinated biphenyl congeners across the nine 
sampling locations. PCB concentrations ranged from below the detection limit (BDL) 
to 3.90 ± 0.03 ng/L for PCB18 in IGHC samples, the highest single-congener value 
recorded; estimated ΣPCB (sum of congener means) ranged from approximately 0.25 to 
10.0 ng/L across locations (see Sect. 3.3). The degree of chlorination significantly influ -
ences PCB persistence and mobility in aquatic environments. According to Grimm et al. 
[13], PCBs with low chlorination frequently metabolize faster than those with high chlo -
rination, though they may exhibit greater mobility in groundwater systems.
The investigation revealed a variety of PCB congeners, from mono- to octa-chlori -
nated biphenyls, with significant variations between locations (p < 0.05). One of the most 
remarkable findings was the elevated concentration of various PCBs, especially in the 
IGHC samples. PCB6, a dichlorinated biphenyl, varied significantly between locations 
(F = 20.329, p < 0.001), with IGHC having the highest concentration (0.07 ± 0.01 ng/L), 
approximately 3.5 times greater than other locations. PCB31, a trichlorinated biphenyl, 
was considerably higher in both IGHC (3.00 ± 0.10 ng/L) and IRGH (3.39 ± 0.34 ng/L) 
samples, indicating substantial elevation compared to other locations (F = 353.824, 
p < 0.001).

uniformly dominant. This pattern is consistent with the greater environmental mobility 
and aqueous transport of lower-chlorinated congeners, which are more likely to migrate 
through permeable sandy aquifer materials than highly chlorinated congeners [13].

3.4 Human health risk assessment
Tables 7 and 8 present the calculated risk metrics for various PCB congeners, including 
Average Daily Dose (ADD), Chronic Daily Intake (CDI) values for ingestion (CDI_ing) 
and dermal contact (CDI_derm), and the associated Hazard Quotients (HQ) for both 
exposure routes. The CDI values indicate estimated daily intake levels, and the HQ val -
ues represent the potential risk of adverse non-cancer effects. The cumulative Hazard 
Index (HI) reflects the combined risks from all exposure routes.
Tables 7 and 8 present the corrected risk metrics for various PCB congeners. Previous 
calculations contained a unit conversion error where ng/L concentrations were errone -
ously treated as mg/L, leading to massive overestimations of risk. The corrected hazard 
quotient (HQ) values for both adults and children were found to be significantly below 
the threshold of 1.0, with the maximum adult HQ being 0.187 for PCB31 and the maxi -
mum child HQ being 0.005 for PCB31. These results indicate that the non-carcinogenic 
health risks associated with PCB exposure via groundwater ingestion and dermal con -
tact are within acceptable limits. As clarified in Sect. 2.7, the C_i values in Table  7 (e.g., 
0.9853 ng/L for PCB18, 1.534 ng/L for PCB31) are arithmetic mean concentrations at 
each location (Table 5), not 95% UCL values; the Methods have been revised accordingly 
to describe the mean-based exposure-point concentration approach actually used, since 
replicate-level data needed for a rigorous CUCL calculation were not available.
The values in the Tables 7 and 8 highlight the variability in risks among different PCB 
congeners, where some exhibit higher potential risks for non-cancer effects compared 
to others, though all remain safely below the threshold of 1.0. Overall, these results 
underscore the importance of considering both ingestion and dermal contact path -
ways when evaluating the potential health hazards linked to exposure to particular PCB 
contaminants.

While measured PCB concentrations and modelled non-carcinogenic health risks 
were found to be within acceptable regulatory limits, the concurrent occurrence of 
fungal genera exceeding commonly cited but non-regulatory reference values warrants 
further microbiological monitoring and investigation. The fungal isolates, particularly 
genera such as Aspergillus, Fusarium, and Penicillium, that are capable of mycotoxin 
production in some contexts, were detected, though pathogenicity, viability and toxin 
production were not directly tested in this study and mycotoxicosis/opportunistic-infec-
tion risk is not demonstrated. Meanwhile, PCB contamination presents non-carcino -
genic risk through the ingestion and dermal pathways evaluated here; carcinogenic risk 
was not assessed.
It is important to keep three distinct concepts separate when interpreting these 
results. First, measured PCB concentrations remained below the U.S. EPA regulatory 
benchmark of 500 ng/L. Second, modelled non-carcinogenic hazard quotients for indi -
vidual congeners were below 1.0, which represents a separate risk metric with its own 
exposure assumptions. Third, microbiological water quality was assessed qualitatively 
and is not established by either of the PCB-based metrics. None of these three findings 
alone establishes that the water is generally safe or unsafe. Although the U.S. EPA maxi -
mum allowable quantity of 500 ng/L for PCBs in drinking water was not exceeded, the 
presence of any detectable PCBs implies anthropogenic groundwater contamination that 
warrants continuous monitoring. Cumulative non-carcinogenic risk, as represented by 
the Hazard Index in Supplementary Table S4, was consistently higher for children than 
adults across all sampling locations due to greater ingestion rates per unit body weight. 
This cumulative metric provides a more appropriate basis for characterizing overall pop-
ulation risk than single-congener maxima. The spatial heterogeneity in contamination 
patterns, with hotspots such as IGHC showing elevated levels of both fungal and PCB 
contaminants, suggests localized pollution sources that require source identification 
and remediation. The dominance of lower-chlorinated PCB congeners in groundwater is 
consistent with preferential mobility of these compounds through sandy aquifer materi -
als, though this pattern does not independently establish recent anthropogenic inputs.

PCB-containing equipment, improvement of waste disposal infrastructure, and protec -

need for integrated water quality management that considers both biological and chemi-

4 Conclusion and recommendations
4.1 Conclusion
This study provides an integrated occurrence assessment of fungal genera and PCBs 
in coastal groundwater of Ondo State, Nigeria. The findings reveal that measured PCB 
concentrations and associated non-carcinogenic health risks remain within acceptable 
regulatory limits, while the detection of fungal genera — including Aspergillus, Fusar -
ium, and Penicillium — across sampling locations warrants microbiological monitoring 
and further investigation of their potential public-health significance; pathogenicity and 
toxin production were not demonstrated in this study. It is important to note that spe -
cies-level identifications presented are tentative, based on morphological characteristics 
alone. The dominance of lower-chlorinated PCB congeners is consistent with, but does 
not on its own establish, recent anthropogenic inputs such as localized e-waste or trans -
former oil disposal; source apportionment was not performed. A key limitation of this 
study is that sampling was conducted exclusively during the dry season; therefore, the 
findings may not fully represent annual exposure dynamics, as rainfall during the wet 
season could alter contaminant transport through dilution or increased runoff. Further -
more, carcinogenic risk could not be evaluated because the necessary polychlorinated 
biphenyl congeners were not measured, limiting the assessment to non-carcinogenic 
effects. Future investigations should include carcinogenic PCB congeners to provide 
a more comprehensive assessment of potential human health risks. Future research 
should incorporate seasonal monitoring, molecular fungal identification, mycotoxin and 
pathogenicity testing, and bioavailability-adjusted risk assessments. As a precautionary 
measure pending confirmatory microbiological and chemical assessment, point-of-use 
water treatment and continued groundwater quality monitoring are recommended to 
safeguard public health.
4.2 Recommendations
Based on these findings we recommend: (1) implementation of routine monitoring 
programs for both microbial and chemical contaminants in coastal groundwater; (2) 
point-of-use treatment interventions such as chlorination and activated carbon filtra -
tion for households relying on contaminated sources as general public health measures;

industrial activities; and (4) community health education on safe water handling prac -

ability-adjusted risk assessment to refine exposure estimates.
