Abstract
Neuromodulation is a procedure relying on the delivery of specific stimuli to selected sites of the nervous
system and hereby influencing its activity. Even though the underlying neurobiological mechanisms are not
yet fully elucidated and the research is still ongoing, neuromodulation is already being used as an
intervention for various mental conditions and insomnia, or it may even improve the physical performance
of athletes. This narrative review summarizes current evidence on the effects of specific neuromodulation
techniques on performance and sleep quality in athletes, focusing on cranial electrotherapy stimulation
(CES) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS). PubMed, SCOPUS, Web of Science and Ovid
MEDLINE databases were searched for relevant articles. All types of publications were taken into account,
but original studies, randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and meta-analyses were given priority. Current
evidence shows that while tDCS may improve athletic performance, especially endurance and sport-specific
skills, its impact on sleep in athletes is inconclusive. This method may increase subjectively reported total
sleep time and subjective sleep quality, but no objective improvement has been demonstrated. CES findings
are preliminary: evidence from the small trials suggests improvement of selected objective sleep parameters,
but the evidence base is limited to two small studies, one of which lacked a sham condition. In conclusion,
non-invasive neuromodulation methods, specifically tDCS and CES, may impact athletic performance and
help treat sleep disorders in athletes. Neuromodulation should not be viewed as a standalone method, but
rather a complementary tool to other known interventions for insomnia in athletes. Although the
aforementioned techniques show promising results, the research on this topic is limited and requires further
evaluation.

Introduction And Background
Neuromodulation is a targeted modification of nervous system activity through the delivery of a specific
stimulus (e.g., electrical, ultrasonic, optical, magnetic, or even, in preclinical settings, genetic stimulation)
to selected sites within the system 
[1-4]
. It aims to modulate neural activity, restore impaired functions, and
treat certain neuropsychiatric disorders 
[5,6]
. Among neuromodulation techniques, non-invasive physical
methods are of particular interest, including transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), cranial electrotherapy
stimulation (CES), transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), and transcranial alternating current
stimulation (tACS) 
[2-4,6]
. This review focuses on publications on tDCS and CES and their influence on
athletic performance and sleep quality.
Among the commonly used methods of transcranial electrical stimulation, tDCS is the most extensively
investigated modality in the context of sports performance. It is a non-invasive method used for modulation
of cortical excitability by applying 0.5-2 mA direct current. tDCS exerts polarity-dependent effects: anodal
stimulation shifts the resting membrane potential toward the firing threshold, raising the likelihood of
neuronal discharge and thus cortical excitability, whereas cathodal stimulation has the opposite,
excitability-lowering effect 
[7,8]
. During the tDCS procedure, stimulation is delivered with sponge
electrodes placed on the participant’s head: the anode depolarizes neurons and increases excitability of the
cortex, whereas the cathode hyperpolarizes neurons and decreases excitability 
[9,10]
. However, the
neurobiological mechanisms are not yet fully understood, despite its rapidly increasing clinical uptake and
ongoing scientific research 
[11]
. One of the non-pharmacological methods used as a helpful device for

Another neuromodulation method being investigated that may affect sleep quality and sports performance
in athletes is CES. In contrast to tDCS, which relies on direct current, CES uses high-frequency pulse trains.
CES is a non-invasive technique that modulates central nervous system activity and is considered a safe and
accessible tool for treating mental disorders 
[15]
. CES devices deliver low-intensity pulsed currents, typically
in the microampere range rarely exceeding 4 mA, thus posing a low risk of adverse effects 
[16-18]
. During the
procedure, electrodes are typically clipped to the earlobes or placed over the mastoid processes, the temples,
or the supraorbital region 
[15,19]
. The current transmitted through the electrodes penetrates soft tissue and
bone to certain extent, reaching the cerebral cortex and subcortical structures, affecting local blood flow and
the electrical activity of the brain 
[19,20]
. Despite the long record of CES utility in psychiatry, its mechanism
of action has not been fully elucidated. Various explanations of the influence on the central nervous system
have been proposed, including alteration of neurotransmitter release, modification of cell membrane
potential, cell excitability, and neuromodulation of subcortical structures 
[21-23]
. The
electroencephalography studies indicate the effect of CES on the electrical activity of the brain, including an
increase in alpha-band power, which is associated with cognitive functions and memory 
[21,24]
. Meta-
analytic data suggest that CES may reduce depressive and anxiety symptoms 
[25]
; however, a critical review
has emphasized substantial methodological limitations of the underlying trials, so this connection is not
robust as lacking conclusive evidence 
[21]
. Furthermore, several studies have shown that CES may reduce
sleep latency, increase sleep duration, improve sleep quality, and reduce subjective daytime drowsiness
[15,18,26]
. Although the efficacy of CES for insomnia itself remains unconfirmed, it improved mood and
subjective well-being in individuals with subclinical insomnia 
[27]
. Insomnia and poor sleep are common in
athletes; pre-competition stress and anxiety impair sleep, and insufficient sleep may in turn compromise
mood and performance 
[28]
.
Review
Materials and methods
This narrative review compiles the current knowledge on the role of neuromodulation in sports performance
and sleep disorders. The narrative form was chosen in order to integrate data related to the mechanisms of
action of neuromodulation with clinical data. The neuromodulation modalities considered were tDCS and
CES. Four researchers were delegated to conduct the research of databases. Each database was searched by
all four researchers, who strictly followed the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The databases were searched
in June 2026 by title and abstract using search strings in which the keywords were combined with the
Boolean operators AND and OR: "cranial electrotherapy stimulation", "transcranial neurostimulation",
"transcranial direct current stimulation", "sleep", "insomnia", "athletes", "sports". No date restrictions were
applied, in order to maximize the amount of literature included. All types of publications were taken into
account, but original studies, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), systematic reviews and meta-
analyses were prioritized. The inclusion criteria were adult humans and studies of athletes assessing
physical performance or sleep parameters and studies of tDCS/CES for insomnia or sleep disturbance in
general adult populations, which were used solely for the sleep section. The exclusion criteria were
paediatric populations and animal studies. As a result of this targeted search, 781 records were obtained
from PubMed (n = 145), SCOPUS (n = 180), Web of Science (n = 256) and Ovid MEDLINE (n = 200). Title and
abstract screening yielded all articles of which 72 articles qualified for full-text assessment. Twenty-nine
articles met inclusion criteria during full-text assessment. Publications that were citation-tracked or were
relevant in terms of a significant contribution of knowledge to the given subject, key findings, and
mechanisms allowing an understanding of the issues addressed in this narrative review were also added
manually (n = 10). Articles identified as duplicates from different database searches were removed.
Ultimately, 39 publications were selected and synthesized, taking into account the most important
mechanisms and the clinical and epidemiological data. The search strategy, including databases, search
algorithm, filters and limits, and retrieved record number, is presented in Table

tDCS in athletic performance
The Specificity Model: Primary Motor Cortex (M1) vs Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex (DLPFC)
A comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying tDCS, its potential benefits, and its optimal
application requires consideration of the methodological specificity of this form of stimulation. The effects
of tDCS appear to depend substantially on the selected stimulation site, making the mapping of stimulation
targets to specific performance outcomes a central issue 
[29,30]
. Protocols targeting the dorsolateral
prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) were more strongly associated with perceived recovery, cognitive performance,
and sport-specific skill execution, whereas stimulation of the primary motor cortex (M1) was more
frequently linked to motor adaptations 
[29]
. In addition, electrode montage and the current return pathway
also appear to be important determinants of the stimulation outcome. The most pronounced benefits were
observed when electrode placement and stimulation timing were aligned with the presumed neural
substrates underlying the target function. In contrast, nonspecific or poorly matched protocols often yielded
null effects and, in some cases, undesirable behavioural consequences. Together with an appropriate
stimulation protocol, these factors may constitute prerequisites for achieving the maximal benefit from tDCS
[29]
. A potential explanation for this principle, as well as for the observation that athletes derive greater
benefits from stimulation than non-athletes, is hypothesized to be the notion that tDCS strengthens
existing, well-established neural networks rather than creating new neuronal connections 
[30,31]
. In some
soccer-specific studies, tDCS protocols administered 12-48 hours after a match were associated with lower
perceived fatigue and improvements in selected subjective recovery outcomes, including reduced muscle
soreness, better perceived recovery, and improved sleep quality. Although a single tDCS session generally
produced inconsistent effects on strength and endurance 
[29,31]
, individual studies have reported acute
improvements in explosive performance following M1 stimulation 
[30]
. By contrast, repeated M1
stimulation administered concurrently with training appeared to exert beneficial effects on functional
performance, as reflected by improvements in vertical jump height and sprint performance 
[29]
.
Furthermore, combining tDCS with high-intensity interval training (HIIT) was associated with enhanced
aerobic capacity, including increases in maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max) 
[29]
.
Pooled Evidence on Physical Performance and Application of tDCS in Specific Sport Disciplines
Based on recent meta-analyses, the current evidence supporting the benefits of tDCS should be considered
moderate, and the effect of this stimulation method appears to be statistically significant. Jiang et al.
reported a statistically significant, moderate pooled effect of acute anodal tDCS on sport-specific
performance (SMD = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.23-0.54), with significant subgroup effects for endurance, primarily
reflected by longer time to exhaustion, and competitive-collaborative skill tasks but not for strength or
precision-skill outcomes 
[32]
. The stimulation applied in the analysed studies was exclusively tDCS and, in
most cases, targeted mainly the primary motor cortex (M1), prefrontal cortex (PFC), temporal cortex (TC)
and cerebellum (CB); statistically significant pooled effects were observed only for M1 and PFC stimulation,
although differences between the stimulation-site subgroups were not significant 
[32]
.
A broader meta-analysis conducted by Liu et al. included 40 studies with a total of 778 participants (both
athletes and non-athletes) 
[31]
. The analysis demonstrated a statistically significant, although small overall
effect of tDCS in both groups. The greatest benefits were shown in terms of improvements in muscular and
cardiopulmonary endurance, as well as static balance. The magnitude of improvement was, however, greater
in athletes than in non-athletes. The authors themselves note that this result is inconsistent with their
initial assumption of a ceiling effect in athletes, given that individuals with superior motor skills are thought
to possess more efficient cortical functioning and require lower levels of cortical activation 
[31]
. In contrast,
other authors have proposed that tDCS may exert greater effects in athletes because it primarily strengthens
pre-existing neural networks, presumed to be more highly developed in trained individuals, through long-
term synaptic potentiation 
[30,33]
. However, these mechanisms have not been directly demonstrated, and
apparent differences between athletes and non-athletes may also reflect variation in tasks, stimulation
protocols, baseline performance, and study design 
[30,31,33,34]
.
The current evidence suggests that the effects of tDCS in athletes may be sport-specific and largely depend
on the cognitive and physiological demands characteristic of a given discipline. Although the reported
effects are generally modest and heterogeneous, the available studies suggest that tDCS may enhance
selected aspects of performance when appropriately matched to the target function and training context
[8,34]
. In sports requiring a high degree of cognitive control and psychological resilience, such as collegiate
volleyball, repeated stimulation of DLPFC was associated with reduced pre-competition anxiety and
improvements in selected cognitive functions 
[35]
. In another review synthesizing evidence in basketball
players stimulated with tDCS in M1 region, psychological fatigue and cognitive outcomes remained limited
and inconsistent; however, M1 stimulation was associated with improvements in motor performance, such

Conclusion and Practical Implications
The effects of tDCS are highly dependent on the interaction between stimulation parameters, the
characteristics of the athlete, and the specific motor or cognitive demands of the task. Whether the reported
changes translate into meaningful competitive advantages remains to be demonstrated. Because active tDCS
produces perceptible cutaneous sensations, blinding integrity is a recurrent concern in sham-controlled
trials, and positive findings in small sports-science samples may be inflated by publication bias; both issues
warrant caution when interpreting the evidence summarized here. Therefore, tDCS should be considered a
complementary and highly individualized tool rather than a universal method for enhancing sports
performance 
[8,29,31]
.

Cranial Electrotherapy Stimulation (CES)
CES is used in mental disorders 
[15]
, and studies suggest it may alleviate anxiety and depressive symptoms
[25]

[21,24]

conducted a pilot study of 25 professional athletes with poor sleep quality (12 receiving CES for 30 minutes

[38]

actigraphy-measured wake after sleep onset and increased objective sleep efficiency, whereas total sleep

efficiency did not improve above baseline 
[22]
. The authors of these studies emphasized the need for further
research of CES interventions on sleep quality in athletes.
Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation (tDCS)
Apart from its use as a non-pharmacological treatment in psychiatry 
[14]
, tDCS may improve motor learning,
motor performance, working memory, attention and emotional regulation in athletes 
[8]
. In addition, tDCS
may have an impact on subjective total sleep time. Charest et al. conducted a randomized, sham-controlled
study of the effect of tDCS on the sleep quality of student-athletes 
[9]
. They recruited 30 participants (15
males, 15 females) with PSQI > 5 and Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) > 7, and randomized them into
experimental and control groups (15 athletes each). The experimental protocol consisted of five nights. One
adaptation night was followed by two nights of baseline polysomnography. During two final nights, bifrontal
tDCS (2mA, 20 min, polarity and electrode montage as specified in the source) targeting the "top-down"
pathway of sleep regulation was delivered 90 minutes before bedtime. The control group received sham
stimulation. Psychomotor Vigilance Test (PVT) was administered pre- and post-intervention to assess the
impact of tDCS on cognitive performance. Mean reaction time in PVT decreased after the stimulation, but
there was no significant difference between experimental and control groups. Sleep-diary data indicated a
marginal increase in subjective total sleep time, whereas polysomnographic analyses showed no significant
objective improvement after active tDCS. In the tDCS group, PSQI, ISI and Athlete Sleep Screening
Questionnaire (ASSQ) results showed statistically significant improvement post-intervention when
compared with the baseline values. However, only PSQI and ASSQ post-treatment scores were significantly
better than in the sham stimulation group 
[9]
. Odds ratio product (ORP)-based sleep-depth outcomes,
calculated from polysomnographic data, failed to show any significant benefit of tDCS. Interestingly, men in
the experimental group increased their time spent at ORP values of 0-1 by 41.57 minutes, while women
decreased by 11.03 minutes on average. Time spent in lower ORP index (0-1) is related to better sleep quality
[10]
. In conclusion, tDCS may increase total sleep time and improve subjective sleep quality, but no objective
effect was observed. Further research is required to verify if tDCS is beneficial for athletes with sleep
disorders, but current evidence is not supportive enough to recommend it in this context.
Conclusions
Non-invasive neuromodulation techniques, in particular tDCS and CES, seem to be a promising option for
sports medicine specialists. Both techniques are generally well tolerated; the most common adverse events
of tDCS are transient tingling, itching, skin redness and headache, with rare skin burns under the electrodes,
and low-intensity CES has a similarly benign profile. Nevertheless, safety data in athletic populations are
scarce and should be collected systematically. These techniques may influence both athletes’ performance
and crucial recovery processes like sleep architecture. Neuromodulation effects are target-specific and can
differ in various sport disciplines. The effect of tDCS depends on the targeted stimulation site. Different
electrode localizations have been associated with, among others, better motor learning and performance,
attention, and emotional regulation. Preliminary evidence suggests that tDCS may be used as a supportive
tool for reducing pre-competition anxiety. Its protocols also target different areas of athletes’ performance.
Protocols targeting M1 appear to be most consistently associated with improvements in motor function,
such as improved shooting accuracy, dribbling time, or repeated sprints. In contrast, stimulation of DLPFC
was linked to potential reduction of anxiety and better selected cognitive functions. CES and tDCS may have
a positive effect on athletes with sleep disorders. CES may positively affect some objective sleep parameters
and may reduce awake time before falling asleep, but the effects observed to date are moderate. This method
may also reduce sleep deterioration observed during the pre-competition period in athletes. The effects of
tDCS on sleep in athletes remain understudied; to date, a single randomized trial has shown improvement
of total sleep time and subjective sleep quality (measured by PSQI, ISI and ASSQ questionnaires), but no
objective benefits were observed in conventional polysomnographic data or ORP. Neuromodulation
techniques should not be treated as an autonomous solution for athletes’ problems. Rather, they should be
an addition to other known methods like CBT-I for managing insomnia in athletes. Although the
neuromodulation techniques appear to be promising approaches in athletic performance enhancement and
sport-related sleep disturbances, further research in this field is necessary. Our knowledge of
neuromodulation in athletes is limited by small sample sizes or heterogeneity of the athlete populations.
Further research is needed to establish neuromodulation as a safe and ethical tool. The integration of
aforementioned neuromodulation techniques into athletic training raises questions about the competitive
fairness and equal opportunity. The issues of unequal access to the necessary equipment, patented
stimulation protocols and specialized experts should be discussed thoroughly. Another concern lies in the
lack of detectable physiological markers or biological trace in blood or urine of athletes which makes it
impossible to detect. To this end, reliable double-blind randomized controlled trials with standardized
parameters of neuromodulation techniques are needed.
