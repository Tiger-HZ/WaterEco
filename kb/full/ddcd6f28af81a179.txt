Abstract
While there has been accumulating evidence of the association between long-term exposure to air pollution and 
various health endpoints, most epidemiological studies relied on ambient air pollution which showed substantial 
deviation from indoor. This difference could lead to biased or imprecise health effect estimates, particularly for 
susceptible populations who spend most time indoors. Although a few studies focused on indoor air pollution, 
most monitoring was limited to one-time and/or relatively short-term periods. We aimed to provide an overview 
of an indoor monitoring campaign that was designed to assess long-term exposure to residential indoor PM 2.5 
for epidemiology and applied to 130 older adults with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis in the Seoul Metropolitan 
Area, South Korea, for 2021–2023. Specifically, our design focused on (1) representation of long-term exposure, (2) 
identification of pollution sources, (3) assessment of infiltration, and (4) implementation of non-contact monitoring. 
Using RTI MicroPEMs, we measured concentrations of indoor PM 2.5 and chemical components for five days 
including weekdays and weekends in four seasons as well as household information. We also collected matched 
outdoor samples to assess infiltration, and applied non-contact monitoring using postal mails for repeat visits. Our 
preliminary analyses of indoor hourly PM 2.5 measurements (mean: 14.90 µg/m 3 [standard deviation: 36.12]) showed 
temporal patterns with higher concentrations in winter (20.17 [50.54]) than in summer (11.19 [32.76]) and during 
the daytime than nighttime. Indoor concentrations were lower than outdoor (20.44 [SD: 61.63]) with consistent 
seasonal patterns but contrasted hourly patterns. Thirty-six chemical components of PM 2.5 showed mostly similar 
temporal patterns to those of PM 2.5 with lower concentrations and smaller differences between indoors and 
outdoors in summer that in other seasons. The infiltration factor characterized by median indoor to outdoor 
ratios of sulfur, nickel, and iron were 51–70%. Average PM 2.5 concentrations tended to be high at households

 Introduction
While there has been accumulating evidence of the 
association between long-term exposure to air pollu -
tion including fine particulate matter (PM 2.5) and human 
health, one of the key limitations was indicated as the 
exposure assessment relying on ambient air pollution [38, 
40]. Many epidemiological studies of long-term air pollu -
tion used ambient measurements mostly obtained from 
fixed monitoring sites without considering the level of 
exposure within domestic environments. Air pollution 
monitoring to assess indoor air quality across partici -
pants’ homes has been sparse or limited to small sample 
sizes because of high compliance requirement of partici -
pants and relatively large burden of technicians [ 29, 30]. 
However, there was a good understanding of significant 
deviation of indoor air pollution from outdoor, since 
indoor air quality is affected by indoor microenviron -
ments which are different from outdoor sources [ 3, 26, 
33, 39]. Furthermore, as people spend 80–90% of their 
time indoors [ 19], the exposure assessment solely based 
on ambient measurements could result in substantial 
exposure measurement error. These differences may sub-
sequently affect health effect analyses.
The impact of the difference in indoor exposure from 
outdoor on health analyses could be large for susceptible 
populations who stay longer indoors mostly at homes 
than the general population. Individuals with chronic 
lung diseases spent more time at homes than those with -
out diseases in the same age groups and older individuals 
spent an ample majority of time indoors mostly at their 
homes [ 19, 22]. Large health effects of long-term ambi -
ent air pollution found in such populations could be 
even larger, when replaced with indoor air pollution in 
their domestic dwellings. However, most epidemiological 
studies of indoor air pollution employed short-term mea-
surements and investigated temporary changes in lung 
function or disease status within a few days, weeks, or 
months [29]. Indoor air pollution assessed over extended 
periods can elucidate the role of air pollution in dis -
ease progression or health deterioration of susceptible 
populations.
The design of indoor monitoring is crucial to assess and 
characterize individual long-term exposure to indoor air 
pollution at people’s residences, when the ultimate goal 
is the assessment of the health effect. Previous indoor 
monitoring of air pollution included several limitations 
in their designs to be overcome to achieve the goal. 
Indoor monitoring campaigns often collected samples 
for limited hours, days, and seasons, possibly because 
of financial and/or logistical constraints, which may not 
represent the collective exposures over a long period. 
Although household information questionnaires are 
commonly applied to identify potential pollution sources 
such as cooking, cleaning, and housing conditions, a 
few characteristics successfully explained the pattern of 
indoor air pollution across households [ 33]. For PM 2.5, 
its chemical components related to specific sources can 
be a powerful resource to help the identification [ 1]. 
However, handy and affordable portable samplers fre -
quently deployed to indoor monitoring mostly provide 
nephelometer measures of PM 2.5 which do not allow the 
quantification of chemical components, as opposed to 
gravimetric measures. Another difficulty to assess indoor 
PM2.5 particularly in highly populated urban areas is the 
accurate quantification of infiltrated PM 2.5. Since indoor 
PM2.5 is primarily originated from outdoor PM 2.5, the 
amount of indoor PM 2.5 infiltrated from outdoors needs 
to be distinguished from those emitted indoors [ 39]. In 
densely populated urban areas where people tend to live 
in domestic buildings, many studies located their sam -
plers in gardens of buildings or indoor terraces of house -
holds where the outdoor measurements could be less 
comparable to corresponding indoor measurements [ 26, 
49]. Lastly, indoor monitoring designs should adopt an 
approach to reduce visits to participants’ homes which 
is one of the major barriers to participate in residential 
monitoring particularly when there are severe public 
health concerns such as COVID-19.
Leveraging a well-established cohort of participants 
aged mostly over 65 years and with idiopathic pulmo -
nary fibrosis (IPF) in the Seoul Metropolitan Area, South 
Korea, we aimed to provide the overview of a monitoring 
campaign designed to assess the long-term exposure to 
indoor PM 2.5 in a susceptible population for epidemio -
logical application. Specifically, the design focused on the 
data collection to allow (1) representation of long-term 
exposure, (2) identification of potential pollution sources, 
(3) assessment of infiltration, and (4) implementation of 
non-contact sampling. These four focuses will help future 
studies accurately quantify individual exposures com -
bined by indoor-originated and infiltrated PM 2.5 over an 
extended period for subsequent health analyses. In this 
paper, we introduced the key features of our monitoring 
designs with respect to the four focuses and provided 
early results for PM 2.5 measurements and household 
characteristics. Although this study was based on PM 2.5 
and participants with IPF, our indoor monitoring design 
can be applicable to future indoor monitoring studies 
and smokers (16.76 vs. 11.79). Our indoor monitoring approach provides a practical guidance to future indoor 
monitoring studies that intend to assess the health effect of PM 2.5.

Overview of indoor monitoring campaign
Study population
Our study population included 130 people who are a 
part of an idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis cohort, lived in 
the Seoul Metropolitan Area (SMA), visited one of the 
12 tertiary hospitals in the SMA between 2021 and 2023 
(Fig. 1; Table 1). The SMA is the fourth largest metro -
politan area worldwide and consists of Seoul, Incheon, 
and Gyeonggi Province where 26 million population 
resides in 12,685 km 2 in 2020. Ambient PM 2.5 concen -
trations in the SMA shows a strong seasonal variation 
with higher annual average concentrations (21 µg/m 3 in 
2020) compared to the 12 µg/m 3 of the air quality stan -
dard proposed by the World Health Organization. IPF 
is a progressive lung disorder by scarring of the lungs 
from unknown causes, mostly prevalent in older males, 
with poor prognosis and an increasing pattern of inci -
dence [ 21]. Given few identified risk factors other than 
smoking, recent studies of patients with IPF suggested 
air pollution as a potential risk factor of disease progres -
sion and mortality [ 4, 18, 31, 35, 44, 47] (Table S1). Air 
pollution could exacerbate disease progression through 
various biological mechanisms including oxidative stress, 
telomere, and inflammation [ 36]. In the IPF cohort, par -
ticipants visited hospitals every three or six months for 
monitoring their disease status and carried out medical 
examinations including pulmonary tests. These regular 
visits provide the unique opportunity to investigate the 
association of short-term as well as long-term exposure 
to air pollution with disease prognosis when we monitor 
the level of air pollution. The details of the study partici -
pants and their health data collection are provided else -
where [48]. The study participants were 87% of males and 
the average age was 69.6 years with 93% of people greater 
than 60 years (Table 1). This study was approved by the 
Institutional Review Board of each site, including Asan 
Medical Center, Seoul, Republic of Korea (2021-0072).
IPF indoor monitoring campaign
We designed the IPF indoor monitoring campaign to 
measure PM 2.5 concentrations within 130 homes of 130 
participants for consecutive five days in four seasons 
from July, 2021 through August, 2023 with a subset also 
collecting outdoor PM 2.5 measurements. The field team 
visited participants whose hospital visits were sched -
uled within the maximum four weeks and installed RTI 
MicroPEMs (RTI International, Research Triangle Park, 
NC, U.S.A.) in rooms where participants spend most of 
their time such as a living room or bedroom (Table S2, 
Figure S1). MicroPEMs operated continuously for five 
days at 0.4 L per minute and cycled 30 s on and 30 s off. 
The MicroPEM is a lightweight (~ 230 g) and quiet (3 dB 
above office background at 1 m) portable real-time PM 
sensor, which was widely applied in previous studies of 
indoor PM 2.5 monitoring [ 9, 37]. Apart from most por -
table PM samples that provide optical measurements of 
PM2.5, MicroPEMs concurrently collect filter samples 
using PTFE filters that provide gravimetric measure -
ments, in addition to real-time optical measurements, 
and allow the quantification of PM 2.5 chemical compo -
nents. The gravimetric concentration was used to adjust 
the real-time PM mass concentration for improved accu -
racy [ 6]. We multiplied each real-time measurement by 
a correction factor, which is the ratio of the gravimetric-
based PM 2.5 concentration to the average value of all 
real-time concentrations. The adjusted real-time con -
centrations were averaged to a 1-minute scale for further 
analyses. The filter sample was subsequently analyzed for 
black carbon (BC), environmental tobacco smoke (ETS), 
and brown carbon (BrC) by 6-wavelength integrating 
sphere and 33 inorganic elements by X-ray fluorescence 
(XRF) at RTI international laboratories [ 20, 46]. The 
details of quantification and quality control procedure 
of PM 2.5, carbon species, and inorganic elements are 
provided in supplemental materials (See “S1. Details of 
quantification and quality control procedure of PM2.5 and 
of PM2.5 chemical components”). For the measurements

Key features of indoor monitoring design
Representation of long-term exposure
To assess long-term exposure and annual-average PM 2.5 
concentration, we designed the IPF indoor monitor -
ing campaign to collect continuous samples on different 
days and seasons. As shown in Table S3, previous studies 
that applied extensive indoor monitoring campaigns for 
epidemiological application were often limited to selec -
tive days and/or seasons. We carried out a five-day sam -
pling for each of four seasons, including weekdays as well 
as weekends from Friday through Wednesday (Figure 
S2). The four seasons of spring, summer, fall, and winter 
were defined as March to May, June to August, Septem -
ber to November, and December to February (average 
temperature for 2021–2023: 12.8, 24.2, 14.9, and 0.3  °C, 
respectively). Before the visit to participants’ homes on 
Friday for installation of MicroPEMs, our preparation 
began from Monday of the week. We made phone calls to 
each participant in order to notify our visit and confirm 
the visit time, prepared MicroPEMs including instal -
lation of filters and correction of flow rate, and packed 
all sampling items in the lab. One technician visited up 
to 5–6 participants within a day, given long driving dis -
tances between participant’s homes with the maximum 
two driving hours (Table S2, Figure S4). We visited up to 
11 participants a day depending on the recruitment of 
participants, the location of participants’ homes, and the 
availability of technicians. Four repeated weekly-based 
sampling across four seasons for each of the 130 partici -
pants required more than two years of monitoring.
Implementation of non-contact sampling
Given that study participants are less inclined to accept 
repetitive visits, we received the sensors via postal mails. 
In particular, since our IPF monitoring campaign began 
in July 2021 in the middle of the COVID-19 pandemic, 
we developed a design that minimizes physical interac -
tions. For successful implementation particularly for 
older adults, we provided the participants with detailed 
written instruction and follow-up phone calls (Figure S3). 
During the two days before the ending of the sampling 
on Wednesday, we made reservation for the shipping 
service and made phone calls to help participants pack 
devices for the return shipping (Figure S2). We also pro -
vided packaging materials such as mailing boxes, packing 
tapes, and bubble wraps. Starting from the third visit, we 
switched completely to non-contact monitoring by mail -
ing to participants’ homes for installation in addition to 
participants’ mails to return PM sensors.
Identification of pollution sources
PM2.5 chemical components and household information 
questionnaire were used to identify potential sources of 
indoor air pollution. We constructed household informa-
tion questionnaire composed of more than 100 questions 
based on previous studies of indoor monitoring [ 8, 11, 
12, 28, 43]. The questions consisted of demographic fea -
tures, housing conditions, behavioral characteristics, and 
indoor and outdoor environments (Table S3). For hous -
ing conditions, each participant provided the constructed 
year of a dwelling building, the size, floor level, and 
dividers of a residential dwelling, materials for walls and 
floors, and condensation or mold. Behavioral characteris-
tics included heating, cleaning, smoking, and pet. Ques -
tions for Indoor and outdoor environments were related 
to ventilation, air cleaner, humidifier, and proximity to 
roads. We particularly intended to obtain the specific 
information on the characteristics that derived the PM 
changes in households of middle and high income coun -
tries: solid fuel burning, cooking, candle and incense, 
cleaning, and humidifier, in addition to smoking [ 42]. 
For example, the questions included the frequency, dura -
tion, and application type of cooking. Moreover, PM 2.5 
chemical components, considered as surrogates of spe -
cific pollution sources, can help confirm major sources 
such as outdoor traffic, cooking, and cleaning. Although 
there were limited attempts that included PM2.5 chemical 
components to indoor monitoring, a study in older adults 
with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) 
showed indoor BC, considered as a marker of traffic, 
was associated with larger decline in lung function than 
indoor PM2.5, suggesting the need of further investigation 
of indoor chemical components [14].
Assessment of infiltration
We selected 20% of participants living at the 5th floor or 
lower to obtain matched outdoor measurements in order 
to assess infiltrated PM 2.5 from outdoors. The matched 
outdoor and indoor measurements for the same partici -
pant can provide the indoor-outdoor (I/O) ratio, as the 
infiltration rate, for a specific PM 2.5 chemical component 
that is considered to be primarily of ambient origins with 
few indoor sources. For matched outdoor sampling, we 
placed MicroPEMs outside participants’ homes with 
sampling devices hung on balcony bars or window bars, 
as opposed to gardens or within closed balconies often 
used in previous studies. This design allowed us to mea -
sure outdoor PM 2.5 to be comparable with indoor PM 2.5 
at the same floor level as participants live. For the PM 2.5 
chemical components to assess the infiltrated PM 2.5, we

Data exploration
For the two years of monitoring period from July, 2021 
through August, 2023 including nine 3-month peri -
ods that represent different seasons, we collected 579 
PM2.5 filter samples (62,130 hourly measurements) from 
130 participants. These samples were composed of 448 
indoor samples from all 130 participants, 105 outdoor 
samples from 35 (27%) participants, and 26 duplicated 
samples from 25 (19%) participants (Table S5). In indoor 
monitoring for all 130 people, 95 participants (73%) com-
pleted all four visits. We excluded invalid samples result -
ing from instrument errors such as early stop and pump 
failure, and human errors such as filter damage, sample 
switch, lost real-time data file, and incorrect metadata 
(e.g. filter identification number).

summer-winter, and fall-winter) comparable to those

one high- and one low-concentration days to represent

the nighttime. In particular, morning and evening hours 
(7–10 a.m. and 6–8 p.m.) including cooking and clean -

indoor PM2.5 concentrations were more variable between 
households than within a household (Figure S6). Out -

PM2.5 chemical components
Carbonaceous components of 460 samples showed rela -
tively higher mean concentrations (0.57 [SD: 0.33], 1.27 
[0.84], and 2.01 [4.88] µg/m 3 for BC, BrC, and ETS, 
respectively) than 33 inorganic elements where sulfur 
gave the highest concentration (0.89 [0.72] µg/m 3) (Table 
S6, Figure S7). Across four seasons, all PM2.5 components 
generally showed similar patterns with lower concentra -
tions in the summer than the other seasons (Figure S9). 
Carbon and inorganic species comprised 50% of PM 2.5 
mass on average with some seasonal differences (48, 
34, 60, and 57% on average for the spring, summer, fall, 
and winter, respectively). When we compared matching 
indoor and outdoor measurements of 35 participants, 
most PM 2.5 chemical components showed higher con -
centrations in outdoors than indoors with larger differ -
ences in the winter than the other seasons (Table S6). 
The median I/O ratios of sulfur, nickel, and iron which 
are commonly used for assessing infiltration factor were 
ranged between 0.51 and 0.70, indicating 51–70% of infil-
trated PM2.5 from outdoor air depending on the elemen -
tal tracer (Table S7).
Household information characteristics
About 85% of 130 participants spent more than 15  h at 
home and almost all participants performed regular 
ventilation by opening windows (98%) (Table  2). Three 
fourth of the participants used air cleaners (74%), while 
one third had humidifiers (33%). Most participants cook 
their meals at homes (93%) with cooking fuel of natu -
ral gas (92%). The most common heating fuel was also 
natural gas (69%). One fifth of participants had pets 
(17%). About 5% of participants smoked or had smok -
ers in family members. Average PM 2.5 concentrations 
were higher at 130 households with cooking activities 
(15.14 vs. 10.96  µg/m 3), humidifiers (19.19 vs. 13.16), 
pets (24.09 vs. 13.35), and smokers (16.76 vs. 11.79) than 
those without (Table  1). Although PM 2.5 concentrations 
were similar between the households with and without 
air cleaners (15.29 and 14.85, respectively), those having 
multiple air cleaners tended to show lower concentra -
tions (12.56) compared to those having no or one cleaner 
(15.17 and 16.41, respectively). Households having venti -
lation behaviors using both open windows and machines 
showed lower PM 2.5 (13.39) compared to those using 
open window only (15.53). These patterns were generally 
similar to those in 95 households that provided complete 
measurements across four seasons.
Discussion
This paper illustrated our intention to design a moni -
toring campaign with the specific interest in assess -
ing the long-term residential exposure to indoor PM 2.5 
and identifying factors that influence indoor air quality 
for investigating the health effect. This targeted inter -
est shaped our indoor monitoring campaign to extend 
temporal coverage to represent long-term exposure and 
to identify sources of indoor PM 2.5 composed of indoor 
and ambient origins. Specifically, our monitoring design 
included different hours, days, and seasons that will allow 
us to compute annual-average indoor PM 2.5 concentra -
tions representative to each individual. Including PM 2.5 
chemical components, home questionnaires, and match -
ing outdoor-to-indoor PM2.5 monitoring will help distin -
guish between indoor-generated and infiltrated PM 2.5, 
and characterize total indoor PM2.5.
Our study is aligned with the emerging research inter -
est in the health effect of long-term exposure to indoor 
air pollution. Given the extended time spent indoors and 
the reported difference in indoor air pollution from out -
door, there is a growing interest in studying the health 
effect of indoor air pollution. However, most of these 
studies examined relatively short-term changes over a 
day or a week in indoor air pollution. A study in Mas -
sachusetts, U.S. reported that increased weekly average 
concentrations of indoor PM 2.5 and BC were associated 
with decreased lung function on the following week in 
125 COPD patients with stronger associations than those 
of outdoor concentrations [14]. Another U.S. study for 44 
children also showed the association of indoor PM 2.5 for 
the previous 48 h with lung function decline but no asso -
ciation of outdoor [16]. Building upon the evidence of the 
association of long-term exposure to air pollution with 
disease development and progression relying on ambient 
air pollution, recent studies attempted to look at the same 
association by replacing with indoor air pollution. How -
ever, their monitoring was often based on limited tempo-
ral coverage such as a single day across two seasons or 
each month [ 11, 28]. Recently, the Subpopulations and 
Intermediate Outcome Measures in COPD and AIR (SPI-
ROMICS AIR) study applied an extended indoor moni -
toring period over two weeks but limited to two or three 
seasons, instead of all four, for each of 216 adults from 12 
clinical centers in the continental U.S. Hansel et al., [ 12]. 
Furthermore, based on the indoor monitoring data along 
with their household information questionnaire and infil-
tration rate obtained from another study, the SPIROM -
CIS AIR developed a prediction model (cross-validation 
R2 of 0.45) to estimate indoor PM 2.5 concentrations for

more than two thousands participants who did not have 
indoor PM 2.5 measurements and investigated the asso -
ciation with lung function [ 13, 51]. More studies need to 
help elucidate the role of exposure to indoor air pollution 
for extended periods in disease progression and diverse 
populations and environments.
Our well-designed monitoring can contribute to our 
future work where we can address longstanding ques -
tions related to indoor air pollution assessment. First, we 
plan to investigate cost-effective monitoring designs that 
contain reduced hours, days, or seasons and provide the 
long-term exposure estimates comparable to those of the 
complete design including the extended temporal cover -
age as our study. Our suggestion of cost-effective moni -
toring designs can minimize efforts and resources with 
the maximized data quality. Second, we will identify the 
most responsible indoor sources. In the present study, we 
found little differences in indoor PM 2.5 concentrations 
between households according to their air cleaner usage 
and cooking activities as opposed to previous findings 
in high-income countries [ 42]. Since these little differ -
ences could be due to the impact of other individual and/
or household characteristics, we will apply regression 
approaches to investigate the association with potential 
indoor sources after accounting for such characteris -
tics. PM 2.5 chemical components will also help improve 
our understanding of potential sources, when they are 
applied to source apportionment approaches [ 7, 15]. 
Third, we will quantify infiltrated PM 2.5 relying on the 
ratios of a specific PM 2.5 component which best satisfies 
the criteria after the investigation of candidate compo -
nents used in previous studies. Fourth, given infiltration 
and indoor sources as well as our previously-validated 
prediction models which allow us to estimate outdoor 
concentrations at people’s homes, we will develop a pre -
diction model to estimate individual indoor concentra -
tions of PM2.5 for people without indoor measurements.
In addition to the four focuses intended in our moni -
toring designs, this study has some additional design 
suggestions to future studies of indoor air pollution 
monitoring. Although our study was dedicated to PM 2.5 
and patients with IPF, our monitoring design targeting 
long-term exposure assessment for health analyses can 
be easily extended to other susceptible populations such 
as asthmatic children and cancer patients as well as the 
healthy population. In addition, we did not apply the 
emerging monitoring approach using low cost sensors 
with online data transfer service [ 27, 32]. Although these 
devices are powerful tools to obtain data without individ-
ual visits leading to the substantial reduction in logisti -
cal efforts, internet service was frequently not accessible 
or available in elderly households. Future studies should 
consider unique features of monitoring environments 
related to population characteristics in their choices for 
indoor monitoring.
In summary, recent increasing interests in the health 
effect of individual long-term exposure to indoor air pol -
lution require active data collection in order to overcome 
the lack of monitoring data. However, the logistical and 
financial difficulties in indoor monitoring seek effec -
tive solutions in designs that help achieve data accuracy, 
understand the nature of exposure, and minimize the 
burdens of participants and technicians. Our practical 
guidance can provide such solutions and allow further 
epidemiological investigation.
