 A B S T R A C T 
This study examines the impact of energy consumption on the environment for ten newly 
industrialized countries through panel data methods using the data covering the period 
1970-2022. Due to the presence of cross -section dependence in the panel data, the CIPS 
unit root test is performed. The cointegration test shows the existence of a long -run 
relationship between energy consumption and ecological footprint. Long -run 
heterogeneous panel elasticities are estimated through the Common Correlated Effects 
Mean Group (CCEMG) estimator and the Augmented Mean Group (AMG) estimator. 
Empirical results reveal that energy consumption increases environmental pollution in all 
examined countries, with statistically significant effects observed for Brazil, China, India, 
Indonesia, Malaysia, Mexico, the Philippines, South Africa, Thailand, and Türkiye. Causality 
results sho w the presence of unidirectional causality from energy consumption to 
ecological footprint. This empirical analysis provides policy -makers with policy 
recommendations for the effective and efficient use of renewable energy resources —such 
as providing ince ntives and promoting environment -related technologies —for a 
sustainable environment in the long run.

INTRODUCTION 
 
In recent years, with climate change and global warming, 
environmental sustainability has become a topic of 
discussion [1]. Increasing industrialization and urbanization 
due to the globalization process have made an impact on 
growth rates [2]. As a result of this situation, there has been 
an increase in energy demand with the increase in growth 
rates. As energy demand is met with non -renewable energy 
resources, the negative impacts of globalization and growth 
on the environment have started to emerge. 
These developments have prompted a growing demand for 
alternative indicators that account for broader 
environmental degradation beyond merely air pollution. In 
this context, the ecological footprint emerges as a more 
comprehensive metric, as it encompasses not only carbon 
emissions but also environmental consequences such as land 
use, natural resource consumption, and waste generation. 
Unlike traditional indicators such as CO₂ emissions, the 
ecological footprint reflects the total pressure that human 
activities exert on the planet’s biological carrying capacity. 
Therefore, the adoption of this indicator enables a more 
accurate assessment of the environmental impacts of energy 
consumption and economic growth, particularly in countries

Studies in the literature use CO2 emissions to examine the 
impact of growth or energy consumption on the environment 
[3]. However, using data such as CO2 or greenhouse gas 
emissions to analyze the impact of energy consumption on 
the environment only reveals the effect of energy 
consumption on air pollution. This represents a shortcoming 
in terms of determining appropriate policies; in that case, a 
more appropriate variable should be used to investigate the 
impact of energy consumption on the environment. This 
study contributes to the existing literature in the following 
ways: examining the impact of energy consumption on the 
environment through panel time series analysis using a large 
dataset for ten newly industrial ized countries; using 
ecological footprint data to measure the impact of energy 
consumption on the environment; checking the stationarity 
of the series with the Pesaran [4] CIPS (Cross -sectionally 
Augmented Im, Pesaran, and Shin) test, which takes into 
account cross -sectional dependence; using the Westerlund 
[5] cointegration test, one of the second -generation 
cointegration tests, to assess the existence of a long -run 
relationship between the series; utilizing the Eberhardt and 
Teal [6] AMG (Augmented Mean Group) estimator and

LITERATURE REV IEW 
 
There are numerous studies in the literature on testing the 
Environmental Kuznets curve and on energy consumption 
and its impacts o n the environment (Table 1). In studies 
testing the EKC hypothesis, Beyene and Kotosz [9] found no 
significant association between economic activities and CO₂ 
emissions in 12 East African countries. Sarkodie and Owusu 
[10], analyzing Senegal, reported that energy consumption, 
financial development, and industrialization contributed to 
rising carbon emissions, while urbanization and GDP had a 
mitigating effect in the long run. Similarly, Azam et al. [11] 
confirmed a positive relationship between CO₂ emissions and 
economic growth. In their study on Pakistan, Mohiuddin et al. 
[12] identified a long -run unidirectional causal relationship 
from energy consumption to CO₂ emissions. In Sri Lanka, 
Sarkodie and Owusu [13] reported a long -run equilibrium 
between energy consumption and industrialization, with 
bidirectional causality. They also observed a unidirectional 
causality from CO₂ emissions to energy use. 
Wang et al. [3] concluded that shocks in CO₂ emissions 
exerted limited feedback effects on GDP and energy 
consumption. Apergis and Öztürk [14] confirmed the EKC 
hypothesis in Asian countries, while Osabuohien et al. [15] 
validated it for a group of African nations. Baek [16], in a 
study covering 12 nuclear energy-intensive countries, did not 
find support for the E KC hypothesis but noted that nuclear 
energy contributed to long-run reductions in CO₂ emissions. 
Remuzgo and Sarabia [17] found that global inequality in CO₂ 
emissions declined by 22% between 1990 and 2010, driven 
by global development. Lin et al. [18], in a study on Nigeria, 
reported no significant impact of industrialization on CO₂ 
emissions. In Malaysia, Gul et al. [19] identified a 
unidirectional causality from energy consumption to CO₂ 
emissions. Salahuddin et al. [20], focusing on the Gulf 
Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, observed a 
unidirectional causality from electricity consumption to CO₂ 
emissions. Using the ARDL approach, Saidi and Mbarek [21] 
tested the EKC hypothesis in 19 developing countries 
between 1990 and 2013 and found no evidence sup porting 
the EKC for this group. 
Tiwari et al. [22] investigated the EKC hypothesis for India 
and concluded that it holds in both the short and long run. 
Similarly, Shahbaz et al. [23] confirmed the long -run validity 
of the EKC in Pakistan. For the BRICS co untries, Pao and Tsai 
[24] found support for the EKC hypothesis and observed a 
bidirectional causality between CO₂ emissions and foreign 
direct investment. In their study on Pakistan, Nasir and 
Rehman [25] identified positive effects of energy 
consumption and foreign trade on CO₂ emissions and 
confirmed the presence of the EKC. Acaravci and Ozturk [26], 
in their analysis of European countries, established a long-run 
equilibrium relationship between energy use and CO₂ 
emissions driven by economic growth and validated the EKC 
in Denmark and Italy. In the case of China, Jalil and Mahmud 
[27] also reported results consistent with the EKC hypothesis. 
Only a few studies treat energy consumption as the 
dependent variable, and these generally focus on its 
relationship with economic growth. Lee and Chang [28], 
examining 16 Asian countries between 1971 and 2002, found 
a long -run positive relationship between energy 
consumption and real GDP, along with unidirectional 
causality running from energy consumption to growth. In 
contrast, short-run causality was not observed. Romano and 
Scandurra [29] studied Italian regions between 1980 and 
2007 using panel cointegration and vector error correction 
models. Their findings revealed long -run cointegration and 
bidirectional causality in both the short and long term. 
Sarkodie and Strezov [30], analyzing China, India, Iran, 
Indonesia, and South Africa between 1986 and 2016, 
reported that energy consumption strongly and positively 
influenced greenhouse gas emissions. Using the ARDL 
approach for India, Mahalik and Mallick [31] concluded that 
energy consumption was positively influenced by urban 
population and negatively influenced by financial 
development, economic growth, and industrial output. 
Shahbaz et al. [32] found that globalizat ion and financial 
development reduced energy consumption, while economic 
growth and urbanization contributed to increased energy 
demand. Bashir et al. [33], studying Indonesia, discovered a 
short-run causal relationship between CO₂ emissions and 
energy consumption. 
Despite the growing relevance of broader sustainability 
indicators, studies employing the ecological footprint remain 
scarce. One such study by Destek and Sarkodie [1] found a 
bidirectional causal relationship between the ecological 
footprint and economic growth. 
Recent studies increasingly explore the complex 
relationships between economic growth, energy 
consumption, and environmental degradation through both 
traditional and alternative environmental indicators. In this 
context, Recepoglu et al. (41) investigated the impact of 
ecological footprint components on agricultural productivity 
in MENA countries over the 1992–2021 period. Their findings 
revealed that all components negatively affected 
productivity, with forest footprint exerting the stron gest 
adverse effect. Similarly, Hayaloglu et al. (42) approached the 
EKC hypothesis from an institutional quality perspective and 
found that institutional structures significantly increased 
environmental degradation up to a certain threshold, after 
which the effect reversed in SADC countries. Qing et al. (43) 
examined the relationships among green energy 
consumption, environmental degradation, economic 
development, trade openness, and oil prices, concluding that 
environmental degradation reduces green energ y 
consumption, whereas oil prices and economic growth 
increase it. Kostakis (44), focusing on ASEAN countries, found 
that the EKC hypothesis held only for Singapore, and that 
renewable energy supports environmental sustainability, 
while financial openness contributes to rising CO₂ emissions. 
Bashir et al. (45) analyzed the effects of energy transition, 
natural resource consumption, geopolitical risks, and banking 
development on environmental sustainability. Their 
empirical results showed that while energy t ransition and 
banking developments contribute positively to sustainability, 
natural resource use, economic growth, and geopolitical risk 
exacerbate environmental degradation.

METHODOLOGY 
 
This article aims to examine the relationship between 
ecological footprint and energy consumption using a panel of 
10 newly industrialized countries. With globalization, these 
countries are likely to increase their economic and financial 
relations with each other. Ignoring cro ss-sectional 
dependence across units may lead to misleading empirical 
results. In order to avoid this problem, cross -sectional 
dependence between units was tested. 
 
Empirical Model and Dataset 
 
The effect of energy consumption on ecological footprint was 
examined for 10 newly industrialized countries using annual 
data covering the period 1970 -2022: Brazil, China, India, 
Indonesia, Malaysia, Mexico, the Philippines, South Africa, 
Thailand, and Tür kiye. The panel version of the empirical 
model employed can be written as follows: 
lnEFit = φ0 + φ1 lnECit + µit (1) 
where t, i and µ it denote the time dimension, cross -sectional 
dimension, and residual terms, respectively. In addition, ln EF 
signifies the logarithm of the ecological footprint per capita, 
and ln EC signifies the logarithm of energy consumption per 
capita. Ecological footprint data was obtained from Global 
Footprint Network [36] and energy consumption data was 
obtained from the British Petroleum Statistical Review of 
World Energy [37].

CONCLUSIONS AND POLICY IMPLICATIONS 
 
Recently, with the increasing impacts of growing energy 
consumption on the environment due to globalization in 
newly industrializing countries such as India, it is observed 
that there are studies examining energy consumption and its 
impact on the environment [32]; [40]. There are very few 
studies that examine the negative effects of energy 
consumption on the environment by addressing the impact of 
energy consumption on the ecological footprint, and to the 
best of our knowledge, no study has systematically addressed 
the causality between ecological footprint and energy 
consumption for newly industrializing countries. Based on 
this research gap, the relationship between ecological 
footprint and energy consumption was analyzed for newly 
industrialized countries. In this context, the study fills a 
research gap in the existing literature by analyzing the 
negative impacts on the environment due to the increase in 
energy consumption within the scope of ecological footprint. 
The dataset of this study, which examines the relationship 
between ecological footprint and energy consumption, covers 
the period 1970 -2022 for 10 newly industrialized countries. 
The countries that make up the panel are Brazil, China, India, 
Indonesia, Malaysia, Mexico, the Philippines, South Africa, 
Thailand, and Türkiye. The stationarity of the variables was 
tested utilizing Pesaran's [4] CIPS test, which can be used in 
the presence of cross-sectional dependence. The existence of 
a long -run relationship between ecological footprint and 
energy consumption was determined by applying the 
Westerlund [5] cointegration test. The long-run cointegration 
coefficient estimates for heterogeneous panels were 
calculated using the Pesaran [7] CCE estimator and the 
Eberhardt and Teal [6] AMG estimator. The causality 
relationships between the variables were analyzed by 
applying Dumitrescu and Hurlin's [11] causality test. 
The empirical results prove the existence of a relat ionship 
between ecological footprint and energy consumption in 10 
newly industrialized countries. According to the results of 
Eberhardt and Teal's [12] AMG estimator, energy 
consumption has a positive and significant effect on the 
ecological footprint of B razil, China, India, Indonesia, 
Malaysia, Mexico, the Philippines, South Africa, Thailand, and 
Türkiye. According to the results of Pesaran's [24] CCE 
estimator, energy consumption positively affects the 
ecological footprint of Brazil, China, India, Malays ia, Mexico, 
the Philippines, South Africa, Thailand, and Türkiye. 
Dumitrescu and Hurlin's [8] causality test results reveal 
unidirectional causality from energy consumption to 
ecological footprint. 
In line with the findings of this study, important policy 
recommendations can be made. The increase in energy 
consumption has a negative impact on the environment in 
almost all countries in the dataset. These results show that 
the energy sources, the use of which is increasing with 
globalization, are non -renewable energy sources. From a 
policy perspective, it can be said that the increasing demand 
for energy resources should be met by renewable energy 
resources. To achieve this, governments should implement 
comprehensive incentive schemes to promote the adoption of 
renewable energy technologies, including subsidies for green 
investments, tax reductions for clean energy producers, and 
grants for R&D in environment -related technologies. In 
addition, regulatory frameworks that prioritize energy 
efficiency standards an d the integration of renewables into 
national energy mixes should be strengthened. Otherwise, the 
world will face longer -term environmental consequences in 
terms of climate change and global warming with the 
continued use of non-renewable energy resources. 
This study is subject to several limitations. First, the analysis 
focuses on total energy consumption without differentiating 
between energy sources. Since the environmental effects of 
renewable and non -renewable energy sources differ 
significantly, future studies may yield more detailed insights 
by disaggregating energy types. Second, although the 
ecological footprint provides a broader indicator of 
environmental pressure compared to conventional proxies 
such as CO₂ emissions, it may not fully capture sector-specific 
environmental effects. Future research could address these 
limitations by incorporating more disaggregated 
environmental indicators and energy data.
