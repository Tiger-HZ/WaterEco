Background Korsakoff syndrome (KS) is a chronic neuropsychiatric condition resulting from thiamine deficiency, most often in the context of prolonged alcohol misuse. People with KS commonly require long-term residential care due to enduring impairments in memory, executive functioning, initiative, and awareness. In these settings, quality of life and quality of care are important outcomes, but their assessment is complicated by the distinct cognitive and behavioral profile of KS.Objective To synthesize current evidence regarding quality of life and quality of care among people with Korsakoff syndrome living in long-term residential care.Methods This integrative narrative review synthesized empirical and conceptual studies examining quality of life and quality of care in people with KS living in long-term care settings.Results Available studies indicate that quality of life in long-term care is generally stable over time, although improvements have been observed in specific quality-of-life domains and following targeted interventions. Self- and proxy-reported quality of life may differ, highlighting the importance of considering both perspectives. The development of Korsakoff-specific instruments, including the QUALIKO, QUALIKO Self-Report, and Korsakoff Consumer Quality Index (KCQI), has enabled assessment of quality of life and quality of care that more specifically addresses characteristics and care needs associated with KS. Intervention studies suggest that structured training and engagement may improve aspects of quality of life.Conclusion Quality of life in people with KS living in long-term care is not simply determined by the severity of cognitive impairment and may remain stable or improve despite persistent neurocognitive deficits. Korsakoff-specific assessment instruments and the complementary use of self- and proxy-report provide opportunities for a more comprehensive evaluation of quality of life and quality of care in this population.
