Integrating HEC-RAS with machine learning and deep 
learning models as a rapid tool for river water level 
prediction

Abstract
For flood management, accurate water surface elevation predictions are necessary. hydraulic models such as 
HEC-RAS produces detailed water surface elevation predictions but requires extensive input data and com -
putational time. This study tested a hybrid approach on the Khorram Abad River in Lorestan Province, Iran, 
combining HEC-RAS with machine learning and deep learning models. HEC-RAS version 6.0 generated 856 
records from 167 cross-sections and eight return periods (2 to 500 years). The dataset was split by cross-section 
(74 for training, 33 for testing) to prevent information leakage. Four models were trained: Random Forest (RF), 
Support Vector Machine (SVM), Deep Neural Network (DNN), and Recurrent Neural Network (RNN). RF 
had the highest accuracy in testing, with an R 2 of 0.9909, an RMSE of 0.619 meters, and an MAE of 0.446 
meters. Channel bed elevation accounted for 32% of the prediction importance, and log-transformed discharge 
accounted for 24%. RF maintained its accuracy across all return periods. Repeated cross-validation gave a 
mean R2 of 0.9933 with a standard deviation of 0.0029. The Wilcoxon test showed RF outperformed SVM (p = 
0.000), DNN, and RNN (p < 0.001) with statistical significance. Bootstrap uncertainty analysis produced 95% 
confidence intervals for RMSE [0.5546, 0.8363] meters and R 2 [0.9834, 0.9927]. The framework provides a 
faster alternative to HEC-RAS for this river and may be useful for design flood estimation and hazard mapping. 
Testing on other rivers and under unsteady flow conditions is needed before operational flood warning use.

Introduction
Riverine areas are suitable for human habitation, but residents there have historically been vulnerable to flooding 
from rising water levels 1. Floods are among the most destructive natural disasters, causing significant damage 
worldwide every year2. As climate change intensifies, flood-related damage is increasing 3. Accurate prediction 
of water surface elevation in rivers is a primary method for flood risk assessment. Traditional methods fall into 
two categories: hydrological (e.g., the Muskingum-Cunge method4) and hydraulic (e.g., the HEC RAS method5.

These models have a strong physical basis, but they require substantial input data and considerable computational 
time6. Data-driven approaches have emerged as an alternative 7. Machine learning and deep learning algorithms 
can identify complex, nonlinear patterns. Several studies have applied these models to flood prediction. Gao et 
al.8 used an enhanced Random Forest model to estimate rating curves and achieved high accuracy8. Sharafkhani 
et al.9 compared four models—MLP, LSTM, Transformer, and LSTNet—for multi-step-ahead water level fore -
casting at 20 river gauging stations. The LSTNet model outperformed the others under data drift conditions, 
achieving a median RMSE of 0.00724 for 4-step-ahead predictions, with each step corresponding to 30 minutes9.
Kılıçarslan et al.10 used a machine learning surrogate model to improve flood inundation mapping. They used 
the HAND FIM method as the baseline and HEC RAS outputs as the target data. The surrogate model reduced 
false alarms by 18% and improved the Critical Success Index by 26%. The authors note the approach is fast 
and low-cost, which makes it useful for areas with limited data and resources 10. Yükseler11 compared Random 
Forest with HEC RAS and found Random Forest had better accuracy 11. Duangkhwan et al12 developed an inte-
grated deep learning framework with LSTM for water level prediction and CNN for flood inundation mapping. 
The framework replaces the HEC RAS 1D/2D model. They trained the models on observed hydrological data 
and HEC RAS outputs. The LSTM predicted water levels with satisfactory accuracy, and the CNN converted 
those predictions into flood depth maps that matched HEC RAS outputs closely12.Kordi Karimabadi et al.13 used 
LSTM for streamflow forecasting over a 20-year horizon. They then put the extracted peak discharge values into 
HEC RAS 2D to produce flood inundation and hazard maps. The LSTM model had R 2 values of 0.98 during 
training and 0.92 during testing. The study used a 40-year daily time series from the Nesa River in Iran. Estimated 
reservoir inflow volumes for 25-, 100-, and 500-year return periods were 76.26, 148.73, and 149.22 million cubic 
meters13. Alamu et al.14 reviewed AI and machine learning surrogate models in Computational Fluid Dynamics. 
The review covered Reduced Order Models, Physics Informed Neural Networks, Neural Operators, Transform -
ers, and Generative Models14. Haces Garcia et al.15 showed that Deep Neural Networks (Feedforward, Bayesian, 
Physics Informed, and LSTM) work as surrogate models for 2D HEC RAS simulations. The models achieved 
speedups of 15 to 52 times over conventional models. Median RMSE for cell flooding depths reached as low as 
2 mm after fine-tuning15.
Holmberg et al.16 developed a recurrent neural operator surrogate for HEC RAS that accelerates simulation 
speed16. Feng et al.17 used a CNN-LSTM-ATT architecture to predict flood depth and inundation extent 17. Sal-
isu et al.18 reviewed 30 studies and found that machine learning models, especially Random Forest and LSTM, 
consistently outperform traditional hydrological methods for flood prediction 18. Qu et al.19 proposed a dynamic 
framework for chain risk assessment of floods. The framework integrates the HiPIMS hydrodynamic model, a 
physics-informed deep learning network (PG-ST-MoE), and complex network theory. The authors report the 
framework is effective for identifying hidden systemic risks in data-scarce regions19. Lee and Chang20 introduced 
MC-PINN, a modular coupled physics-informed neural network with a 1D PINN for sewer flow and a 2D PINN 
for surface flow. The network enforces mass and momentum conservation through physics-based loss func -
tions20. Oluwadare et al.21 integrated an ensemble model (Random Forest, SVM, and XGBoost) with HEC RAS 
2D and achieved an AUC of 0.97921. In their systematic review, Maddodi et al.22 confirmed that machine learn-
ing models outperform traditional methods. Most of these studies were conducted in data-rich basins22. Regions 
with limited data, such as the Khorram Abad River basin, have received less attention23. The area has consistent 
flood risk from its topography and torrential rainfall, but has few active hydrometric stations.Recently, hybrid 
approaches combining physically based hydraulic models with machine learning have gained attention. Pour -
saeid24 studied water level fluctuations in Miqan Lake in Iran using an optimized SLFFN model with evolution-
ary algorithms (PSO, GA, ICA) and RROC/PDF uncertainty analysis. That study focused on a lake system. The 
present research addresses riverine flood prediction, which involves different hydraulic dynamics Poursaeid 24 
relied on field observations, while the present study uses HEC RAS simulations as the physics-based data source. 
This approach enables systematic scenario generation and is useful for data-scarce regions where continuous field 
records are not available24.Hybrid approaches combining HEC RAS and machine learning have been applied in

Materials and methods
Study area introduction
The Khorramabad watershed is in Lorestan Province in western Iran. It covers 2,351 km2 and is part of the larger 
Karkheh River basin, which drains into the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman 23,25. The Khorram Abad River 
starts at the confluence of the Robat and Karganeh rivers in the center of Khorram Abad city and is a major 
tributary of the Kashkan River. The studied section of the Khorram Abad River runs about 10 km between the 
Bahramju and Cham Anjir hydrometric stations (Fig. 1).
Research methodology
This applied developmental study aimed to provide a hybrid framework for predicting water surface elevation in 
the Khorram Abad River. Fig. 2 summarizes the main steps. The framework uses HEC RAS to simulate flow and 
generate hydraulic data. Machine learning algorithms, particularly Random Forest, are then trained to identify 
patterns in the data and predict water surface elevation rapidly. The hybrid approach combines the accuracy of 
the physical model with the computational speed of the data-driven models.
Data collection and preparation
The required data were collected in three categories: hydrological, geometric, and hydraulic. The hydrological 
data included peak flood discharge for eight return periods from 2 to 500 years (Table 1). The geometric data 
consisted of 167 cross sections with boundary conditions of a normal slope (0.008 upstream and 0.001 down -
stream) and a subcritical flow regime.After screening for missing values, outliers, and hydraulic inconsistencies, 
60 cross-sections were excluded, leaving 107 cross-sections with complete and reliable data for the final machine 
learning analysis. Manning''s roughness coefficient was set to 0.01 for the main channel and 0.01 for the flood -
plains, with some cross sections set to 0.015. All data were integrated within a GIS environment. The HEC RAS 
outputs included water surface elevation, velocity, Froude number, shear stress, and flow power for each cross 
section and return period. Before feature engineering, the data were screened for outliers and missing values.
Hydraulic simulation and database generation using HEC-RAS
Version 6.0 of the HEC RAS hydraulic model was used for flow simulation and to generate training data. A one-
dimensional geometric model was constructed with 167 cross sections, Manning''s roughness coefficients, and 
boundary conditions. Compared to 2D models, 1D HEC RAS is faster26, which allowed generation of a compre-
hensive training dataset across eight return periods (2 to 500 years). The choice of 1D HEC-RAS is supported by 
the predominantly longitudinal flow conditions observed in the study reach (Section " Analysis of Longitudinal 
Hydraulic Behavior of the River"). Local water authorities also use 1D models for flood forecasting.A total of 856 
data records were generated. The main output was water surface elevation at each cross section for each return

Feature engineering and final data preparation
Before normalization, the Shapiro-Wilk test was run on the numerical features. Only 20% of the features followed 
a normal distribution (p > 0.05); most did not. Instead of StandardScaler, which assumes normality, RobustScaler 
was used. RobustScaler uses the median and interquartile range, which makes it less sensitive to outliers and 
suited to skewed hydrological data.

Modeling and performance evaluation
Four algorithms were trained to predict water surface elevation: RF, SVM, DNN, and RNN. The RF model used 
200 trees with a maximum depth of seven. The SVM model used an RBF kernel with C = 100 and gamma = 
''scale''. The DNN had three hidden layers with 32, 16, and 8 neurons and a ReLU activation function. The RNN 
had one recurrent layer with 64 units and a ReLU activation function. Model performance was evaluated using 
R2, RMSE, and MAE.
Longitudinal and spatial analysis
Longitudinal profiles of the channel bed and water surface were plotted for the eight return periods. The water 
surface gradient was calculated as S = ΔH/ΔL. A heatmap of water surface elevation as a function of longitudinal 
position and return period was generated. All analyses used HEC RAS outputs and Python.
Results
Results of hydraulic modeling with HEC-RAS
Running HEC RAS for eight return periods (T2 to T500) across 167 cross sections produced 856 records of 
hydraulic data. Calibration using data from the Bahramju station gave a Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency of 0.89 for the 
25-year return period. As discharge increased, the hydraulic parameters showed systematic variations (Table 1). 
At the downstream cross section (Cham Anjir), the Froude number went from critical (around 1) for small floods 
to subcritical (around 0.8) for large floods. The energy slope along the river ranged from 0.0003 to 0.008. For 
more than 90% of the cross sections and for return periods greater than T10, the Froude number was below 1, 
indicating subcritical flow under flood conditions.
Quantitative evaluation of predictive models
Four machine learning models were evaluated for predicting water surface elevation: Random Forest, Support 
Vector Machine, Deep Neural Network, and Recurrent Neural Network. The dataset was split by cross section to 
prevent data leakage, with 74 sections (70%, 592 samples) used for training and 33 sections (30%, 264 samples) 
for testing. Model performance was assessed using R 2, RMSE, and MAE (Table 2).A repeated cross-validation 
strategy was used to evaluate the Random Forest model''s stability. Five-fold cross-validation with 10 repeti -
tions (50 evaluations total) was run on the training data. The mean R 2 was 0.9933 with a standard deviation of 
0.0029, and values ranged from 0.9816 to 0.9970. The low standard deviation indicates the model is stable and 
not sensitive to the training-test split.Random Forest outperformed the other models on the test set, with an R2 of 
0.9909, an RMSE of 0.6192 meters, and an MAE of 0.4464 meters. Training performance was R2 = 0.9979, and 
test performance was R2 = 0.9909, so the model generalizes well without overfitting. SVM ranked second with 
an R2 of 0.9872 and an RMSE of 0.7341 meters. DNN and RNN performed worse, with test R2 values of 0.7381 
and 0.9230, respectively.

The hyperparameters of the DNN and RNN models were optimized using a manual grid search for a fair com-
parison. The best DNN configuration had 32 neurons in the first hidden layer with a dropout rate of 0.1 and a 
learning rate of 0.001. The best RNN configuration had 64 units with a learning rate of 0.005. Both deep learning

of [0.9834, 0.9927].

Discussion
Combining HEC RAS and Random Forest in a hybrid approach can predict water surface elevation in the Khor-
ram Abad River accurately. Physical models like HEC RAS require extensive input data and substantial compu-
tational time5. This study used HEC RAS outputs as ground truth to train a Random Forest model. The Random 
Forest model achieved an R 2 of 0.991, with accuracy comparable to the physical model but with near-instanta -
neous execution, which is useful for rapid flood warning systems.
Zhou et al. 27 also found tree-based decision models effective for predicting water levels 27. Kılıçarslan et al. 
(2025) reduced flood inundation prediction error by 18% 10. Feature importance analysis (Fig. 6) showed chan-
nel bed elevation (35%) and the logarithm of discharge (25%) were the two most important predictors. This 
matches fundamental hydraulic principles. The feature importance analysis shows that channel bed elevation and 
discharge are the main variables controlling water surface elevation in the HEC-RAS simulations. These are the 
physically meaningful variables one would expect to matter. The model identified them as the most important 
features. This suggests the surrogate model learned the dominant physical controls in the training data, not spuri-
ous correlations.
Regarding data preprocessing, the Shapiro-Wilk test showed that most hydrological parameters (River_Sta, 
Q_Total_m3/s, Min_Ch_Elm, and W.S._Elevm) do not follow a normal distribution. Hydrological data are often 
skewed due to the stochastic nature of flood events and variable riverbed topography. RobustScaler was used 
instead of standard Z-score normalization. Z-score normalization relies on the mean and standard deviation and 
assumes normality. RobustScaler uses the median and interquartile range, which handles outliers and non-normal 
distributions better.
Superiority of the hybrid framework and model stability
One finding from this study is the stability of the Random Forest model''s accuracy across a range of hydrologi -
cal conditions. The model produced consistent error rates for both common floods (T10 and T25) and rare floods 
(T500) (Fig. 9). This has two implications. A model that predicts extreme floods as accurately as moderate ones 
is useful for crisis management. The stability comes from the coverage of eight return periods in the HEC RAS 
database and from Random Forest''s robustness against overfitting.The Random Forest model also generalized 
well. Training accuracy was R2 = 0.9979 and test accuracy was R 2 = 0.9909. The small difference suggests the 
model is not tied to a specific training set.A repeated cross-validation strategy was used to check stability. Five-
fold cross-validation with 10 repetitions (50 evaluations total) was run on the training data. The mean R 2 was 
0.9933 with a standard deviation of 0.0029, and values ranged from 0.9816 to 0.9970. The low standard deviation 
confirms the model is stable and not sensitive to the training-test split. The cross-validation mean (0.9933) and 
test performance (0.9909) are consistent, which supports the model''s robustness and generalizability. The boot -
strap uncertainty analysis supports the Random Forest model''s reliability. The 95% prediction intervals in Fig. 
3 cover the actual water surface elevations in the test dataset. The model gives reliable predictions with uncer -
tainty that is well characterized. The 95% confidence intervals for the performance metrics were narrow: RMSE 
between 0.5546 and 0.8363 meters, and R2 between 0.9834 and 0.9927. These intervals indicate that the model''s 
accuracy is stable. The framework works well for rapid scenario simulation, design flood estimation, and hazard 
mapping when steady-state assumptions hold. For operational flood warning systems that need real-time hydro -
graph predictions, the framework would need to be extended to handle unsteady flow conditions. The Wilcoxon 
signed rank test confirmed the statistical superiority of Random Forest over SVM (p = 0.0000), DNN (p < 0.001), 
and RNN (p < 0.001). The performance differences are not due to random variation.
The Random Forest model is computationally efficient, with a prediction time of 42.9 milliseconds for 264 
test samples (0.1625 ms per sample). This is much faster than HEC RAS simulations and makes the framework 
practical for operational flood warning. The DNN and RNN models performed well during training but their 
accuracy dropped during testing (Table 2). This shows the advantage of ensemble methods like Random Forest

for problems with limited data. Deep learning models typically need large datasets, often thousands to millions 
of samples, to generalize well. With only 856 records, the DNN and RNN models struggled. Random Forest, 
as a tree-based ensemble, is robust to overfitting and performs well with limited data 28. This suggests ensemble 
methods may be preferable to deep learning for hydrological applications where data are often scarce.Random 
Forest was selected as the final model for its accuracy (R2 = 0.9909), stability (cross-validation mean R2 = 0.9933, 
standard deviation = 0.0029), computational efficiency (0.2227 ms per sample), and interpretability through fea-
ture importance analysis.
The feature importance analysis
Feature importance analysis showed minimum channel elevation (32%), the logarithm of discharge (24%), and 
water depth (14%) were the most influential predictors. These results are consistent with the understanding that 
bed topography and discharge primarily control water surface elevation. Minimum channel elevation represents 
the bed topography and provides the baseline for all water surface elevations, which makes it the dominant pre -
dictor. The logarithm of discharge had a strong influence, not raw discharge. The stage-discharge relationship in 
open channels follows a power law. For a wide, shallow rectangular channel, the relationship is a power function 
where the coefficient depends on roughness, width, and energy slope, and the exponent is 5.329. Taking the loga-
rithm transforms this nonlinear relationship into a linear form, which the Random Forest model can capture more 
effectively. This follows standard hydrometric practice; logarithmic transformation is a special case of the Box-
Cox transformation30, commonly used for calibrating rating curves and producing normally distributed residuals.
Interpretation of river hydraulic behavior and practical implications
The longitudinal analysis showed the flow in the study reach is predominantly uniform and subcritical. This is 
supported by the parallelism of the water surface lines with the riverbed (Fig. 13) and the consistency of water 
depth along the river (Fig. 17). At some cross sections, the water surface gradient had negative values, likely 
from bed constriction or downstream boundary conditions (Fig. 16). Uniform flow simplifies the relationship 
between discharge and water surface elevation, which is why the machine learning model predicted it accurately. 
The high importance of channel bed elevation (35%) suggests that accurate cross-section surveying and moni -
toring of riverbed changes are needed for accurate prediction.Statistical tests on the longitudinal data confirmed 
that water surface elevation does not vary significantly across return periods (Kruskal-Wallis, p = 0.1074), but 
there are significant differences between cross sections (ANOV A, p < 0.001). The hydraulic behavior of the river 
remains consistent across flood events, while cross-sectional geometry determines local water surface elevations.
The framework performed well for the Khorram Abad River case study, but the findings are based on a single 
river system with 107 cross sections and eight return periods. The practical implications apply primarily to simi-
lar river systems. Further studies on diverse river systems are needed to determine broader applicability for flood 
crisis management and hazard mapping at regional or national scales.
Limitations and future research
1. The training dataset was generated entirely from HEC RAS simulations. The Random Forest model is a surro-
gate for HEC RAS, not an independent predictor. The approach preserves HEC RAS''s physical accuracy but 
reduces computation time from hours to milliseconds. This makes it useful for rapid scenario-based assess -
ments where steady-state assumptions hold, such as design flood estimation for specific return periods. The 
model''s accuracy depends on the quality and precision of the underlying HEC RAS simulations. Incorporat -
ing field measurements and observed hydrological data in future work could make the model more indepen -
dent and better suited to real-world conditions.

2. The proposed framework was developed and evaluated using a limited dataset of 856 records from a single 
river system, the Khorram Abad River in Iran. The model showed high accuracy and stability for this case 
study, but its generalizability to other rivers with different hydraulic characteristics, geometries, and flow 
regimes has not been validated. The dataset size limits the application of more complex models, especially 
deep learning approaches, which need larger datasets. Future research should apply the methodology to mul-
tiple rivers with diverse conditions—steep slopes, meandering channels, tidally influenced reaches, and flood-
plain interactions—using larger datasets to assess transferability and robustness.
3. Spatial dimension and flow dimensionality: study used one-dimensional modelling. Extending to two-dimen-
sional (HEC RAS 2D) or three-dimensional models would make the approach applicable to floodplains and 
urban areas.
4. Temporal dimension and unsteady conditions: The current framework predicts water surface elevation for 
steady-state conditions. This limits its use for real-time forecasting systems that need continuous hydrograph 
predictions. The steady-state approach works for design flood estimation and hazard mapping. Future work 
should extend the framework to unsteady conditions by including temporal dynamics.
Conclusion
This study developed a hybrid framework combining HEC RAS with machine and deep learning models to pre-
dict water surface elevation in the Khorram Abad River. Random Forest was the most effective model, with an 
R2 of 0.9909 and an RMSE of 0.6192 meters. Feature importance analysis showed channel bed elevation (35%) 
and the logarithm of discharge (25%) were the most significant predictors. The model maintained accuracy across 
all return periods (2 to 500 years). Longitudinal and spatial analyses indicated uniform and subcritical flow in 
the study reach. Integrating HEC RAS with Random Forest combines the accuracy of the physical model (R 2 ≈ 
0.9909) with near-instantaneous predictions (42.9 ms for 264 test samples), a substantial reduction in computa -
tional time compared to HEC RAS alone. For the Khorram River case study, the framework shows potential as a 
quick, cost-effective tool for simulating flood scenarios in similar river systems. Further validation across diverse 
river systems is needed to establish general applicability. The framework is designed for steady-state conditions, 
suitable for design flood estimation and hazard mapping. Extending to unsteady flow conditions would be needed 
for real-time hydrograph prediction in operational warning systems.
