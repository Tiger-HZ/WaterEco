Abstract
Native pollinator-dependent plants are particularly vulnerable to habitat loss and other
disturbances that disrupt pollen transfer. Understanding their reproductive biology is critical
for assessing pollination dynamics, seed production, and their vulnerability to environmental
change. This study addressed the reproductive strategies and dependence on cross-pollination of
three native species in northeastern Patagonia ( Larrea divaricata , Schinus johnstonii ,a n d
Neltuma flexuosa var. depressa ). We applied controlled pollination treatments (cross-, self-,
spontaneous-, and open-pollination) and measured the subsequent seed set and seed germin-
ation rates.L. divaricataexhibited higher seed set and higher germination capacity when natural
pollination was allowed, compared to all manually pollinated treatments. This suggests that
repeated visits and multiple pollen donors maximize seed production and seed quality. In
S. johnstonii, a polygamous-dioecious species, seed production occurred even under pollinator-
exclusion treatments, suggesting a contribution of wind pollination and/or alternative repro-
ductive mechanisms.N. flexuosa var. depressadid not produce fruits under any treatment. These
findings reveal contrasting reproductive strategies among Monte shrub species, highlighting that
reproductive success may depend on different pollen-transfer pathways, from strong reliance on
pollinator activity to potential airborne pollen contributions. Understanding these strategies is
essential for conserving pollination processes, seed production, and the long-term resilience of
arid Patagonian ecosystems.
Resumen
Las plantas autóctonas dependientes de polinizadores son especialmente vulnerables a
perturbaciones que afectan la transferencia de polen. Comprender su biología reproductiva
es fundamental para comprender la dinámica de la polinización, la producción de semillas y
su vulnerabilidad ante disturbios. Este estudio evaluó las estrategias reproductivas y la
dependencia a la polinización en tres especies nativas del noreste de la Patagonia ( Larrea
divaricata, Schinus johnstonii, and Neltuma flexuosa var. depressa). Aplicamos tratamientos
de polinización controlada (autopolinización, polinización cruzada, polinización espontá-
nea y polinización natural), evaluando la producción de semillas y su capacidad germinativa.
En L. divaricata la polinización abierta produjo más semillas y mayor capacidad germina-
tiva, resaltando que las visitas repetidas de polinizadores y el polen proveniente de muchos
individuos diferentes maximiza la producción y calidad de las semillas. S. johnstonii, especie
polígamo-dioica, registró producción de semillas incluso bajo exclusión de polinizadores,
indicando una posible polinización anemófila u otros mecanismos reproductivos. N. flexuosa
var. depressa no produjo frutos bajo ningún tratamiento, lo que sugiere potenciales limit-
aciones reproductivas. Estos resultados revelan estrategias reproductivas contrastantes y
destacan la importancia de comprenderlas para conservar la polinización, la producción de
semillas y la resiliencia de los ecosistemas áridos de la Patagonia.
Impact statement
Pollination is a fundamental ecological process that sustains plant reproduction, biodiversity,
and the many ecosystem services on which people depend. However, habitat loss, changes in
land use, and other human disturbances are increasingly disrupting plant –pollinator inter-
actions worldwide, particularly in drylands where environmental conditions are already chal-
lenging. This study provides new evidence that native shrub species in arid northeastern
Patagonian ecosystems rely on different reproductive strategies to ensure seed production.
While some species depend heavily on pollinators and benefit from pollen exchange among
multiple individuals, others combine insect- and wind-mediated pollination. This increases their
chances of producing seeds when pollinator activity is limited. Understanding how native plants

Introduction
In the current global scenario, where complex native ecosystems are
being replaced by homogeneous agricultural landscapes, plant
species with high pollinator dependency often fail to receive the
pollination rates required to achieve a sturdy fruit set (Garibaldi
et al., 2011, 2014). Land-use change reduces biodiversity and affects
ecosystem services because many pollinators essential for seed
production face increasing challenges in meeting their basic needs
for food and shelter in simplified landscapes (Garibaldi et al.,2014),
and are further impacted by agrochemicals use (Kennedy et al.,
2013). As a result, insect diversity declines (Kennedy et al., 2013),
which in turn diminishes pollination success in both crops and
remnant native vegetation, ultimately affecting the provision of this
vital ecosystem service (Garibaldi et al., 2014).
Plant–pollinator interactions are central to understanding the
quality and resilience of pollination services. Flowers of semiarid
shrubland plants, typically associated with insect pollination syn-
dromes (small, yellow, or whitish flowers), display a wide range of
floral traits that suggest diverse pollination strategies (Petanidou,
2005; Tadey et al., 2009). Yet, reproductive biology in some plants,
like Argentinian Monte species, remains poorly documented (Rossi
et al., 1999; Tadey et al., 2009; Chacoff and Aschero, 2014), limiting
our ability to predict their responses to environmental change.
Animal-mediated pollination is vital for both human food pro-
duction and the reproduction of wild plants. The latter of which
underpins broader ecosystem services. Around one-third of global
crop production relies on animal pollinators, and between 60% and
90% of plant species depend on them (Kremen et al.,2007). However,
human disturbance is increasingly disrupting these interactions,
including cross-pollination (Eckert et al., 2010). Therefore, under-
standing mating systems is critical for dryland conservation and
agricultural management. Furthermore, examining interspecific pol-
lination among native species may provide valuable insights into the
mechanisms by which alien plants disrupt native reproductive suc-
cess, particularly through the usurpation of pollinator interactions
(Morales and Traveset,2009). As reproductive systems have a strong
influence on evolutionary responses to environmental change
(Barrett, 2010), studying them is essential. Reproductive failure is
commonly driven by pollen limitation in disturbed landscapes
(Aguilar et al., 2006) and by limitations in the pollination of plants
that rely on animals (Larson and Barrett,2000; Wilcock and Neiland,
2002). These effects can be so profound that even restoration sites can
face barriers to re-establishing pollination services. Such barriers are
associated with habitat fragmentation and isolation from undisturbed
populations, as well as with unsuitable conditions for pollinators and
landscape heterogeneity (Menz et al.,2011;D e v o t oe ta l . ,2012). All of
these factors affect pollination success, as well as seed quantity and
quality (Aguilar et al.,2006), which are often assessed through mating
system parameters, such as the outcrossing rate (Krauss et al.,2024).
Thus, changes in mating systems serve as indicators of population
processes and inform conservation strategies (Coates et al.,2007).
In this context, livestock grazing introduces an additional signifi-
cant level of disturbance. Domestic animals degrade vegetation
attributes (Peter et al.,2013), alter pollinator assemblages by modifying
vegetation cover (Tadey,2023), and consume reproductive structures
(Vázquez and Simberloff,2004). These disturbances also reduce seed
production services (Leder et al.,2015) and may be particularly severe
for heterogamous species reliant on specialized interactions. Therefore,
research into the reproductive biology of native species is crucial in
order to assess the impact of grazing and habitat fragmentation on
pollination and seed production in northeastern Patagonia and to
evaluate the role of remnant native vegetation patches in sustaining
reproductive success within these mixed crop-livestock systems.
Because pollinator-dependent species are particularly vulnerable
to disturbances that disrupt plant –pollinator interactions, and
because cross-pollination is often more sensitive to reductions in
pollinator availability and habitat connectivity than self-fertilization,
understanding the reproductive biology and mating systems of native
plants is essential. This information is critical for predicting how
species will respond to ongoing habitat loss and land-use change in
northeastern Patagonia. The aim of this study was to characterize the
mating systems of three widespread shrub species of northeastern
Patagonia and to assess their dependence on cross-pollination for
successful reproduction. We hypothesized that these particular shrub
species rely strongly on cross-pollination.
In order to achieve the objective, we selected three dominant and
distinctive shrubs of the study area (Torres Robles and Rodríguez,
2023). Neltuma flexuosa (DC.) C.E. Hughes & G.P. Lewis var.
depressa (F.A. Roig) C.E. Hughes & G.P. Lewis (Fabaceae) possess
a robust subterranean trunk and a hemispheric and lax canopy with
stems bearing large axillary thorns (3 –4 cm) that reach a height of
~3 m. Their small and hermaphrodite yellowish flowers are
grouped in pendulous dense 4 –14 cm long racemes with ~200
flowers each, which later produce sweet and edible legumes
(Anton and Zuloaga, 2025). Due to these characteristics and its
abundance throughout the region, this species has a considerable
potential use for apiculture, livestock grazing, and restoration
strategies in semiarid ecosystems of Argentina (Renzi et al., 2023).
Schinus johnstonii F.A. Barkley is a shrub that can reach a
maximum height of 2 m tall (Steibel and Troiani,2008). It possesses
gray and thorny stems gathered in a dense hemispheric canopy. It
produces whitish to pale yellow flowers, with an approximate
diameter of 1.5 –2 mm, arranged in dense pseudoracemes (Anton
and Zuloaga, 2025). This species is polygamous-dioecious; the
female flowers are characterized by the presence of rudimentary
stamens and a spherical ovary. In contrast, the male flowers are
distinguished by linear stamen filaments and the absence or rudi-
mentary state of a gynoecium (Steibel and Troiani, 2008). Cur-
rently, there is no published evidence of the functionality of
staminoids in S. johnstonii. Female individuals produce small pur-
ple drupes (Anton and Zuloaga, 2025), which are greatly consumed
by the native fauna (Martín et al., 2001). In addition, this is an
endemic species highly characteristic of the Monte shrubland, listed
as vulnerable in Buenos Aires Province, and highly regarded for its
medicinal properties and its significance in apiculture (Deluchhi,
2006; Doria, 2010; Torres et al., 2020).

Materials and methods
This experiment was conducted in Patagones District, northeastern
Patagonia, Argentina, where the vegetation is characteristic of the
Monte-Espinal transition. Patagones is the largest district in Bue-
nos Aires Province (1,356,971 ha), and contains the most extensive
native shrubland in the region (Torres Robles and Rodríguez,
2023). Historical policies focused on the Humid Pampas encour-
aged deforestation to make way for pastures and cereal crops,
particularly wheat and oats. Further clearing is currently being
carried out to reduce shrub cover and give livestock access to the
grasslands beneath it. Between 2010 and 2020, the area of native
shrubland decreased to a mere 29% of the total district area (Torres
Robles and Rodríguez, 2023). This ongoing habitat loss poses a
significant threat to the pollination and sexual reproduction of
numerous plant species. The climate of the region is subtemperate
dry, with warm summers (averaging 24°C), mild winters (averaging
7°C), and an annual precipitation of 300–350 mm, characterized by
significant variability (Godagnone and Bran,2009). Phytogeographic
analyses classify the region as an ecotone between the Espinal and
Monte provinces (Torres Robles and Rodríguez, 2023). This is
characterized as a shrubland steppe dominated by L. divaricata ,
Chuquiraga erinacea D. Don, N. flexuosa var. depressa, Lycium
chilensis Miers ex Bertero, and Condalia microphylla Cav., with an
herbaceous layer of winter-growing grasses (Cabrera,1971;O y a r z a -
bal et al., 2018; Torres Robles and Rodríguez, 2023).
Fieldwork was carried out in areas with natural vegetation
fenced off from livestock at the Patagones Experimental Ranch
(40°390S, 62°54 0W). The vegetation, topography, climate, and soil
conditions of this site are representative of the region. The Experi-
mental Ranch covers 1210 ha and that is under the jurisdiction of
the government of Buenos Aires Province. It is located in the
Patagones District, 23 km from the city of Carmen de Patagones.
In addition to the cultural and economic relevance of the
aforementioned shrub species, previous research in the region have
indicated that S. johnstonii and L. divaricata are the most abundant
shrub species in the seed rain (Leder et al., 2015). These species
represent a selected group of shrub species that contribute to the
soil seed bank (Leder et al., 2021). Additionally, there is evidence of
low germination of both species in the study area (Peter et al., 2016;
Zeberio and Pérez, 2020), raising questions about potential issues
with the capacity of seeds to germinate that could be linked to
deficiencies in pollination. We added N. flexuosa var. depressa to
our study to explore three important shrub species with signifi-
cantly different flowering traits: small hermaphrodite flowers in
dense racemes in this case, small unisex flowers in dense pseudor-
acemes for S. johnstonii and medium-sized hermaphrodite solitary
flowers for L. divaricata.
Individuals of N. flexuosa var. depressa (15 individuals),
S. johnstonii (7 individuals), and L. divaricata (10 individuals) were
randomly selected based on their abundance at the study site, with a
distance of at least 5 m between the same species. From each
individual, two shoot/branch tips with 10 to 30 buds were randomly
selected to establish “floral units” (except for S. johnstonii for which
four shoots/branches were selected from each individual). This
method is commonly preferred over selecting individual flowers
whenever species produce small flowers (maximum 2 cm in diam-
eter) arranged in clusters with weak peduncles (FAO, 2011).
To avoid an overestimation of cross-pollination, all pre-opened
flowers were manually removed using scissors, and the buds were
covered with tulle bags. In summary, each individual had six bagged
branches and two marked branches without bags (to allow open
pollination). Periodic visits were conducted to monitor bud open-
ing within the bags. Once the buds had opened, one of the following
treatments was applied to two branches:
- Cross-pollination: Each floral unit was enclosed in a tulle bag
and, once the flowers had opened, manual pollination was
performed using pollen from other plants of the same species.
To achieve this objective, open flowers from different individ-
uals within the same population were placed in a sterilized flask.
Pollen was then collected with a brush directly from the flask
and carefully applied to the stigma of the bagged flowers, with
the transfer of pollen being confirmed visually.
- Self-pollination: Each floral unit was enclosed in a tulle bag, and
upon flower opening, manual pollination was performed using
pollen from the same plant. A brush was used to collect pollen
from open flowers of the same individual, which was then
meticulously applied to the stigma of the bagged flowers, ensur-
ing visual confirmation of pollen transfer.
- Spontaneous pollination: Each floral unit was enclosed in a tulle
bag and not manually pollinated. These flowers were neither
artificially pollinated nor visited by pollinators.
- Open-pollination: Marked floral units were left without tulle
bags, to allow for natural pollination.
The selected flowers were monitored weekly until fruit production.
Subsequently, the fruits were collected and the seeds were manually
extracted from them in order to assess seed set and seed germin-
ation capacity. Seed set was calculated by dividing the number of
seeds obtained by the number of ovules per flower in the floral unit.
Each L. divaricata flower contains five ovules, whereas S. johnstonii
flowers are uniovulate. Due to the variable number of ovules, and
therefore seeds, in the legumes of N. flexuosa var. depressa (10–30),
seed set was calculated as the ratio of the number of seeds per
legume harvested from branches with open pollination to the
number of seeds per legume harvested from bagged branches.
To estimate germination capacity, the recommended pre-
germination treatment for each species was applied. The seeds
were then placed in Petri dishes with moistened cotton and filter
paper and transferred to a germination chamber under controlled
conditions of constant light and temperature (24-hour continuous
light at 25°C). The number of replicates and seeds per replicate varied
depending on the number of seeds obtained from the treatments for

(Hernandez et al.,2020) and then placed in groups of 30 in five Petri

ments = 600 seeds). S. johnstonii seeds did not undergo a pre-
germination treatment (Zeberio and Pérez,2020) but were immersed
in warm water for 24 hours to accelerate imbibition. Ten seeds per
replicate were then placed in five Petri dishes per treatment (10 seeds

Results and discussion
The findings for L. divaricata indicated that there was a significant
effect of the treatments applied (LRT = 37.31, p < 0.0001). Seed set
was higher under open pollination than under the three manual
pollination treatments ( Figure 1A), suggesting that natural pollin-
ator activity significantly enhances reproductive success. This pattern
may be related to the generalist pollination strategy of the species,
which was identified as the most generalist plant species within the
Monte pollination network studied by Tadey et al. (2009), interacting
with a broad diversity of floral visitors. In addition, Vázquez et al.
(2012) demonstrated that pollinator species differ markedly in their
contribution to plant reproductive success, highlighting the import-
ance of interaction quality in addition to visitation frequency. Thus,
flowers exposed to natural pollination may receive larger amounts of
compatible pollen, pollen from multiple donors, or repeated visits by
highly effective pollinators, resulting in greater seed production than
that achieved through manual pollination. Furthermore, Rossi et al.
(1999) reported incomplete protogyny and early stigma receptivity in
Larrea flowers, traits that favor the receipt of outcross pollen before
self-pollen becomes available.
Although autonomous selfing can provide reproductive assur-
ance in L. divaricata when pollinators are scarce, our results sup-
port the hypothesis proposed by Rossi et al. ( 1999) that floral traits
favor the receipt of outcross pollen. In this context, the greater seed
set observed in open-pollinated flowers suggests not only a prefer-
ence for outcross pollen, but also that reproductive success is
strongly dependent on pollinator activity.
The self-incompatibility index for L. divaricata was 0.55, indi-
cating a moderate degree of self-compatibility in the species. In
addition, the spontaneous self-pollination index was 0.90, suggest-
ing a notable capacity for self-pollination. These findings are con-
sistent with Tadey et al. ( 2009), who classified the species as
facultatively self-compatible. Together, these indices suggest that
while the species is capable of autonomous reproduction, cross-
pollination mediated by floral visitors plays a major role in maxi-
mizing reproductive success under natural conditions.
The significant effect of pollination treatment, as indicated by
the beta-binomial GLMM, denotes that reproductive success in
S. johnstonii is influenced by pollen availability and/or pollen
source (LRT = 9.89, p < 0.05). However, the comparatively weak
pairwise differences observed among the treatments suggest that

pollination (Figure 1B). The production of seeds in bagged flowers,
including those that did not receive manual pollination, suggests the
existence of alternative mechanisms contributing to reproductive
success. One plausible explanation is wind-mediated pollen transfer,
as the bags used in this study effectively excluded floral visitors but
did not prevent the arrival of airborne pollen. This interpretation is
consistent with findings reported forSchinus pilifera, a closely related
species, in which seed set was recorded in bagged inflorescences
and an ambophilous pollination system was proposed (Benci Arqued
et al., 2019). In that species, both experimental evidence and floral
traits commonly associated with anemophily, including unisexual
flowers, exposed reproductive organs, and a low ovule number per
flower (also present in S. johnstonii), support the contribution of
wind pollination to reproductive success.
The potential significance of wind pollination inS. johnstoniimay
be further enhanced by its reproductive phenology. The species
flowers early in the season, exhibiting a restricted flowering period,
with flowering beginning in September and concluding in early
October (Steibel and Troiani, 2008). The flowering period of other
dominant entomophilous shrubs and herb species begins in mid-
October and can extend until February (Naab and Tamame, 2007;
Tamame, 2011), which is consistent with the peak pollinator activity
(Michelette and Camargo, 2000; Peralta et al., 2017). Consequently,
early flowering (in periods of relatively low pollinator activity)
increases the importance of alternative pollen-transfer mechanisms
that provide reproductive assurance when insect visitation is limited.
Furthermore, studies conducted on other species of Schinus have
reported the presence of a thick stigmatic membrane (Zapater et al.,
2018; Benci Arqued et al., 2019). In the event of S. johnstonii
presenting similar structures, variation in fruit set may be influenced
not solely by the quantity of pollen deposited but also by the
effectiveness of pollinators in disrupting it (Zapater et al., 2018).
An additional explanation for fruit production in flowers that
were not manually pollinated is the occurrence of facultative apo-
mixis. While the present results are unable to demonstrate apo-
mictic reproduction, the formation of fruits under pollinator-
exclusion treatments raises this possibility and warrants further
investigation. However, given that the exclusion bags did not
entirely prevent the arrival of wind-borne pollen, the current
experimental design is unable to distinguish between apomixis
and wind-mediated pollination. Research conducted on other spe-
cies of Schinus has indicated the presence of apomixis in some cases,
while in others, it has been ruled out (Lenzi and Orth,2004; Iponga,
2010). Consequently, it can be concluded that any inferences
regarding apomixis in S. johnstonii should be considered prelim-
inary until experiments specifically designed to exclude all external
pollen sources can be conducted. The results of this study indicate
that S. johnstonii may possess a reproductive strategy that is more
flexible than expected for a functionally dioecious shrub. Wind
pollination is potentially playing an important role in reproductive
assurance under the environmental conditions of the Monte eco-
system. This strategy, which combines insect- and wind-mediated
pollination, may be particularly advantageous in arid environ-
ments, where pollinator availability is often unpredictable (Hall
et al., 2026; Pereira-Guaqueta et al., 2026).
No fruits were obtained fromN. flexuosa var. depressa; therefore,
statistical analyses could not be performed in its case. Despite the
fact that this species produces numerous inflorescences, each com-
prising a considerable number of flowers, there are precedents for
significant losses between this stage and fruit formation in related
species (Agüero, 2009). In N. flexuosa var. flexuosa, the proportion
of inflorescences that successfully initiate fruit development is less
than 3%, and of those, only half or a third complete the process
(Solbrig and Cantino, 1975). This could explain why, by chance, the
inflorescences selected for our treatments did not produce legumes.
To establish whether different pollination types also affect seed
functionality, we evaluated the germination capacity of seeds obtained
from each pollination treatment. InL. divaricata, seeds obtained from
cross-pollination and open-pollination treatments showed higher
germination rates than those obtained from the other treatments
(Figure 2), which reinforces the importance of pollen exchange for
the functionality of seeds in this species. On the other hand, the pre-
germination treatment applied in this study resulted in a higher
germination capacity than that reported by Hernandez et al. (2020)
for the same species. This difference could be attributed to the seed
scarification in the mentioned study, which employed a mechanical
tool, while in this experiment, scarification was done manually, seed
by seed, allowing for greater precision and uniformity in the treatment
of the seed coat.
None of the S. johnstonii seeds germinated under the conditions

for this species. Zeberio and Pérez (2020) and Diaz Arias et al. (2020)

experiments with twice as many seeds as those used in this study.
Therefore, this, combined with the low number of seeds obtained
from our treatments, could explain the inability to obtain analyzable
data to assess differences in germination capacity for this species.
Our hypothesis that these species rely strongly on cross-pollination
was only partially supported. In the case of L. divaricata, the highest
seed set was obtained under open-pollination treatment. Furthermore,
seeds produced through open- and cross-pollination showed superior
germination capacity to those obtai ned from self-pollination treat-
ments. These findings suggest that pollen exchange substantially
enhances reproductive success. These results are consistent with our
prediction that self-pollination would reduce both seed production and
seed performance. In contrast,S. johnstoniiproduced seeds even under
pollinator-exclusion treatments, suggesting the possible contribution
of wind pollination or other reproductive assurance mechanisms. This
finding partially contradicts our prediction that self-pollination would

Conclusions
Our results demonstrate that reproductive success in the studied
shrub species depends on both the quantity and source of pollen,
although the relative importance of different pollination mechanisms
varies among species. In L. divaricata, open-pollinated flowers pro-
duced more seeds, and these exhibited higher germination capacity
than those produced by self-pollinated flowers. This finding indicates
that although the species is capable of autonomous selfing,
pollinator-mediated cross-pollination maximizes reproductive suc-
cess and seed quality. These findings support previous evidence that
the floral traits of this species favor outcrossing while retaining self-
compatibility as a mechanism of reproductive assurance.
In S. johnstonii, the study of seed production under pollinator-
exclusion treatments suggests that reproductive success may not
depend exclusively on insect visitation. The results obtained are
consistent with a potential contribution of wind pollination and/or
alternative reproductive mechanisms that provide reproductive
assurance in response to the challenges associated with early flower-
ing. However, further studies specifically designed to exclude air-
borne pollen are required to evaluate these hypotheses.
Conclusions could not be drawn for N. flexuosa var. depressa as
no fruits were produced in the selected experimental units. Never-
theless, the absence of fruit set is consistent with the high levels of
flower abortion and fruit loss reported for related taxa, thus empha-
sizing the necessity for further research involving a greater number
of reproductive structures.
The study under review here demonstrates that the species of
shrubs in question at the Monte-Espinal transition possess a wide
variety of reproductive strategies, and underscores the ecological
importance of maintaining pollination and pollen transfer pro-
cesses. It is imperative to conserve pollinator communities and
the environmental conditions that facilitate pollen movement in
order to sustain seed production, restoration processes, and the
long-term resilience of these ecosystems.
