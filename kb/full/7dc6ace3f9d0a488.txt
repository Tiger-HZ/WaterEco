ABSTRACT 28 
Objective: This study investigated the effects of neutral detergent fiber (NDF) content and 29 
NDF source (forage- or concentrate-derived) on growth performance, rumen characteristics, 30 
nutrient digestibility, and methane (CH4) concentrations in growing Hanwoo heifers. 31 
Methods: Thirty heifers (296 ± 25.7 kg body weight [BW]; 10 months old) were blocked by 32 
BW and assigned to three treatments: high forage-to-concentrate ratio (42.5:57.5) with high -33 
energy concentrate (HFHC), low forage -to-concentrate ratio (30:70) with low -energy 34 
concentrate (LFLC), and low forage-to-concentrate ratio (30:70) with high-energy concentrate 35 
(LFHC). NDF-level effects were assessed by contrasting HFHC and LFLC with LFHC, 36 
whereas the NDF-source contrast compared HFHC with LFLC at similar total NDF levels. Dry 37 
matter intake (DMI) was recorded daily and BW every four weeks. Rumen fluid and fecal 38 
samples were collected, and CH4 concentrations measured, after 12 weeks. 39 
Results: Average daily gain (ADG) and feed conversion ratio were unaffected by NDF level 40 
or source ( P > 0.05), whereas DMI tended to increase with lower NDF ( P = 0.099). Lower 41 
NDF reduced NDF digestibility and increased total volatile fatty acid concentration (P < 0.05), 42 
decreased acetate proportion and acetate -to-propionate ratio, and increas ed propionate and 43 
butyrate proportions (P < 0.001). NDF source did not affect nutrient digestibility (P > 0.05). In 44 
the NDF -source contrast, LFLC had lower acetate and propionate and higher butyrate 45 
proportions than HFHC ( P < 0.05). Lower NDF also decrease d CH4 concentration per DMI 46

INTRODUCTION 55 
Alongside global efforts to reduce greenhouse gases, reducing methane (CH 4) emissions 56 
from ruminants, which account for 16% of global CH 4 emissions [1], and represent a loss of 57 
2–12% of gross energy intake in ruminants [2], remains an important area of research in 58 
ruminant nutrition. Various strategies have been developed for reducing CH 4 emissions in 59 
ruminants through animal and feed management, dietary strategies, and manipulation of the 60 
rumen, with numerous studies conducted on these approaches [3,4]. 61 
Dietary strategies are considered practical approaches that can reduce CH 4 emissions from 62 
ruminants while minimizing negative impacts on productivity [5]. Various dietary strategies, 63 
including the supplementation of oils or fats, adjusting feed ratios, and additive s, have been 64 
evaluated for their effect on reducing CH4 emissions [6,7]. Particularly, reducing dietary neutral 65 
detergent fiber (NDF) level s or improving its digestibilit y has been considered an effective 66 
nutritional strategy to mitigate CH4 emissions [8]. Lowering dietary NDF levels by increasing 67 
the proportion of concentrate mix in the diet can result in lower CH4 emissions compared with 68 
high-forage diets characterized by high dietary NDF levels [2,9]. This reduction may be partly 69 
associated with the fermentation characteristics of starch contained in grains. Unlike structural 70 
carbohydrates in forages, rapid starch degradation can shift ruminal fermentation pathways 71 
toward propionate and butyrate production, which may reduce the availability of metabolic H2 72 
for methanogenesis [10-12]. Conversely, diets high in fibrous forages are generally associated 73 
with greater acetate production and H 2 generation, which can favor CH4 formation [13]. 74 
Beyond fermen tation pathways, the physical kinetics of digestion may also influence CH4 75 
production. High-concentrate intake can increase the passage rate of digesta and may shorten 76 
ruminal retention time, thereby limiting the extent of microbial fermentation and potent ially 77 
contributing to lower CH4 emissions [3,14]. Additionally, rapid fermentation of grains can 78

decrease ruminal pH, which may suppress methanogenic activity and alter protozoal 79 
populations, potentially contributing to reduced CH4 formation [15]. 80 
Various studies have investigated the effects of varying dietary NDF levels through the 81 
adjustment of forage-to-concentrate (FC) ratios on CH4 emissions. Aguerre et al. [16] reported 82 
that reducing the forage proportion from 68% to 47% in lactating Holstein cows, which lowered 83 
dietary NDF levels from 3 9% to 31% dry matter (DM), resulted in an approximately 17% 84 
decrease in CH4 emissions (P < 0.05). Similarly, Olijhoek et al. [17] observed that reducing the 85 
forage ratio from 70% to 40% in Holstein and Jersey dairy cows, thereby decreasing NDF 86 
levels by approximately 6% (from 3 4% to 27% DM), led to a reduction in average CH 4 87 
emissions of about 10% (P < 0.05). These results were also observed in beef cattle. Lovett et 88 
al. [18] found that increasing the proportion of concentrate mix from 35% to 90% in finishing 89 
heifers, which reduced NDF levels by approximately 14% DM, increased average daily gain 90 
(ADG) by 0.4 kg and decreased CH4 emissions by approximately 18% (P < 0.01). Kang et al. 91 
[19] also reported that increasing the concentrate mix proportion by 10% (from 6 5% to 75%) 92 
in growing Hanwoo steers, which reduced NDF levels by 11% DM, decreased CH 4 emissions 93 
via respiration and eructation by approximately 33% and 36%, respectively ( P < 0.05). 94 
Furthermore, a ccording to a meta-analysis by Arndt et al. [3] , reducing the FC ratio (i.e., 95 
decrease NDF level in diets) led to a 9% decrease in CH4 emissions per milk production and 96 
weight gain. These results are attributed to the fact that a lower FC ratio increases feed intake, 97 
milk production, and weight gain without increasing CH4 emissions. 98 
Although the effects of reducing dietary NDF through adjustment of the FC ratio on CH 4 99 
emissions are well established, mos t previous studies have focused primarily on changes in 100 
total NDF content, largely through manipulation of the FC ratio. However, changes in forage 101 
inclusion are often accompanied by simultaneous changes in both total NDF content and the 102

relative contribution of forage-derived NDF. In addition, as the inclusion of fibrous ingredients 103 
such as agricultural by-products in concentrate mixes has increased, a substantial proportion of 104 
total dietary NDF may originate from the concentrate fraction rather than from forage [20]. 105 
Forage-derived NDF ( fNDF) and concentrate -derived NDF (cNDF) may differ markedly in 106 
physical effectiveness, fermentability, and their effects on H2 metabolism, a precursor process 107 
for methanogenesis [14,21]. Unlike chemically determined NDF concentration, physical 108 
effectiveness reflects the ability of fiber to stimulate chewing and contribute to ruminal mat 109 
formation and is influenced by physical characteristics such as particle size [21]. Considering 110 
both dietary NDF level and the predominant source of NDF may provide a more 111 
comprehensive understanding of their relations hips with ruminal fermentation, productivity, 112 
and CH4 emissions and may contribute to the development of effective dietary strategies for 113 
CH4 mitigation. Despite this relevance, relatively few studies have considered dietary NDF 114 
level and predominant NDF s ource within the same experimental framework, and, to our 115 
knowledge, no such studies have evaluated these two NDF characteristics together in growing 116 
Hanwoo heifers. Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate the effects of NDF 117 
content and its source on growth performance, rumen characteristics, nutrient digestibility, and 118 
enteric CH4 concentrations in growing Hanwoo heifers. 119

Animals, housing, and diets 127 
Thirty 10-month-old Hanwoo heifers (296 ± 25.7 kg body weight [BW]) were used in this 128 
experiment. The heifers were stratified into five blocks based on initial BW, with each block 129 
consisting of six heifers housed in three pens. Two heifers of similar BW were housed together 130 
in each pen (5 m × 5 m), and the three pens within each block were randomly assigned to one 131 
of the three dietary treatments according to a randomized complete block design [22], resulting 132 
in five pens per treatment. Each pen was equipped with one TMR feed bunk that allowed 133 
individual feed intake to be recorded automatically by identifying each animal using a radio -134 
frequency identification tag (Dawoon Co., Incheon, Korea). The experiment lasted for three 135 
months, after a seven-day adaptation period. 136 
The diet was prepared as a total mixed ration (TMR) based on oat hay, perennial ryegrass, 137 
corn, corn gluten feed, palm kernel meal, and other ingredients. The TMR was adjusted to vary 138 
the FC ratio, with the energy levels being regulated through low energy concentrate mix (LC) 139 
and high energy concentrate mix (HC). There were three dietary treatments: 1) high FC ratio 140 
with HC (HFHC, 42.5% forage, and 57.5% HC), 2) low FC ratio with LC (LFLC; 30% forage 141 
and 70% LC), and 3) low FC ratio with HC (LFHC; 30% forage and 70% HC). The diets were 142 
formulated to meet nutrient requirements for target ADG of 650 g/d for both the HFHC and 143 
LFLC treatments and 800 g/d for the LFHC treatment, according to the Korean feeding 144 
standards for Hanwoo heifers [20]. The ingredient combinations were selected to create 145 
contrasts in total NDF level and predominant NDF source. HFHC combined a higher forage 146 
proportion with a high-energy concentrate, whereas LFLC contained more fibrous concentrate 147 
ingredients, particularly palm kernel meal, to achieve a similar total NDF concentration. LFHC 148 
combined the lower forage proportion with a high-energy concentrate to achieve a lower NDF 149 
concentration. The higher target ADG for LFHC reflected its accompanying energy density 150

rather than an independently imposed growth treatment. Consequently, the NDF-level contrast 151 
also involved differences in energy supply, and the NDF-source contrast involved differences 152

factor.[23] The formulation and chemical composition of the experimental diets are presented 154

drinking water were freely accessible to the animals throughout the experiment. During the 156 
experiment, one heifer (LFLC treatment) was excluded from the study due to health issues and 157 
was therefore omitted from all sampling and data analysis. 158

Measurement and sample collection 160 
The individual daily intake was recorded individually through the TMR feed bunk (Dawoon, 161 
Co., Incheon, Korea). The TMR feed bunk recognizes the heifer's neck radio-frequency 162 
identification tag and automatically records the indivi dual intake by measuring the weight 163 
difference before entry and after exit of the heifer, using two installed load cells. Every four 164 
weeks, all daily feed intake records for each heifer were collected and processed separately. 165 
The mean and standard deviation were calculated from the daily intake records of each heifer 166 
during each four -week period. Intakes that were more or less than three times the standard 167 
deviation from the mean intake were treated as outliers and removed. The intakes of the day 168 
when management activities took place that could have affected feed intakes, such as bedding 169 
replacement, BW measurement, and sampling periods, were also removed. The BW was 170 
measured every four weeks before morning feeding, and the feed samples for chemical 171 
composition analysis were sampled once every four weeks. 172

treatment immediately before the digestibility sampling period. Selection was performed at the 174

Rumen fluid of all heifers was collected three times (–1, +3, and +6 h after morning feeding) 182

Methane concentrations of all heifers were measured four times daily (–2, –1, +1, and +2 h 189

calculated independently from each measurement, and the mean of the two measurements was 196 
used as the individual an imal value for subsequent statistical analysis . The LMD measures 197 
path-integrated CH4 concentration in ppm·m [16]. At the fixed measurement distance of 1 m, 198

RESULTS 280 
Neither NDF level nor NDF source had a significant effect on ADG and feed conversion 281 
ratio (FCR, DMI [g]/ADG [g]; Table 3; P > 0.05). However, dry matter intake (DMI) tended 282 
to increase as the dietary NDF level decreased (P = 0.099; DMI means of 7.17, 7.46, and 8.02 283 
kg/day for HFHC, LFLC, and LFHC, respectively), while the NDF source did not significantly 284 
influence DMI (P > 0.05). 285 
Neither NDF level nor NDF source affected rumen fluid pH (Table 4; P > 0.05). The overall 286 
treatment effect on NH 3-N concentration was significant ( P = 0.047), with higher values in 287 
LFLC and LFHC than in HFHC (11.1 and 11.7 vs. 7.6 mg/dL, respectively; Table 4). In the 288 
planned contrasts, NH3-N concentration was not affected by NDF level (P > 0.05), but tended 289 
to be affected by NDF source ( P = 0.052). Decreasing NDF level significantly affected total 290 
VFA concentration, with LFHC exhibiting the highest VFA concentration (Table 4; P < 0.05). 291 
Reducing the dietary NDF level significantly altered VFA proportions (Table 4; P < 0.001). A 292 
lower NDF level led to a significant decrease in the proportion of acetate (P < 0.001), resulting 293

DISCUSSION 335 
Reducing CH 4 emissions from ruminants is a primary focus of global greenhouse gas 336 
mitigation efforts. Nutriti onal strategies, particularly the modulation of dietary NDF levels, 337 
have been widely recognized as effective methods to reduce CH 4 emissions. However, most 338 
previous studies have focused primarily on total NDF levels or FC ratios, often overlooking 339 
potential differences arising from the source of the fiber. Since fNDF and cNDF possess distinct 340 
Accepted Article
16 
 
physicochemical and fermentative characteristics, they may influence rumen fermentation and 341 
methanogenesis differently. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of not only 342 
NDF levels but also NDF sources on growth performance, rumen characteristics, nutrient 343 
digestibility, and CH4 concentrations in growing Hanwoo heifers. 344 
It is generally accepted that DMI in ruminants is negatively correlated with dietary NDF 345 
concentration due to the physical filling effect [33,34]. In the present study, although the effect 346 
was not significant, DMI tended to increase as dietary NDF level decreased (P = 0.099), with 347 
no difference between NDF sources (P > 0.05). The relatively small increase in DMI observed 348 
in the low-NDF group may reflect a greater contribution of metabolic regulation of feed intake 349 
relative to physical fill [35]. Although the LFHC group was fed the diet with the lowest NDF 350 
level, which could be expected to reduce physical fill in the rumen, its higher energy density 351 
may have promoted metabolic satiety and thereby limited further increases in feed consumption 352 
[33]. Previous research has also reported that DMI was not affected by increased NDF level in 353 
animals consuming high -energy diets, despite variations in dietary starch and NDF levels 354 
[16,19]. 355 
Generally, reducing dietary NDF levels implies an increase in the proportion of readily 356 
fermentable carbohydrates, which is expected to stimulate rumen fermentation and improve 357 
nutrient digestibility, potentially leading to enhanced growth performance [34]. Serment et al. 358 
[36] reported that organic matter (OM) digestibility improved by approximately 6% (P < 0.05) 359 
in goats when dietary NDF levels were decreased by adjusting the concentrate mix proportion 360 
of the TMR from 30% to 70% DM. Similarly, Chen et al. [34] observed an improvement in 361 
OM digestibility (59% vs. 67% DM; P < 0.05) and an increase in ADG (0.75 vs. 1.1 kg/day; P 362 
< 0.05) in Angus cows when dietary N DF levels were decreased by raising the dietary 363 
concentrate mix ratio by 30%. However, in this study, despite differences in dietary NDF levels 364 
Accepted Article
17 
 
as well as dietary energy levels, ADG did not differ among treatments ( P > 0.05). Moreover, 365 
decreasing NDF levels significantly reduced the digestibility of CP and NDF (Table 5; P < 366 
0.05). The lower nutrient digestibility may have contributed to the absence of a detectable 367 
growth response despite the higher dietary energy density; however, this association does not 368 
establish a causal relations hip. Although ruminal retention time was not directly measured in 369 
the present study, one possible explanation for the lower fiber digestibility observed at the 370 
lower NDF level is a reduction in ruminal retention time. Dietary NDF plays a critical role in 371 
retarding the flow of digesta and forming a physical matrix within the rumen, providing 372 
sufficient time for microbes to attach to and degrade feed particles [37]; however, when dietary 373 
NDF levels are reduced, as in the LFHC group, ingested feed may pass through the rumen more 374 
quickly, potentially reducing the time available for microbial fermentation. Therefore, a faster 375 
passage rate could have reduced the time available for microbial degradation, particularly for 376 
fiber, which generally requires a longer period for digestion [38]. Furthermore, the high NFC 377 
level corresponding to low NDF levels may have induced a negative associative effect on the 378 
ruminal microbial ecosystem. When the proportion of rapidly fermentable carbohydrates is 379 
high, starch-degrading bacteria may dominate in substrate competition against fiber-degrading 380 
bacteria or cause acidification of the local micro -environment, thereby inhibiting the activity 381 
of fibrolytic enzymes. Furthermore , one possible explanation for the reduction in CP 382 
digestibility observed at lower NDF levels is increased fecal nitrogen excretion resulting from 383 
shifts in the site of fermentation. Low dietary NDF levels may accelerate the passage rate, 384 
reducing the extent of carbohydrate fermentation in the rumen and allowing more fermentable 385 
carbohydrates to reach the large intestine [39]. These shifts may promote microbial growth in 386 
the hindgut. While microbial protein synthesized in the rumen is largely digested and absorbed 387 
in the small intestine with only a small portion excreted, microbial protein synthesized in the 388 
Accepted Article
18 
 
hindgut is completely excret ed in the feces [40]. Since apparent digestibility calculations do 389 
not distinguish between undigested dietary nitrogen and metabolic fecal nitrogen (including 390 
microbial debris), a possible increase in fecal microbial nitrogen cou ld lower apparent CP 391 
digestibility without demonstrating a reduction in dietary protein digestion. Passage rate, site 392 
of digestion, and fecal microbial nitrogen were not measured in this study. 393 
In this study, reducing the NDF level increased the total VFA concentration, whereas it 394 
decreased the proportion of acetate and increased those of propionate and butyrate (Table 4; P 395 
< 0.05). These changes are attributed to the high NFC level in the low-NDF diet. Compared to 396 
structural carbohydrates, NFC are degraded more rapidly and extensively by rumen microbes, 397 
thereby increasing the fermentation rate and the total production of organic acids [41]. 398 
Furthermore, high d ietary NFC levels promote starch degradation in the rumen and shift 399 
fermentation pathways toward propionate production, consequently decreasing acetate and 400 
increasing propionate proportion [14]. These shifts are consistent with changes in the 401 
availability of fermentation substrates, as reduced fiber availability and increased fermentable 402 
starch supply generally favor lower acetate and greater propionate production [42]. Moreover, 403 
the molar proportion of butyrate increased as NDF levels declined, which is a typical response 404 
to diets rich in N FC. Rapid fermentation of NFC can increase lactat e production, which can 405 
subsequently be converted to butyrate through established ruminal fermentation pathways [42]. 406 
Previous studies [34,36,43,44] have also reported results consistent with the findings of the 407 
present study. 408 
In addition to the NDF level, the source of NDF also significantly influenced the ruminal 409 
VFA fermentation profile. Specifically, the group fed cNDF (LFLC) exhibited significantly 410 
lower proportions of acetate and propionate compared to the group fed fNDF (HFHC; Table 4; 411 
P < 0.05). Conversely, the proportion of butyrate was significantly higher in the LFL C group 412

19 
 
(P < 0.001). Interestingly, despite the simultaneous reduction in acetate and propionate in the 413 
LFLC group, there was no significant difference in the A:P ratio between the two groups (P > 414 
0.05). These results indicate that the LFLC diet was associ ated with a shift in the overall 415 
fermentation pattern toward alternative VFAs without disrupting the balance between acetate 416 
and propionate. The higher acetate proportion observed in the HFHC group may be associated 417 
with its high forage (fNDF) level, as structural carbohydrates in forage are primarily fermented 418 
into acetate by cellulolytic bacteria [45]. Dietary starch can favor propionate formation [11], 419 
but the analyzed starch concentration was not higher in HFHC than in LFLC (136 vs. 148 g/kg 420 
DM; Table 2). Therefore, starch concentrat ion alone does not explain the higher propionate 421 
proportion in HFHC. Differences in ingredient composition and carbohydrate availability may 422 
also have contributed to the higher butyrate proportion in LFLC. [14] These results appear to 423 
be attributable to th e fact that the cNDF in the LFLC diet was primarily composed of palm 424 
kernel meal (331 g/kg DM). Palm kernel meal is a representative fibrous by-product containing 425 
high levels of hemicellulose, particularly mannans [46]. These hemicellulosic substrates serve 426 
as a preferred energy source for Butyrivibrio fibrisolvens [47]. Butyrivibrio fibrisolvens 427 
efficiently hydrolyzes these structural polysaccharides and produces butyrate as a primary 428 
metabolic end-product [48]. 429 
Decreasing the dietary NDF level significantly reduced total CH 4 concentrations, CH4 430 
concentration per DMI and ADG (ppm/kg) from both respiration and eructation (Table 6; P < 431 
0.05). These findings are consistent with previous studies [16,18,19] reporting that increasing 432 
NFC and decreasing NDF lead to reduced CH 4 concentrations. In this study, the reduction in 433 
NDF level shifted the fermentation pattern towards lower acetate and higher propionate 434 
proportions, resulting in a significantly decreased A:P ratio ( P < 0.05). These shifts may help 435 
explain the lower CH 4 concentrations, although hydrogen fluxes and microbial populations 436

20 
 
were not measured. Stoichiometrically, the fermentation pathway yieldin g propionate 437 
consumes metabolic H2, whereas the pathway producing acetate releases H2, the principal 438 
substrate for methanogenesis [14]. Consequently, the shift toward propionate production 439 
reduces the total H2 pool available to methanogens, thereby limiting CH 4 synthesis. 440 
Furthermore, the reduction in fiber digestibility observed at lower NDF levels supports this 441 
mechanism. The reduced fiber digestion may be consistent with lower activity of fibrolytic 442 
bacteria, which are the major producers of acetate and the primary suppliers of H2 to 443 
methanogens [49]. However, no significant differences in ruminal pH were detected among 444 
treatments in the present study (P > 0.05). These results may be attributable to the limitations 445 
of the rumen sampling method used in this study. The oral stomach tube technique employed 446 
for ruminal sampling may have reduced sensitivity in detecting low pH conditions [50], and 447 
pH was measured only from ruminal fluid collected at three time points (–1, +3, and +6 h after 448 
the morning feeding). Consequently, this approach could not capture the full 24 -hour 449 
fluctuations in ruminal pH. 450 
In this study, not only the dietary NDF level but also the NDF source significantly affected 451 
CH4 concentrations (Table 6; P < 0.05). Interestingly, despite maintaining an NDF level similar 452 
to that of the HFHC group and showing no significant difference (P > 0.05) in the A:P ratio—453 
a key indicator of the stoichiometric potential for CH4 production—the LFLC group exhibited 454 
significantly lower CH 4 concentrations from both respiration and eructation than the HFHC 455 
group (Table 6; P < 0.0 5). One possible explanation for these results may be related to 456 
differences in the physical characteristics of forage - and concentrate -derived fiber. 457 
Concentrate-derived fiber may have a smaller particle size and greater functional specific 458 
gravity than f orage-derived fiber, which could affect ruminal mat formation and passage 459 
kinetics [21]. Such physical attributes could facilitate movement of particles out of the ruminal 460

21 
 
mat and increase passage through the reticulo-omasal orifice [18]. Faster passage could shorten 461 
the time available for fermentation, but similar fiber digestibility in LFLC and HFHC (Table 5; 462 
P > 0.05) does not establish either faster passage or faster degradation. A possible difference 463 
between the time required for fiber degradation and for methanogen colonization and 464 
syntrophic H2 transfer has been proposed in the literature [48,49]; its contribution to the present 465 
concentration-based responses remains hypothetical. [21,51,52]Moreover, the significant 466 
difference in CH 4 concentrations from respiration and eructation resulted in a significant 467 
difference in CH 4 concentration per ADG (ppm/kg) , even though the NDF source did not 468 
significantly affect ADG (Table 6; P < 0.05). Intere stingly, however, while DMI, like ADG, 469 
was not significantly influenced by the NDF source, the NDF source did not exert a significant 470 
effect on CH 4 concentration per DMI (ppm/kg; Table 6; P > 0.05). The different statistical 471 
outcomes for CH4 concentration per DMI and per ADG (ppm/kg) may reflect variation in their 472 
denominators and associated uncertainty. A nonsignificant difference in the DMI -based ratio 473 
does not demonstrate equivalent hydrogen production or methane -generating capacity of the 474 
two fiber sources. Furthermore, the moderate reduction in total CH4 concentrations in the LFLC 475 
group was partially offset by a numerical increase in DMI (i.e., a 4% increase relative to HFHC), 476 
rendering the reduction in CH4 concentration per DMI (ppm/kg) statistically indistinguishable 477 
given individual animal variations. In contrast, CH4 concentration per ADG (ppm/kg) exhibited 478 
a significant decline. Because CH4 concentration per ADG (ppm/kg) is expressed as the ratio 479 
of CH4 concentration to ADG, the lower value observed in LFLC occurred concurrently with 480 
significantly lower CH4 concentration from respiration and eructation and a numerically 7% 481 
higher ADG than HFHC. However, the difference in ADG was not statistically significant and 482 
therefore cannot be considered evidence of improved growth performance. Accordingly, the 483 
significant reduction in CH4 concentration per ADG (ppm/kg) should be interpreted as lower 484

22 
 
CH4 concentration per unit of ADG, rather than as evidence of a biological interaction or 485 
synergistic effect between CH4 concentration and growth performance. The numerical 486 
difference in ADG warrants further investigation in studies with greater statistical power . A 487 
major limitation is that NDF was analyzed only in the complete TMR, not in the individual 488 
ingredients. Thus, actual fNDF and cNDF concentrations and their proportions of total NDF 489 
could not be quantified. Particle-size distribution, peNDF, functional specific gravity, ruminal 490 
mat formation, and passage rate were not measured. Mechani sms involving these 491 
characteristics therefore re main hypotheses. In addition, differences in palm kernel meal, urea, 492 
and other ingredients and in energy and nutrient composition prevent the responses from being 493 
attributed exclusively to NDF level or source. These accompanying differences, including the 494 
non-protein nitrogen supplied by urea in LFLC, may have contributed to the fermentation 495 
responses. 496

CONCLUSIONS 498 
In conclusion, dietary NDF level and source were associated with changes in ruminal 499 
fermentation and CH 4 concentration responses without significantly affecting growth 500 
performance in growing Hanwoo heifers. Lower dietary NDF was associated with increased 501 
total VFA concentration, decreased acetate proportion and acetate -to-propionate ratio, 502 
increased propionate and butyrate proportions, and lower fiber digestibility. These responses 503 
were accompanied by lower CH4 concentration per DMI and ADG. In the NDF-source contrast, 504 
LFLC exhibited lower acetate and propionate proportions and a higher butyrate proportion than 505 
HFHC, while nutrient digestibility did not differ between the two diets. LFLC also exhibited 506 
lower CH4 concentration per ADG than HFHC, although CH4 concentration per DMI did not 507 
differ between the two diets. Overall, lower dietary NDF levels showed a more consistent 508 
Accepted Article
23 
 
association with lower CH4 concentration-based responses, whereas differences in NDF source 509 
were primarily reflected in ruminal fermenta tion characteristics and CH4 concentration per 510 
ADG. These associations should be interpreted within the dietary contrasts tested because NDF 511 
characteristics were not separated from accompanying differences in ingredients and nutrient 512 
supply. 513
