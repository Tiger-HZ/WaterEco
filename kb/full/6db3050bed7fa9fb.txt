Since its inception, mining in Zambia has predominantly operated as a privately driven enterprise. This framework has consistently failed to deliver meaningful benefits to host communities or to protect the environments in which it operates. Successive legislative and policy frameworks have proven chronically inadequate. Protective provisions are permissive rather than mandatory; enforcement mechanisms remain weak and under-resourced; environmental and social liability waivers are routinely employed; domestic value-addition capacity is limited; acute capital and capability gaps persist between local and foreign investors; and illegal mining continues to thrive in poorly demarcated mineral-rich zones. Through doctrinal legal analysis and an embedded historical approach, the paper argues for a comprehensive overhaul of Zambia's mining governance framework to realign the sector's objectives with the interests of host communities and environmental sustainability. Situated within the Middle Path theory, Dependency theory, Anthropocentric theory, and the Polluter Pays Principle, the analysis establishes that foreign investment can generate economic benefits only where robust regulatory frameworks centre host community interests. The four frameworks are integrated sequentially rather than applied in parallel: Dependency theory explains the structural origins of the extractive model, Middle Path theory supplies the normative standard against which investment governance is evaluated, and the Anthropocentric theory read together with the Polluter Pays Principle furnishes the criteria for allocating environmental liability and the cost of remediation. The paper advocates mandatory national and community equity participation in large-scale mining ventures, a statutory prohibition on liability waivers, strengthened and ring-fenced enforcement institutions, modernisation of geological mapping, and accelerated development of domestic processing capabilities. These reforms are essential to reorient the extractive sector toward sustainable development and equitable benefit-sharing, as envisaged by Article 8 of the Constitution of Zambia and the right to a clean and healthy environment under Article 42.
