Understanding groundwater flow in a buried valley and bedrock 
aquifer system in Kurikka, Finland, using hydrochemical indicators 
and residence time tracers 3H and 14C
Marie‑Amélie Pétré1,3  · Niko Putkinen1  · Timo Ruskeeniemi1  · René Lefebvre2 
Received: 5 September 2025 / Accepted: 21 August 2026 
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract
Growing demand for groundwater in western Finland has drawn attention to a regional network of buried valleys with high 
potential for domestic and municipal water supplies. This study aims at understanding the groundwater flow in the buried 
valley and bedrock aquifer system using hydrochemical data and residence time indicators (3H, 14C). A multivariate statisti-
cal analysis (hierarchical clustering on principal components) revealed six water groups reflecting the origins and different 
stages of groundwater evolution. Overall, a modern groundwater component (< 60 years old) and active flow system are 
present in all parts of the system. Longer residence times are found deeper within the buried valleys (3H-free waters) and at 
greater depth in a few locations in the bedrock (14C ages > 5000 years). The occurrence of geochemically evolved waters in 
the deeper, downgradient areas of buried valleys indicates suitable (in terms of quantity and quality) and safe (low vulner -
ability) water supply relative to demand. More broadly, the multi-tracer approach provides insights into the geochemical 
evolution of groundwater, revealing groundwater flow conditions, geological history and aquifer unit composition. Residence 
time indicators also illustrate the relative hydraulic isolation of buried valleys, which has implications on vulnerability and 
sustainable exploitation. To fully clarify the exchanges between bedrock and granular aquifers, a sampling scheme targeting 
deep flow paths beneath the Paloluoma and Kyrönjoki valleys would be necessary. This study will support the development 
of a numerical model of the aquifer system and contribute to informing future management decisions.

Introduction
Understanding the functioning of hydrosystems is a pre-
requisite for the sustainable management of groundwater 
resources. This is especially challenging in complex con-
texts, such as glacial buried valley aquifer systems where 
sediment heterogeneity, geological structures and exchanges 
with bedrock influence the flow system and recharge pro-
cesses. Nevertheless, buried valley aquifers can repre -
sent significant water resources (Cummings et al. 2012; 
Bajc et al. 2018; Steelman et al. 2018; Yager et al. 2019; 
Goetz et al. 2021; Kehler et al. 2024). However, specific 
challenges remain in understanding the groundwater flow 
dynamics, the hydraulic connections between bedrock and 
granular aquifers, and the groundwater residence time. At 
the regional scale, geochemical multi-tracer approaches are 
useful to understand groundwater origin, flow paths, and 
geochemical evolution. These approaches typically include 
hydrochemical data (major, minor, and trace elements), field 
parameters, isotopic tracers, and other indicators of ground-
water residence time (Cloutier et al. 2006; Jaunat et al. 2012; 
Roques et al. 2014; Pétré et al. 2016; Beaudry et al. 2018). 
A multi-tracer approach is well suited to provide an under -
standing of regional flow systems because hydraulic data 
alone cannot provide clear indications about the provenance

The large number of parameters usually included in a 
multi-tracer study represents a complex dataset and it 
is therefore necessary to apply adapted data analysis and 
interpretation tools, such as multivariate statistical analysis, 
to identify relationships, patterns, and processes affecting 
groundwater geochemistry (Cloutier et al. 2008; Voutsis 
et al. 2015; Yang et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2023). Besides geo-
chemical composition, groundwater residence time indica-
tors can further support the understanding of hydrosystems 
(Jaunat et al. 2012; Pétré et al. 2016; Beaudry et al. 2018). 
Groundwater dating tracers of young groundwater (less than 
65 years) include chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), sulfur hex-
afluoride (SF6), tritium (3H) and helium (3He) while radio-
carbon (14C) and chloride-36 (36Cl) are used to characterize 
old or very old groundwaters (Clark and Fritz 1997; Kazemi 
et al. 2006).
The goal of this study is to examine a regional net-
work of buried valleys by characterizing both the granular 
aquifers within the buried valleys and the adjacent and 
underlying fractured bedrock. This buried valley aquifer 
system is expected to play a key role in the development 
of drinking water resources in western Finland, for the 
cities of Kurikka and Vaasa. Previous geological and 
seismic surveys suggested that the buried valley aquifer 
system might hold significant water resources, mainly due 
to the widespread occurrence of permeable sediments at 
depth within the buried valley (Malinowski et al. 2023; 
Putkinen et al. 2026). A major part of the study area lies 
within the Suupohja passive ice area, where minimal gla-
cial erosion during the latest glaciation has resulted in the 
preservation of an ultra-thick (up to 120 m) sedimentary 
sequence.
Preliminary work by Purkamo et al. (2026) in the central 
Paloluoma Valley, indicated the presence of sub-modern 
water in the deepest aquifers of the buried valleys as well 
as in bedrock. However, this study also showed the signifi-
cant limitations in using the 3H/3He method and CFCs/SF 6 
for groundwater dating in the Kurikka area. These limita-
tions were due to contamination (CFCs), geological influ-
ence affecting SF6 and radiogenic 4He, and the absence of 
those tracers in several sampled locations. Rashid (2022) 
developed a numerical groundwater flow model of one of the 
buried valleys in the Kurikka area and simulations of long-
term pumping tests (several months) indicated a substantial 
groundwater input from the bedrock to the sediments.
In this context, this study aimed to conduct a systematic 
hydrogeochemical assessment of the regional valley aquifer 
system to understand the hydrodynamics of the system, com-
prising both sediments and bedrock. In addition, the study 
aimed to determine the depth of active groundwater flow 
and the relationship between bedrock and sediment aquifers, 
and the interactions between the different buried valleys. 
The study area includes four buried valleys connected to 
the main Kyrönjoki buried valley. Considering the limited 
applicability of some groundwater dating methods in this 
geological context (Purkamo et al. 2026) radiocarbon ( 14C 
and δ13C in dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC)) and 3H were 
used as residence time indicators.
Materials and methods
Study area
The study area (600 km2) is located in western Finland, 
near the municipality of Kurikka, within a subarctic climate 
zone. The landscape is a mix of forested and agricultural 
land. The topographic highs of the study area (194 m a.s.l.) 
are found in the west and the southeast (Pitkämönkylä area) 
while the valleys represent the lowest points, with eleva-
tions as low as 34.5 m a.s.l. The bedrock surface is below 
the modern sea level in many locations. The distance from 
the present shoreline is about 60 km. Four buried valleys are 
connected to the main Kyrönjoki valley located to the east 
of the study area: from north to south, Nenättömänluoma, 
Paloluoma, Häjyluoma, and Lohiluoma valleys (Fig.  1). 
In the study area, annual evapotranspiration is estimated 
to be in the range of 250–300 mm/year (Kolhinen et al. 
2025) while the mean annual precipitation is 573 mm/year 
(Pirinen et al. 2022). Ongoing work indicates that average 
yearly recharge is in the order of 100 mm/year over the 
study area.
Geology and hydrogeology
Kurikka is located within the Archean and Proterozoic 
Fennoscandian Shield. The crystalline Paleoproterozoic 
bedrock in the study area is composed of paragneisses of 
turbiditic graywacke origin in the east, while the buried 
valley sediment aquifers and the western catchments are 
underlined by intrusive granodioritic–granitic–dioritic 
lithologies (Kähkönen 2005; Nironen 2005, 2017). Dis -
tinct N–S, NE–SW, and E–W-trending deformation or 
fault zones characterize the topography and contributed to 
the formation of the valley system (Putkinen et al. 2026; 
Ruuska et al. 2023).
The ancient craton underlying the region has under -
gone a complex, multi-phase history of burial and erosion 
(Hall et al. 2021). The prolonged periods of surface cover 
over the basement, i.e., crystalline bedrock, in conjunc-
tion with the area's position within the stable cratonic 
region, have contributed to the preservation of an exten-
sive and ancient bedrock valley system that traverses the 
region (Putkinen et al. 2026). The area underwent its most

recent phase of exhumation approximately 40 million 
years ago, initiating the latest stage of surface denuda-
tion characterized by intense tropical weathering, which 
altered the upper portions of the bedrock. Over tens of 
millions of years, sedimentary rocks that once covered the 
region were almost entirely removed, culminating during 
the Pleistocene glaciations, which progressively exposed 
the Kyrönjoki River valley along with the adjacent Nenät-
tömänluoma, Paloluoma, Häjyluoma, and Lohiluoma sub-
valleys (Fig.  1). This geomorphological evolution defined 
the major topographic features of the region, characterized 
by alternating hills and valleys. Meanwhile, the sedimen-
tary infill at the base of the valleys was primarily depos-
ited during the last glaciations, resulting in the present-day 
hydrogeological and sedimentological settings (Putkinen 
et al. 2026).
Åberg et al. (2026) described a highly complex sedi-
mentary cover forming the unique aquifer system of the 
region. Moderate topographic variation, in combination 
with the heterogeneous sedimentary deposits and frac-
tured bedrock, control the groundwater flow pathways. 
Infiltrated groundwater in the upland areas is transmitted 
through these routes and flows within the valley aquifers. 
Of particular significance is the lowermost coarse-grained 
sedimentary aquifer unit, which has demonstrated its suit-
ability for high-rate municipal drinking water supply. This 
lowest sedimentary aquifer unit is confined beneath a sed-
imentary sequence that is tens of meters thick, composed 
of alternating aquifer and aquitard units. Together, these 
sedimentary units as well as the underlying fractured bed-
rock constitute a distinctive aquifer system, whose char -
acteristics vary locally due to spatial differences in geo-
logical structure and composition across the study area.
Numerous hydraulic tests conducted in both the sedi-
ments and fractured bedrock indicated the presence of 
extensive, “highly” transmissive zones within the sedi-
ments, the bedrock, and their interface (Ruskeeniemi et al. 
2023, 2024). Measurements and related investigations 
suggested that along the flow paths at the valley margins, 
particularly in sloping bedrock areas, there are multiple 
zones where the hydraulic conductivity of the local till, 
underlying bedrock and bedrock fracture zones and their 
interface is on the order of 10⁻⁶ m/s. Further downslope, 
toward the valley floors, groundwater becomes confined 
beneath aquitard layers composed of fine-grained lacus-
trine sediments and till. These aquitards overlie aquifer 
units whose hydraulic conductivities range from 10⁻ 5 to 
10⁻4 m/s. In these confined zones, flowing artesian condi-
tions are often present. Regionally, groundwater both dis-
charges and flows predominantly eastward in the Kyrönjoki 
River valley region (Fig.  1).
The Nennättömanluoma Valley and the northern part 
of the main Kyrönjoki Valley are different from the other 
valleys in the study area with more shallow sediment 
accumulations and poorly permeable conditions, with the 
exception of last deglaciation eskers occurrence at the top 
of the sedimentary sequence. These areas coincide with 
the principal groundwater recharge zones but are separated 
by the buried till-dominated end moraine zone (Fig.  1). 
The natural dam, formed from fine-grained tills within the 
buried end moraine and the surrounding fine-grained sedi -
ments, allows only slow seepage through it (Putkinen et al. 
2026; Fig.  1), and together with the direction of ground-
water flow from south to north, sets the groundwater level 
approximately 22.5 m higher than in the northern area. 
This creates a unique groundwater environment in the area, 
which has the greatest significance for public water supply 
and also exhibits potential for groundwater extraction; con-
sequently, these northern valleys have been incorporated 
into the study.
The bedrock is exposed on scattered hilly areas. 
Sparsely fractured bedrock has been resistant to weather -
ing and is therefore standing out in the topography. How -
ever, most of the bedrock is hidden under thick overbur -
den in lowlands and can be studied only by drilling or 
geophysics.
Detailed investigations revealed a fracture network in the 
upper part of the bedrock occasionally extending as deep 
as to 120 m (Putkinen et al. 2026). A large part of the frac-
turing is formed within an extensional stress field, gener -
ating tensional fracturing with transmissivity. The fracture 
surfaces are rusted, indicating the circulation of fresh, oxic 
groundwater. Evidently, the recharging meteoric water has 
been weakly acidic, and the flow must have been active for 
considerable time because fracture-infilling calcites are 
depleted from the fracture network. Calcite reappears at the 
deeper levels where the other structural and mineralogical 
flow indicators are absent.
Structural analysis showed that subhorizontal to gently 
dipping fractures are common in the hydrogeologically 
active bedrock unit, consistent with the extensional stress 
and unloading concepts. Hydraulic testing confirmed that 
the horizontal hydraulic conductivity is consistently ele -
vated (10⁻7–10⁻5 m/s) in the upper bedrock underlying the 
sedimentary aquifers situated in the valleys and in the area 
extending toward the western catchment. Bedrock boreholes 
in the center of the buried sedimentary aquifer units show 
flowing artesian behavior, thus indicating the groundwater 
from the bedrock is laterally connected to uplands and has 
a tendency to flow upward under valleys. Nearby boreholes 
are hydraulically connected, and the high yields observed in 
all the ten boreholes drilled in bedrock within the granular 
aquifer system suggest that the potential flow system within 
the bedrock is extensive. Four other boreholes beyond the 
southeastern water divide belong to a separate hydrogeo-
logical system.

The crust in the Kurikka region has repeatedly subsided 
into a depression during the Pleistocene glaciations. Dur -
ing these periods, and especially during deglaciation, fast-
flowing glaciers further increased the depth of the Kurikka 
Valley, as the sediments filling the valley indicate multiple 
glaciations, deglaciation melting phases, and long-dura-
tion subaqueous sedimentation together with brackish-
water intrusions (Putkinen et al. 2026). During the last 
deglaciation and the Holocene, the Kurikka area did not 
experience significant input of saline water, because the 
Baltic Sea water was more likely brackish. The peak salin -
ity of the Littorina Sea has been estimated to be about 12 
g/l (Westman et al. 1999 ). However, during the Eemian 
interglacial period (~ 135,000 years ago), Atlantic and 
Barents seawater spread to the Baltic Sea basin and these 
waters naturally extended as far as Kurikka. Subsequently, 
during the Weichselian Glaciation, those saline waters 
deposited muds that became mixed into the fine-grained 
regional till known as “Mäkisavi till” (Bouchard et al. 
1990). These sediments are in contact with the Kurikka 
aquifer units and may contribute trace amounts of sodium, 
chloride, and metals to the groundwater. In addition, resid-
ual old marine waters may influence bedrock groundwater 
chemistry.
Data collection and analysis
The dataset used in this study is publicly available on 
Zenodo (cf. Availability of Data and Material). The corre-
sponding sampling location names are mapped in Figure S1 
of the electronic supplementary material (ESM).
Well construction and integrity of sampled wells
Drilling was conducted using casing tubes with an outside 
diameter (OD) of 101 mm and an internal diameter (ID) of 
78 mm. Sediment samples were collected from the principal 
sedimentary units, and after the installation of the 60 mm 
OD and 50 mm ID groundwater observation well tubing, the 
steel casing tubes were removed. No sand packs or bentonite 
seals were used, as screens were installed directly in the 
primary deep aquifer unit.
Experimental field tests using active-distributed tem-
perature sensing (A-DTS) fibers indicated that potentially 
vertical water flows across the observation well tube wall 
between the aquitards above and below the screened interval 
do not occur and do not compromise the aquifer investiga-
tions (Putkinen et al. 2026). This is due to the rapid collapse 
and compaction of the surrounding sediments against the 
well tubing shortly after installation, which effectively limits 
horizontal flow.
Groundwater sampling in sediments
All data described below were compiled into a single data-
base including 160 samples across the study area. The meth-
ods used for sampling, chemical analyses, and data interpre-
tation are described in the following sections.
Physicochemical data from previous phases of the 
Kurikka research project (2011–2021) were gathered from 
private well sampling and two bedrock boreholes, and 
included analyses of major and minor ions, trace elements, 
and field parameters. The analyses were carried out by dif-
ferent commercial laboratories (Eurofins Labtium and Bot-
nia Lab/KVVY). The two bedrock boreholes sampled in 
2021 were flowing under artesian conditions and samples 
were collected using a submersible pump to facilitate con-
trolled sampling. These historical data were complemented 
with a regional geochemical sampling program conducted 
between June and September 2023. The 2023 sampling pro-
gram focused on the unconsolidated sedimentary aquifers 
and two bedrock boreholes. A total of 53 water samples were 
collected from 34 observation wells in sedimentary aqui-
fer units, two bedrock boreholes, six production wells in 
the sedimentary aquifer units and five springs. Observation 
wells and production wells in sediments have screened inter-
vals between 2 and 65 m long (average interval length = 13 
m). Groundwater samples were collected with an electrical 
submersible pump and spring water was collected as grab 
samples. In boreholes, at least three well volumes were 
pumped before sampling to ensure that the groundwater 
samples were representative of the sedimentary aquifers.
Field parameters including temperature, pH, electrical con-
ductivity (EC), and dissolved oxygen (DO) were measured in 
the field using multiparameter probes (YSI ProDSS, or YSI/
EXO Torpedo). Laboratory analyses for the 2023 campaign 
were conducted by a commercial laboratory (Eurofins Seinä-
joki, Finland) for major ions (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, K+, Cl−, 
 SO4

B, Ba, Be, Bi, Cd, Co, Cr, Cu, I, Fe, Hg, K, Li, Mn, Mo, Ni, P, 
Pb, Rb, S, Sb, Se, Sn, Si, SiO2, Sr, Th, Tl, U, V, Zn). Alkalinity 
was measured in the laboratory, sometimes a few days after 
sampling. This parameter may vary with time and is thought 
to be the cause of poor ionic balance for some of the analyses.
Isotopic analyses were conducted by the research labora-

L2130i the analytical uncertainty for δ18O is < 0.1 ‰ and for 
δ2H < 0.3 ‰. Strontium isotopic analyses ( 87Sr, 86Sr) were

Groundwater sampling in bedrock aquifers
Four additional groundwater sampling campaigns specifi-
cally targeted bedrock boreholes. Groundwater samples 
were collected from two boreholes in the Paloluoma val-
ley in October 2020 and from six bedrock boreholes in the 
western part of the study area in November 2022. In April 
and October 2024, groundwater samples were collected from 
bedrock boreholes located in the western (eight boreholes) 
and southeastern parts of the study area (Pitkämönkylä, 
four boreholes). Bedrock groundwaters were sampled using 
a tube sampler, allowing for the collection of a continu-
ous water profile from the borehole (Nurmi and Kukkonen 
1986). Depending on the length of boreholes, three to five 
groundwater subsamples were retrieved, each representing 
20 to 25-m-long sections. The sampler is composed of a 
polyamide tube (diameter of 12/10 mm) with a back-pres-
sure valve at the lower end and shutoff valves every 20–25 
m. The sampler is slowly lowered into the borehole allow -
ing groundwater to enter the tube. Once the bottom of the 
borehole is reached, the tube is lifted, the shutoff valves are 
closed, and the joints are separated. Water from the sub-
samples is retrieved for processing in field by nitrogen gas.
The same chemical parameters as previously described 
were analyzed, with the addition of bromide (Br). The 
October 2024 campaign did not include isotopic analyses 
and samples at two locations (KAL1 and KAL2) were 
only analyzed for 3H. Temperatures from bedrock bore-
holes sampled in April 2024 were not all recorded in the 
field due to freezing conditions and a temperature of 5 °C 
was assigned to all missing values, based on the meas-
urements of previous sampling campaigns and downhole 
electrical conductivity–temperature–depth (CTD) profil-
ing. Additionally, sodium fluorescein dye added to the 
drilling water was measured for the April 2024 samples. 
All boreholes were found to be representative for hydro-
geochemical investigations as they did not include drilling 
water, except for two locations in the southeast (SAN1 
and VAS1, not pictured).
Data processing
Several parameters of interest for data interpretation were cal-
culated using the Diagrammes software (Simler 2012) or in 
Microsoft Excel: partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2), 
saturation indices, dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC), and 
ionic balance. All values reported as 'non-detected' (below 
the analytical method's detection limit) were replaced with 
a value equal to 50% of the detection limit (Sanford et al. 
1993). The two bedrock boreholes that contained some drill-
ing waters (see Groundwater sampling in bedrock aquifers 
section) were excluded from the analysis. The ionic balance 
acceptance limit was fixed at ± 15% to maximize the number 
of analyses available for interpretation (Beaudry et al. 2018). 
Samples whose ionic balance is beyond this acceptable mar-
gin of error of ± 15% are considered inadequate and were 
rejected. A total of seven samples had to be discarded for 
geochemical interpretation. Nevertheless, all the analyses for 
the bedrock boreholes were considered for the interpretation, 
even though they have a greater ionic balance and uncertainty 
due to their low mineralization.
Figure 1 shows the spatial distribution of the 67 sampling 
locations. Most of the observation wells and two production 
wells in sediments are located in the Paloluoma Valley. The 
bedrock boreholes are in the topographic highs of the study 
area in the Lohiluoma and Häjynluoma valleys, which are 
considered the recharge areas of the system (where bedrock 
outcrops or subcrops). Two other bedrock boreholes are 
located beneath the central Paloluoma Valley.

The Diagrammes software (Simler 2012) was used to create 
the Piper diagrams. Given the quantity of samples and the 
large number of chemical parameters, an analysis using sta-
tistical methods is necessary to process the data. Multivari-
ate statistical analysis was successfully applied to understand 
the hydrogeochemical evolution of groundwater systems at 
the regional scale by numerous authors (e.g., Cloutier et al. 
2006, 2008; Beaudry 2013; Montcoudiol et al. 2015; Bea-
udry et al. 2018).
Due to the large amount of geochemical data, the mul-
tivariate statistical analysis was combined with graphical 
hydrogeochemical interpretation. A detailed description of 
data preprocessing and multivariate statistical analysis is 
provided in the ESM.

After selecting and standardizing the data, principal com-
ponent analysis (PCA) and hierarchical ascending classifi-
cation (HAC) were performed. Both PCA and HAC were 
performed using the package “FactoMineR” (Lê et al. 2008) 
and its hierarchical clustering on principal components 
(HCPC) function (Husson et al. 2010). PCA was used to 
identify the variables that explain most of the variation in 
chemical composition. In addition, unsupervised hierarchi-
cal clustering was applied to the PCA to group the samples 
according to their chemical similarity.
Corrected radiocarbon ages were calculated using the 
interpretation spreadsheet created by Janos (2017), which 
was also applied in Beaudry et al. (2018). This method iden-
tifies the geochemical processes that influence radiocarbon 
and selects the most appropriate correction methods for each 
sample.
Results
HCPC results
The descriptive statistics for the 118 groundwater samples 
selected for the HCPC are shown in Table S1 of the ESM. 
Table 1 presents correlations (loadings) between each vari-
able and the principal components. The first two components 
together express 44.4% of the total dataset variance (25.3% 
for Component 1 and 19.1% for Component 2). The third 
component also accounts for 15.5% of variance. The first 
five components extracted have eigenvalues greater than 1, 
and account for 74.8% of the total variance in the dataset. 
Only the first two components will be further described as 
they were the ones used for the geochemical interpretation. 
The first component (Dim.1) is strongly positively correlated 
with Sr, HCO3, Ca, Na, Mg, F, and K. The second compo-
nent (Dim.2) is positively correlated with pH, U, Cu, Zn, and 
Ni, and negatively correlated with Si and Mn.
The hierarchical clustering was performed on the first 
five components of the PCA, and resulted in the definition 
of six clusters, or water groups, that are represented on the 
hierarchical tree (Fig. 2a). While the majority of the samples 
in clusters 1, 3, and 4 come from observation wells in sedi-
mentary aquifer units, the majority of the samples in clusters 
2, 5 and 6 come from bedrock boreholes. Observation of the 
hierarchical tree reveals some indications of the level of sim-
ilarity between the six clusters. There is a statistical proxim-
ity between clusters 1 and 2, as well as between clusters 3 
and 4. Clusters 5 and 6 are closely related and appear more 
distinct than the other clusters. Figure 2b shows the variable 
factor map in the plane defined by the first two components. 
The 118 samples are represented on the factor map produced 
by the first two principal components and colored according 
to their cluster in Fig.  2c. The first component is related to 
salinity because of the association of the variables Na, Cl 
and HCO3. The second component is an important indicator 
of geochemical evolution through different parameters. It 
opposes groundwater samples from the bedrock (high pH, 
low Si, Fe, Mn, and As) to those from the sediments (lower 
pH, higher Si, Fe, Mn, and As) (Fig.  2b). Variables factor 
maps for the third and fourth components are presented in 
Figs. S2 and S3 of the ESM.

Major cations and anions were used to define the ground-

Most groundwater samples from the sediments and 
bedrock boreholes in the upgradient areas (C1, C2, C3, 
C5) are of Ca–HCO 3 or Ca–Mg–HCO 3 water types, 
reflecting less evolved groundwaters, typical of recharge 
areas (Cloutier et al. 2008; Beaudry et al. 2018). In 
contrast, groundwater from bedrock boreholes deep 
beneath the buried valleys (C6) showed a Na–HCO 3 type 
Na–HCO3–Cl, reflecting more evolved groundwater. The 
stiff diagram (Fig.  4a) shows the distinct composition of 
each water type, based on average ionic composition for 
each cluster. These compositions reflect a geochemical 
evolution of groundwater from recharge areas to the bur -
ied valleys in the bedrock and sediments. The interpreta-
tion of this geochemical evolution relies on the spatial 
distribution of clusters (previous section) and on the geo-
chemistry of the clusters. A similar groundwater evolution 
from Ca–HCO 3 to Na–HCO3 water types was observed in 
both sediments and bedrock in the recharge area (C2–C5) 
(Fig.  4b). The line connecting water compositions in the 
cation triangle starts parallel to constant Mg and then 
curves down toward the Na apex, which is typical when 
ion exchange occurs (Hounslow 1995). Both Ca and Mg 
are progressively removed from the water and replaced 
by Na. However, except for the bedrock boreholes in C6, 
almost all samples are undersaturated with respect to cal-
cite, which is due to the general calcite depletion in the 
system (Putkinen et al. 2026). This overall lack of calcite 
likely indicates an active groundwater flow system over 
a very long-term span.
EC ranged from 27–382 µS/cm with a progressive 
increase from C1 to C6. Cluster 6 showed the highest EC 
values (> 200 µS/cm). Nevertheless, the overall EC val-
ues of the sampled waters are generally low, indicating 
low levels of mineralization. One bedrock borehole in C1 
(TOI, Fig.  3) displayed very low EC values (27.1 µS/cm) 
up to a depth of 110 m, implying deep active groundwater 
recharge.
The partial pressure of CO2 (pCO2) provides insight 
on the degree of system “openness” with respect to soil 
 CO2. As water infiltrates the subsurface, it initially passes 
through the unsaturated soil zone, where it equilibrates with 
 CO2produced by root respiration and the decomposition of 
organic matter. This results in pCO₂ levels that are higher 
than those in the atmosphere (Freeze and Cherry 1979; 
Appelo and Postma 2004). In open system conditions, water 
is in equilibrium with the soil zone CO2 and pCO₂ remains 
constant as carbonate minerals dissolve until equilibrium is 
reached. Conversely, in a closed system where there is no 
subsurface source of CO ₂ available, pCO ₂ will gradually 
decrease and pH will increase.
The pCO2–pH relationship (Fig.  5) shows a progression 
from open to closed system conditions across different areas. 
Sediment waters described by C1–C3–C4 reflect open sys-
tem conditions, as well as bedrock waters from C5. In con-
trast, closed system conditions are found in bedrock ground-
waters from C2 (Lehtivuori area, AIT1), and C6 (Paloluoma 
Valley and the Pitkämonkylä area). This implies that radio-
carbon dating can only be applied to the few bedrock bore-
holes samples under closed conditions.
An indication of the geochemical evolution of groundwa-
ter within the system is given by the relationship between pH 
and well depth (Fig. 6). There is a general trend in which pH 
increases with well depth. This illustrates the groundwater 
evolution from shallow, acidic waters from the sediments 
(C1–C3–C4) to deeper and more alkaline waters from the 
bedrock (C2–C5–C6).
Redox conditions can be indicated by Fe, Mn, and U lev-
els in groundwater. Clusters 3, 4, and 5 showed the highest 
Fe and Mn concentrations and the lowest U concentrations, 
indicating reducing conditions. Conversely, C2 and C6 
had relatively lower Fe and Mn concentrations and higher 
U concentrations, suggesting oxidizing conditions (Fig.  7, 
Table 2).

for regional estimates (Kaislaniemi 2011). The ratios display 
wide range in both sediment and bedrock samples, although 
sediment clusters 1 and 3 tend to show the highest values

Age dating
Tritium (3H)
The closest GNIP station to the study site is Kuopio where 
3H in precipitation records are only available from 2005 
onwards. Since the monthly 3H values for Kuopio are very 
similar to the annual mean 3H values in Vienna (Fig.  8), 
Vienna's long-term 3H precipitation records were used as 
a proxy for this analysis. Therefore, the very long Vienna 
input curve (blue curve) was used as well as its decay-
corrected curve scaled to 2021 (black curve). The decay-
corrected curve represents the concentrations in 3H that 
would be observed today for rainwater that fell in previous 
years. Using historical 3H data from precipitation, the time 
period when the observed 3H concentration in precipita-
tion would have decayed to the measured concentration in 
groundwater in 2021 (year of sampling) was determined. 
This provides an indication of the period of recharge, 
assuming no dispersion and no mixing with more recent 
or older water.
Recent water (< 15 years) has undergone limited effects 
of dispersion and mixing, so values near or above 5 TU are 
clear indications of a recent water. Mixing and dispersion 
can have more effect on older waters, so the mere presence 
(detection) of 3H, even under 2 TU is an indication of at 
least a modern component as part of the water. Pre-1980 
3H concentrations are rarely observed as these older waters 
have generally undergone much mixing with older (3H-free) 
waters and dispersion, which reduce concentrations. Beyond 
50 or 60 years, 3H is not generally observed in groundwater 
originating from the infiltration of precipitation. The con -
centration of 3H is thus a qualitative indicator of the occur -
rence of recent water (> 5 TU) or of modern component 
(< 50–60 years) in the water when 3H is detected.
Thus, 3H concentrations > 5 TU indicate a recent com-
ponent (< 15 years) with low dilution. 3H concentrations 
between 2 and 4.5 TU indicate a modern component (15 
to 40 years). 3H concentrations below 2 TU suggest the 
presence of a modern component with significant dilution 
with older water. The absence of 3H (< 0.8 TU) indicates 
sub-modern waters with ages exceeding 60 years (and the 
absence of a modern component in this “old” water). 3H 
concentrations over 6 TU could also indicate water between 
40 and 60 years without mixing.
A modern component is present in C1, C2 and C4 waters, 
with 3H concentrations generally between 2 and 5 TU. C3, 
C5 and C6 waters are generally 3H-free and represent sub-
modern waters.

Discussion
Geochemical evolution
The identification of groundwater types with statistical anal-
ysis, along with the determination of residence times, pro-
vide a better understanding of regional groundwater flow and 
its geochemical evolution in the Kurikka bedrock and buried 
valley aquifer system. Table 4 summarizes the main charac-
teristics of the six groundwater clusters and their residence 
times based on 3H and radiocarbon. This table provides an 
overview of the evidence offered by groundwater clusters 
about the dynamics of the aquifer system.
Local complexities in the sediments and the bedrock as 
well as the layering influence the groundwater type. This 
is especially visible in the central Paloluoma Valley where 
three groundwater types are present. This occurrence of mul-
tiple types does not reflect mixing of groundwaters but rather 
the variety of origins of samples taken in shallow sediments, 
deep sediments and the underlying bedrock.
C1 waters are the least evolved and correspond to 
recharge waters in sediments. They are weakly mineralized, 
acidic and occur under open-CO 2 system conditions. Simi-
larly, in the bedrock, C2 waters also correspond to recharge 
waters, as evidenced by tritiated waters observed at depths 
reaching 114 m. However, C2 waters showed higher pH 
values and low pCO2 values associated with closed system 
conditions. This may reflect an evolution within bedrock, 
or a soil relatively depleted in organic matter, which would 
explain the drop in pCO2. The wide depth range of such 
modern groundwater could also imply active flow to great 
depths in the bedrock.

stage than C1. They are typically observed in sediments

Kyrönjoki Valley over a wide range of depths (0 to 100 
m). At greater depth in the bedrock or beneath the buried 
valleys, older and geochemically evolved waters of the C5 
and C6 types are found. C4 waters are modern and rich in 
sulfates and sulfur and differ from other groundwaters in 
the rest of the system. They appear to be isolated from the 
rest of the water groups. Sulfide minerals are rare in the 
local crystalline bedrock and fractures, suggesting that the 
elevated sulfate concentrations are unlikely to originate from 
bedrock sources. Instead, sulfate may be derived from over-
lying Quaternary sediments. Large parts of the Kurikka area 
remained submerged for several millennia following degla-
ciation, during which thick sequences of lacustrine–marine 
fine sediments accumulated in topographic depressions 
(Silvennoinen et al. 2025). These deposits may represent a 
potential source of sulfate that is mobilized and flushed into 
the groundwater system, which could explain the distinctive 
chemistry of the C4 waters.
Overall, groundwater evolves from C1 or C2 to C3 in 
the sediments, and C2 to C5 and C6 in the bedrock, with 
mineralization and pH increasing as residence time grows.
The groundwater chemistry observed in the Kurikka bur-
ied valleys is broadly consistent with what has been reported 
in other buried valley aquifers, including studies from the 
United States (Rowe et al. 1999), Canada (Cloutier et al. 
2006; Cummings et al. 2012), and Poland (Dragon 2006). 
In these systems, groundwater in recharge areas is typically 
characterized by Ca–HCO 3 or Ca–Mg–HCO 3 water types, 
with progressive geochemical evolution along the flow path 
toward waters enriched in Na, SO4, and Cl. However, these 
studies report significantly higher total dissolved solids 
(TDS) concentrations than those observed in the Kurikka 
groundwater system, indicating comparatively lower miner-
alization in the study area. Several reference aquifers occur 
in sedimentary settings, which may partly explain their 
higher TDS concentrations compared to the crystalline bed-
rock environment of Kurikka.
The Na/Ca molar ratio (Fig. S8 of the ESM) illustrates the 
overall low sodium content in the hydrosystem and indicates 
that a slight effect of a freshening process (Na–Ca exchange) 
occurs in C5 and C6 waters. If there was a marine event in 
the system, some marine water would have been introduced. 
The fact that a marine “signal” is not observed (except in 
Harja 13, see the Database) suggests that almost all poten-
tial Cl− and Na+ were already flushed out of the system, 
along preferential flowpaths of high permeability both in the 
sediments and the bedrock. Thus, release of marine signal 
from adjacent sediments diminishes in time and becomes 
insignificant. Overall, the approach followed in this study 
proved effective for classifying the large number of ground-
water samples and identifying the main hydrogeochemical 
processes controlling groundwater composition. This study 
follows the recommendation of Güler et al. (2002) to com-
bine multivariate statistical techniques with hydrogeochemi-
cal graphical methods in order to improve the interpretation 
of complex groundwater datasets. The multivariate statistical 
framework used here followed the methodology described 
by Cloutier et al. (2008), which has been widely applied in 
a variety of hydrogeological settings (Voutsis et al. 2015; 
Beaudry et al. 2018; Ren et al. 2021; Duan et al. 2022; Patel 
et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025; Sodomon et al. 2025) and is 
well adapted to the Kurikka hydrogeological context. These 
previous studies have generally used similar geochemical 
variable selections in multivariate analyses of hydrogeo -
chemical datasets. Additional tracers, such as environmen-
tal isotopes or groundwater residence time indicators are 
also used to constrain recharge conditions and groundwater 
residence time, but they are not typically incorporated into 
the PCA as the method requires variables to be consistently 
available across the full dataset (e.g., Beaudry et al. 2018).
Implications for groundwater flow system
The geochemical evolution and spatial distribution of the 
water types have direct implications on the dynamics and 
connectivity between bedrock and sediment aquifer units of 
the groundwater flow system.
The general low mineralization in both sediment and bed-
rock groundwaters, even at depth, and the Ca–HCO 3 water 
type (except beneath the Paloluoma valley) indicate a largely 
dynamic flow system. Calcium concentration remains nearly 
constant across clusters, with only slight cation exchange 
effects in C5 and C6, consistent with a dynamic system or a 
lack of Ca2+ and Na+ sources.
This is also supported by 3H data, which indicates the 
presence of a modern component in most clusters (C1, C2, 
C4), except for some C3 sedimentary waters and bedrock 
groundwaters in C5 and C6. Thus, radiocarbon dating is of 
limited use in this system, as most samples exhibit open-sys-
tem pCO2 conditions, making 14C measurements unusable. 
In addition, δ13C values span a very wide range, preventing 
the use of simple graphical age-correction based on the rela-
tion between radiocarbon and δ 13C (Han et al. 2012 ; Han 
and Plummer 2016). Only at limited locations (deep in the 
buried valleys or in the bedrock), is older water with a long 
residence time observed.
Consistency in groundwater evolution in the three smaller 
buried valleys (Paloluoma, Lohiluoma and Häjyluoma) indi-
cates that they are part of a single regional groundwater flow 
system connected to the Kyrönjoki Valley. However, ground-
water from the Nenättömanluoma and northern Kyrönjoki 
valleys (C4 water type) appears to be hydrogeologically 
disconnected from the southern part of the system as these 
regions are separated by the buried end moraine consisting 
 Hydrogeology Journal
of fine-grained tills despite having the same recharge zone 
in the northwestern part of the study area.
In bedrock, the general presence of freshwater containing 
3H down to a depth as far as 110 m supports the concept of 
an active flow system in bedrock, recharged rapidly from the 
topographic highs of the study area. The regional topogra-
phy provides an important driver for downward flow along a 
low-angle fracture network, while vertical fractures facilitate 
vertical connectivity attenuating with depth. Artesian condi-
tions in the bedrock boreholes beneath the Paloluoma buried 
valley imply that fracturing is continuous from the higher 
locations of the study area to the lower part.
Flowing artesian conditions are common in the deepest aqui-
fers of the buried valleys, due to the thick sequence of imperme-
able till and post-glacial and Holocene clay-silt and the connec-
tion of granular aquifer units to groundwater in topographic 
highs. Together with longer residence times, this indicates that 
production wells in southern Paloluoma and southern Kyrön-
joki valleys, would be protected from anthropogenic effects, 
while still benefiting from groundwater renewal due to active 
recharge in connected upgradient topographic highs.
The conceptual cross section (Fig.  11) synthesizes the 
hydrochemical evidence into a simplified representation of 
groundwater flow and geochemical evolution in the sedi-
ments and upper fractured bedrock. This illustrates how 
water types evolve from modern recharge where bedrock 
outcrops or subcrops to geochemically evolved waters in 
deeper valley aquifer units and fractured bedrock. The colors 
in Fig. 11 are consistent with those used in the clustering.
Conclusions
A network of buried valleys with glaciofluvial sediments 
up to 120 m thick connected to fractured and transmissive 
bedrock has the potential to meet the growing demand for 
water in western Finland. A compilation of historical hydro-
chemical data, supplemented by a new regional sampling 
campaign in five buried valleys and the underlying fractured 
bedrock, was conducted to improve the understanding of the 
hydrosystem. Multivariate statistical analysis and residence 
time indicators showed that a modern water component and 
active flow system were present in all parts of the system. 
Longer residence times are found deep within the buried 
valleys (3H-free waters) and at greater depth in a few loca-
tions in the bedrock (radiocarbon ages > 5000 years. Deep, 
downgradient areas in buried valleys with evolved water 
type indicate suitable water supply for production, protected 
from anthropogenic influences.
Combining hydrochemical data and multivariate statis-
tical analysis is a transferable approach for characterizing 
complex aquifer systems. This study illustrates how the 
geochemical evolution of groundwater can be used as an 
indicator of flow conditions, geological history, and the 
mineralogical composition of aquifer units in sediments 
and bedrock. Long residence time in bedrock and in certain 
deep parts of the buried valleys demonstrates their relative 
isolation, which is essential for sustainable exploitation and 
the natural protection of the resource against contaminants 
(low vulnerability).
