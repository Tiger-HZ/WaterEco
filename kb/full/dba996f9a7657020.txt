Abstract
Agricultural soils are increasingly threatened by contamination from pesticides, 
herbicides, and potentially toxic metals, posing significant risks to food security, 
ecosystem functioning, and long-term agricultural sustainability. Conventional 
remediation strategies primarily focus on contaminant removal but often fail to 
promote resource recovery and soil regeneration. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) 
have emerged as versatile materials capable of addressing both environmental 
remediation and nutrient management challenges within a green and circular 
chemistry framework. Recent advances demonstrate that MOFs can achieve 
contaminant removal efficiencies exceeding 90% and pesticide loading capacities of 
up to 90.79%, highlighting their potential as highly effective remediation materials. 
Beyond pollutant sequestration, MOFs undergo microbial and environmental 
transformations that facilitate the gradual release of plant-essential nutrients, 
including phosphorus and iron, thereby enhancing nutrient-use efficiency, supporting 
nutrient recycling, and improving plant performance. Unlike previous reviews that 
primarily discuss MOFs either as adsorbents for environmental remediation or as 
carriers for agrochemical delivery, this review presents an integrated perspective on 
MOFs as regenerative materials that connect pollutant sequestration with nutrient 
regeneration through biologically mediated transformation. It brings together recent 
developments in the field of MOF–microbiome interactions, microbial degradation 
pathways, nutrient recycling, and circular soil remediation in a single conceptual 
framework. Furthermore, this review identifies critical knowledge gaps related to 
field-scale validation, standardized environmental risk assessment, scalable green 
synthesis, economic feasibility, and the long-term fate of MOF transformation 
products. By integrating these emerging research directions, this review may provides 
a roadmap for developing next-generation MOF technologies for resilient and circular 
agricultural systems.

 1 Introduction
Agricultural soils worldwide are increasingly loaded by the long-term accumulation of 
persistent contaminants resulting from decades of intensive agrochemical use [1, 2]. The 
use of pesticides, herbicides and inorganic amendments has led to the accumulation of 
substances that are not biodegradable, such as organochlorine compounds, triazine her -
bicides and toxic metals such as cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb) and arsenic (As) [ 3, 4]. These 
pollutants are not confined to the upper surface of the soil, but they move through the 
soil pore systems, dissolve into the soil water and ultimately enter the groundwater sys -
tems or accumulate in crops [ 5, 6]. In polluted paddy soils, the level of As can reach 
50 mg kg− 1, which is about 250 times higher than the generally accepted safety limit of 
20 mg kg− 1, posing a significant risk of entering the food chain and long-term human 
exposure [7, 8].
The traditional remediation methods remain limited in their effectiveness, as they 
often reduce contaminant concentrations at the loss of soil structural integrity and bio -
logical functionality [ 9]. Soil excavation, chemical washing, and bulk amendment with

like activated carbon and biochar are also used extensively for contaminant removal, but 
their adsorption performance and selectivity depend on the contaminant and environ -

can also under certain conditions adsorb certain beneficial nutrients, which can affect 
nutrient availability in soil [ 13]. Nanotechnology-based materials have also shown con -
siderable potential for improving soil remediation efficiency. However, further research 
is still required to better understand their long-term environmental fate and ecological 
impacts under field conditions [14–17].
In this context, MOFs have emerged as a promising class of green functional materi -
als that combine high remediation performance with environmental sustainability and 
resource circularity [ 18]. Consistent with the principles of green chemistry, MOFs are 
designed not only to capture and immobilize contaminants but also to undergo con -
trolled transformation into beneficial soil constituents, minimizing secondary pollution 
and waste generation. Their tunable structure, high surface area, and multifunctional 
properties enable efficient pollutant sequestration while supporting sustainable soil 
restoration. MOFs have been shown to be effective remediators in certain laboratory 
and greenhouse experiments. For instance, Fe-based MOFs, UiO-66 derivatives, and 
MOF–biochar composites have been shown to achieve contaminant removal efficiencies 
greater than 90% in controlled aqueous environments and artificially contaminated soils, 
though the effectiveness depends on the chemistry of the contaminant, soil properties, 
pH, and competing ions [ 19, 20]. Simultaneously, these materials can stabilize useful 
microorganisms under stressful conditions, and the activity of microorganisms can be 
preserved more during a long period of time in nutrient-limited or toxic environments 
[21, 22]. This dual functionality enables MOFs to eliminate contaminants and facilitate 
biological remediation at the same time. Unlike persistent nanomaterials, MOFs are syn-
thesized using biologically metal nodes, such as iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), magnesium (Mg), 
and calcium (Ca), and metabolizable organic linkers, which are designed to be degraded 
by natural soil processes [ 23, 24]. The framework structure breaks down and releases 
these elements gradually into the soil and plant-available form [ 25]. Nutrient uptake 
improvements of approximately 57–64.4% have been reported primarily for phospho -
rus and Mg released from Fe- and Mg-based MOFs in greenhouse experiments with 
crops such as maize and faba bean grown under nutrient-deficient conditions [ 26, 27]. 
This shows the MOFs contribution to soil fertility as a remediation agent and a source of 
nutrients simultaneously [28].
Although numerous reviews have been published on MOFs for environmental reme -
diation, pollutant adsorption, controlled agrochemical delivery and sustainable agri -
culture, most of these reviews focus on one application at a time. There is a lack of 
understanding regarding the combined role of MOFs in pollutant sequestration, nutri -
ent regeneration by biological processes, microbial interactions and circular soil res -
toration. This review addresses this knowledge gap by providing a unified perspective 
on the complete life cycle of MOFs in agricultural soils, from contaminant capture and 
microbial transformation to nutrient recycling and ecosystem restoration. In addition, it

2 Tailored sorption and sequestration dynamics
The application of MOFs as soil remediation agents is based on their high porosity and 
the chemical programmability of their inner surfaces. In contrast to conventional soil 
amendments, MOFs can be designed to be highly selective, targeting hazardous legacy 
pollutants and important soil constituents. The synergistic action of the topology of the 
framework and functionalization of its surface controls this selective sequestration [29].
2.1 Selective capture of organic micropollutants
Agricultural soils are usually difficult to remediate because several pollutants can occur 
together, such as persistent organochlorines, modern herbicides, and other organic 
micropollutants [ 30, 31]. Such compounds have a high affinity for soil particles and 
cannot be degraded by natural processes [ 32]. MOFs offer a structurally tunable mate -
rial capable of capturing these contaminants by molecular recognition and entrapment 
within well-defined pore structures [ 33]. Adsorption of aromatic pesticides is primar -
ily done by non-covalent interactions between the π-electron systems of the framework 
linkers and the aromatic rings of the pesticide molecules, as illustrated in Fig. 1 [34]. As 
an example, UiO-66 and UiO-66-NH₂ with an amino-functional group allow π–π stack -
ing and hydrophobic interactions to increase the capture of legacy pesticides. The hydro-
phobic pyrethroid pesticide Lambda-cyhalothrin is highly interactive with the aromatic 
cavity of zirconium-based structures and achieves an encapsulation efficiency of 87.71% 
[35]. Adsorption performance is commonly interpreted using the Langmuir and Freun -
dlich isotherm models, while adsorption kinetics are typically described by pseudo-first-
order and pseudo-second-order models. These models provide valuable insights into 
adsorption behavior and facilitate comparisons among different MOF systems.
The adsorption of relatively polar pesticides is further enhanced by the introduction 
of functional groups to organic linkers, e.g., aminoterephthalic acid [ 36]. These groups 
permit further hydrogen bonding and dipole–dipole interaction with pesticide mol -
ecules. For example, amino-modified UiO-66 exhibits an acetamiprid loading capacity 
of 90.79% in pesticide-delivery studies. This value reflects the strong affinity of the MOF 
toward the pesticide molecule and demonstrates its adsorption capability [ 19]. Besides 
adsorption via weak intermolecular forces, iron-based MOFs, including MIL-101(Fe), 
may be involved in surface redox reactions that facilitate the conversion or stabilization 
of reactive organic pollutants. The specific surface area of these materials is often more 
than 2000 m2 ·g− 1, which gives a high concentration of active adsorption sites to pesti -
cides like Azoxystrobin (AZOX) as shown in Fig. 1 (C) [37].
In natural soil, competitive adsorption by the soil organic matter (SOM) may restrain 
the removal of the pollutants since the SOM molecules have the potential to occupy the 
same binding sites as the target contaminants [ 38, 39]. Nevertheless, the rigid and pre -
cisely defined pore openings of MOFs serve as molecular sieves. Small micropollutants, 
which are usually less than 1.5 nm, are allowed to enter the internal pore system, but 
large organic molecules like humic and fulvic acids are not as illustrated in Fig. 2. This

even in high-SOM soils [17].
2.2 Biochar-MOF nanocomposites for enhanced sorption
Combining MOFs with biochar is a good approach to soil remediation. Pure MOFs are 
highly porous with well-defined chemical structures; however, they tend to be mechani -
cally weak and costly to use at a large scale [ 40, 41]. Conversely, biochar is cheap and 
mechanically stable, but is not very selective to certain contaminants. The direct growth 
of MOF crystals within the carbon structure of biochar will combine the benefits of 
both materials and minimize their respective drawbacks, as demonstrated in Fig. 2 [29]. 
Biochar is produced by pyrolysis of biomass such as wheat straw or bamboo under lim -
ited oxygen conditions [42]. Its structure contains interconnected macropores (>50 nm) 
and mesopores (2–50 nm), which allow dissolved contaminants in soil solution to dif -
fuse rapidly into the solid matrix. When MOFs such as MIL-100(Fe), UiO-66, or ZIF-8 
are synthesized in situ within these pores, they introduce a large number of micropores 
(<2 nm). This creates a hierarchical pore system; larger pores enhance mass trans -
port, while micropores provide high-energy adsorption sites. This structure improves

The specific surface area of the WSB@MIL-100 composite is six times higher than that 
of raw biochar, and it is 419 m2 g− 1, and the total pore volume is 0.194 cm3 ·g− 1 [29]. The

the MOF phase is the dominant microporosity [ 44]. An H4-type hysteresis loop is also 
observed, which proves that the mesoporous structure of biochar is preserved. This equal 
distribution of pores minimizes the bottleneck effect that is usually realized in purely 
microporous materials, where low diffusion rates lower the adsorption efficiency [ 29].

The hybrid material contains a higher density of functional groups than either bio -
char or MOF alone. Biochar contributes oxygen-containing functional groups such as 
hydroxyl (–OH) and carboxyl (–COOH) groups. The MOF phase provides coordinated 
metal centers that act as specific adsorption and complexation sites. Synchrotron-based 
X-ray absorption fine structure and extended X-ray absorption fine structure analyses 
confirm that Cu 2 + ions coordinate with four equatorial and two axial oxygen atoms, 
with bond distances ranging from 1.16 to 2.02 Å. This coordination structure shows the 
formation of stable surface complexes [ 29]. MIL-100(Fe)-modified biochar has a sorp -
tion capacity of about twice that of unmodified biochar, immobilizing 142 mmol kg− 1 of 
Cu2 + and 156 mmol kg− 1 of Pb 2 + under soil conditions. The immobilization process is 
involves the complexation of the surface and ion exchange, as indicated by measurable 
decreases in exchangeable Na+, Mg2 +, K+, and Ca2 + concentrations after treatment [29]. 
In general, the MOF- biochar composite is a structurally stable and chemically active soil 
remediation material. It combines high specific surface area, hierarchical porosity and 
increased metal-binding capacity into one engineered system, leading to increased con -
taminant immobilization efficiency.
2.3 Heavy metal immobilization through coordination
MOFs can reduce the concentration of heavy metals in soil because MOFs can be 
designed to include chemical groups with high affinity to toxic metal ions. Unlike tra -
ditional soil amendments, which mostly act on the surface, MOFs work both on the 
surface and inside the pores, which minimizes the amount of heavy metals available for 
plant uptake [29, 45]. Unsaturated metal sites and Lewis basic groups capture and form 
complexes with toxic metal like As, Cd, and Pb. The organic linkers of MOFs such as 
ZIF-8 and ZIF-67 provide pairs of electrons that strongly interact with Cd and make ions 
form stable complexes that do not migrate to the groundwater [ 46]. Iron-based MOFs, 
such as MIL-101(Fe), are highly efficient in the case of As, as Fe sites can substitute the 
ligands with As species, transforming mobile As into a stable mineral-like form in MOF 
pores and making it less toxic to crops [ 8]. Some MOFs are also applied alongside mag -
netic materials, where the contaminated particles can be easily collected after remedia -
tion and decrease the risk of secondary contamination [47].
The long-term effectiveness of MOFs is determined by their stability in agricultural 
soil under different redox conditions because of irrigation and microbial activity [ 29]. 
MOFs, such as CALF-25, contain phosphonate monoester linkers that create a hydro -
phobic environment, preventing the hydrolysis of coordination bonds and stabilizing the 
structure over a wide pH range, so that the metals trapped in them are not released into 
the soil during seasonal changes [ 43]. Even when MOFs are degraded slowly, they can 
form stable secondary minerals, such as goethite or hematite, through the oxalate car -
bonate route, which also entraps heavy metals and has long-term remediation effects

The comparative evaluation of the studies summarized in this review shows that 
the MOF incorporation into hybrid or composite systems has improved the adsorp -
tion performance. In several reports, MOF materials were found to have high adsorp -
tion capacities for pesticides and heavy metals. Although many pesticide-related values 
(e.g., loading capacities and controlled-release efficiencies) originate from agrochemi -
cal delivery studies, they demonstrate the strong host–guest interactions and tunable 
pore structures that also govern pollutant adsorption and sequestration in remediation 
applications.
In addition to surface area, the adsorption performance of MOFs can be influenced by 
a variety of other factors such as pore size, pore accessibility, surface functional groups, 
metal coordination, and structural stability. MOFs with appropriate pore structures and 
accessible active sites can promote the diffusion and adsorption of contaminants, and 
functional groups like amino, hydroxyl, and carboxyl groups can increase the interaction 
between the MOF and pollutants. The synthesis methods, such as linker modification 
and composite formation, may affect the adsorption selectivity, stability, and reusability. 
MOFs offer more structural tunability and multifunctionality than conventional adsor -
bents like biochar, activated carbon, zeolites, clay minerals, and engineered nanoparti -
cles, but conventional materials are typically more cost-effective and scalable. Thus, the 
adsorption ability of MOFs is probably related to the structural and chemical character -
istics of the MOFs, not to one specific property. The regeneration efficiency, adsorption–
desorption performance and structural stability of MOFs in repeated use are crucial for 
their practical application. While many MOFs are able to achieve high adsorption effi -
ciency over multiple regeneration cycles in the laboratory, the long-term environmental 
performance of these materials could be affected by changes in soil pH, redox potential, 
salinity, phosphate concentration, flooding, and microbial activity, both of which have 
the potential to impact framework stability and contaminant remobilization. Moreover, 
the environmental stability of MOFs is different, like UiO-66 and CALF-25 are more 
chemically stable than ZIF-8, HKUST-1 and MIL-101 in acidic or phosphate-rich envi -
ronments. Thus, long-term leaching and field studies are needed to assess the environ -
mental safety and applicability of the various MOF systems.
To facilitate future comparisons, we propose the Hybrid Performance Index (HPI) as a 
conceptual framework rather than a validated index. The HPI may integrate adsorption 
performance, structural stability, regeneration, and environmental compatibility into a 
unified assessment. However, because standardized datasets are currently unavailable, 
no universal calculation method can yet be established, and the HPI should be regarded 
as a hypothesis for future validation.
3 MOF-microbiome synergies for bioremediation
Biological restoration of polluted soil is frequently constrained by the low survival rates 
of specialized microbial inoculants in hostile, competitive, or polluted environments 
[51]. The combination of MOFs and beneficial microorganisms forms a synergistic plat -
form in which the MOFs offer physical protection and a local nutrient reservoir, and the 
microbes execute specific metabolic degradation of pollutants [52, 53].

3.1 The exoskeleton paradigm for microbial protection
In situ biomimetic mineralization is the basis of the idea of a microbial exoskeleton, 
where a thin layer of MOF is deposited on the surface of single bacterial cells. The pro -
cess forms a conformal and semi-permeable shell that controls the exchange of mole -
cules between the cell and the external environment [ 54, 55]. Bacterial strains that can 
degrade pollutants, such as those that metabolize p-nitrophenol (PNP), can be encap -
sulated in a ZIF-8 shell and remain metabolically active. The oligotrophic conditions of 
7 days preserve the viability of encapsulated cells by 79%, but the viability of unprotected 
cells usually decreases rapidly during the same period [ 21]. The microporous ZIF-8 
structure is a molecular sieve. It prevents the diffusion of large toxic macromolecules 
and lytic enzymes but does not prevent the diffusion of smaller pesticide molecules into 
the cytoplasm, where enzymatic biodegradation occurs, as shown in Fig. 3.
There are evident performance variations in the soil systems. ZIF-8-encapsulated cells 
in unsterilized black soil fully degraded 100 mg kg−1 PNP in 5 days. The same encapsu -
lated system in acidic red clay (pH 5.2) resulted in 100 mg kg −1 being fully degraded in 
12 days. Conversely, native uncoated cells deteriorated less than 20 mg kg−1 in the same 
acidic soil matrix in 14 days [ 21]. The mineral coating also increases the mechanical 
stability and ecological performance. Novosphingobium cells coated with ZIF-8 have a 
higher capacity for adhesion and colonization of roots. Root-associated populations of 
colony-forming unit (CFU) assays are 1.54 × 107 to 2.08 × 107 CFU g − 1 in comparison 
with 0.17 × 107 to 0.30 × 107 CFU g− 1 of bare MOF materials. The improved colonization 
is linked to measurable changes in saline alkali soils. The pH of the soil reduces by 0.6, 
from 8.5 to 7.9, and the salt content reduces by 0.11 g kg− 1, from 4.3 to 3.1 g kg− 1 [56]. 
The root malondialdehyde levels are reduced by 42.00%, and proline levels are reduced 
by 39.13%, which means that the oxidative stress and osmotic stress in the rhizosphere 
are reduced [ 56]. MOF nucleation takes place by coordination of metal ions with func -
tional groups on the bacterial cell surfaces, creating a porous protective shell. This semi-
permeable exoskeleton allows nutrient and metabolite diffusion and at the same time 
allows microbial metabolic activity, which slowly breaks down by hydrolysis, enzymatic 
activity and pH changes in the rhizosphere. There is currently evidence indicates of com-
patibility with a variety of microbial species, but species-specific performance needs to

must be able to withstand environmental stress, migrate to contaminated microsites,

protect cells when applied to the soil and enhance spatial localization at the soil-root 
interface [60]. Zeolitic imidazolate frameworks have been utilized as protective carriers 
for specialized degraders. Encapsulation alleviates osmotic and oxidative stress in oligo -
trophic soil [21]. This finding shows that the microporous matrix balances environmen -

3.3 Metabolic support of pollutant-degrading microorganisms by MOFs
MOFs may also play a direct role in the metabolism of soil microorganisms other than 
protection. These materials can be used as temporary nutrient stores when they are syn -
thesized using biologically associated ligands and metal nodes [ 53]. They provide the 
necessary factors that sustain microbial activity in the process of bioremediation, espe -
cially in oligotrophic soils with low carbon and mineral levels [55].
Iron-based MOFs with phosphorus- and nitrogen-containing ligands, including 
those of empirical composition C₂H₁₅Fe₂N₂O₁₈P₃, contain about 6.03% N, 14.48% P , 
and 14.69% Fe [26]. These elemental fractions enable the framework to serve as a multi-
nutrient source. Nutrients are released gradually as the crystalline structure is gradu -
ally biodegraded, instead of being released in one pulse. Fe-MOFs exhibit 82% structural 
degradation in soil systems in 98 days, which is significantly faster than in aqueous sys -
tems, suggesting that mineral dissolution in soil systems is mediated by microbes [ 26]. 
Nutrient release follows a comparable temporal pattern. After 98 days, mineral N, P , 
and Fe have cumulative release of 85, 75, and 34%, respectively [ 26]. This intermittent 
release is in line with the growth needs of microbes and the dynamics of plant nutrient 
uptake. MOFs thus combine nutrient provision and contaminant elimination through

controlled mineral decomposition. This synchronized mechanism establishes a chemi -
cally integrated system where the bioremediation and metabolic support take place 
simultaneously [ 21, 56]. The compatibility of MOFs and microorganisms might rely 
on the microbial properties, MOF composition, and environmental conditions. Strains 
that form more biofilm or are more tolerant to metal ions could show better interac -
tions with MOFs. However, their performance under field conditions may be influenced 
by environmental variability and competition with native soil microorganisms. There -
fore, further field studies are needed to evaluate the stability and effectiveness of MOF–
microbiome systems in agricultural soils. In addition to their physicochemical sorption 
properties, MOFs are now being shown to have a biological effect on soil, indicating the 
formation of an integrated network of MOF–microbiome–plant interactions. The use 
of MOF formulations, especially those that deliver agrochemicals or micronutrients in 
a controlled manner, has been reported to enhance microbial activity and plant growth 
in several studies reviewed here. Based on the available evidence, we propose the Rhizo -
sphere Engineering Triangle as a conceptual framework describing the potential inter -
actions among pollutant immobilization, microbial activity, and plant nutrient uptake. 
Rather than representing a validated model, it is intended to guide future hypothesis 
development and experimental investigation.
4 Biologically driven dissolution of MOFs
The biologically mediated disassembly processes control the transformation of MOFs 
into functional remedial agents into integrated soil components [ 62]. In contrast to 
synthetic polymers that can remain in the form of microplastics, the structural units of 
specially designed MOFs are to be subjected to microbial mineralization and enzymatic 
degradation to enter natural biogeochemical cycles [63, 64].
4.1 Microbial mineralization of organic linkers
MOFs structure is determined by organic linkers and constitutes the primary interface 
between the soil microbial communities and the MOFs. Such carbon-rich ligands are 
chemically active in the soil [ 18]. Once they have been applied, they are subjected to 
extracellular enzymes and microbial metabolism. Their change regulates the stability of 
the framework as well as the cycling of nutrients [ 65]. Advanced design of MOF-based 
agrochemicals includes the integration of certain chemical bonds that react to biological 
catalysts or other environmental alterations, such as Schiff base linkages, natural poly -
amine coordination, biodegradable polymer conjugation, and phosphate-amine systems. 
As an example, acid-sensitive Schiff base (imine) bonds in structures like ZIF-90 can be 
introduced to facilitate multimodal antimicrobial action; in the presence of acidic condi-
tions caused by the infection of Magnaporthe oryzae, the bonds break down, releasing 
kasugamycin (KSM) and Zn2 + ion as illustrated in Figure. 4 [48].
In iron-based MOFs with oxalate-based linkers, degradation proceeds primarily via 
the oxalate–carbonate pathway. Half the coordination network of metal–ligand is hydro-
lyzed, and oxalate is liberated into the soil solution. Oxalate is then used as a source of 
carbon and energy by oxalotrophic bacteria [ 26]. This biological consumption changes 
dissolution equilibria and enhances structural disintegration. In aqueous systems, the 
release of nutrients does not exceed 10%. Conversely, the soil systems encourage rapid 
mineralization, and up to 82% of the mass can be lost in 98 days [ 26]. The difference

linkers, and soil conditions. Some of the biodegradable MOFs degrade quickly, while 
others are more stable and can remain in place for a longer period of time, keeping the 
contaminants immobilized throughout the growing season. Released metals can also be 
bound to soil minerals or organic matter, which can result in continued contaminant 
retention even after partial framework degradation.
Ligand turnover is greatly affected by temperature. In a wheat growing season, the rate 
of degradation rose to 13.8% at 19.8 °C and 4.2% at 4.7 °C monthly, which indicated ther-
mally stimulated microbial and chemical activity [ 16]. After more than six months, soils 
to which these MOFs were added had a higher concentration of mineral N, available P , 
and Fe than the untreated controls and urea-fertilized soils [ 16]. Linker mineralization 
thus helps in long-term nutrient supply as the structure breaks down. The oxalate-car -
bonate pathway also influences the soil pH. Localized buffering effects can be generated 
by microbial oxidation of oxalate to carbonate, and protons can be produced by oxida -
tion of Fe2 + to Fe3 +. Experimental evidence indicates that the observable pH shifts are 
primarily observed during the first two months of application, and then stabilization is 
observed when redox balance and carbonate equilibria are restored [ 7, 16]. These redox 
and carbon reactions are coupled and combine MOF degradation into more extensive 
soil mineral and biogeochemical cycles, as shown in Fig. 5.

MOFs have a high potential for soil remediation but their environmental fate and 
long-term safety are important considerations for field deployment. There is currently 
no evidence that they are ecotoxic or biodegradable, which is dependent on the com -
position of the metal nodes, the particle size, and degradation behavior. The long-term 
environmental compatibility of Zr- and Cu-based MOFs should be assessed. In contrast, 
Fe-, Mg-, and Ca-based MOFs are generally considered more suitable for sustainable 
agriculture applications, since their degradation products can be beneficial for natural 
nutrient cycles, but have lower ecotoxicological risks. In this regard, the design of MOFs 
in the future should focus on the use of environmentally friendly metal nodes, biode -
gradable linkers, and controllable degradation to ensure high remediation efficiency and 
long-term ecological safety. To support future environmental risk assessment, we pro -
pose the Environmental Safety Threshold (EST) as a conceptual framework rather than 
a standardized safety index. The conceptually derived EST could be used to represent 
concentration ranges where MOFs do not cause measurable adverse ecological effects 
under defined conditions. More laboratory and field research is needed, however, before 
quantitative thresholds can be determined.
4.2 Environmental factors influencing the degradation kinetics
The degradation of MOFs in agricultural soil is not a fixed process but is very sensitive to 
the abiotic factors of the soil matrix [28]. Soil moisture and soil temperature are the main 
external factors that control the hydrolytic stability of coordination bonds and the meta-
bolic activity of the microbial communities that mediate linker mineralization as illus -
trated in Fig. 6. The stability of MOFs is a major factor that is controlled by soil moisture 
[66]. Water is used as a reactant in the hydrolytic cleavage of metal-ligand bonds, and as 
a medium of transport of dissolved metal ions and organic ligands [ 67]. Field moisture 
leads to an extreme structural transformation in iron-based materials such as Fe-MOF 
(OPA). A wheat growing season has been reported to lose 50.9% of its mass, a gradual 
degradation of the framework in the natural pedoclimatic conditions [ 16]. Water mol-
ecules move into the pore structure and organize the metal nodes destabilizing coor -
dination bonds and initiating hydrolysis. This process facilitates the continuous release 
of nutrients in moist conditions, which are maintained. The levels of minerals like N, 
available P , and Fe in MOF-amended soils are significantly greater than the urea-treated 
controls after six months, which demonstrates a slow release of nutrients and long-term 
retention [16].
The soil water content also changes, enhancing degradation. The drying and wet -
ting cycles lead to alternating chemical and mechanical stress [ 68]. Drying leads to an 
increase in the concentration of solutes and an increase in the ionic strength of the soil 
Page 18 of 36
Haidri et al. Discover Green Chemistry (2026) 1:27 
pores, whereas rapid rewetting results in the formation of osmotic gradients and volu -
metric expansion [69]. These repetitive alterations promote the formation of microfrac -
tures and dissociation of ligands. Some MOFs have been chemically modified to increase 
the hydrolytic stability. As an example, the functional groups of ethyl esters in CALF-
25 sterically hinder the water access to the hydrolysis-sensitive coordination sites. The 
structural integrity of this design is 90% at 353 K and a relative humidity of 90% [43]. The 
temperature is in contact with the moisture to regulate the persistence of MOF. Accord-
ing to the field data, the correlation between the soil temperature and the monthly rate 
of Fe-MOF degradation is nearly linear (R2 = 0.99). The degradation rate is approximately 
4.2% per month at 4.7 °C. At 19.8 °C, the rate increases to 13.8% per month, which is 
more than three times higher than the rate observed at 4.7 °C. This temperature depen -
dence suggests enhanced hydrolysis and increased microbial metabolic activity under 
warmer soil conditions [16].
MOF-based delivery systems can also be designed to be environmentally responsive. A 
thermoresponsive poly (N-isopropylacrylamide) shell regulates the release of pesticides 
in IDC@UiO-66-(COOH)₂-PNIPAm. The polymer has a lower critical solution tempera-
ture (LCST) of about 32 °C. At temperatures lower than this, diffusion of encapsulated 
indoxacarb (IDC) is inhibited by the hydrated polymer network. Polymer chain collapse 
above the LCST, which enhances pore permeability. Cumulative release of IDC is 79.0% 
at 40 °C and 26.58% at 15 °C [ 70]. This temperature-dependent permeability coincides 
with the release of pesticides with the times of increased temperature, which is usu -
ally accompanied by increased pest activity and crop metabolic demand. In general, the 
transformation of MOF is regulated by soil moisture and temperature in a complex of 
hydrolytic, mechanical, and biological processes, as illustrated in Fig. 6. MOF degrada -
tion is a climate-sensitive process in soil environments involving the interaction of mate-
rial chemistry and dynamic physical and biological conditions of the rhizosphere, and 
not an abiotic decay process.
Based on the overall evidence, MOF transformation in soil should be understood in 
the context of a model of degradation that is responsive to the rhizosphere environment,

4.3 Predictive modelling of long-term persistence
To determine the long-term environmental compatibility of MOFs in agricultural soils, 
the residence time of these materials has to be estimated to avoid the unwanted accu -
mulation of nanomaterials [ 28]. The kinetics of degradation is highly reliant on the soil 
texture and the SOC content [ 66]. High macroporosity and high hydraulic conductivity 
enhance water flow and oxygen diffusion in sandy or light-textured soils with low SOC 
(<15.0 g kg− 1). Such circumstances favor hydrolytic fracture of metal–ligand bonds and 
microbial colonization of the exposed framework surfaces, leading to accelerated struc -
tural degradation compared to fine-textured soils [16].
Semi-empirical kinetic models are typically used to describe degradation and nutrient 
release. In the case of Fe-based MOFs, the cumulative release of N, P , and Fe has been 
modeled using the Higuchi model and the Korsmeyer–Peppas equation. The values of 
diffusion exponents (n) of 0.43 to 0.85 suggest non-Fickian, anomalous transport [ 26]. 
These values imply that the release of nutrients is regulated by a combination of pro -
cesses, such as diffusion through the pores, erosion of the matrix by microorganisms, 
and degradation of ligands by microorganisms, and not by diffusion alone. Cargo release 
half-life and structural half-life should be differentiated. For example zirconium-based 
UiO-66-NH₂ releases the herbicide MCPA to 0.037 mg mL− 1 in distilled water, and the 
crystalline structure is mostly preserved under the same conditions [ 17]. Conversely, 
ZIF-8 systems tend to release encapsulated compounds rapidly at relatively low acidic 
pH (3–5), and the release of a large cargo occurs within 12 h. Nevertheless, the Zn–
imidazolate coordination network is more stable in neutral soils, in which hydrolysis is 
slower [71]. These variations indicate that active compound release kinetics and matrix 
persistence cannot be considered together in environmental risk assessment.
Structural and spectroscopic verification is necessary to achieve long-term transfor -
mation. Fe-based MOFs show progressive mineralization by reduction and eventual loss 
of typical powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) reflections, and weakening of Fourier trans -
form infrared (FTIR) bands of coordinated carboxylate groups, especially asymmetric 
and symmetric vibrations at 1650 and 1430 cm− 1 [ 26]. During iron-based OPA-MOF 
soil amendments, the nitrate-N levels grow slowly and stabilize at about 200 mg kg − 1 
after 17 weeks, which is in line with the addition of framework-derived nitrogen into soil 
nutrient pools [7, 16].

term persistence and degradation behavior in soil systems. For example, biochar–MOF

properties. Furthermore, the degradation products may have ecotoxicological effects on 
soil microorganisms and non-target organisms that needs to be investigated under long-

5 Translocation and multi-trophic transfer
When MOFs break up in the soil matrix, the metal nodes and organic linkers are 
released into the soil solution and are accessible to biological uptake [ 74]. The mecha -
nisms of these degradation byproducts within the plant and into the wider food web are 
crucial to assessing the effectiveness of biofortification as well as the safety of the agri -
cultural product [75].
5.1 Plant mediated uptake of degradation byproducts
Plants constitute the primary biological route of the elements of MOFs to be moved out 
of soil into higher trophic levels. The uptake of elements is dependent on the chemi -
cal speciation and mobility of ions that are released during framework degradation [ 76]. 
Structural disassembly can create a long-term and bioavailable pool of nutrients against 
rapid release of highly soluble mineral salts. Following the breakdown of structures like 
Fe-MOF (OPA) or ZIF-8, coordinated metal ions, e.g., Fe 3 +, Zn 2 +, and Mg 2 +, are dis -
charged into the soil solution and into the known rhizosphere transport systems [27, 58].
The physiological activity of plants proves that these materials are sources of nutrients.

62.96% and 75.0% respectively. The stem diameter grows by 31.5% and the leaf surface 
area grows by 46.22%, which means that the vascular development and photosynthetic 
capacity improve [ 58]. These growth effects are linked to a 1.91-fold growth in carot -
enoid concentration and a 2.07-fold growth in chlorophyll b content, which indicates 
stimulation of pigment biosynthesis and light-harvesting efficiency. As well as the GR-

Analysis of the reviewed literature shows clear relationships between MOF structural 
properties and environmental performance, which is a key challenge that needs to be 
tackled by long-term field trials and pilot-scale studies. The high surface area and hierar-
chical porosity increase the adsorption capacity, and the functionalized linkers increase 
the selectivity towards the pollutants. Moreover, the use of biodegradable linkers and 
environmentally friendly metal nodes helps to minimize environmental hazards. The 
observations allow for the development of initial design guidelines for future generation 
of agricultural MOFs, highlighting the need to combine high sorption efficiency, con -
trolled degradability and biocompatibility. MOFs can enhance resource-use efficiency by 
releasing nutrients from the framework in a controlled manner, which minimizes nutri -
ent losses through leaching and increases nutrient availability to plants, compared to 
conventional soluble fertilizers. While current studies show improved nutrient utiliza -
tion and recovery, there is a lack of standardized quantitative comparisons of nutrient 
recovery efficiency and circular recycling pathways under field conditions, which needs 
to be investigated further.
5.2 Impacts on non-target soil macrofauna
Since MOFs are currently being used as amendments to functional soils but are cur -
rently being converted into mineralized residues, their ecological impacts need to be 
considered at the level of entire-soil communities. Ecotoxicological assessment offers a 
quantitative method of establishing the presence of degradation products on soil fauna, 
microbial diversity, or trophic interactions [ 76]. Eisenia fetida is a common earthworm 
that is used as a bioindicator due to its sensitivity to xenobiotic compounds and its cen -
tral position in organic matter turnover and nutrient cycling. MIL@A@D@C nano -
composites have high biosafety margins in acute toxicity assays. Adult E. fetida have a 
survival rate near 100% at surface loadings of 251.6 μg cm− 1 and above in contact tests 
that last 48 h [21]. There are no abnormal reactions, such as coiling, paralysis, or epider -
mal damage. These findings suggest little toxicity of dermal or ingestion. It seems that 
encapsulation in the MOF matrix decreases the bioavailability of active ingredients in 
the short term, thus limiting the exposure of organisms, as shown in Fig. 7.

Reduced acute toxicity compared to traditional pesticide preparations has been 
observed in several systems. FeMOF-pectin composite loaded with pyraclostrobin 
results in an eight-fold decrease in acute toxicity to zebrafish relative to commercial 
suspension concentrates [ 48]. Effects on positive rhizosphere microorganisms are also 
significant. Cu-BTC is a copper-based material that improves the growth of faba beans, 
boosting the dry biomass by 40.42% and leaf surface area by 46.22% (Mahmoud, 2025). 
Nevertheless, due to the accumulation of Cu 2 + , the dosage needs to be optimized to 
avoid sensitive microbial taxa. Ecological compatibility is also shown by microbial com -
munity analyses. The native genera, such as biofilm-associated Sphingobium and Lac-

There is an increase in absolute population and a change in soil chemistry, such as a 
drop in pH by 0.63 units and a drop in salt content by 0.11 g kg− 1 [56]. These results indi-
cate that the degradation products are added to the rhizosphere matrix without inter -
fering with functional microbial diversity. During the last phase of the MOF life cycle, 
crystalline structural characteristics are lost, metal ions are segregated into exchange -
able or mineral-bound fractions, and organic connectors are integrated into the pools 
of soil organic matter. The high macrofaunal survival, low aquatic toxicity, and consis -
tent microbial community structure are all indicative of the fact that, at controlled rates, 
MOF-based systems break down to components that can be incorporated into the exist -
ing soil biogeochemical processes [26, 56].

While there are reports of low acute toxicity for several MOFs, the majority of the 
evidence is from short-term laboratory testing. Long-term exposure may lead to the 
gradual release and accumulation of metal ions (e.g., Fe, Zn, and Cu) in soil, which 
could affect soil organisms. Furthermore, sublethal effects on growth, reproduction and 
behavior are not well known. Therefore, long-term field studies are needed to confirm 
the environmental safety of MOFs.
5.3 Aqueous mobility and groundwater risk
The long-term environmental safety of MOFs and their degradation products is defined 
by their vertical mobility. Downward water flux is caused by rainfall and irrigation, 
and remaining nanoparticles enter groundwater aquifers. That is why the mobility of 
MOF-derived species in soil should be assessed quantitatively [ 26, 49]. The primary fac-
tors that regulate the leaching of MOF-derived metal nodes are the soil pH and cation 
exchange capacity. These parameters regulate the solubility of the ions, adsorption and 
retention in the soil profile [79]. Despite this pH sensitivity, there is a limitation of verti -
cal transport by soil buffering mechanisms. As an example, Fe 3+ released by structures 
such as MIL-101(Fe) or OPA-MOF is rapidly hydrolyzed to insoluble iron oxyhydroxide 
or binds to clay minerals and soil organic matter. These sorption processes cause iron 
to accumulate in the upper 0–20 cm of soil, reducing the risk of iron being washed to 
groundwater [7, 26].
Besides dissolved ions, the potential mobility of intact or partially degraded MOF 
nanoparticles has also been studied. Colloidal transport relies on surface charge (zeta 
potential) and interaction with natural organic matter. Hydrophobic functionalization of 
surfaces with stearic acid (SL) or polydopamine (PDA) enhances the particles’ aggrega -
tion. Enhanced aggregation improves physical straining and retention in soil pore net -
works. Sustained cargo release was found in UiO-66-NH₂ systems with SL modification 
over 11–52 h, yet intact nanoparticles were still mostly restricted to the topsoil because 
of hetero-aggregation with mineral surfaces [ 49, 50]. Framework composition also has 
an impact on mobility. MOFs based on Fe- and Zn-tend to mineralize comparatively 
fast in soil. Conversely, zirconium-based nodes based on UiO-66 exhibit a high chemical 
stability and hydrolysis resistance. Nevertheless, zirconium has a high affinity to phos -
phate, and thus, zirconium forms insoluble zirconium–phosphate complexes in soil. 
This reaction fixes zirconium species and restricts the movement of groundwater [ 17]. 
Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry is usually used in long-term monitor -
ing of leachate to identify trace metal concentrations. The existing field and simulation 
data show that, at the recommended agricultural application rates, the concentrations 
of MOF-derived metals in groundwater are significantly lower than the drinking water 
guideline values, such as Zn < 3 mg·L− 1 and Fe < 0.3 mg·L− 1 [ 8, 26]. These results indi -
cate that the soil chemical and physical retention processes restrict vertical migration of 
MOF-derived components under controlled use conditions.
6 Safety, regulation, and soil health benchmarks
As MOFs begin to be used in agriculture, it is important to ensure that they are safe for 
soil, crops, and human health. The safe-by-design approach focuses on selecting materi -
als and designing structures that can safely break down in the soil without harming the 
environment [80].

6.1 Defining “safe-by-design” agricultural MOFs
Safe-by-design approach takes into account the compatibility with the environment dur-
ing the synthesis of MOFs. This strategy combines the choice of metals, structural labil -
ity, and end-of-life transformation into a single design principle [ 8]. One element of this 
strategy is the application of metal nodes, which are associated with vital plant micronu-
trients. Fe, Zn, Mg, and Ca are preferred because, upon degradation of the framework, 
they contribute to the existing nutrient pools in the soil rather than introducing non-
native elements [26].
The important design parameter is the synthetic methodology that uses water or etha -
nol instead of high-boiling aprotic solvents like N,N-dimethylformamide, which will 
decrease the residual amount of solvent and enhance the sustainability process, as illus -
trated in Fig. 8. In the encapsulation of AZOX in NH₂-MIL-101(Fe), the highest load -
ing capacity of 6.73% was obtained using ethanol-based synthesis, which shows that the 
functional performance is not impaired by solvent substitution [ 21]. Structural lability 
may also be adjusted to agricultural time scales. Experiments conducted in the field indi-
cate that Fe-MOFs lose 50.9% of their mass in six months of wheat growth [16]. Verifica-
tion of end-of-life transformation is required to confirm that the designed degradability

6.2 Compliance with global food and soil standards
The commercialization of MOF-based technologies in laboratory research and agri -
cultural practice must adhere to international regulatory standards, as shown in Fig. 8. 
This guarantees that crop protection and nutrient delivery functions do not produce 
unwanted residues in soils or food products [ 82]. Regulatory assessment is based on 
three interrelated criteria, such as elemental loading, active ingredient residues and bio -
degradability [83].
In the guidelines established by the Food and Agriculture Organization and the World 
Health Organization through the Joint FAO/WHO Meeting on Pesticide Residues, agri -
cultural applications should not exceed the set maximum residue limits (MRLs) of soils 
and edible plant tissues. In the case of micronutrient-based systems like ZIF-8 and Cu-
BTC, the calculations of dosage are aimed at keeping soil metal levels below generally 
accepted precautionary levels, usually less than 300 mg kg− 1 and 100 mg kg− 1 of Zn and 
Cu, respectively, in agricultural soils [ 58]. Experiments in the field have shown that ions 
released under controlled conditions in MOF matrices can be kept at concentrations sig-
nificantly lower than these limits. In hybrid systems of pesticides and fertilizers, Zn 2 + 
is emitted in low amounts that ensure that plant tissue does not exceed 100 mgkg − 1 , 
thus, satisfying food-safety requirements and micronutrient sufficiency [ 84, 85]. In the 
case of nanoparticles used for pesticide delivery, regulatory compliance focuses on the 
minimization of terminal residues in crops produced. Acetamiprid@UiO-66-NH₂-CMC 
encapsulation systems can load up to 90.79% and decrease the total environmental input 
by reducing volatilization, spray drift, and runoff [ 19]. The same results are obtained 
with Boscalid@ZIF-67 and IMI@Fe₃O₄-PDA-UiO-66 (15.87% loading). These systems 
offer sustained-release profiles similar to commercial water-dispersible granules, and 
reduce final crop residues by 30–50% compared to conventional foliar treatments [ 46, 
47]. These cuts enhance the difference between measured residues and statutory MRLs.
Existing research indicates that the concentrations of metals in the drinking water 
from MOFs are not expected to exceed drinking water guidelines under controlled 
experimental conditions, but the results are from a relatively small number of studies 
with short monitoring periods. The vertical mobility of the degradation products of dis -
solved and colloidal MOFs in realistic irrigation, rainfall and seasonal field conditions 
is still not well understood. Future studies should involve multi-season field studies to 
assess the transport of metals in the form of colloids, groundwater movement, and the 
overall environmental fate of metals released from MOFs under various soil and climatic 
conditions.
The analysis of post-harvest plant tissues gives ultimate confirmation of compliance 
with the regional food-safety regulations, including the control of the European Food 
Safety Authority. In the case of nitrogen-delivery materials like N-P-MIL-156-H (32.3% 
total nutrient content), regulated release of nutrients prevents high levels of nitrates, 
keeping the concentrations in leafy vegetables within the 2,000–5,000 mg kg− 1 range of

Commission Regulation (EU) No 1258/2011. GR-MOF-27 also promotes the uptake of 
Mg and P , with a 10.5% and 13.1% increase in shoot and root dry mass, respectively, 
without reaching nutritional safety limits or altering organoleptic characteristics [ 27]. 
Ecotoxicological assessment also promotes regulatory acceptance. CLO@ZIF-8 raises 
the acute toxicity threshold of honeybees by at least 120-fold over the free active com -
pound, and PYR@FeMOF–pectin decreases the toxicity of zebrafish by eightfold [ 48, 
71]. Similar rises in EC₅₀ values compared to non-encapsulated formulations are signs of 
increased ecological safety margins and decreased risk to non-target organisms [ 8, 21]. 
Combined, these results indicate that MOF-based agricultural inputs designed with a 
compliance-based design framework can comply with international residue standards, 
be subject to significant in-season mineralization, and be able to sustain crop concentra-
tions within legal safety limits. This combined strategy helps in protecting the environ -
ment and the food chain.
6.3 Long-term soil health indicators
The recovery of soil biological integrity and physical structure following remediation is 
used as a measure of the long-term performance of MOF-based soil amendments. Eval -
uation thus goes beyond the elimination of contaminants and is aimed at stabilizing the 
ecology, so that the detoxification process is accompanied by the restoration of natural 
soil processes [28]. A primary indicator of recovery is the restoration of microbial com -
munity structure and functional diversity after alleviation of stress caused by pollutants. 
Selective pressure on the soil microbiome reduces when MOFs bind heavy metals such 
as Cd and Pb and break down the persistent organic pollutants like PNP and atrazine. 
This enables the ecologically important, but hitherto suppressed, taxa such as Acidobac-
teriota and Actinomycetales to re-colonize polluted microhabitats [ 26, 29]. Experiments 
in the field and mesocosm indicate that frameworks based on iron experience significant 
structural dissociation in a single growing season (usually 90–180 days). This reduces 
the residual metal bioavailability and does not increase the overall Fe concentrations to 
agronomically acceptable levels [26, 29].
The degradation of iron-based MOFs also activates oxalotrophic bacteria, whose oxa -
late linkers are released and metabolized by the oxalate–carbonate pathway. This route 
facilitates mineralization of the remaining framework elements and increases the stabil -
ity of microbial networks [ 7, 26]. Following remediation, soils often have a higher level 
of microbial richness and evenness. The high-throughput 16S rRNA gene sequencing 
shows that the genes related to nitrogen fixation and phosphorus solubilization revert to 
the near-baseline abundance after MOF treatment, which proves the restoration of self-
regulating nutrient cycling processes [8, 21].
Besides microbiological restoration, MOF mineralization has an effect on the physical 
properties of soil. Framework degradation emits Fe, Al and Ca ions, along with organic 
ligands which act as binding agents. These elements enhance the development of stable 
micro-aggregates in the topsoil layer of 0–20 cm. Secondary minerals, such as iron oxy -
hydroxides formed based on the degraded structures, also contribute to the strengthen -
ing of particles and their connectivity [ 16, 29]. WHC is enhanced by the incorporation 
of mineralized linkers into the SOC pool, and by the hierarchical pore structure of 
MOF-biochar composites like WSB-MIL-100. MOF amendments enhance the available 
water capacity in sandy soils by 1.16–2.95% per unit mass addition of organic carbon.

The formation of mesopores with a 0.5–50 µm size also increases moisture retention and 
hydraulic conductivity [ 49]. Responsive MOF systems with sustained-release behavior 
lasting up to 11–52 h allow gradual integration of the mineral and avoid sharp physico -
chemical disruptions, which promote progressive structural stabilization [49].
Future research should aim for an integrated soil regeneration indicator framework 
to allow for a standardized assessment of the remediation achieved by MOF. We sug -
gest the concept of a MOF Soil Regeneration Index (MSRI) that combines four groups 
of indicators: (i) microbial resilience indicators (community diversity, functional gene 
recovery), (ii) nutrient cycling restoration (N fixation, P solubilization, SOC stabiliza -
tion), (iii) contaminant immobilization persistence (residual bioavailability and leach -
ing risk), and (iv) soil structural recovery (aggregate stability, porosity, water retention). 
A multi-dimensional indicator system would allow cross-study comparison, long-term 
field monitoring and life-cycle assessment of MOF technologies. Creating common soil 
health metrics will be key to moving soil health lab results to regulatory approval and 
adoption by farmers.
7 Future directions: autonomous and circular remediation
The future of agricultural MOFs will move from material-based remediation and pas -
sive delivery to autonomous and circular remediation platforms that can sense, respond 
and degrade the environment. Future MOFs can be designed to be self-regulating rhi -
zosphere platforms that actively regulate soil health instead of just removing contami -
nants, as shown in Figure 9 (Bharti et al., 2024). This development is a move towards 
closed-loop soil remediation, where the capture, remediation and recovery of contami -
nants take place in parallel. These intelligent remediation systems are in line with the 
new paradigm in agriculture, which is circular agriculture, where external chemical 
inputs are reduced and in-situ regeneration of soil functionality, nutrient cycling and 
ecosystem resilience are optimized.
7.1 Smart sensors and theranostic MOFs
The concept of theranostic MOFs in agriculture integrates contaminants detection local-
ized remediation. These systems are evaluated using quantitative measurements and 
comprise detection limits, response time, release kinetics, and adsorption capacity [ 86]. 
Unlike the conventional passive sorbents, the advanced MOFs are designed as dual-
functional platforms that identify the contaminants at low concentrations and with high 
sequestration capacity, as shown in Fig. 9. Future research should focus on developing 
autonomous MOF systems that can dynamically respond to fluctuations in contaminant 
concentration, soil pH, redox potential, and microbial activity without external interven-
tion. Beyond laboratory-scale demonstrations, future research should prioritize large-
scale field validation under diverse soil, climatic, and cropping conditions to evaluate the 
robustness, reproducibility, and long-term performance of theranostic MOF platforms. 
The development of standardized field evaluation protocols and long-term environ -
mental monitoring frameworks may also facilitate cross-study comparison and support 
future regulatory assessment.
Luminescent MOFs (LMOFs) have detection limits of the low parts-per-billion of 
metal ions and agrochemicals of environmental interest. Frameworks based on zirco -
nium functionalized with fluorescent ligands have fluorescence quenching efficiencies

quenching constants (Ksv) are typically 104 −105 M−1 heavy metal sensing, meaning that 
the analyte and the framework interact strongly, and response times are in the range of a

LMOFs are used with surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) substrates, which also

to approximately 1–3 mmol g − 1 . This increased the sensitivity of the analysis and the 
adsorption capacity. Fe- and Zr-based MOFs typically possess Pb2 + and Cd2 + adsorption

be programmed to take place when the free Pb 2+ concentrations in the soil pore water 
become 0.1–0.3 mg L −1, which are phytotoxicity-relevant concentrations. GR-MOF-20

7.2 AI-assisted selection of circular linkers
The development of a zero-waste agricultural system requires materials whose func -
tional activity is directly linked to controlled and predictable environmental degrada -
tion. Artificial intelligence (AI) assisted high-throughput screening provides a systematic 
method to select MOF components that maintain structural stability during application 
but undergo programmed dissipation after use [ 87]. Computational toxicology also lim -
its the choice of material before manufacturing. In silico systems assess the predicted 
bioaccumulation potential, redox reactivity and cytotoxicity. It is only structures that are 
projected to retain ≥ 79% of microbial viability following seven days in nutrient-limited 
environments that are advanced to be developed [ 21]. Climatic variables are also used 
in integrated predictive models to estimate environmental persistence. Indicatively, 
regression models replicate the experimentally determined growth of monthly MOF 
degradation between 4.2% and 13.8% at 4.7 °C and 19.8 °C, respectively, with R 2 = 0.99, 
a direct correlation between temperature profiles and structural half-life [ 16]. Future 
studies should expand these predictive systems by integrating soil microbial diversity, 
rhizosphere chemistry, and seasonal climatic variability into AI models to improve field-
level accuracy and long-term environmental forecasting. By optimizing the structural 
stability, degradability, remediation efficiency, and environmental compatibility simulta-
neously, machine learning and AI-assisted material discovery can further expedite the 
identification of biodegradable MOFs and hybrid MOF–biochar composites. Integration 
of high-throughput computational screening with experimental validation could sub -
stantially reduce material development time while improving design reliability.
Deep learning techniques assist in structural optimization by matching the synthesis 
parameters to porosity properties. The algorithm-guided tuning of the WSB@MIL-100 
composite reached a certain surface area of 419 m2 g − 1 , which is significantly greater 
than that of similar rice straw/MIL-100(Fe) hybrids (133.9 m2 g− 1) [29]. The higher the 
surface area, the better the kinetics of adsorption and immobilization of contaminants, 
thus the less material is needed per unit area of soil. Stimuli-responsive release behavior 
is also controlled using AI models. Multivariate algorithms combine soil pH, pathogen 
activity and molecular diffusion coefficients to establish activation thresholds that reflect 
biologically induced microenvironmental changes, including localized acidification dur -
ing infection by Magnaporthe oryzae . This targeted response is linked to an eightfold 
lower acute toxicity to zebrafish in pyraclostrobin-loaded Fe-MOF systems than in non-
encapsulated formulations, which implies a lower off-target exposure [ 48]. Predictive 
algorithms have the potential to estimate adsorption performance under field-relevant 
geochemical conditions, although further experimental validation is required. MOF 
composition and pore architecture have been optimized to soils with up to 142 mmol 
kg− 1 Cu 2 + and be effective at immobilizing the soils without compromising compat -
ibility with native microbial communities and mineral matrices [ 29]. Despite these 
advances, successful translation into agricultural practice will also require techno-eco -
nomic analysis, life-cycle assessment, scalable green synthesis, and regulatory approval 
to evaluate manufacturing costs, environmental sustainability, and practical feasibility.

Addressing these commercialization challenges may accelerate the responsible deploy -
ment of AI-designed MOF technologies for precision and circular agriculture.
At the ecosystem level, AI-based environmental risk models estimated microbial com -
patibility and toxicity levels of MOF materials. Simulations of laboratories suggest that 
microbial respiration in soil does not drop below 85–90% of baseline activity following 
exposure to Fe-MOF concentrations less than 250 mg kg−1 soil, whereas higher concen -
trations start to decrease microbial biomass carbon by 12–18% over 30 days of incuba -
tion [ 88]. AI-driven methods could complement traditional empirical optimization by 
incorporating degradation kinetics, ecotoxicological data, climate factors, and sorption 
thermodynamics in computational models. Moving forward, there is a need for the cre -
ation of explainable AI frameworks and standardized environmental datasets to enhance 
the transparency, reproducibility, and potential regulatory acceptance of AI-assisted 
MOF technologies. As these methods develop and are further validated, they can help 
to achieve more sustainable agronomic performance, precision remediation and better 
nutrient management.
7.3 Scaling strategies for large-area deployment
The transition of MOF-based remediation technologies from small laboratory produc -
tion to large-scale field use is an important step for their practical application [ 89]. At 
this larger scale, success depends not only on how well the material works but also on 
several other factors, including the availability of raw materials, efficiency of synthesis, 
behavior in agricultural soils, and how the material breaks down in the environment at 
the end of its use [40]. Agronomic return is becoming more and more considered in eco-
nomic models. GR-MOF-27 boosts shoot, root and total dry biomass by 10.5%, 11.0%, 
and 13.1%, respectively, relative to conventional MgSO₄ fertilization (Morales-Camara, 
2025). These findings indicate the potential to offset synthesis costs; however, confirma -
tion under large-scale field conditions is still required.
Adaptability to the current agricultural infrastructure also promotes adoption. The 
seed-coating system, like IPC@UiO-66-TA-Zn 2 + is composed of monodisperse spheri -
cal particles (~296 nm) which can be easily used with the traditional mechanical seeders, 
thus avoiding the need to modify equipment. Efficacies of reported pathogen control are 
84.09–93.10%, which is higher than conventional suspension concentrates. The emer -
gence rates of seedlings are 88–92.67%, which means that the germination performance 
is maintained [8]. This is an operational compatibility, which enables it to integrate into 
the existing agronomic practices. Commercialization is likely to be enhanced by regu -
latory developments. With more stringent rules and restrictions on heavy metal soil 
concentration, Fe-based MOFs offer a degradable alternative to the non-biodegradable 
chelating agents. Slow transformation into stable mineral phases facilitates adherence 
to the regulations of trace-metal mobility and environmental persistence [ 7]. Taken 
together, these developments suggest the emergence of a data-driven, self-optimizing 
remediation paradigm in which MOFs evolve from static materials into adaptive envi -
ronmental technologies. Future research should therefore prioritize the integration of 
sensing networks, long-term field trials, and regulatory frameworks to enable the tran -
sition of MOF technologies from experimental demonstrations to resilient agricultural 
infrastructure.

8 Conclusion
This review underscores the potential of MOFs as a revolutionary material for contami -
nated soil remediation and regeneration in agriculture. MOFs are engineered to perform 
multiple functions, such as pollutant adsorption, controlled delivery of agrochemicals, 
microbial compatibility, and environmentally synchronized degradation, transforming 
the concept of remediation from a one-function intervention to a multifunctional soil 
management strategy. MOFs allow for the removal of contaminants without compro -
mising soil structure or biological activity, unlike traditional approaches that can damage 
soil structure and biodiversity. One of the major benefits of MOFs that are environmen -
tally friendly is their life-cycle functionality. Once pollutants are captured or agrochemi -
cals released, many MOFs degrade slowly over time via microbial activity, leaving behind 
stable mineral products and releasing plant nutrients. This property enables MOFs to be 
used as regenerative materials that can integrate pollution control with nutrient recy -
cling, soil fertility improvement and ecosystem restoration, which will facilitate a tran -
sition to circular soil management. Future developments in stimuli-responsive release, 
smart sensing, and AI-driven material design may further advance MOF technologies, 
although their practical performance requires validation under diverse field conditions. 
But, field validation, scalable synthesis, life-cycle assessment and regulatory frameworks 
are still essential for real-world implementation. In summary, the synergy of nanotech -
nology, soil science, and sustainable agriculture suggests a circular agro-nanotechnology 
approach where innovative materials contribute to pollution reduction, nutrient recov -
ery, and sustainable food production. Interdisciplinary research will continue to be cru -
cial to bring innovations from the lab to the field and to the farm.
