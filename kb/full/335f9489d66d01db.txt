Abstract Nutrient deficient foods are widely recognized as a major cause of malnutrition and also impact global 
food security. This has increased focus on nutrient-rich, underutilized food crops as a potential solution. Macrotyloma 
uniflorum (Horse gram) is an important yet neglected legume, rich in proteins, carbohydrates, vitamins, fatty acids, 
fiber, minerals, and valuable phytochemicals, making it useful for both human and animal consumption. Horse 
gram is mentioned in various traditional food systems, underscoring its cultural significance. The crop can withstand 
harsh environmental conditions such as drought, and low soil fertility. Considering the nutritional benefits of horse 
gram, there is a need to develop conservation strategies and value-added products to boost its market potential 
as a sustainable solution against global food insecurity and malnutrition. This review provides an insight into horse 
gram’s botanical features, nutritional profile, and development of value-added products. The overall aim is to highlight 
the benefits of horse gram and encourage its value addition, and utilization to address malnutrition, enhance food 
security, and support sustainable agriculture.

 Background
Out of the approximately 3,74,000 known plant spe -
cies that have been accepted worldwide, more than 
50,000 plant species contribute to edible plants includ -
ing 7,039 wild species [ 1, 2]. India alone comprises 8% 
of the world’s plant species, with 1,403 wild edible plants 
belonging to 184 families out of the 45,000 globally 
recorded species [ 3, 4]. Despite such abundance, there 
are only 15 food crops that serve 90% of the human pop -
ulation’s energy intake and only 3 staple crops, including 
maize, wheat and rice, serving more than 4 billion people 
across the world [5].
The 2023 Global hunger Index (GHI) data report a 
moderate score of 18.3, with undernourishment rising 
to 735  million people. Malnutrition and micronutrient 
deficiencies result from diets high in calories but lacking 
essential nutrients. Utilizing underexploited edible crops 
could support a sustainable food system and help achieve 
SDG − 1 (Zero hunger) by 2030 [6].
Macrotyloma uniflorum , commonly known as horse 
gram, is an under-utilized, nutrient-dense legume of the 
Fabaceae family [ 7]. It is a herbaceous, annual plant rich 
in proteins, carbohydrates, fatty acids, vitamins, miner -
als, and phytochemicals such as phenols, flavonoids and

anthocyanins [ 8, 9]. Adapted to drought and poor soil 
conditions, horse grams’ resilience makes it suitable for 
rainfed agriculture in arid or drought-prone regions [ 11]. 
Its nutritional profile and environmental tolerance posi -
tion it as a potential candidate for addressing malnutri -
tion and enhancing food security through sustainable 
agriculture in developing countries [11, 12]. Traditionally, 
seeds are soaked before cooking, as authentic cuisines 
are known to improve digestion and provide a nutritious 
diet. The leaves are also consumed in some regions as a 
rich source of vitamins and minerals [10, 13].
Despite its nutritional properties, the crop has been 
overlooked in favor of more commercially viable crops 
due to low market demand and limited consumer aware -
ness. Factors such as shorter shelf-life caused by pest 
infestation, and the presence of nutrient-limiting com -
pounds that impair mineral bioavailability, further hin -
der its cultivation and utilization. Therefore, developing 
advanced processing techniques and product innovation 
is critical to promote its cultivation and consumption.
This review emphasizes the significance of increas -
ing awareness about the nutritional profile and health-
promoting properties of horse gram, advocating for 
enhanced research aimed at agronomic yield optimi -
zation and nutritional enhancements. Such efforts are 
intended to facilitate greater adoption and commercial 
exploitation through value-added processing.

Horse gram: habit and habitat
The name Macrotyloma originates from a combination 
of Greek words, “makros” , “tylos” and “loma” , mean -
ing “larger knob” , and “margin” ,, respectively referring to 
Macrotyloma as “knobby stature on pods” [ 14]. Horse 
gram is an annual perennial crop (Fig.  1) that thrives in 
soils like clay loam paddy, deep red loam and black cotton 
soil with an annual temperature requirement of 18–27 °C 
[7]. It tolerates 40 °C, but grows more slowly below 20 °C 
[15]. Reaching an average height of 30–40  cm, it has 
a weak and slender stem that makes it grow like a bush 
[16]. Tilling, sowing, and nutrient management methods 
significantly improve horse gram production [ 17]. The 
crop is cultivated worldwide in subtropical, temperate, 
and semi-arid areas at an elevation of 1800 m above sea 
level (asl) or higher, and on low or marginal fertility soil 
with rainfall as low as 380 mm [18]. It is mostly cultivated 
for food and fodder in Africa, South-Eastern Asia and 
Australia [19]. Crop residue and straw are used as green 
manure, fodder and fuel booster.
Nutritional profile
Horse gram is a rich source of carbohydrates, protein, 
fats, fibers, vitamins and minerals and nutrients like iron, 
molybdenum and calcium [ 20]. Its nutritional profile is 
influenced by factors including genotype, soil, fertilizer 
application, weather and climatic conditions, cultural 
practices, postharvest handling and storage [ 11]. Despite

majorly limited to rural farming communities and low-

legume [ 21]. Several studies have attempted to quantify 
the nutrients in horse gram seeds. The carbohydrates 
in whole and dehulled horse gram seeds are known to 
vary from 51.9 to 60.9% and 56.8–66.4%, respectively 
and include soluble sugars like glucose, inositol, arabi -
nose, galactose, fructose, sucrose and maltose [ 22, 23]. 
Raw seeds contain 22–44% starch (about 85% digestible 
starch, 14.47% resistant starch with 3.38% linked with 
insoluble dietary fibers) [ 24]. The protein content in 
horse gram seeds varies from 18.5 to 31.2%, depending 
on the cultivar [ 25]. Dehulled seeds exhibit higher pro -
tein levels (18.4–25.5%) compared to whole seeds (17.9–
25.3%). The wild variety Macrotyloma sargarhwalensis , 
has a higher protein content (38.4 ± 1.03%) as compared 
to other commonly grown cultivars [ 26]. Globulin con -
stitutes 60–90% of total protein content, with glutelin 
and albumin each accounting for 10–20% [ 27]. Horse 
gram makes a good complement to a cereal-based diet 
due to higher lysine content (0.52 g g − 1 of N) than black 
gram (0.48  g g − 1 of N) and pigeon pea (0.40  g g − 1 of 
N) and surpasses kidney beans in several amino acids: 
threonine (3.8 mg/100 g), leucine (9 mg/100 g), iso-leu -
cine (6.1 mg/100 g), phenylalanine (6.3 mg/100 g), valine 
(6.5 mg/100 g) and histidine (3.2 mg/100 g), compared to 
lower levels in kidney beans [7, 28]. These attributes posi-
tion horse gram as a valuable plant-based protein source. 
The fat content in horse gram ranges from 0.6% to 2.6%, 
with dehulled seeds containing 0.8 to 2.1%, and whole 
seeds exhibiting 0.7 to 2.1% crude fat [ 20]. The seeds are 
rich in essential fatty acids, comprising 27.5% saturated 
and 72.5% unsaturated fatty acids, including linoleic, 
oleic, and linolenic acids [ 20, 29]. Total dietary fiber con-
tent is 28.8%, predominantly insoluble fiber (27.8%) and 
soluble fiber (1.1%), with an IDF: SDF ratio of 24.6. Con -
versely, horse gram flour contains 16.3% dietary fiber- 
14.9% insoluble, 1.4% soluble, and 2.2% resistant starch 
[30]. The fiber exists as beta-glucan, pectin, hemicellu -
lose, and cellulose [31]. Moisture content in seeds ranges 
from 6.5% to 10.8%, and ash content varies from 3.4% 
to 4.3%, indicating mineral richness [ 25, 32, 33]. Horse 
gram is also an exceptional source of minerals, notably 
calcium, iron, zinc, magnesium, phosphorus and potas -
sium [ 34]. The leaves contain about 4.5% mineral con -
tent, higher than that of other vegetables, which range 
from 1.5 to 2.4% [ 35]. The seeds also provide vitamins 
such as niacin, pantothenic acid, riboflavin, pyridoxine, 
thiamine, tocopherol, and folic acid, which are essential 
for physiological functions; deficiencies of which may 
cause diseases such as night blindness, rickets, and beri -
beri [36]. Nutritional profiles of different plant parts and 
comparative analyses with other legumes are summa -
rized in Tables 1 and 2.
Phytochemical profile
Horse gram is also a rich source of phytochemicals. The 
seeds contain about 0.9 mg/g of total phenols, predomi -
nantly p- coumaric acid (40.9  µg/g dry weight dw) and 
p-hydroxy benzoic acid with 20.1  µg/g dw). The cotyle -
dons are rich in phenolics, including, flavonoids, such 
as ferulic acid, p-hydroxybenzoic acid (7.8  mg/100 dw), 
quercetin (129.5 mg/100 g dw), myricetin (35.5 mg/100 g 
dw) and kaempferol (117.2  mg/100  g dw). The embry -
onic axes have been reported to contain isoflavonoids- 
daidzein (22.2 mg/100 g) and genistein (44.7 mg/ 100 g) 
[42, 43, 44]. Tannin concentrations range from 763.7 
to 895.9  mg/ 100  g, exceeding those in other legumes 
[34]. Phytic acid, or inositol hexaphosphate, consti -
tutes the primary phosphorus reservoir, with levels of 
184 mg/100 g in seeds (accounting for approximately 57% 
of total phosphorus) and about 10.2  mg/g in flour [ 16]. 
The structures of these phenolic compounds, flavonoids, 
and tannins are depicted in Fig. 2.
Nutrient-limiting compounds and processing methods to 
mitigate their effect
Pulses contain compounds called nutrient-limiting fac -
tors, such as alkaloids, saponins, tannins, phenols, phytic 
acid, and protein inhibitors. Horse gram seeds specifically 
contain amylase and trypsin inhibitors, and phytates. The 
flour prepared from seeds has been found to contain 
phytic acid (10.2 ± 0.4  mg/g), polyphenols (14.3 ± 0.4  mg 
GA/g), oligosaccharides (26.8 mg/g), and trypsin inhibi -
tor (9246 ± 18 trypsin units inhibited TIU/g) [ 45]. These 
compounds, while offering some health benefits-includ -
ing reduced risk of colon cancer, constipation, gallstones, 
heart disease, diverticulosis, dental caries and diabetes, 
significantly impair mineral bioavailability [ 46]. Phy -
tates, tannins and oxalic acid chelate minerals like iron 
and calcium, forming insoluble salts that hinder absorp -
tion, despite the high mineral content of horse gram 
[47]. Other antinutritional factors include proteinase and 
trypsin inhibitors, flatulence factors and hemagglutinins. 
Protease inhibitors hinder protein digestion by targeting 
proteolytic enzymes, thereby impacting the nutritional 
quality [23]. Trypsin inhibitors, predominantly abundant 
in horse gram compared to other legumes, significantly 
block the trypsin activity [ 48]. Oligosaccharides such 
as verbascose, stachyose, and raffinose—α-galactosyl 
sucrose derivatives in horse gram can cause gastrointes -
tinal discomfort [49]. Hemagglutinins (lectins and phyto-
agglutinins), mainly located in seeds, but also present in 
roots, stems and leaves [ 50], may induce intestinal dam -
age, nutritional anemia by interacting with immune fac -
tors and erythrocytes [ 51]. Proper processing reduces

Processing methods

its nutritional efficacy by affecting bioavailability, absorp-
tion and digestibility, thereby decreasing its dietary uti -
lization [ 52, 53]. Processing techniques such as soaking, 
roasting, cooking, dehusking, and germination effectively 
reduce these antinutrients, enhancing both acceptability 
and nutritional profile, as illustrated in Fig. 3 [32].

compounds, including trypsin inhibitors [32].
Dehulling Removal of seed hulls reduces oxalic acid, 
trypsin inhibitors, phytic acid and tannins, concurrently 
increasing protein, lipid, and sugar content. The process 
may decrease the levels of antioxidant compounds, such 
as flavonoids, thereby affecting their antioxidant proper -
ties. When combined with germination, dehulling syn -
ergistically enhances horse gram’s nutritive value and 
reduces antinutrients [53].
Fermentation Treatment with sodium hypochlorite fol -
lowed by 48-hour fermentation decreases pH from 6.4 to 
4.6, with reductions in phytate, oxalate, and tannins by 
69.5%, 66.8%, and 69.4%, respectively. Phytate degradation 
is mediated by phytase activity from lactic acid bacteria at 
low pH; oxalate and tannins are utilized and hydrolyzed 
during fermentation [52].
Roasting Roasting dried seeds at 160 °C in an iron pan 
for 10 min reduces tannins, flavonoids, polyphenols, phy-
tates, and oxalis by 28%, 48.7%, 28.3%, 22.4%, and 22.45%, 
respectively. These reductions result from thermal dena -
turation and insoluble complex formation; flavonoids and 
polyphenols decline due to thermal degradation [52, 55].
Impact of value-addition to horse gram-based food 
products: opportunities for achieving sustainable 
development goals
Value addition is a vital process for enhancing the mar -
ketability of agricultural commodities by improving 
product quality and brand recognition, thereby facili -
tating increased income generation and employment 
opportunities [ 56]. It enables year-round accessibility 
and prolongs shelf-life, effectively reducing post har -
vest losses. Additionally, value addition can strengthen 
off-farm employment, promote technology transfer, and 
augment workforce capacity, ultimately contributing to 
economic development of a nation [57, 58]. The extent of 
value addition can be quantified by comparing the costs 
associated with raw material acquisition and inputs to the 
returns from the processed, value added products [59].
Horse gram presents a viable candidate for value-addi -
tion through biofortification to enhance its nutritional 
and phytochemical profiles, enabling the development 
of diverse horse gram-based food products. Several 
companies have developed various horse gram-based 
food products. Table  3 provides a list of some globally 
reported food items made from horse gram, that can be 
seamlessly integrated into daily dietary practices.
Research gaps and methodological limitations
Research on horse gram has primarily focused on its 
nutritional composition, phytochemical properties, and 
applications in value-added food products, establish -
ing the crop as a rich source of protein, dietary fibers, 
and minerals [ 25, 26, 34]. Studies have also investigated 
the effects of processing treatments, including dehull -
ing, soaking, roasting, germination, and fermentation, on 
improving nutrient bioavailability and reducing antinu -
tritional factors like phytates and tannins [ 23, 32]. Fur-
thermore, the incorporation of horse gram into diverse 
food products has demonstrated considerable poten -
tial for nutritional enhancement, particularly through 
increased protein and fiber content [ 62, 64]. Despite 
the growing body of research, several important knowl -
edge gaps continue to hinder the effective utilization and 
commercialization of horse gram. Existing studies are 
frequently constrained by limited germplasm representa -
tion, small sample sizes, and a lack of standardized meth -
odologies for nutritional and phytochemical analysis, 
making comparisons across studies difficult. Moreover, 
the influence of environmental and agronomic factors on 
nutrient composition and bioactive compounds remains 
inadequately understood. Research on shelf-life stabil -
ity, storage characteristics, industrial-scale processing 
feasibility, consumer acceptance based on sensory attri -
butes is also limited. Furthermore, many of the proposed 
health-promoting and functional properties of horse 
gram require stronger mechanistic and clinical valida -
tion. By critically synthesizing the available evidence, this 
review identifies key research priorities and provides a 
comprehensive framework for future investigations. In 
doing so, it enhances the current understanding of horse 
gram and supports its broader utilization in sustainable 
agriculture, functional food development, and nutrition-
sensitive food systems.

this crop’s inherent resilience, rich nutritional and phy -
tochemical profile, advanced processing approaches, and 
clinical validation will position it as a key component in 
addressing food security and climate resilience.
Conclusion
Horse gram is an ancient, nutrient-dense legume crop 
rich in bioactive compounds with potential applications 
in the food and pharmaceutical sectors, thus contributing 
to public health. Efforts need to focus on enhancing yield, 
quality, and genotypic and phenotypic traits, as well as on 
germplasm conservation. The crop’s underutilization is 
primarily due to nutrient-limiting factors; however, pro -
cessing techniques and breeding low-nutrient-inhibiting 
cultivars can mitigate these issues. Further research is 
also needed to identify its bioactive constituents, and elu-
cidate the mechanisms underlying their effects on human 
health. A sustainable production of horse gram would 
require integrated approaches in conservation, standard -
ized nutritional profiling, product development, con -
sumer acceptance and market infrastructure to realize its 
full potential.
