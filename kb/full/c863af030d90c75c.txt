Bāules, a forest-dependent occupational group, traverse the precarious ecologies of the Sundarban, where survival hinges on ritual mastery as much as on physical abilities. Positioned at the liminal threshold between human settlement and wilderness, they enact knowledge that blurs the boundaries between technique and devotion. Their engagements with unseen forces emerge in everyday acts of mantras, gestures, and rituals, rather than doctrinal categories. These practices resonate with a mode of knowing that is operative, sensory, and relational. Grounded in ethnographic fieldwork, this article explores how bāules navigate the forest while not claiming to be tantric specialists, even though their practice falls broadly in the academic classification of tantra. It asks: How do bāules deploy mantra, bodily discipline, and ritual improvisation to negotiate agency in a space where human dominance is tenuous? And what does their praxis reveal about tantra not as a fixed system but as a dispersed, vernacular, and materially embedded field of action? By centring their experiential knowledge, including the significance of dream visions, this study contributes to ongoing conversations on vernacular tantra, ritual ecologies, and the sensory dimensions of religious practice in South Asia.
