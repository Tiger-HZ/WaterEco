ABSTRACT 
This investigation on assessing the impact of fly -ash slurry discharge due to Ash Pond breach reveals 
pronounced spatial and seasonal dynamics in the abundanc e, structure, and diversity of plankton 
communities in freshwater bodies located near Ib Thermal Power Station. (ITPS).The transition from pre-
monsoon to post -monsoon conditions served as a key environmental driver, eliciting clear ecological 
responses across both phytoplankton and zooplankton assemblages. Post-monsoon conditions favored an 
increase in total phytoplankton abundance at both the control and experimental sites. Taxonomic shifts 
were characterized by an expansion of Chlorophyceae and Bacillario phyceae, alongside a sharp decline 
in Cyanophyceae representation. While phytoplankton Shannon diversity remained stable at the control 
site, it exhibited a notable post-monsoon increase at the experimental site. The corresponding reduction in 
Simpson’s dominance index reflects reduced single-taxon dominance, whereas the concurrent decline in 
species evenness highlights a less uniform distribution of individuals across the recorded taxa. Seasonal 
restructuring was even more pronounced within the zooplankton community. Pre-monsoon assemblages 
were primarily characterized by Copepoda and Rhizopoda, whereas Cladocera emerged as the dominant 
taxon post -monsoon. Although post -monsoon Shannon diversity increased across both study sites and 
Simpson’s dominance decreased, the simultaneous drop in evenness indicates that overall diversity gains 
stemmed from shifting taxon representation rather than balanced numerical distribution. The study was 
carried out after almost one year of ash pond breach. Therefore,no conclusive evidence could be obtained 
on its negative impact on aquatic planktonic community. Two-way ANOVA test indicates that these 
variations were statistically significant. Both site and season independently influenced phytoplankton ( 𝑝

INTRODUCTION 
Thermal Power Plants are mega projects that require huge quantity of coal. Consequently, they have 
negative environmental impacts and put pressure on the local ecosystem. Ash sludge and fly ash represent 
a significant proportion of the solid waste arising from the combustion of coal in these plants. The soil and 
water bodies of the neighboring areas are physically and chemically contaminated by fly a sh (Kakde & 
Sitre (2015).Fresh water is becoming a global problem as the rate of deterioration in water quality is 
increasing every day due to Municipality and Industrial waste water contamination (Mahananda et al. 
2005).The aquatic organisms which include phytoplankton, zooplankton, benthos, fish and macrophytes 
are significantly impacted due to such contamination resulting in reduced aquatic productivity. Release of 
Coal fly ash in to water adverselyaffectects the aquatic system’s chemical composition an d physical and 
biological components (Borm,1997 ,Manz et al.1999, Adriano et al, 1980,Ghio et al 2002,Saha et al.2020). 
Rankhamb, & Mulgir,(2024) evaluated planktonic diversity as Bioin dicators of environmental stress 
thermal/industrial discharge conditions.They demonstrated how changes in physicochemical parameters 
(turbidity, pH, trace metal loading) alter the composition, abundance, and dominance patterns of 
zooplankton species (Rotifera, Cladocera, Copepoda). Jaunjal and Gaupale (2025) in their review article 
on Impact of Coal Fly Ash on Freshwater Fishes and the Ecosystem” opined that excess accumulation of 
fly ash in the water ecosystem significantly affects organisms by altering the physical properties of 
ecosystem, physiological biomechanisms, toxicity stress, and health issues. Consumption of contaminated 
foods results in the bioaccumulation and biotransformation of hazardous elements in the food chain that 
threaten to aquatic biodiversity. 
The leaching of metals in aquatic systems leads to bioaccumulation, induction of oxidative stress, tissue 
damage, and alteration in blood biochemistry has been emphasized in the fishes. Excess accumulation of 
fly ash in the water ecosystem significantly affects organisms by altering the physical proper ties of 
ecosystem, physiological biomechanisms, toxicity stress, and health issues. Consumption of contaminated 
foods results in the bioaccumulation and biotransformation of hazardous elements in the food chain that 
threaten to aquatic biodiversity. Theref ore, the present review aims to overview and highlight the 
toxicological impact of fly ash pollution on aquatic ecosystems and fish health. In addition, it is helpful to 
understand the level of toxicity and assess its effect on the environment. 
The breach of ash pond/dyke C at the IB Thermal Power Station (ITPS) in Banharpal li of Jharsuguda 
district, Odisha, on December 9, 2023, led to the spillage of fly ash and slurry into surrounding agricultural 
fields, grazing lands, and water bodies. Fly ash, a by-product of coal combustion, contains trace elements 
and heavy metals such as arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), mercury (Hg), and iron (Fe), which pose 
significant environmental and health risks ( Spencer et. al.1983, Walia and Mehra, 1998, Yi et al.2024). 
As planktons are the most important components of the aquatic ecosystem to maintain its stability and to 
adapt to any environmental changes, observation of the structure of phytoplankton communities can be a 
reliable method for biological monitoring stud ies to assess the state of pollution of aquatic organisms .

MATERIALS AND METHODS 
The collection of water samples were made during in pre -monsoon (May -June), and post -monsoon 
(October- November) between 9 to 10 am. The area was not accessible during mons oon due to spread -
over water from the Hirakud Reservoir. For collection of samples, 14 stations (7 stations from each from 
Experimental and Control sites. 
Plankton samples were collected by means of a bucket (50 litre) and filtered through plankton net of 50µ. 
The filtrate was transferred to 50ml plastic bottle and preserved immediately in 4% formalin. The sample 
was thereafter taken to the laboratory for qualitative and quantitative analysis. Supernatant plankton was 
removed from free water and sedimentary plankton was counted by Sedgewick rafter counting cell (1 ml 
capacity) and then the planktons were identified under compound microscope. 
To record the seasonal nature of plankton diversity in these location Shannon Diversity Index, Simpson 
Dominance Index (D), Evenness Index, Similar ity Index (S) were used. These are the most suitable 
statistical tools to determine the phytoplankton species diversity

variation. During the pre -monsoon season, Chlorophyceae was dominant in both control (48.71%) and

monsoon season, Chlorophyceae increased to 65% in the control and 43.57% in the experimental site, 
while Bacillariophyceae also increased markedly to 51.71% and 37.14%, respectively. In contrast, 
Cyanophyceae declined to 20.43% and 22.43%. Overall, the post -monsoon season was characterized by 
higher contributions of Chlorophyceae and Bacillariophyceae and a decline in Cyanophyceae.

DISCUSSION 
This study tried to examine any likely impact of Ash Pond Slurry discharge of Planktonic communities 
the seasonal dynamics of the plankton communities. The spread-over water of Hirakud Dam Reservoir as 
well as other small waterbodies close to ITPS, Banharpali as experimental site and Rampela as control 
site. The composition and abundance of plankton was assessed during the pre-monsoon and post-monsoon 
periods at seven sampling stations in each site. The study shows distinct spatial and seasonal variations in 
the phytoplankton and zooplankton communities as well as in the zooplankton population. 
Seasonal variation in phytoplankton 
Phytoplankton communities showed clear differences between the pre- and post-monsoon period. During 
the pre-monsoon period, chlorophyll was the major phytoplankton group in both control and experimental 
sites, accounting for 37.60 and 34.61 percent of the total. The second main group was Cyanophyceae, 
accounting for 33.62 percent in the control setting and 40.34 percent in t he experimental samples. 
Bacillophyceae accounted for 13.56 and 13.00 percent of the total, while Euglenophyceae and 
Chrysophyceae accounted for a relatively smaller share. Vasculariaceae were only observed in the control 
site (0.55%) 
During the post-monsoon period, the relative contribution of chlorophyll increased to 43.88 percent in the 
control and 40.61 percent in the experimental setting. Bacillariophyceae also increased significantly, with 
34.91 and 34.62 percent. In contrast, cyanophyceae decreased to 13.79 percent in the control and 20.91 
percent in the experimental samples. Euglenophyceae also decreased, while Chrysophyceae decreased in 
the control with no decrease in the experimental samples. Vaculariaceae were not recorded in either of 
these locations during the post-monsoon period 
During the post-monsoon period, phytoplankton abundance increased across both locations. The control 
site recorded rise in average numbers from 129.57 Nos./ml in the pre-monsoon season to 148.14 Nos./ml 
in the post-monsoon season, while the experimental site increased from 74.71 to 107.29 Nos./ml. 
A two-way ANOVA confirmed that these trends were statistically significant. Season was a key factor 
driving phytoplankton density ( 𝐹 = 18.596, 𝑝 = 0.000239 ), and the overall abundance differed 
substantially between the control and experimental sites (𝐹 = 65.133, 𝑝 = 2.7 × 10−8). 
Because the interaction between site and season was not significant (𝐹 = 1.394, 𝑝 = 0.249), both location 
and time of year affected phyto plankton levels independently, meaning the seasonal shifts occurred 
similarly at both sites. 
Phytoplankton Community Dynamics and Diversity 
Phytoplankton diversity metrics highlighted clear shifts between seasons. At the control site, Shannon 
diversity remained steady, shifting marginally from 3.213 before the monsoon to 3.208 after. Conversely, 
the experimental site experienced a rise from 2.976 to 3.113, demonstrating a more pronounced seasonal 
enhancement in species diversity. 
Simpson’s dominance index decreased post-monsoon at both locations—dropping from 0.050 to 0.044 at 
the control site and from 0.071 to 0.049 at the experimental site. Given that lower values reflect diminished

dominance by specific individual taxa, this drop points to a less to heavy phytoplankton community 
following the rains. 
However, species evenness fell sharply post -monsoon, dropping from 0.896 to 0.46 2 at the control site 
and from 0.844 to 0.471 at the experimental site. This indicates that while overall diversity remained stable 
or increased, individuals were distributed far less uniformly across taxa. 
Zooplankton Seasonal Restructuring and Composition 
Zooplankton populations also underwent prominent seasonal shifts. Pre -monsoon sampling revealed 
Copepoda as the dominant t axon at the control site ( 10.57 Nos./ml), followed by Rhizopoda ( 7.71 
Nos./ml). At the experimental site, Rhizopoda led ( 5.00 Nos./ml), with Copepoda secondary ( 3.29 
Nos./ml). 
Following the monsoon, Cladocera replaced these groups as the prevailing zooplankton at both locations. 
Its mean density grew from 4.71 to 12.00 Nos./mL at the control site and from 
0.71 to 8.43 Nos./ml at the experimental site, whereas Copepoda abundance dropped to 
7.71 and 3.14 Nos./ml, respectively. The post-monsoon emergence of Branchionide and Crustacea further 
underscores this structural turnover. 
Relative abundance trends mirrored these density shifts. Pre -monsoon zooplankton s were primarily 
composed of Copepoda and Rhizopoda, but post-monsoon communities shifted heavily toward Cladocera. 
At the experimental site, Cladocera jumped from 6.10% to 45.04% of the community, while Rotifera 
disappeared entirely from 23.17%. 
Zooplankton Diversity Metrics 
Zooplankton diversity improved across both sites after the monsoon. Shannon diversity values climbed 
from 2.436 to 2.72 at the control site and from 1.809 to 2.3645 at the experimental site, confirming richer 
community diversity during the post-monsoon period. 
Simpson's dominance index declined from 0.104 to 0.066 at the control site and from 0.222 to 0.100 at the 
experimental site, signaling reduced numerical dominance by single groups. However, species evenness 
fell from 0.878 to 0.502 at the control site and from 0.823 to 0.485 at the experimental site, showing that 
higher post-monsoon diversity was driven by taxa presence rather than an even balance of individuals 
among groups. 
Statistical and Ecosystem Synthesis 
A two-way ANOVA confirmed that location and season both significantly altered zooplankton abundance. 
Inter-site differences were highly significant ( 𝐹 = 45.406, 𝑝 = 5.71 × 10−7), as was seasonal variation ( 𝐹 
= 6.163, 𝑝 = 0.02043). The interaction between site and season was not significant (𝐹 = 0.458, 𝑝 = 0.505), 
establishing that seasonal gains occurred independently of location. 
In summary, the planktonic ecosystem showed strong seasonal reorganization. Post -monsoon conditions 
boosted phytoplankton abundance through expanded Chlorophy ceae and Bacillariophyceae populations 
shifting away from pre -monsoon Cyanophyceae prominence while zooplankton transitioned from 
Copepoda- and Rhizopoda-heavy communities to Cladocera dominated assemblages.
