Intensifying food–forage production on medium land in 
Assam through teosinte–ricebean intercropping, 
integrated nutrient management, and relay rapeseed 
 
Joshila Enghipi and Karuna Kanta Sharma 
 
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/2618060X.2026.v9.i9Sa.6489 
 
Abstract 
A two-year field experiment was conducted during 2016 –17 and 2017 –18 at Assam Agricultural 
University, Jorhat, to evaluate an intensive food –forage cropping system under medium -land conditions. 
The study examined sole teosinte , sole ricebean and teosinte –ricebean intercropping in 3:2 and 3:3 row 
ratios under four nutrient -management treatments: 100% recommended dose of fertilizer (RDF) , 50% 
nitrogen (N) through RDF + 50% N through farmyard manure (FYM) , 50% N through RDF + 50% N 
through vermicompost , and an organic combination of FYM and vermicompost. The effects of these 
treatments on succeeding rice and relay rapeseed , system productivity and economics were also assessed. 
The 3:2 arrangement produced the highest teosinte forage yield among intercrops , whereas the 3:3 
arrangement produced superior ricebean forage yield and overall intercropping performance , with land 
equivalent ratios of 1.37 and 1.40 and the highest green -forage-equivalent yields of 859.19 and 901.79 q 
ha-1 during the two years. The applicat ion of 50% N through RDF + 50% N through vermicompost 
consistently improved forage production , nutrient uptake , succeeding rice and relay rapeseed yields and 
economic returns. Economically , sole ricebean followed by rice and relay rapeseed generated the hi ghest 
net returns (Rs. 73, 896 and Rs. 80, 312 ha-1) and benefit–cost ratios (1.41 and 1.53) in the two years, ahead 
of the 3:3 teosinte – ricebean system (Rs. 71 , 244 and Rs. 77 , 634 ha-1; B:C 1.16 and 1.27) , while 
cultivation cost and profitability were m ost favourable under 50% N through RDF + 50% N through 
vermicompost (B:C 1.37 and 1.55) compared with sole inorganic fertilization or full organic substitution. 
Overall, 3:3 teosinte + ricebean followed by rice with recommended integrated nutrient manageme nt and 
relay rapeseed, combined with 50% RDF -N and 50% vermicompost -N in the forage phase , was identified 
as a suitable and economically viable intensive cropping strategy for medium -land situations of Assam , 
while sole ricebean–rice–rapeseed offered the greatest net profitability among the systems tested. 
 
Keywords: Food–forage system, teosinte, ricebean, intercropping, integrated nutrient management , relay 
rapeseed, land equivalent ratio 
 
Introduction 
Livestock production is an important component of sma llholder farming systems in India , 
particularly in the northeastern region , where crop residues, green fodder, and livestock manure 
are closely integrated with crop production. However , the availability of cultivated forage 
remains inadequate in relation t o livestock requirements. This shortage is aggravated by limited 
land resources , seasonal variation in forage supply , increasing competition between food and 
fodder crops , and the need to maintain household income from small holdings. Under these 
conditions, intensifying production through suitable food –forage cropping systems offers an 
opportunity to increase total output without requiring additional land. Food –forage systems can 
be intensified through both spatial and temporal diversification. Spatial div ersification involves 
growing cereal and legume crops together , while temporal diversification involves growing 
successive crops within the same year. Intercropping cereals with legumes can improve land-use 
efficiency through differences in canopy structur e, rooting pattern , nutrient requirement , and 
growth duration. A cereal component generally contributes high biomass production, whereas 
the legume component can improve forage protein content and contribute to biological nitrogen 
fixation. The productivity advantage of intercropping is commonly evaluated using the land

equivalent ratio (LER) , with values greater than one indicating 
that the intercrop uses land more efficiently than the 
corresponding sole crops [1, 2]. 
Teosinte (Euchlaena mexicana Schrad.) is a fast-growing cereal 
forage crop with considerable biomass -producing ability and 
adaptability to warm , humid conditions. Ricebean ( Vigna 
umbellata (Thunb.) Ohwi & Ohashi) is a forage legume that can 
complement cereal crops by contributing protein -rich biomass 
and improving the biological diversity of the cropping system. 
Earlier studies have demonstrated the potential of teosinte and 
legume mixtures, including teosinte–ricebean combinations, but 
the performance of an intercrop depends strongly on the re lative 
proportion and spatial arrangement of the component crops. An 
inappropriate row ratio may intensify interspecific competition , 
reduce the yield of one component , and weaken the residual 
benefits to subsequent crops. Therefore , identifying a suitable 
row arrangement is essential for obtaining a balance between 
cereal forage production , legume contribution , total biomass , 
and subsequent crop performance. 
The nutrient -management strategy is equally important in an 
intensive food–forage sequence. Continuous removal of biomass 
can accelerate nutrient depletion, particularly when forage crops 
are harvested at an immature stage and large quantities of 
nutrients are removed from the field. Exclusive dependence on 
mineral fertilizers may sustain short -term productivity but may 
not adequately improve soil organic matter and nutrient -
retention capacity. Conversely, organic sources such as farmyard 
manure (FYM) and vermicompost release nutrients more 
gradually and can improve soil physical , chemical, and 
biological properties, but nutrient release may not coincide with 
the rapid nutrient demand of high -yielding forage crops. 
Integrated nutrient management (INM), which combines mineral 
fertilizers with organic nutrient sources, may therefore provide a 
more synchronized supply of nutrients y while maintaining soil 
fertility [3, 4]. 
The residual effects of nutrient management are particularly 
important in a multi -season system. In the medium - land 
situations of Assam, a summer forage crop may be followed by 
transplanted rice during the kharif season and a short -duration 
oilseed crop after rice. Ri hcebean in the first crop may benefit 
the succeeding rice crop through biological nitrogen fixation , 
residue return, and improved nutrient cycling. Similarly, organic 
nutrient s ources applied to the forage phase may continue to 
influence rice growth and the performance of a relay crop. A 
relay rapeseed crop established in standing rice can use residual 
soil moisture and nutrients while reducing the fallow period 
between rice and the next cropping season. However , the 
success of relay rapeseed depends on the fertility remaining after 
the preceding crops and on the ability of the entire system to 
maintain timely crop establishment. 
Assam has substantial potential for such intensifie d systems 
because rice is the dominant crop and many medium- land fields 
remain available for a pre -rice crop and a post -rice relay crop. 
Nevertheless, high rainfall , acidic sandy -loam soils , low to 
medium soil fertility, and limited access to costly external inputs 
create challenges for sustained production. A suitable system 
must therefore be evaluated using multiple criteria , including 
forage yield , grain and oilseed yield , land-use efficiency , 
nutrient uptake , residual soil fertility , economic return , and 
energy productivity. Assessment of individual crop yield alone 
may fail to identify the most appropriate system for farmers who 
depend simultaneously on food production , livestock feeding , 
and soil fertility maintenance. 
 
The present study was conducted to evaluate an intensive food–
forage cropping system comprising summer teosinte and 
ricebean, succeeding kharif rice , and relay rapeseed under the 
medium-land conditions of Assam. The specific objectives were 
to: (i) assess the growth , forage yield , quality of sole and 
intercropped teosinte and ricebean; (ii) determine the effects of 
row ratio and integrated nutrient management on succeeding 
rice; (iii) quantify residual effects on relay rapeseed grown 
without additional fertilizer; and (iv) compare the sy stems in 
terms of land equivalent ratio, green-forage-equivalent yield and 
economics. It was hypothesized that a suitable teosinte–ricebean 
row ratio combined with integrated application of mineral 
fertilizer and organic manure would improve total system 
productivity and enhance the residual fertility available to 
succeeding rice and relay rapeseed. 
 
Materials and Methods 
A two-year field experiment was conducted during 2016–17 and 
2017–18 at the Instructional -cum-Research Farm of Assam 
Agricultural University, Jorhat. The summer phase used a split -
plot design with three replications. Main plots contained sole 
teosinte, sole ricebean, teosinte + ricebean in a 3:2 row ratio , or 
teosinte + ricebean in a 3:3 row ratio. Subplots received: 100% 
recommended dose o f fertilizer (RDF); 50% of nitrogen (N) 
through RDF + 50% N through farmyard manure (FYM); 50% 
N through RDF + 50% N through vermicompost (VC); or an 
organic treatment supplying 50% N through VC + 50% N 
through FYM. Rice was grown in the kharif season in a split-
split arrangement with either a recommended integrated nutrient 
management (INM) package or the same package with 50% 
organic manure (OM). Rapeseed was broadcast as a relay crop 
after rice without additional fertilizer. Crop growth , forage yield 
and quality, rice and rapeseed performance, land equivalent ratio 
(LER), soil properties , economics, and energy indices were 
measured. Data were subjected to analysis of variance 
appropriate to the split -split-plot design , and treatment means 
were compared at P = 0.05. 
 
Results and Discussion 
Teosinte–Ricebean Intercropping Performance 
Plant height and dry matter accumulation: Sole teosinte and sole 
ricebean produced taller plants and greater per - plant dry matter 
than either intercropped combination , due to reduced inter-plant 
competition (Tables 1 –2). Among intercrops , the 3:3 ratio 
outperformed 3:2 for both components. INM -wise, 50% N 
(RDF) + 50% N (VC) gave the best growth , followed by 50% N 
(RDF) + 50% N (FYM) , RDF alone , and fully organic , 
reflecting the benefit of combining readily available and slow -
release nutrient sources. 
 
Forage yield: Sole teosinte produced the highest green forage 
yield, recording 233.73 and 238.65 q ha-1 during 2016–17 and 
2017–18, respectively. Among the intercrops , the 3:2 teosi nte–
ricebean row ratio produced greater teosinte forage yield than 
the 3:3 ratio, with yields of 163.74 and 170.74 q ha-1. In contrast, 
ricebean performed better in the 3:3 arrangement , producing 
127.75 and 136.50 q ha-1 green forage compared with 98.73 an d 
111.03 q ha-1 in the 3:2 arrangement. Sole ricebean produced the 
highest ricebean forage yield, at 194.06 and 201.06 q ha-1 (Table 
3). 
 
Land equivalent ratio (LER): Both intercropping ratios 
recorded LER > 1 , confirming a land -use advantage (Table 2).

The 3:3 arrangement recorded the highest LER , with values of

Residual Effects on Succeeding Rice 
Plant height and yield attributes: Rice grown after sole 
ricebean recorded the tallest plants , most panicles/m², and most 
grains and filled grains per panicle , followed by the 3:3 
intercrop, the 3:2 intercrop, and sole teosinte (lowest) (Table 5) , 
reflecting the residual nitrogen and improved soil fertility left by 
the legume [5, 6]. 
 
Grain yield, straw yield and harvest index: Grain and straw 
yield followed the same order: highest after sole ricebean 
(47.24–49.62 and 76.14 –79.26 q ha-1), then the 3:3 intercrop , 
then 3:2 , lowest after sole teo sinte (Table 6) , consistent with 
reported cereal -yield benefits after legumes [7, 8, 9]. Residually , 
50% N (RDF) + 50% N through VC again gave the highest grain 
and straw yield , ahead of the FYM combination and inorganic -
only treatment , due to vermicompost 's slow nutrient release , 
improved P availability , and better soil water -holding capacity 
[10, 11, 12]. The recommended rice INM package outperformed the 
50%-organic package , showing that adequate direct nutrition 
remains essential alongside residual benef it. Harvest index did 
not differ significantly across treatments.

Residual Effects of Cropping System and INM on Relay 
Rapeseed 
Plant height: Plant height of rapeseed responded significantly 
to the residual effect of the preceding cropping system at both 
the 80 days -after-sowing (DAS) and at -harvest stages in both 
years (Table 7). Plots that had carried sole ricebean –rice in the 
previous season produced the tallest rapeseed plants (82.55 cm 
and 85.68 cm at harvest in 2016 -17 and 2017 -18, respectively), 
followed by the 3:3 teosinte + ricebean intercrop , while s ole 
teosinte–rice left the shortest plants. Because ricebean is a 
legume, its residue and root biomass add fixed nitrogen and 
organic matter to the soil , and this carry -over nutrient pool , 
rather than any fresh input, appears to be what pushed vegetative 
growth of the following rapeseed ahead of the non -legume 
sequences. 
A similar pattern held for the residual effect of integrated 
nutrient management: plant height was tallest where the 
preceding crop had received 50% N through RDF together with 
50% N throug h vermicompost , closely followed by the FYM -
substituted treatment , and shortest under 100% inorganic 
fertilization (RDF alone). Organic amendments evidently leave 
behind more than just nutrients — their slow mineralization and 
associated micronutrient supply continue to support crop growth 
well after the manure itself was applied to the previous crop. 
The INM package carried to rice also left a significant residual 
imprint, with the recommended package producing taller 
rapeseed than the same package diluted with 50% organic 
matter, in both years. None of the two - or three-way interactions 
among cropping system , INM, and the INM package to rice 
were significant for plant height in either season , indicating that 
each factor exerted its residual influence large ly independently 
of the others. 
 
Siliquae per plant and seeds per siliqua: Yield-attributing 
characters followed the same broad trend as plant height. The 
number of siliquae per plant and seeds per siliqua were both 
significantly higher where rapeseed foll owed sole ricebean–rice, 
and lowest under sole teosinte –rice, in both years of the study 
(Table 7). Reproductive development appears to be shaped by 
the nitrogen economy the preceding crop leaves behind: a 
stronger residual nitrogen and organic-matter pool translates into 
more pods setting per plant and more seeds filling within each 
pod. Residual INM produced the same ranking seen for growth: 
the vermicompost -substituted treatment gave the highest 
siliquae/plant and seeds/siliqua (21.65 and 18.70 in 2016 -17; 
22.11 and 18.32 in 2017 - 18), ahead of the FYM -substituted 
treatment, while sole RDF gave the fewest of both — a 
difference of roughly 55 –60% between the best organic -
substituted treatment and inorganic -only fertilization. The 
recommended INM package ca rried to rice again outperformed 
its 50% organic -matter variant for both attributes in both years. 
As with plant height , the C × M × I interaction was non -
significant, so the residual gains from cropping system , INM, 
and the rice -level INM package are best read as additive rather 
than mutually dependent.

Seed yield and stover yield: Seed yield of rapeseed mirrored 
the growth and yield -attribute trends closely (Table 8). Sole 
ricebean–rice gave th e highest residual seed yield (3.34 q/ha in 
2016-17 and 3.70 q/ha in 2017 -18), an advantage of roughly 
44% and 25% over sole teosinte –rice in the respective years , 
with the 3:3 teosinte + ricebean intercrop placed second. The 
legume component in the preced ing sequence therefore does 
more than support its own productivity — the nitrogen it fixes , 
and the organic matter it returns to the soil , carry through rice 
and into the following rapeseed crop , lowering the fertilizer 
burden the rotation as a whole has t o bear while still sustaining 
seed yield of the third crop in the sequence. Residual INM

Harvest index: Harvest index followed a comparable but flatter 
pattern than seed and stover yield: it differed significantly with 
cropping system, residual INM and the INM package to rice in 
both years, again favouring the sole ricebean–rice sequence, the 
vermicompost-substituted INM treatment, and the recommended 
INM package to rice , though the spread between the best and 
poorest treatments was narrower than for the yield components 
themselves, indicating that the resi dual inputs raised seed and 
stover yield roughly in step with one another rather than 
skewing partitioning strongly toward either sink. 
 
Oil content and oil yield: Oil content of the seed itself proved 
far less responsive to residual treatment effects than the other 
characters examined. The preceding cropping system , the 
residual INM, and the INM package carried to rice all failed to 
produce a statistically significant difference in oil content in 
either year (Table 8) , even though the numerical values stil l 
tracked the same direction as the other traits — highest under 
sole ricebean–rice and vermicompost substitution , lowest under 
sole teosinte–rice and RDF alone. Oil content behaves as a 
largely genotype-fixed trait that residual soil fertility can nudge 
only marginally. Oil yield , by contrast, is the arithmetic product 
of oil content and seed yield , and it inherited the significance of 
the seed-yield differences that drove it. Sole ricebean –rice gave 
the highest residual oil yield (103.53 kg/ha in 2016 -17 and 
114.31 kg/ha in 2017 -18), and 50% N of RDF through 
vermicompost gave the highest oil yield among the INM 
treatments (104.99 kg/ha and 119.22 kg/ha) , both significantly 
ahead of their respective lowest -yielding counterparts. The INM 
package to rice, however, did not significantly affect oil content 
or oil yield of rapeseed in either year , and no interaction among 
the three factors was significant for either trait — so the oil-yield 
advantage carried over from a legume -based, organically-
managed preceding system is essentially a seed -yield effect 
rather than a change in seed quality.

System Productivity and Economics of the Whole System 
System production (GFEY): The teosinte + ricebean (3:3) –
rice–rapeseed sequence gave the highest green forage equivalent 
yield (859.19 and 901.79 q/ha) , followed by sole ricebean–rice–
rapeseed, due to the added legume fodder component boosting 
biomass (Table 9). Among nutrient treatments, 50% N of RDF + 
50% N through vermicompost gave the highest GFEY (891.42 
and 958.10 q/ha) , while the fully organic treatment gave the 
lowest — showing that a balanced inorganic –organic 
combination outperforms either extreme. The recommended 
INM package to rice a lso gave higher GFEY than its 50% 
organic-substituted version. Interaction effects were non - 
significant. 
 
Economics: Among cropping systems , the highest cost of 
cultivation (Rs. 57 , 665/ha) was incurred under teosinte + 
ricebean (3:2)–rice–rapeseed, while the highest gross return (Rs. 
1, 28, 879/ha and Rs. 1 , 35, 269/ha during 2016 - 17 and 2017 -
18) came from teosinte + ricebean (3:3) , consistent with its 
superior GFEY; however, sole ricebean– rice–rapeseed gave the 
highest net return (Rs. 73 , 896/ha and Rs . 80, 312/ha) and B:C 
ratio (1.41 and 1.53) due to its lower cultivation cost , followed 
by teosinte + ricebean (3:3) (B:C 1.24 and 1.36) , while sole 
teosinte gave the lowest (B:C 0.88 and 0.98) , agreeing with [13] 
on the higher economic efficiency of legum e-based sequences 
over sole cereal-fodder systems. Among nutrient treatments , the 
100% organic treatment (50% VC + 50% FYM) was the 
costliest (Rs. 60 , 955/ha), whereas 50% N of RDF + 50% N 
through vermicompost gave the highest gross return , net return

Summary of Findings 
The 3:2 arrangement maximized the teosinte component , 
whereas the 3:3 arrangement was the strongest overall intercrop: 
its LER was 1.37 in 2016 and 1.40 in 2017. The 3:3 system 
produced 859.19 and 901.79 q ha-1 green- forage-equivalent 
yield (GFEY) across the two years. Among nutrient treatments , 
50% N through RDF + 50% N through VC consistently 
improved forage biomass , rice performance through residual 
effects, rapeseed yield and net return. Rice following sole 
ricebean yielded 47.24 and 49.62 q ha-1, while the 3:3 intercrop 
was the strongest intercropping predecessor , producing 43.32 
and 45.04 q ha-1. Relay rapeseed following the sole ricebean –
rice sequence yielded 3.34 and 3.70 q ha-1; the 3:3 intercrop–rice 
sequence yielded 3.04 and 3.34 q ha-1. Across the complete 
system, sole ricebean–rice–rapeseed generated the highest net 
returns (Rs. 73, 896 and Rs. 80, 312 ha-1) and benefit–cost ratios 
(1.41 and 1.53) , while the 3:3 intercrop produced the highest 
GFEY and the highest energy productivity ratio among the 
intercropping options. The strongest combined recommendation 
was 3:3 teosinte + ricebean , with 50% N from RDF + 50% N 
from VC in the forage phase , recommended INM in rice , and 
relay rapeseed on residual fertility. 
 
Conclusion 
For medium -land situations of Assam , a 3:3 teosinte –ricebean 
intercrop followed by rice and relay rapeseed can intensify food 
and forage output without sacrificing system -level productivity. 
Integrating inorganic fertilizer with vermicompost was more 
reliable than either sole inorganic fertilizer or the fully organic 
combination tested. The results support a system -level approach 
in which forage , grain, oilseed, soil condition, profitability, and 
energy performance are optimized together rather than crop by 
crop.
