Fire and ungulate grazing historically shaped tallgrass prairie, yet their long-term consequences for streamwater quality and how these consequences compare with modern cropland legacies remain poorly resolved because few sites offer multi-decadal, whole-watershed chemistry. We asked how prescribed fire frequency, ungulate grazing (bison, cattle, or none), and legacy cropland fertilization have shaped long-term stream and groundwater nutrients, and which driver most strongly controls water quality. We analyzed up to 37 years (1985–2022) of NO3−, soluble reactive P (SRP), and total P (TP) from bison-grazed, ungrazed, and downstream-cropland sites in Kings Creek, and from 2009 to 2022 in Shane Creek before and after cattle reintroduction, at Konza Prairie Biological Station, Kansas, USA. Grazing raised stream nutrients: bison increased NO3− by roughly 100% relative to ungrazed watersheds, whereas cattle produced larger seasonal increases (33% for NO3−; 9–12% for P fractions). More frequent fire modestly elevated nutrients. Stream NO3− downstream of restored croplands fell from 450 to 110 μg N/L over 25 years. Groundwater NO3− in former cropland declined 70% but remained elevated at 950 μg N/L after 2 decades, vs 200 μg N/L in grassland reference wells, and surface-water improvements followed fertilizer reduction with 4-to-5-year lags. Across drivers, legacy cropland fertilization exerted the strongest control on water quality, grazing an intermediate effect, and fire the weakest. Because agricultural N legacies persisted for decades in groundwater, effective water-quality management in these grasslands requires accounting for cumulative effects, multi-scale baseline conditions, and long recovery lags with fertilizer reduction offering the largest and most durable improvement.
