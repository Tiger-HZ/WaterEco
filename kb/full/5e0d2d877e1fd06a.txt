Abstract
Temperature-driven crop development is a major determinant of 
phenology, yield and quality in seed spices such as fennel 
(Foeniculum vulgare L.). The present study evaluated the inﬂuence 
of accumulated Growing Degree Days (GDD) on phenological 
development, seed yield and quality attributes of fennel under semi-
arid ﬁeld conditions. Field experiments were conducted at the ICAR-
National Research Centre on Seed Spices, Ajmer, India, over three 
consecutive seasons (2019, 2020 and 2021). Umbels were 
harvested at different maturity stages ranging from 25 to 95 days 
after anthesis (DAA), corresponding to cumulative thermal 
accumulation of 1317.25 to 2894.25 GDD.
Phenological progression showed a stronger association with 
cumulative GDD than with calendar days. Panicle emergence, king 
umbel emergence and anthesis occurred at approximately 853.75, 
956.25 and 1034.25 GDD, respectively. Stage-wise thermal 
requirements (ΔGDD) indicated that early vegetative growth was the 
most thermally demanding phase (853.8 GDD), whereas 
reproductive transitions occurred over comparatively shorter thermal 
intervals, followed by progressively higher ΔGDD during later harvest 
stages.
The harvest stage and associated GDD signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced seed 
moisture, biochemical composition, essential oil content, and yield. 
Seed moisture declined from 75.15% at 25 DAA to 14.18% at 95 
DAA. Total oil and essential oil contents were highest at early harvest 
stages (4.41% and 2.61%, respectively) and decreased steadily with 
increasing GDD, while crude ﬁbre content increased from 12.31% to 
38.36%. Seed yield increased with cumulative GDD and peaked at 
24.15 q/ha at 75 DAA (2349.25 GDD), followed by a slight decline at 
later stages. Correlation analysis revealed strong negative 
relationships between cumulative GDD and oil, essential oil and 
protein content, and positive associations with crude ﬁbre, seed 
index and yield.

Introduction
Fennel ( L.), belonging to the family Foeniculum vulgare
Apiaceae, is an important aromatic and seed spice 
crop cultivated worldwide for its culinary, medicinal and 
industrial value. The seeds are rich in essential oils and 
phenolic compounds that contribute to their 
characteristic aroma, ﬂavour and health-promoting 
properties (Noreen 2023; Lal ., 2025). Fennel et al., et al
seeds are widely used as ﬂavouring agents in food 
products and are traditionally consumed as a mouth 
freshener and digestive aid due to their carminative, 
antimicrobial and antioxidant activities (Swathi ., et al
2016; Manohar ., 2020). Because of its increasing et al
demand of chewing and culinary types in domestic and 
international markets, maintaining high seed yield 
along with superior quality attributes has become a 
major focus of fennel research. Temperature is one of 
the most critical environmental factors inﬂuencing plant 
growth and development, as it regulates physiological 
and biochemical processes such as enzyme activity, 
photosynthesis, assimilate translocation and 
secondary metabolite synthesis. In aromatic crops like 
fennel, temperature not only determines crop 
phenology and productivity but also signiﬁcantly 
affects essential oil accumulation and composition, 
which are key determinants of seed quality (Saharkhiz 
and Tarakemeh, 2013; Shojaiefar ., 2015). et al
Therefore, understanding crop responses to thermal 
regimes is essential for optimizing agronomic practices 
such as harvest scheduling. 
Growing Degree Days (GDD), also known as thermal 
time or heat units, provide a quantitative measure of 
accumulated temperature above a crop-speciﬁc base 
temperature and are widely used to predict crop 
phenology more accurately than calendar days 
(McMaster and Wilhelm, 1997; Akyuz .,2017et al ; 
Climate Central, 2022). Phenological stages such as 
emergence, ﬂowering, umbel initiation and 
physiological maturity occur after the accumulation of 
speciﬁc GDD thresholds, as temperature largely 
governs the rate of developmental processes (Zude-
Sasse and Makouate, 2025). The use of GDD allows 
better synchronization of crop developmental stages 
with favourable environmental conditions, thereby 
improving crop management decisions related to 
sowing, irrigation and harvest timing. Numerous 
studies across different crops have demonstrated that 
optimal accumulation of GDD enhances yield 
attributes by ensuring adequate duration of vegetative 
and reproductive phases, whereas thermal deﬁcits or 
excessive heat accumulation may shorten growth 
duration, impair reproductive development and reduce 
yield potential (Jeffery ., 2025; Akyuz ., 2017; et al et al
Bondade and Deshpande, 2021; Pathak and 
Stoddard, 2018 In fennel, non-synchronous ﬂowering ).
and differential seed maturation among umbels further 
complicate harvest decisions, making the crop 
particularly sensitive to temperature variations during 
the ﬂowering and seed-ﬁlling stages (Brahmi et al., 
2021; El-Gamal ., 2017). Excessive heat et al
accumulation may accelerate phenological 
development, reduce seed-ﬁlling duration and 
adversely affect seed weight and essential oil 
biosynthesis, while insufﬁcient heat may delay maturity 
and reduce yield.
Quality attributes such as seed size, sweetness, 
essential oil content and composition are strongly 
inﬂuenced by the duration and conditions of seed 
development, which are governed by thermal time 
accumulation (Msaada ., 2009; Canavar and et al
Kaynak. 2010).
Appropriate GDD during seed ﬁlling promotes 
assimilate partitioning and synthesis of secondary 
metabolites, whereas imbalanced thermal regimes can 
lead to inferior sensory quality and reduced market 
value. Despite the recognized importance of 
temperature in fennel production, limited information is 
available on the direct relationship between 
accumulated GDD and phenological development, 
seed yield and quality attributes of fennel under ﬁeld 
conditions. In this context, the present study was 
undertaken to evaluate the inﬂuence of Growing 
Degree Days (GDD) on phenology, seed yield and

Material and Methods
The ﬁeld experiment was conducted at the Indian 
Council of Agricultural Research - National Research 
Centre on Seed Spices (ICAR-NRCSS), Ajmer, 
Rajasthan, India, located in the north-western region of 
the country (26.3661° N, 74.5933° E). The fennel 
variety AF-1, released by ICAR-NRCSS, Ajmer, was 
used as the experimental material. Fennel seeds were 
sown on 16 October during three consecutive cropping 
seasons (2019-20, 2020-21 and 2021-22). Each 
experimental plot measured 10 × 6 m², and the crop 
was planted at a spacing of 50 cm between rows and 30 
cm between plants.
Fertilizer was applied as per recommended practices, 
with 25 kg N/ha and 25 kg P₂O₅/ha applied as basal 
dose at sowing, while the remaining 25 kg N/ha was 
top-dressed in two equal splits at one-month intervals. 
To estimate yield and quality, twenty-ﬁve umbels each 
from main, primary and secondary orders were tagged 
at anthesis. Umbels were harvested at eight maturity 
stages at 10-day intervals, namely 25, 35, 45, 55, 65, 
75, 85 and 95 days after anthesis (DAA).
Anthesis occurred over an average duration of 101 
days. Harvested umbels were shade-dried, and seeds 
were manually separated. Seeds from all umbel orders 
were pooled to estimate seed yield (kg/ha) and quality 
parameters. Standard agronomic practices were 
followed throughout the crop growth period to ensure 
healthy crop establishment. Dried seeds were packed 
in paper bags and stored at room temperature until 
further quality analysis.

When the calculated daily mean temperature was 
below the base temperature, the corresponding GDD

accumulation. No upper temperature threshold was 
imposed, as the prevailing temperature range during 
the growing season did not exceed critical limits for 
fennel development.
Daily weather data were obtained from the 
agrometeorological observatory located at the 
experimental site of ICAR-NRCSS, Ajmer. Cumulative 
GDD values were used to characterize stage-wise 
phenological development and to determine 
incremental GDD (ΔGDD) requirements between 
successive growth and harvest stages.
Seed quality 
The moisture content of freshly harvested fennel seeds 
was determined using the oven-drying method. Total 
Soluble Solids (TSS) were calculated using a portable 
refractometer (Atago PAL-1) (AOAC, 1994). A 
standard micro-Kjeldahl method was used to 
determine the total nitrogen concentration and crude 
protein content (N × 6.25). Crude ﬁber content was 
 
estimated as per AOAC (1994).
Oil content was extracted using an Accelerated Solvent 
Extraction System (Dionex India Pvt. Ltd.). Oil was 
obtained after evaporating the solvent in a rotary 
evaporator. Thirty-gram seed powder was utilized for 
oil estimation. Essential oil content was determined by 
hydro-distillation using a Clevenger-type apparatus 
(Clevenger, 1928). Accurately weighed ground seed 
samples (50 g) were placed in a round-bottom ﬂask 
with distilled water (ratio 1:10 w/v) and distilled for 3 h 
until no further increase in oil volume was observed. 
The collected oil was separated, dried over anhydrous

sodium sulfate to remove residual moisture, and the 
volume was recorded. All analyses were carried out in 
triplicate and stored at –20ºC until gas chromatography 
-head space (GC-HS) analysis. The oil content was 
expressed as percentage (%) with respect to the initial 
material.
Gas chromatography-headspace (GC-HS) was used 
to identify and determinate the essential oil 
components of fennel seeds. Twenty-mL headspace 
vials sealed with silicone septa coated in aluminum 
were ﬁlled with a one-gram sample of dried coriander 
seeds. To volatilize the essential oils, these vials were 
heated to 100°C for ten minutes in an HS autosampler. 
Following that, the volatiles were moved to a gas 
chromatograph (GC) ﬁtted with an HP5 column. With 
an oven temperature program that began at 60°C for 
one minute, increased to 100°C at 4°C/min, and ﬁnally 
reached 260°C at 50°C/min, the GC inlet temperature 
was adjusted to 250°C. With the loop and transfer line 
temperatures kept at 105°C and 110°C, respectively, a 
split injection ratio of 1:50 was employed. The 
components of essential oils were identiﬁed by 
comparing their retention times to established 
benchmarks, and their quantiﬁcation was based on the 
peak areas observed by a ﬂame ionisation detector 
(FID).
Statistical analysis
The experiment was laid out on a Randomized 
Complete Block Design with three replications. The 
obtained data were subjected to analysis of variances, 
and the signiﬁcant differences among treatment means 
were compared using the LSD test according to Gomez 
and Gomez (1984).
Results and Discussion 
Phenological development and cumulative 
growing degree days (GDD)
The phenological development and harvesting 
sequence of fennel umbels exhibited a strong and 
progressive association with cumulative growing 
degree days (GDD) accumulated from sowing (Fig. 1). 
Panicle emergence occurred at 853.75 GDD, marking 
the thermal threshold for transition from vegetative to 
reproductive growth. This was followed by king umbel 
emergence at 956.25 GDD, indicating a relatively short 
thermal interval between these early reproductive 
stages. Flowering (anthesis) commenced at 1034.25 
GDD, suggesting that approximately 180 GDD beyond 
panicle emergence were required for ﬂoral 
differentiation and development, underscoring the 
sensitivity of fennel reproductive initiation to thermal 
accumulation.
The ﬁrst harvest was initiated at 1317.25 GDD, 
corresponding to the attainment of physiological 
maturity in the earliest formed umbels. Subsequent 
harvests occurred at cumulative thermal sums of 
1456.75, 1633.25, 1830.75, 2131.75, 2349.25, 
2619.00 and 2894.25 GDD for the second through 
eighth harvests, respectively. Thus, the harvesting 
period extended across a wide thermal window of 
approximately 1577 GDD from the ﬁrst to the ﬁnal 
harvest. The progressively increasing GDD 
requirements for successive harvests reﬂect staggered 
ﬂowering and asynchronous seed maturation among 
primary, secondary and tertiary umbels, a 
characteristic feature of fennel and other indeterminate 
or semi-indeterminate seed spice crops.
All phenological events and harvest stages followed a 
consistent sequence when expressed in terms of 
cumulative thermal time rather than calendar days, 
highlighting the superiority of GDD as a predictor of 
crop development. Growing degree days integrate 
temperature effects on physiological processes such 
as cell division, organ differentiation and biomass 
accumulation, thereby providing a more reliable 
measure of developmental progress than elapsed time 
alone (Parent & Tardieu, 2014; Baskaran et al., 2020). 
The dual-axis representation further illustrates that 
advanced maturity stages were achieved only at higher 
GDD levels, which likely promoted prolonged seed 
ﬁlling and metabolic activity necessary for seed weight 
accumulation and essential oil biosynthesis (Msaada et 
al., 2009; (Brahmi et al., 2021 and El-Gamal et al., 
2017). However, excessive thermal accumulation 
during later stages may predispose seeds to over-
maturity, shattering and quality deterioration. Overall, 
the results demonstrate that GDD-based phenological 
assessment offers a robust framework for optimizing 
harvest timing, improving yield stability and maintaining 
quality attributes of fennel under variable thermal 
environments.

Stage-wise thermal requirements (ΔGDD)
Stage-to-stage thermal requirements, expressed as 
incremental growing degree days (ΔGDD), varied 
substantially across phenological and harvesting 
stages of fennel (Fig. 2). From sowing to panicle 
emergence, the crop accumulated the highest single 
thermal input (853.8 GDD), indicating that early 
vegetative growth constituted the longest and most 
thermally demanding phase of development. This 
phase encompasses leaf initiation, canopy expansion 
and biomass accumulation prior to reproductive 
transition, processes that require sustained exposure 
to favourable temperatures (Hatﬁeld and Prueger, 
2015).
Following panicle emergence, progression to king 
umbel emergence required only 102.5 GDD, while a 
further 78.0 GDD were sufﬁcient to reach anthesis. 
These comparatively low ΔGDD values indicate rapid 
advancement through early reproductive stages once 
ﬂoral initiation had occurred. Similar short thermal 
intervals between successive reproductive stages 
have been reported in different crops, reﬂecting the 
strong regulatory inﬂuence of temperature on ﬂoral 
differentiation and reproductive development 
(Jagadish et al., 2021).
The interval between anthesis and the ﬁrst harvest 
required 283.0 GDD, representing the cumulative 
thermal demand for pollination, fertilization and early 
seed ﬁlling. This developmental window is particularly 
sensitive to temperature, as increased thermal 
accumulation accelerates physiological maturity and 
shortens the duration of seed development (Asseng et 
al., 2020). The magnitude of ΔGDD recorded in the 
present study suggests that prevailing thermal 
conditions were conducive to relatively uniform seed 
development prior to the initiation of harvesting.
Subsequent harvest intervals exhibited marked 
variability in ΔGDD requirements. The transition from 
the ﬁrst to second harvest occurred after 139.5 GDD, 
while 176.5 GDD and 197.5 GDD were required 
between the second-third and third-fourth harvests, 
respectively. A notably higher ΔGDD (301.0 GDD) was 
observed between the fourth and ﬁfth harvests, 
followed by ΔGDD values of 217.5, 269.8 and 275.3 
GDD for the ﬁfth–sixth, sixth–seventh and 
seventh–eighth harvests, respectively. This

The elevated ΔGDD requirement between the fourth

phenological and harvesting stages underscores the 
robustness of thermal time as a predictive framework 
for crop development. Unlike calendar days, ΔGDD 
provides a physiologically meaningful metric that 
remains comparatively stable across seasons and 
environments with variable temperature regimes 
(Parent and Tardieu, 2014). The stage-speciﬁc ΔGDD 
thresholds identiﬁed in this study therefore offer 
valuable inputs for phenological modelling and can be 
effectively used to optimize sowing dates, irrigation 
scheduling and labour planning for sequential harvest 
operations.

Effect of harvest stage (DAA) and cumulative GDD 
on seed quality, biochemical traits, and yield 
Harvest stages and corresponding cumulative 
growing degree days (GDD), exerted a signiﬁcant 
inﬂuence on seed moisture, biochemical composition, 
essential oil constituents, and yield of fennel (Table 1). 
Progressive advancement in harvest timing from 25 to 
95 DAA (1317.25 to 2894.25 GDD) resulted in marked 
and consistent trends across most quality and yield 
parameters.
Seed moisture content declined sharply with 
increasing harvest age and thermal accumulation, 
decreasing from 75.15% at 25 DAA (1317.25 GDD) to 
14.18% at 95 DAA (2894.25 GDD). This reduction 
reﬂects progressive desiccation associated with seed 
maturation and physiological dry-down. A similar 
declining trend was observed for total soluble solids 
(TSS), which decreased from 24.21 °B at early harvest 
to 2.55 °B at the ﬁnal harvest stage, indicating the 
conversion of soluble carbohydrates into structural 
and storage compounds during seed maturation.
Total oil and essential oil contents were highest at 
earlier harvest stages and declined signiﬁcantly with 
advancing GDD. Total oil content decreased from 
4.41% at 25 DAA to 1.27% at 95 DAA, while essential 
oil content declined from 2.61% to 1.43% over the 
same interval. These results suggest that oil 
biosynthesis peaks during early seed development 
and subsequently declines due to dilution effects and 
metabolic conversion during later stages of 
maturation. Similar declines in essential oil 
concentration with delayed harvesting have been 
reported in fennel and other seed spice crops, where 
oil accumulation is closely linked to early reproductive 
development and enzymatic activity (Carrubba and 
Lombardo, 2020; Tripathi et al., 2022; Lal et al., 2025).
The composition of essential oil constituents exhibited 
distinct and stage-speciﬁc responses to thermal time. 
Estragole content increased initially, reaching a 
maximum at 35 DAA (32.56%), followed by a gradual 
decline to 19.65% at 95 DAA. In contrast, trans-
anethole content increased steadily with harvest 
delay, peaking at 55 DAA (35.56%), before declining at 
later stages. Fenchone content showed relatively 
smaller variation but remained comparatively higher 
during mid-harvest stages (55-75 DAA). These shifts 
indicate dynamic biochemical transformations in 
essential oil composition during seed maturation, 
inﬂuenced by cumulative heat units and enzymatic 
regulation of secondary metabolite pathways (Brahmi 
et al., 2021 and El-Gamal et al., 2017).
Crude protein content declined with increasing harvest 
age, from 21.44% at 25 DAA to 13.13% at 95 DAA. 
This reduction may be attributed to the dilution of 
nitrogenous compounds as structural carbohydrates 
and ﬁbre accumulate during late seed maturation. 
Conversely, crude ﬁbre content increased 
progressively with delayed harvesting, reaching a 
maximum of 38.36% at 95 DAA, reﬂecting enhanced 
deposition of cell wall components and ligniﬁcation as 
maturity advanced.
Seed index increased steadily up to 75 DAA (7.79), 
followed by a slight decline at later stages, indicating 
that maximum seed weight was attained at mid-to-late 
harvest stages before the onset of over-maturity. Yield 
exhibited a distinct response pattern, increasing 
signiﬁcantly with delayed harvesting and peaking at 75 
DAA (24.15 q/ha), then declining marginally at 85 and 
95 DAA. This suggests that optimal yield was achieved 
at intermediate harvest stages, where sufﬁcient seed 
ﬁlling had occurred without excessive losses due to 
shattering or over-drying. Similar results were also 
reported by Rao (2022) in snap bean under short-
temperate-season climates and by Sümer (2023) in 
pea. GDD can be more effective than season length for 
recognizing physiological responses to temperature 
and has been used to calculate yield potential in 
chickpea, wheat, cowpea, grape, tomato and fennel 
(Jeffery et al., 2025; Akyuz et al., 2017; Bondade and 
Deshpande, 2021; Pathak and Stoddard, 2018; Lal et 
al., 2025).
The results demonstrated that cumulative GDD 
strongly regulates both qualitative and quantitative 
traits of fennel seeds. Early harvests favored higher oil 
content and protein concentration, whereas later 
harvests enhanced ﬁbre content and yield up to an 
optimum stage. The integration of harvest timing with 
thermal time thus provides a robust framework for 
balancing yield and quality attributes. These ﬁndings 
reinforce the utility of GDD-based harvest scheduling

for optimizing fennel production under variable climatic

Correlation analysis among cumulative GDD, seed 
quality attributes, and yield
Correlation study revealed strong and systematic 
relationships among cumulative growing degree days 
(GDD), seed quality parameters, essential oil 
constituents, and yield (Fig. 3 and Table 2). 
Cumulative GDD exhibited a highly signiﬁcant 
negative correlation with moisture content (r = −0.911), 
total soluble solids (r = −0.918), total oil (r = −0.976), 
essential oil (r = −0.992), and crude protein (r = 
−0.902). These strong inverse relationships indicate 
that as thermal time increased and seeds progressed 
toward advanced maturity, moisture and metabolically 
active constituents declined due to desiccation, 
dilution, and structural accumulation. Similar 
association for yield and associated traits were also 
reported by Vanitha et al. (2025) in rice.
In contrast, cumulative GDD showed a strong positive 
correlation with crude ﬁbre (r = 0.993), seed index (r = 
0.803), and yield (r = 0.718). This suggests that 
increasing thermal accumulation promotes seed ﬁlling, 
ﬁbre deposition, and biomass accumulation, ultimately 
enhancing yield up to an optimum maturity stage. The 
strong association between GDD and crude ﬁbre 
reﬂects increased cell wall development and 
ligniﬁcation during later stages of seed maturation.
Moisture content was positively correlated with TSS (r 
= 0.963), total oil (r = 0.938), and essential oil (r = 
0.859), indicating that higher seed moisture at earlier 
harvest stages coincided with elevated concentrations 
of soluble carbohydrates and oil fractions. Conversely, 
moisture content exhibited strong negative 
correlations with seed index (r = −0.947), crude ﬁbre (r 
= −0.886), and yield (r = −0.911), demonstrating that 
immature, high-moisture seeds were associated with 
lower physical maturity and reduced yield potential.
Total soluble solids were positively correlated with total 
oil (r = 0.894) and essential oil (r = 0.878), reﬂecting 
coordinated metabolic activity during early seed 
development. However, TSS showed strong negative 
correlations with seed index (r = −0.941), crude ﬁbre (r 
= −0.909), and yield (r = −0.822), suggesting that 
soluble carbohydrate content decreases as 
assimilates are converted into structural and storage 
components during seed maturation.
Total oil content was strongly and positively associated 
with essential oil (r = 0.951) and estragole 
concentration (r = 0.877), indicating synchronized 
biosynthesis of oil fractions and speciﬁc volatile 
compounds during early to mid-developmental stages. 
In contrast, total oil exhibited strong negative 
correlations with crude ﬁbre (r = −0.949) and yield (r = 
−0.793), reﬂecting a trade-off between oil 
concentration and structural biomass accumulation at 
advanced maturity.
Among essential oil constituents, estragole showed a 
strong positive correlation with crude protein (r = 
0.874) and a negative association with crude ﬁbre (r = 
−0.896), suggesting preferential accumulation of 
estragole in metabolically active, protein-rich seeds at 
earlier harvest stages. Trans-anethole displayed 
comparatively weaker correlations with most 
parameters, indicating greater compositional stability 
across harvest stages. Fenchone showed a modest 
positive correlation with yield (r = 0.393), suggesting its 
relative persistence during the later stages of seed 
maturation (Lal et al., 2025).
Overall, the correlation matrix clearly demonstrates 
that cumulative thermal time is a dominant driver of 
both quantitative and qualitative changes in fennel 
seeds. Increasing GDD favors yield and physical 
maturity traits, whereas biochemical quality attributes 
such as oil, essential oil, protein, and soluble solids are 
higher at lower thermal accumulations. These 
relationships highlight a physiological trade-off 
between yield maximization and quality preservation 
and reinforce the importance of GDD-based harvest 
scheduling to achieve targeted production goals. The 
results in line with Vanitha et al. (2025) in rice, Kailas et 
al. (2025) in long bean and Jeffrey et al (2025) in 
chickpea.
