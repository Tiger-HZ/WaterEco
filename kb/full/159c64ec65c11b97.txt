RESEARCH ARTICLE
Soil–wheat nutrient interactions following fertilization 
with digestate from one‑ and two‑stage molasses anaerobic 
digestion: a field study
Marta Galas  · Anna Detman‑Ignatowska · Jerzy Jonczak · 
Joanna Szewińska · Aleksandra Chojnacka
Received: 4 May 2026 / Accepted: 28 September 2026 
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract 
Aims Sustainable soil management besides eco-
logically friendly alternatives to mineral fertilizers, 
requires also bioconversion technologies capable of 
generating soil amendments with targeted agronomic 
functions. Two-stage system of anaerobic digestion 
with spatial separation of processes into two biore-
actors allows to obtain two-stage digestate (2-SD), 
which possibly differs from conventional one-stage 
digestate (1-SD). This study evaluated whether these 
process-derived differences translate into fertilizing 
properties of digestates, altered soil functioning and 
plant responses.
Methods A field experiment with wheat comparing 
2-SD digestate with 1-SD digestate, synthetic fertili-
zation (SF), reffered to unfertilized control (NF) was 
performed. Soil physicochemical properties, plant 
nutrient status, digestate chemistry and microbial 
consortia were analyzed. Soil–plant interactions were 
assessed through root nutrient transporters genes 
expression and finally crop yield was determinated.
Results Compared with 1-SD, 2-SD digestate 
exhibited a higher mineralization degree and distinct 
microbial diversity, indicating different chemical 
and microbiological functionality. Only bioavailable 
phosphorus was increased in soil treated with 1-SD 
digestate. Both digestates treatments enhanced root 
TaAMT1.2 gene expression, altering nitrogen acquisi-
tion pattern. 2-SD digestate treatment caused stronger 
yield response than 1-SD (2.8 t ha−1 vs 2.1 t ha−1), 
while both maintained soil pH stability unlike SF. 
Crop performance was slightly associated with soil 
pH and TaPHT1.10 gene expression.
Conclusions Findings demonstrate that 2-SD diges-
tate is a functionally distinct, more mineralized, bio-
logically active soil amendment from 1-SD digestate. 
Both with specific effects on soil chemistry, plant 
nutrient uptake patterns and enhanced productiv -
ity, highlighting two-stage anaerobic digestion as a 
promising source for circular nutrient management in 
cereal tillage.

Introduction
Modern agriculture faces many challenges, particu-
larly in cereal crop production, which provides the 
primary staple food for most people. Among cere-
als, wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the most 
widely cultivated species, valued both as a food 
source and for industrial purposes (Vitousek et  al. 
2009; Duan et  al. 2014; Salehi et  al. 2025). How -
ever, wheat has high nutritional demands and requires 
fertile soil for productive cultivation. Additionally, 
a continuously growing global population places a 
premium on increasing crop yields, fostering exces-
sive agricultural intensification. Consequently, mod-
ern agricultural systems remain strongly dependent 
on external nutrient inputs (Hlisnikovský et al. 2023; 
Salehi et al. 2025). Synthetic fertilizers, which mainly 
boost soil nitrogen and phosphorus content in mineral 
forms available for plant uptake, have been widely 
used in agriculture for decades. Despite their posi-
tive impact on crop yields, their excessive use is not 
economically justified and can also pose a threat to 
the natural environment. Long-term application of 
mineral fertilizers, especially those with high soluble 
ammonium sulfate content, contributes to soil acidi-
fication, alters soil microbiome communities (Howe 
et  al. 2024), and increases nitrogen and phosphorus 
leaching, leading to eutrophication and degradation 
of agroecosystems (Reijnders 2008; Morseletto 2020; 
Guo et al. 2023; Salehi et al. 2025). These challenges 
highlight the urgent need for alternative fertilization 
strategies that sustain crop productivity while reduc-
ing negative environmental impacts. Thus, develop-
ing natural, biological approaches to crop fertiliza-
tion, particularly those involving microorganisms, 
appear to be a safer option. Among these, digestate, 
the final product of the anaerobic digestion (AD) pro-
cess, has recently gained considerable attention.
Digestate is an organic matter-rich substance that 
represents a unique type of biofertilizer because it 
combines substantial amounts of organic carbon, 
as well as nitrogen, phosphorus, often in forms that 
can be gradually released into the soil (Engedal et al. 
2025), with diverse complexes of microbial consor -
tia. This integration of both chemical and biological 
functions is highly desired in sustainable agriculture 
and promote soil health (Engedal et al. 2025), posing 
digestate as a biologically active amendment. It is rich 
in microorganisms originating from AD processes, 
which may influence native soil microbial activity 
and promote nutrient cycling (Fernandez-Bayo et al. 
2020; van Midden et  al. 2023). This microbial con-
sortium is particularly important, as an increasing 
number of studies have indicated that the beneficial 
effects of biofertilizers are more often driven by inter-
actions within microbial communities rather than by 
individual strains applied alone (Chen et  al. 2006; 
Malboobi et al. 2009; Raimi et al. 2020; Cheng et al. 
2023). Microbial consortia may significantly enhance 
organic matter mineralization, phosphorus solubili-
zation (Chen et al. 2006; Pankievicz et al. 2019; Pan 
and Cai 2023) and the modulation of rhizosphere 
microbial processes, particularly those involved 
in nitrogen cycling in soil (Guo et  al. 2023). These 
properties distinguish the use of digestate in sustain-
able agriculture from the application of single-strain 
biofertilizers or traditional mineral fertilizers. This 
perspective is especially relevant because it aligns 
with circular economy practices, in which waste from 
one biotechnological process becomes a substrate 
for another reaction or is repurposed as a resource 
of economic value, thereby maximizing resource 
utilization (Morseletto 2020; Chojnacka et  al. 2019; 
Jin et  al. 2022). During sugar production from beet 
roots, molasses is generated as a by-product. Molas-
ses is a high-energy organic compound rich in readily 
fermentable carbon, making it an attractive substrate 
for AD (Fang et al. 2011). It is used in both one-stage 
(traditional) and two-stage systems (Chojnacka et al. 
2015; Detman et al. 2017).
The two-stage (2-SD) system of anaerobic sub-
strate decomposition differs fundamentally from the 
conventional one-stage (1-SD) process by separating 
hydrolysis and acidogenesis from acetogenesis and 
methanogenesis across two distinct bioreactors (Cho-
jnacka et al. 2026). Moreover, the technological sepa-
ration of microbial processes substantially modifies 
digestate composition, including nutrient availability, 
organic carbon fractions, and microbial community 
structure (Chojnacka et al. 2026). To date, digestates 
obtained from one-stage AD have been relatively well 
characterized as soil amendments and biofertilizers, 
with numerous studies reporting their potential to 
improve soil fertility and crop productivity (Slepe-
tiene et  al. 2020; Zhao et  al. 2024; Engedal et  al. 
2025; Zapata-Morales and Moreno-Andrade 2025). 
By contrast, the fertilizing properties of digestates 
derived from two-stage AD systems remain poorly

understood. Given that their composition differs from 
the digestates obtained from one-stage AD process, 
it cannot be assumed that they behave similarly; this 
knowledge gap is particularly evident in cereal crop 
research.
In this study, we compared the effects of one-stage 
(1-SD) and two-stage (2-SD) digestates applied as 
biofertilizers under field conditions. Digestates rep-
resent nutrient-rich organic amendments; however, 
from a plant physiological perspective, it is important 
to emphasize that roots can readily absorb nutrients 
only present in plant-available forms in the soil, pre-
dominantly mineral (inorganic). As digestates also 
exhibit a high mineral content, their application as 
biofertilizers appear to offer an advantage in wheat 
cultivation (Simon et  al.  2015; Barłóg et  al. 2020; 
Winkhart et  al. 2024). Ion uptake in roots occurs 
via specialized affinity protein transporters, whose 
activation depends on ion concentrations in the soil 
(Duan et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2014; Liu et al. 2015). 
Those protein ion transporters are encoded by genes 
in roots. Subsequently, we investigated expression of: 
TaAMT1.2 gene encoding high-affinity transporter 
of ammonium (Liu et  al. 2015; Duan et  al. 2015), 
TaNRT1.1 gene encoding dual-affinity nitrate ions 
transporter (Duan et al. 2015; Taulemesse et al. 2015; 
Fang et  al. 2021) and eventually, TaPHT1.10 gene 
(formerly known as TaPT2 or TaPHT1.10-U), which 
encodes high-affinity phosphates ion transporter 
(Zhang et  al. 2014; Teng et  al. 2017). Linking ion 
transporter gene expression with soil chemical com-
position may therefore demonstrate the plant’s physi-
ological response to treatment in nutrient acquisition 
at the end of the growing season to assess whether 
nutrients uptake derived from digestates are not lost 
too rapidly.
Based on this information, we hypothesized that: 
(i) digestates obtained from one-stage and two-stage 
AD differ in their chemical stoichiometry and micro-
bial community structure, resulting in distinct effects 
on soil nutrient availability under field conditions; (ii) 
digestate-based fertilization modifies wheat nutrient 
acquisition mechanisms, particularly by change in 
the expression of ion transporters in roots. We fur -
ther hypothesized that: (iii) both digestates treatments 
modify nitrogen acquisition mechanisms pattern in 
wheat roots despite equal input of this nutrient at fer -
tilizing dose to synthetic mineral treatment.
Integrating interdisciplinary approaches described 
below, to our knowledge, this study provides the first 
field-based evaluation of the fertilizing properties of 
valuable waste product—digestates derived from two-
stage AD of molasses, while explicitly comparing 
one-stage (traditional) and two-stage digestates with 
mineral fertilization. This work directly contributes to 
the development of sustainable fertilization strategies 
within circular-economy frameworks and to the needs 
of modern ecological agriculture.
Materials and methods
Experiment design under field conditions and 
fertilization regime
The experiment was conducted under environmen-
tal conditions in randomized field microcosms from 
September 2023 until the end of the vegetative sea-
son in July 2024. Two hundred winter wheat (Triti-
cum aestivum L.) ‘Euforia’ grains were sown in each 
microcosm with total area 0.5 m2 (corresponding to 
the recommended density of 400 grains per m2), fol-
lowing a randomized block design with five replicates 
per treatment (Fig.  1B). The experimental variants 
included (i) NF: non-fertilized plants (control), (ii) 
SF: fertilization with the multi-component synthetic 
mineral fertilizer Azofoska, (iii) 1-SD: fertilization 
with one-stage anaerobic digestate, and (iv) 2-SD: 
fertilization with two-stage anaerobic digestate.
To obtain one-stage and two-stage digestates, 
molasses (20  g L⁻ 1) was initially subjected to AD, 
according to the method described by Detman et al. 
(2017) and Chojnacka et al. (2026), as schematically 
presented in Fig. 1A. The fertilizer was at a final dose 
of 140 kg ha⁻1 N (Kotwica et al. 2011). Liquid diges-
tates derived from one-stage (1-SD) and two-stage 
(2-SD) AD systems were applied to meet the nitro-
gen demand of plant mineral nutrition, analogously 
to the mineral fertilization used in the SF treatment 
(Azofoska, Poland; chemical composition: 13.6% 
total nitrogen (N) [5.5% of nitrate nitrogen + 8.1% 
of ammonium nitrogen], 6.4% phosphorus pentoxide 
 (P2O5) soluble in neutral ammonium citrate solution 
and water, 19.1% potassium oxide (K2O), 4.5% mag-
nesium oxide (MgO), 23.0% sulfur trioxide (SO3), 
0.045% boron (B), 0.8% copper (Cu), 0.17% iron

(Fe), 0.27% manganese (Mn), 0.04% molybdenum 
(Mo), and 0.045% zinc (Zn)).
Fertilization was applied twice at the same dose 
during the vegetative season, in early spring (March, 
in BBCH 25 wheat stage) and six weeks later (dur -
ing BBCH 31 wheat stage), according to Zadoks 
et  al. (1974) scale of cereal growth stages. Wheat 
cultivation continued until full grain maturity, after 
which the crops were harvested at the beginning of 
August. Border microcosms were also sown with 
wheat to minimize edge effects; however, they were 
not included in the analysis, as represented in Fig. 1B.
Soil sampling and chemical analyses
Soil samples were collected before the start of the 
experiment and after crop harvest. Three soil subsam-
ples were taken from each plot at a depth of 0–30 cm 
(humus horizon) using a 3-cm soil corer, then com-
bined to form a single composite sample. The sam-
ples were cooled and transported to the laboratory 
immediately after collection. Fresh samples were 
subjected to extraction using a 1% potassium sul-
fate solution, and the contents of NH4-N and NO3-N 
in the extracts were determined using Nanocolor 
No. 91865 and Spectroquant No. 1147520001 tests, 
respectively, with a Nanocolor VISII spectrophotom-
eter. Soil moisture content was determined in each 
sample using the gravimetric method at temperature 
of 105  °C. The remaining portions of the samples 
were air-dried and sieved through the 2.0-mm sieve 
to remove coarse fragments. Subsequent analyses 
of the < 2.0-mm (earth) fraction were performed, a 
portion of which (~ 10  g) was milled into a powder 
using the SPEX SamplePrep ball mill equipped with 
zirconium milling chamber. Final size of soil parti-
cles is < 10  μm according to producer data. Labora-
tory analyses included determination of particle-size 
distribution using pipette and sieving methods, with 
classification of textural fractions and groups accord-
ing to the Polish Soil Science Society (PTG, 2009), 
which is compatible with USDA standards. Soil pH 
was determined via a potentiometric method (Sev -
enDirect SD23, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) in

Chemical characterization of digestates
The chemical characterization of digestates included 
the determination of TOC and TN via dry combus-
tion (Vario MacroCube, Elementar, Germany) and 
 PT via ICP-AES (Avio 200, Perkin Elmer, USA), 
following sample digestion in 65% nitric acid using 
a microwave digestion system (EthosUp, Milestone, 
Italy). Mineral forms of nitrogen (NH4-N and NO3-N) 
were determined using the NANOCOLOR tests 
(Ammonium 200 ISO 7150-1, DIN 38406-E5, EPA 
350.1, and NANOCOLOR® Nitrate 50 ISO 7890-1, 
DIN 38405-D9, ISO 23696-1). Digestate pH was 
measured using an ELMETRON CP-502 pH meter 
equipped with an IJ44C pH electrode (ELMETRON, 
Poland).
Microbiological characterization of digestates
Genomic DNA from both one-stage and two-stage 
digestates was isolated using the DNeasy PowerSoil 
Pro Kit (cat. no. 47014, QIAGEN) according to the 
manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quality and quantity 
were verified via spectrophotometry and agarose 
gel electrophoresis. Bacterial and fungal commu-
nity composition was characterized using targeted 
amplicon sequencing of the bacterial 16S rRNA 
gene (V3–V4 hypervariable region) and the fun-
gal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region, respec-
tively. The V3–V4 region was amplified using the 
universal bacterial primer pair 341 F (5′-CCT ACG 
GGNGGC WGC AG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGA CTA 
CHVGGG TWT CTAAT-3′). The fungal ITS region 
was amplified using the primer pair ITS1F (5′-CTT 
GGT CAT TTA GAG GAA GTAA-3′) and ITS2 (5′-
GCT GCG TTC TTC ATC GAT GC-3′). PCR ampli-
fication was performed using a high-fidelity DNA 
polymerase. Amplicons were purified, indexed, and 
sequenced in paired-end mode on Illumina platforms. 
Raw sequencing reads were demultiplexed and pro-
cessed using a standardized bioinformatics work -
flow. Adapter and primer sequences were removed, 
and reads were quality-filtered to exclude low-qual-
ity bases. Denoising, paired-end merging, and chi-
mera removal were performed to generate amplicon 
sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomic assignment 
of 16S rRNA gene ASVs was conducted against 
the SILVA ribosomal RNA gene reference data-
base, while fungal ITS ASVs were classified using 
the UNITE database for fungal internal transcribed 
spacer sequences. All data collected during sequenc-
ing has been uploaded into NCBI repository with 
BioProject accession number PRJNA1423286.
Analysis of ion transporter gene expression
RNA isolation from wheat roots collected at harvest 
(in August) was performed according to the CTAB 
method described by Wang and Stagemann (2010), 
with modifications (Rucińska et al. 2021; Wang et al. 
2022). Roots were washed three times with sterile 
Mili-Q water to remove residual soil from the tissue. 
Next, 150 mg of wheat root tissue was frozen in liq-
uid nitrogen and ground using a sterile mortar. Sam-
ples were subjected to lysis using 1  mL of CTAB-
based extraction buffer (EurX sp.z o.o, Poland) at 
a final pH of 8.0. The buffer contained 2% (w/v) 
CTAB, 20  mM EDTA, 1.4  M NaCl, and 100  mM 
TRIS, with β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) 
added to a final concentration of 1% (v/v) immedi-
ately before use. The isolated nucleic acids were dis-
solved in 100 μL of TE buffer (10 mM TRIS, 1 mM 
EDTA; pH 8.0) from the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit 
(cat. No. 47014, QIAGEN) and treated with DNase 
(A&A Biotechnology) according to the manufactur -
er’s instructions. RNA quality and quantity (A260/280 
and A260/A230 absorbance ratios, respectively) were 
measured using a NanoPhotometer (NP80, IMPLEN) 
and verified via electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel. 
Samples were used as a template for cDNA synthesis 
in a total reaction volume of 20 μL using the Quanti-
Tect Reverse Transcription Kit (QIAGEN, cat. No. 
205311). The procedure was performed according

to the manufacturer’s protocol. The final cDNA con-
centration after reverse transcription was adjusted to 
20 ng μL−1 for subsequent molecular analyses.
Gene expression analysis of ion transporters was 
performed using digital PCR (dPCR) (Voegel et  al. 
2021; Morcia et al. 2020; Shynggyskyzy et al. 2025). 
Reactions were carried out on a QIAGEN QIAcuity 
One 5-Plex system using EvaGreen Mastermix®. 
For each reaction, 8.5 k nanoplate formats were used. 
Primer pairs and expected amplicon sizes were first 
validated to ensure optimal reaction conditions. For 
each sample, the cDNA template was diluted tenfold 
or 100-fold, as appropriate. Reactions were carried

Plant material collection, chemical analyses, and 
wheat yield determination
All plants were harvested separately from all plots 
and air-dried. Next, the total biomass of grain and 
straw was determined for each plot, and then this 
was converted to tons per hectare. Grain and straw 
samples were milled into a powder and subjected 
to chemical analysis. TOC and TN contents were 
determined via dry combustion (Vario MacroCube, 
Elementar, Germany), while PT was determined via 
ICP-AES (Avio 200, Perkin Elmer, USA), following 
sample digestion in 65% nitric acid using a micro-
wave digestion system (EthosUp, Milestone, Italy).
Statistical analysis
Data analysis was performed in R (version 4.0.3). The 
Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to check whether the 
data followed a normal distribution. If data followed 
normal distribution, a t-test or one-way ANOVA, fol-
lowed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test, was con-
ducted. When the data did not meet the criteria for 
normal distribution, the Mann—Whitney U test or 
nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test was per-
formed using the R-Commander package, followed by 
Dunn’s post-hoc test. Linear regression analyses were 
conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.0). 
Figures were prepared using BioRender software. 
The significance level for all tests was set at α = 0.05. 
All raw data used for statistical analyses have been 
deposited in an open data repository (https:// doi. org/ 
10. 18150/ 7ZYVRV).

Results
Soil chemical properties
The soil used in this experiment exhibited a sandy 
texture, with 82.2% sand, 16.2% silt, and 1.6% clay. 
The initial chemical composition of the soil is pre-
sented in Table  2, including the ranges of total ele-
ment content and their bioavailable forms, to show the 
changes over time following fertilization treatments 
in the SF, 1-SD, and 2-SD microcosms. Initial mean 
soil pH was around 7.35. Post-experiment chemical 
analyses revealed a statistically significant increase 
in PB content in soil samples from the 1-SD diges-
tate treatment, with approximately 23.3% higher con-
tent compared with the NF control (Tukey’s post-hoc 
test; p < 0.05) (Table 2). Additionally, a statistically 
significant slight decrease in pH was observed in SF 
soil samples compared to the NF control variants 
(Table  2). No significant differences were observed 
in other soil parameters, including TOC, TN, NH4-N, 
 NO3-N contents, and the TOC:TN ratio.

Discussion
Identifying a biological fertilizer (biofertilizer) that 
can achieve yields comparable to those of syntheti-
cally fertilized crops, while also being a by-product 
consistent with circular economy practices, remains a 
notable challenge in sustainable agriculture, particu-
larly in wheat cultivation. In this study, we addressed 
this challenge by directly comparing digestates 
derived from one-stage (1-SD) and two-stage (2-SD) 
anaerobic digestion (AD) of molasses when applied 
as fertilizers under field conditions.
Distinct fertilization properties of one-stage and 
two-stage digestates
The first hypothesis proposed that digestates derived 
from different stages of AD differ in their chemical 
properties and microbial composition. Chemical anal-
yses supported this hypothesis, showing that the 1-SD 
digestate had higher TN and PT contents than the 
2-SD digestate. However, analysis of nitrogen specia-
tion revealed that the 2-SD digestate contained higher 
concentrations of mineral nitrogen forms (NH₄-N 
and NO₃-N) than the 1-SD digestate (Table  3). This 
result indicates that a larger proportion of nitrogen 
in the 2-SD digestate exists in forms more readily 
available to plants. This is further supported by the 
fact that the application of 2-SD digestate resulted in 
a higher TOC:TN ratio in wheat grain and a higher 
TN:PT ratio in wheat straw (compared to 1-SD diges-
tate), ultimately translating to a 32% higher wheat 
yield compared to the 1-SD variant (p <0.05). By 
contrast, nitrogen in the 1-SD digestate was predomi-
nantly bound in organic compounds. Nevertheless, it 
exhibited more balanced TOC:TN and TOC:PT ratios, 
which favor microbial mineralization processes and 
improve the balance between nutrient release and 
plant uptake. However, analysis of the TaAMT1.2 
gene expression (Fig.  3B) suggests no differences in 
the plant response between 1-SD and 2-SD diges-
tate treatments. 1-SD and 2-SD treatment led to sta-
tistically higher expression of this gene, potentially 
indicating an enhanced capacity for ammonium ion 
uptake from the soil, which was still at a high level 
(Porras-Murillo et al. 2023). Typically, such upregu-
lation occurs during nitrogen deficiency (Duan et al. 
2015; Bajgain et  al. 2018). Because soil TN, NO₃-
N, and NH₄-N did not differ at harvest (however 
tendency toward their higher content in treated soil 
was maintained), this response may reflect an attempt 
to maximize nitrogen acquisition under changing 
late-season conditions. (Bajgain et  al. 2018; Wang 
et  al. 2025). Furthermore, the lower nitrogen level 
in the grain in the 2-SD treatment suggests slight 
nitrogen deficiency during grain maturing, which 
may have contributed to the elevated gene expres-
sion in that variant, suggesting demand and redistri-
bution of nitrogen for aboveground parts of the plant 
(Guo et  al. 2026). While analyzing fertilizing prop-
erties of digestates, the 2-SD digestate contained a 
less favorable for plants TOC:P T ratio than the 1-SD 
digestate. Although TOC inputs were similar in both 
digestate treatments, this finding indicates less opti-
mal stoichiometry in nutrient content, particularly for 
microbial nutrient processing, including microbially-
mediated phosphorus mobilization and immobiliza-
tion (Schindlbacher et  al. 2011; Lazicki et  al. 2020; 
Luo et al. 2020; Feng et al. 2023; Agostini et al. 2024; 
Engedal et  al. 2025). Moreover, the chemical com-
position of the 1-SD digestate may reduce the risk of 
phosphorus immobilization and nitrogen environmen-
tal losses, thereby supporting a more sustained and 
efficient nutrient supply over time. Consistent with 
the first hypothesis, the digestates also differed in 
microbial composition. Microbial profiling revealed 
distinct bacterial and fungal communities in diges-
tates derived from one-stage and two-stage systems. 
In the 1-SD digestate, the presence of bacterial genera 
such as Lactiplantibacillus and Lactococcus, along 
with a high relative abundance of Bacteroides, is par -
ticularly relevant in the soil–plant context (Fig.  2A). 
Species within Lactiplantibacillus and Lactococ-
cus have been reported in literature data to exhibit 
plant growth-promoting traits, including phytohor -
mone production and the secretion of biologically 
active metabolites and peptides, while Bacteroides, 
in turn, contributes to nutrient cycling and phospho-
rus mobilization in soil (Lidbury et al. 2021; Raman 
et  al. 2022; Zhao et  al. 2024; Shah et  al. 2025). In 
our study, the fungal component of the 1-SD diges-
tate, which was dominated by Trichoderma (Fig. 2B), 
was equally important. Other studies demonstrated 
this genus as widely recognized for its ability to colo-
nize the rhizosphere, stimulate root development and 
enhance phosphorus solubilization, and uptake effi-
ciency, which is mediated through biochemical activ -
ity and modifications to root system architecture in 
Plant Soil 
Vol.: (0123456789)
cereals, including wheat (Sood et  al. 2020; Andrze-
jak et al. 2021; Andrzejak and Janowska 2022; Con-
treras-Cornejo et al. 2024). The introduction of such 
taxa into soil via the 1-SD digestate may therefore 
tend to contribute to localized changes in phospho-
rus availability. This interpretation is consistent with 
the observed increase in bioavailable PB in soil fer -
tilized with 1-SD digestate (Fig.  2B; Table  2). By 
contrast, the 2-SD digestate was characterized by the 
presence of taxa belonging to Alcaligenes and Cloaci-
bacillus, which have been reported in other studies 
to exhibit plant growth-promoting traits, including 
non-symbiotic nitrogen fixation, phytohormone pro-
duction, and indirect enhancement of plant nutrient 
uptake (Egamberdiyeva 2007; Di Benedetto et  al. 
2017; Jia et al. 2022), organic matter degradation and 
enhanced phosphorus mobilization in soil (Luo et al. 
2022; Singh et al. 2022; Murindangabo et al. 2023), 
respectively. However, unlike the 1-SD, the microbial 
community of the 2-SD digestate in our study was 
also dominated by taxa such as Methanosarcina and 
Methanothrix, which are responsible for methanogen-
esis in the AD process (Fig.  2B). These microorgan-
isms are not typically associated with soil nutrient 
cycling or recognized as PGP strains; rather, they are 
essential for efficient biogas production, the process 
from which they are derived (Angelidaki et al. 2018; 
De Vrieze et al. 2018). Their dominance in the 2-SD 
digestate suggests that, despite higher concentrations 
of mineral nitrogen forms, the biological potential of 
the 2-SD digestate to actively modulate soil nutrient 
availability through microbial interactions may be 
more limited than that of the 1-SD digestate. There-
fore, higher yield in 2-SD digestate treated plant is 
rather an effect of its mineralization than microbial 
consortia composition in this treatment.
Changes in soil nutrient availability and plant 
acquisition strategies
In this study, the only elemental soil chemistry 
parameter that changed under field conditions was 
 PB content after treatment with the 1-SD digestate. 
This finding positively correlates with the previously 
noted abundance of Trichoderma spp. fungi and Bac-
teroides bacteria in the 1-SD digestate, both known 
from literature data for their phosphorus-solubilizing 
properties (Sood et  al. 2020; Lidbury et  al. 2021). 
No significant differences were observed among 
variants in TOC, TN, or concentrations of NH4-N 
and NO3-N in the soil. Accordingly, the TOC:TN 
ratio remained unchanged and was maintained at a 
relatively low level compared with values reported for 
other organic fertilizers in the literature (Chuan et al. 
2013; Marques da Silva et al. 2024). This finding is 
relevant from an agroeconomic perspective, as it indi-
cates that, despite the liquid form of digestates, which 
could promote rapid soil nitrogen transformation or 
loss under field conditions, they meet wheat demands 
for mineral nutrition and support effective nutrient 
utilization without posing risks to agroecosystems, 
especially soil health (Rose and Wissuwa 2012; Mel-
oni et  al. 2024). From a chemical perspective, soil 
pH is a key parameter in mineral nutrition and ferti-
lization. In this study, soil pH decreased slightly only 
under the SF treatment (Table  2). This slight reduc-
tion in pH may have caused short-time benefits by 
enhancing ion uptake by the wheat root system, espe-
cially bioavailable nutrients in anions forms which 
require symport with H+ protons, (Busch and Böttger 
1997), which subsequently correlates with the high-
est straw biomass obtained for this variant (Fig.  5B). 
However, it needs to be underlined that prolonged use 
of synthetic mineral fertilizers can cause long-term 
risk leading to progressive soil acidification, which 
may affect soil microbiome composition, alter physi-
ochemical properties, and ultimately contribute to 
soil depletion (Reijnders 2008; Morseletto 2020; Guo 
et al. 2023; Howe et al. 2024; Salehi et al. 2025). By 
contrast, both digestates exhibited a more alkaline 
pH, and their application increased soil pH compared 
to the SF treatment.
The physiological responses of wheat roots further 
refine the comparison between the 1-SD and 2-SD 
digestates and support the second hypothesis that 
digestate-based fertilization modifies wheat nutri-
ent acquisition mechanisms, particularly by changes 
in the expression of ion transporters in roots. In this 
study, several ion transporters relevant to plant nutri-
tion were investigated, enabling evaluation of plant 
responses to treatment with 1-SD and 2-SD digestates 
in comparison with NF control and SF treatment at 
the end of the vegetative season. Both digestate treat-
ments induced a clear increase in the expression of 
the ammonium transporter gene TaAMT1.2 relatively 
to the NF control (Fig.  3A) and SF treatment indicat-
ing enhanced reliance on ammonium uptake under 
digestate fertilization. This response was not observed 
 Plant Soil
Vol:. (1234567890)
in the SF treatment, where nitrogen availability was 
more stable and did not require strong physiologi-
cal adjustment. The upregulation of TaAMT1.2 in 
both 1-SD and 2-SD treatments occurred despite 
the absence of differences in soil NH4-N concen-
trations at the time of harvest (Table  2), suggesting 
that plants may still actively try to uptake ammo-
nium from the soil to utilize nitrogen efficiently and 
metabolism at high level (Liu et al. 2015; Dai et al. 
2023; Esmaeilzadeh-Salestani et al. 2023). This gene 
expression could have been upregulated by high 
ammonium content, demand for nitrogen during 
grain maturity followed by need for nitrogen redistri-
bution from roots to aboveground parts of the plant, 
e.g. grains, which was stated above (Ijato et al.  2021; 
Esmaeilzadeh-Salestani et  al. 2023). No significant 
differences were observed between treatments in 
the expression of TaNRT1.1 (Fig.  3C), which acts 
as a signal sensor, informing the plant about nitrate 
availability in the soil (Duan et al. 2014; Taulemesse 
et al. 2015; Fang et al. 2021), together with the fact 
of unchanged NO3-N content in soil between vari-
ants, it may suggest that nitrate was depleted from 
the root zone prior to sampling, likely due to seasonal 
changes in soil nitrogen cycling (Fortuna et al. 2003; 
Taulemesse et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2021). Analysis 
of the root-specific ion transporter TaPHT1.10 gene, 
whose expression is typically induced under phos-
phorus deficiency (Zhang et  al. 2014; Teng et  al. 
2017), did not reveal a statistically significant change 
between variants.
Although both digestates elicited similar physi-
ological responses relative to NF and SF, the higher 
mineral nitrogen content of the 2-SD digestate did not 
confer a clear physiological advantage over the 1-SD 
digestate. This finding highlights the need for future 
research focused on soil-microbiome-plant interac-
tions throughout the vegetative season, when active 
nutrient uptake occurs, regarding demanding meta-
bolic photosynthesis process.
Determination of wheat yield following 
digestate-based and synthetic treatments
Comparison of total elemental content (e.g., TN 
content) suggests that the 2-SD digestate treat-
ment functioned more similarly to SF, consistent 
with its higher degree of mineralization (Table  2; 
Fig. 4A). By contrast, analysis of the TOC:TN ratio, 
an important determinant of wheat yield, revealed a 
contrasting tendency that reflects differences in car -
bon and nitrogen redistribution not only within the 
digestates but also within treated plants (Dai et  al. 
2016; Meloni et al. 2024; Li et al. 2022). Another key 
parameter used to evaluate fertilization status was PT 
content, which decreased in all fertilized treatments 
relative to the NF control in both straw and grains 
(Fig. 4A). Treatment with SF, 1-SD and 2-SD stim-
ulated more rapid wheat growth, leading to signifi-
cantly higher overall biomass and higher number of 
grains (Fig.  4A). If the plant absorbed similar phos-
phorus level, which can be explained by unchanged 
TaPHT1.10 (Fig.  3D) gene expression level across 
variants the content of PT per kg of dry matter of 
straw and grain is smaller in treated plants (Gaj and 
Górski 2014.). Both digestates increased yield relative 
to the NF control, confirming their fertilizing poten-
tial (Fig.  4B). However, the extent of yield improve-
ment differed between 1-SD and 2-SD digestate treat-
ment and for both remained lower than that achieved 
under SF, reflecting fundamental differences in nutri-
ent availability and retention rather than differences 
in total nutrient input. Direct comparison of the two 
digestates indicated that the 2-SD digestate, contain-
ing higher concentrations of readily available nitrogen 
forms, resulted in a proportionally higher yield than 
the 1-SD digestate, improving the yield for about 32% 
than in 1-SD treatment. However, taking into consid-
eration the fact that fertilization dose was equal to SF 
treatment we reported 2.4 times lower yield in 2-SD 
than in SF, and 3.1 times lower yield in 1-SD than 
in SF, respectively. This outcome strongly suggests 
that a substantial fraction of the mineral nitrogen sup-
plied by 1-SD and the 2-SD digestate could not have 
been retained within the root zone toward the end of 
the vegetative season. Furthermore, as nitrification 
occurs seasonally in soil also loses in liquid diges-
tate by natural processes could occur (Fortuna et al. 
2003; Wang et  al. 2021). This interpretation is con-
sistent with increased TaAMT1.2 expression under 
1-SD and 2-SD digestate treatments. The regression 
analysis model demonstrated that wheat yield was 
slightly determined by soil pH. Slight decrease in pH 
values was positively associated with increased crop 
yield, however R2 was around 0.4, indicating that 
this interaction is not strong (Fig.  5). Notably, only 
the SF treatment resulted in a slight decrease in soil 
pH during the experiment (Table 2) and produced the 
Plant Soil 
Vol.: (0123456789)
highest wheat yield, straw biomass, and TN content 
in both straw and grain. Interestingly, TaPHT1.10 
gene expression in wheat roots also was slightly asso-
ciated with wheat yield (Fig.  5). Reduced expression 
of this gene, which acts as an indicator of phosphorus 
deficiency, may indicate sufficient phosphate uptake 
revealing that plants have fulfilled nutrient demand 
for this nutrient, thereby contributing to increased 
wheat yield. Specifically, SF resulted in the greatest 
yield increase (approximately seven-fold), while the 
1-SD and 2-SD digestate treatments improved crop 
yield by 2.5-fold and 3.3-fold respectively, relative 
to the NF control. Linking linear regression model 
with elemental composition of straw and grains and 
finally change in TaAMT1.2 – high-affinity ammo-
nium transporter expression level in roots, these find-
ings strongly support the third hypothesis, that 1-SD 
and 2-SD digestate treatments modify nitrogen acqui-
sition pattern in wheat cultivated in treated soils, dif-
ferently than SF treatment, resulting in different crops 
yields, despite equal nutrient input.
Conclusions
This study demonstrates that 2-SD digestate is a more 
mineralized biofertilizer, which can be a source of 
nutrients more easily absorbed by plants, while 1-SD 
digestate, with a more complex chemical structure 
and different microbial composition, promotes more 
balanced nutrient supply leading to enhanced phos-
phorus availability in soil. Higher mineralization in 
2-SD digestate was associated with proportionally 
higher wheat yield than the control and 1-SD diges-
tate treatment. However, because both digestates 
were applied in liquid form, yields were lower than 
those obtained with SF. Treatments with 1-SD and 
2-SD digestate despite initial similar inputs of nitro-
gen to this in the synthetically fertilized variant led 
to changes in nitrogen acquisition patterns in wheat 
roots, connected with plants mechanisms to utilize 
ammonium sources in soil. Therefore, further studies, 
especially during the vegetative season, when plants 
absorb nutrients more actively and perform effective 
photosynthesis and anabolic processes, are needed 
to evaluate more detailed physiological responses 
in wheat. Also, these analyses together with results 
demonstrated in this work may be beneficial for find-
ing more stable digestate forms or more adjusted 
strategies to avoid nutrient leaching risk from diges-
tate-based fertilization.
