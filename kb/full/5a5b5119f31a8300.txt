8Abstract Peatlands have an outsized contribution to both carbon storage and methane (CH 4) emissions 
9relative to their land surface area. The factors controlling these fluxes are uncertain, but critical to predicting 
10the future trajectory of greenhouse gas emissions from peatlands. While the biochemical composition of 
11organic matter (termed organic matter “quality”) has been shown to be correlated with its future 
12vulnerability to decomposition under oxic conditions, few experiments have assessed its impact on 
13anaerobic respiration and CH 4 production. In this study, we assessed the relationship between 
14environmental parameters, organic matter quality, and potential greenhouse gas production under both oxic 
15and anoxic conditions. We used samples from multiple depths and microtopographies within a temperate 
16bog to capture peats with a range of decomposition states and botanical origins. A multiple regression model 
17successfully predicted approximately 65% of the variance in carbon dioxide (CO 2) production in oxic 
18incubations. However, we found that the model was less successful in predicting anaerobic CO 2 and CH4 
19production in anoxic incubations, explaining 31% and 42% of the variance in greenhouse gas production, 
20respectively. These results suggest that biochemical composition has an important influence on potential 
21CO 2 production under oxic conditions, but exerts less influence on rates of anaerobic respiration and CH 4 
22production. As climate change and drainage increase the oxygen exposure time of peats, organic matter 
23quality will likely play an increasing role in determining the trajectory of CO 2 emissions from peatlands.

241. Introduction 
25Peatlands are among the largest terrestrial carbon (C) reservoirs, accounting for about one third of 
26the world’s soil organic C pool despite only occupying 2-3% of its land surface area [1,2]. The accumulation 
27of C in these environments is due to plant productivity outpacing the very low rates of organic matter 
28decomposition characteristic of peatlands [3]. Several environmental factors, including persistent anoxia, 
29low pH, and cool temperatures, have been demonstrated to contribute to the stabilization of peat organic 
30matter and its low rate of decomposition. In addition to these environmental factors, Sphagnum mosses, the 
31dominant species in many northern peatlands, typically exhibit low litter quality and have been found to 
32decompose slower than vascular plants even when incubated under the same environmental conditions 
33[4,5].
34While intact peatlands have functioned as net C sinks, their waterlogged and anoxic conditions also 
35facilitate C loss through methane (CH 4) production via fermentation and methanogenesis [6]. Globally, 
36wetlands represent the largest natural source of CH 4, producing 40% of the total CH 4 emitted to the 
37atmosphere, with northern peatlands alone producing 10-13% of all CH 4 emissions [6–8]. While water table 
38position and temperature have been demonstrated to be strong drivers of CH 4 emissions, large differences 
39in CH 4 emissions have been observed among peatlands with similar climate and moisture balance [9]. 
40Understanding current patterns of CH 4 emission and predicting their response to future climate change 
41remain major challenges [10,11]. 
42Differences in organic matter (OM) composition (also referred to as OM “quality”) could 
43potentially explain these discrepancies by playing an important role in regulating the future trajectory of 
44decomposition and CH 4 emissions from peatlands. Such differences can arise from a combination of the 
45botanical origin of the OM and selective microbial decomposition of specific biochemical groups [12]. 
46Several studies have found that OM quality is correlated with peat decomposition rates under oxic 
47conditions. For example, Hogg [13] found that potential respiration rates declined with depth in a peat 
48profile, reflecting lower OM quality in deeper peat with a more advanced state of decomposition. A positive

49relationship between O-alkyl C content (measured using 13C-NMR) and potential respiration has been 
50demonstrated [14,15], as well as a negative relationship between respiration and phenolic C [16]. This is 
51significant because it suggests that lower quality OM can limit C mineralization when other stabilizing 
52factors (e.g. anoxia or freezing temperatures) are lost, such as during peat drainage or permafrost thaw [17].
53The extent to which OM quality affects anaerobic respiration (including both anaerobic carbon 
54dioxide (CO 2) production and CH 4 production) is less understood [18] despite being identified as critical 
55for predicting future emissions trajectories [19]. A limited number of studies have investigated the 
56relationship, and have generally found that parameters associated with OM composition were only weakly 
57correlated with respiration [7,11,20]. On the other hand, the botanical composition of peat has been shown 
58to affect potential CH 4 production, although the specific biochemical parameters were not identified [4,21]. 
59In addition, there is evidence that anaerobic respiration is substrate-limited and is stimulated by the addition 
60of dissolved organic matter (DOM) and labile compounds [6,22]. Parameters related to the biochemical 
61composition of the peat, such as the abundance and bioavailability of potentially fermentable sugars, are 
62therefore hypothesized to be associated with anaerobic respiration, in addition to the previously established 
63correlation with decomposition under oxic conditions.
64Biochemical analyses such as the quantification of individual neutral sugars and amino acids could 
65be a powerful tool for assessing organic matter quality. They have the advantage of molecular specificity, 
66allowing the calculation of multiple indices associated with different aspects of decomposition. For 
67example, a “degradation index” based on the change in the relative abundance of individual amino acids 
68has been utilized to track the extent of decomposition of organic matter in a range of ecosystems [23–25], 
69and the amino acid hydroxyproline has been used a biomarker of plant-derived nitrogen in soils and peats 
70[26,27]. 
71Hydrolyzable neutral sugars have also been used as an indicator of organic matter decomposition. 
72Neutral sugars are primarily derived from carbohydrate polymers, which are abundant in plant biomass but 
73are among the most labile fractions of organic matter [28]. Thus, the yield of hydrolyzable neutral sugars

75insights about the cycling of specific carbohydrate classes. For example, rhamnose is a major constituent 
76of “sphagnan”, a pectin-like carbohydrate shown to contribute to the relative recalcitrance of Sphagnum 
77[31,32], and can be used to track its fate in peat [33]. However, the potential utility of these analyses for 
78evaluating the vulnerability of soil organic matter to future decomposition has not been assessed.
79To address this, we evaluated the relationship between peat biochemical composition, 
80environmental conditions (e.g. pH, depth, nutrient availability, etc.), and greenhouse gas (GHG) production 
81under both oxic and anoxic conditions. We coupled microcosm incubation experiments and biochemical 
82analyses for 60 unique peat samples taken from six different cores in a temperate peatland in southwestern 
83Michigan. Despite their geographic proximity, the samples represent a gradient in redox conditions due to 
84the hummock/hollow microtopography typical to bogs [21,34]. In addition, different species of Sphagnum 
85and vascular plants inhabit hollow and hummocks, resulting in peats with contrasting biochemical 
86composition [5,33,35]. Peats from contrasting depths within cores also represent a continuum of organic 
87quality, as peat near the surface is typically less decomposed than peat buried deeper in the permanently 
88anoxic layer [13,14,36]. 
89Our overall goal was to determine the extent to which organic matter quality (as measured by 
90biochemical composition) improves our ability to predict potential GHG production under both aerobic and 
91anaerobic conditions. Thus, we hypothesized that (1) potential GHG production differs by depth and 
92microtopography, reflecting differences in degree of decomposition and botanical origin; and (2) the 
93inclusion of indices of biochemical composition will improve statistical models predicting GHG production 
94under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions.

1352.3 Ion Analysis
136An additional subsample of the peat was extracted for analysis of major cations and anions using 
137ion chromatography (IC). 3±0.001 g of peat was weighed into 50 mL plastic centrifuge tubes with 30 mL 
138of 10 mmol L -1 KCl. Samples were capped and shaken in a rotary shaker for one hour, then filtered using 
1390.45 µm syringe filters. Cation and anion analyses were performed simultaneously, using a Dionex Aquion 
140system for cations and a Dionex Integrion system for anions. Both systems used suppressed conductivity 
141detection. Cation analysis quantified Na +, NH 4+, Mg 2+ and Ca 2+ concentrations using a CS-12A column, 
142while anion analyses quantified the organic acids acetate, propionate, formate, and butyrate as well as the 
143inorganic ions NO 3- and SO 4- using an AS-12-HC column. The concentration of ions in each sample was 
144calculated using calibration curves made from a mixed ion standard for each analyte. The total inorganic 
145nitrogen (N) content was calculated as the sum of the measured NH 4+ and NO 3- concentrations for each 
146sample. The total organic acid content was similarly determined as the sum of the observed acetate, 
147propionate, formate, and butyrate concentrations for each sample.

1482.4 Total hydrolyzable amino acid analysis
149Total hydrolyzable amino acids (THAA) were analyzed using high performance liquid 
150chromatography (HPLC) following derivatization with ortho-phthaldehyde (OPA) and 9-
151fluorenylmethylchloroformate (FMOC-Cl) reagents [42]. 25±1 mg of dried sample material were weighed 
152into 2 mL glass ampules with 1.5 mL of 6 M HCl. Ampules were flame-sealed and hand-shaken to distribute 
153the sample. Samples were then heated to 110°C in an oven for 20 hours for hydrolysis. Ampules were 
154cracked open, the contents transferred into 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 
155minutes. 200 µL of supernatant were then transferred to 2 mL glass vials with 40 µL of internal standard (5 
156mmol L -1 norvaline). The vials were vortexed to mix prior to being evaporated under a gentle stream of N2 
157gas. Samples were either frozen for later analysis (-20°C) or redissolved with 200 μL of 0.1 mol L -1 HCl 
158and vortexed to mix. Samples were analyzed with HPLC within 10 hours of having been thawed and diluted.
159HPLC analysis was performed on an Agilent 1100 with a diode array detector (DAD) and an 
160Agilent AdvanceBio Amino Acid Analysis (AAA) column (Agilent). Derivatizations were performed 
161within the needle of the autosampler in a borate buffer (10.2 pH) prior to injecting sample into the column 
162during HPLC analysis to detect both primary and secondary amines. Primary amino acids were derivatized 
163with an OPA reagent and secondary amino acids were derivatized with a FMOC-Cl reagent. The mobile 
164phase for HPLC analysis consisted of an aqueous buffer (10 mM Na 2HPO4 and 10 mM Na2B4O7 at pH 8.2) 
165and acetonitrile:methanol:water (45:45:10, v:v:v). The flow rate was 1 mL min -1 and the eluent gradient 
166followed Henderson and Brooks (2010). OPA derivatives were detected at a wavelength of 338 nm with a 
167390 nm reference wavelength, while FMOC derivatives were detected at 262 nm with a 324 nm reference 
168wavelength. Samples were measured for the amino acids aspartic acid, glutamic acid, serine, histidine, 
169glycine, threonine, arginine, alanine, tyrosine, valine, phenylalanine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, and 
170hydroxyproline, with norvaline as the internal standard. Methionine and tryptophan were destroyed in 
171hydrolysis. The different amino acid concentrations of each sample were calculated using calibration curves 
172made from a mixed amino acid standard of the aforementioned amino acids with a 1 mmol L -1 norvaline 
173internal standard. The full amino acid composition is provided in Online Resource 1.

1742.5 Greenhouse Gas Production
175Two microcosms were constructed for each peat sample, with one for aerobic and one for anaerobic 
176incubation. For each microcosm, 8 g of field-moist homogenized peat were added to a 70 mL serum bottle 
177and wrapped in aluminum foil to exclude light. Microcosms for aerobic incubation were covered with 
178parafilm with holes to allow for gas exchange, pre-incubated for 7 to 10 days, then sealed with rubber septa. 
179The CO 2 concentration in the headspace was measured immediately after sealing and after 24 hours using 
180an Agilent 6890 gas chromatograph with a flame ionization detector (GC/FID) equipped with a methanizer 
181(Restek Corporation, Bellefonte, PA, USA). Aerobic CO 2 production was calculated as the change in CO2 
182concentration over 24 hours. 
183Microcosms for anaerobic incubation were sealed with a rubber stopper immediately after adding 
184peat and the headspace flushed with N 2. These microcosms were incubated for 20-24 days, after which 
185cumulative CO 2 and CH 4 production was measured by analyzing the headspace with GC/FID. All 
186incubations were conducted at room temperature. Reported rates of CO2 production in both the aerobic and 
187anaerobic incubations include production of dissolved inorganic C species calculated using Henry’s Law 
188[43] and the measured pH of each sample. 
1892.6 Data Analysis

194whole in each peat sample. The parameter mole % of glycine was also calculated, representing the ratio of 
195glycine present in comparison to the other amino acids.

2463.2 Relationship of greenhouse gas production to environmental and chemical parameters
247A principal components analysis of potentially significant biochemical parameters was conducted 
248in order to visualize the associations between organic matter quality, environmental conditions, and GHG 
249production of peat samples (Fig. 2). In general, we found that parameters with positive loadings on PC1 are 
250those typically associated with more degraded organic matter (lower OM quality) and lower rates of gas 
251production, whereas parameters on the negative axis of PC1 were most indicative of a less degraded state 
252(higher OM quality) and higher rates. Parameters with negative loadings on PC1 were C:N, anaerobic and 
253aerobic CO 2 production, CH 4 production, and % of total C as sugars. Notably, all three of the GHG 
254production parameters were positioned on the negative axis of PC1. Chemical parameters with positive 
255loadings on PC1 were pH, % of total sugars as rhamnose, % of total sugars as glucose, % of total C as 
256amino acids, the C-normalized yield of hydroxyproline, and total inorganic N content. Several amino acid 
257parameters (%N as THAA, %C as THAA, mole % hydroxyproline, and hydroxyproline yield) had negative 
258loadings on PC2, while the low-molecular weight organic acid concentration had the largest positive 
259loading on PC2.

2813.3 Prediction of greenhouse gas production using multiple regression
282A stepwise multiple regression model was constructed to evaluate the predictability of log-
283transformed aerobic and anaerobic CO 2 and CH 4 production based on the measured environmental and 
284biochemical parameters. Our model for predicted CO 2 production under aerobic conditions identified pH, 
285total organic acids, total inorganic N, mole % glucose, %N as THAA, mole % hydroxyproline, mole % 
286glycine, and depth as significant parameters (Table 1). The multiple regression analysis for aerobic CO 2 
287production had the strongest fit among the three GHGs measured (adjusted r 2 = 0.6487; p < 0.001). There 
288was also a relatively strong linear relationship between predicted and measured aerobic CO 2 production (r2 
289= 0.66; p < 0.001; RMSE = 47.83; Fig. 4a).

3074. Discussion
3084.1 Patterns of GHG production with depth and microtopography
309We hypothesized GHG production would vary with depth and microtopography, reflecting 
310differences in the extent of organic matter decomposition, botanical origin, and redox conditions. First, 
311GHG production rates were hypothesized to decline with depth, as labile biochemicals are selectively 
312removed from older peats as decomposition proceeds [13,14,45]. Second, samples from hummock 
313microtopographies were hypothesized to have lower rates of CO 2 and CH4 production than hollow or sedge 
314meadow samples, reflecting the lower litter quality observed in the Sphagnum species typically inhabiting 
315hummocks [46,47], as well as longer exposure to oxic decomposition before burial beneath the water table 
316[27].
317Both hypotheses were supported for the aerobic incubation experiments. Aerobic CO 2 production 
318was highest in surface layers in all six cores, across all three microtopographies. This is consistent with 
319previous studies, which have shown that labile substrates are generally depleted with increasing depth and 
320age of the peat, resulting in lower rates of decomposition and CO 2 production [14]. We also observed that 
321aerobic CO 2 production was higher in samples from the hollow cores throughout the peat profile, consistent 
322with previous literature. Sphagnum mosses produce differing amounts of structural or metabolic

324the formation of structural carbohydrates, such as sphagnan, that are less-easily degraded than the metabolic 
325carbohydrates associated with faster growth [46]. Since the hollow species are generally inundated and 
326experience fewer seasonal and diurnal water table movements than hummocks, these species tend to devote 
327more energy into the formation of relatively labile metabolic carbohydrates [48]. Accordingly, a 
328decomposition experiment conducted at our field site found that Sphagnum magellanicum collected from 
329the hummocks had higher sphagnan content and lower decomposition rates than Sphagnum cuspidatum 
330collected from the hollows [33]. This is consistent with moss decomposition experiments from other 
331peatlands [47]. Our results demonstrate that these differences in organic matter quality persist to the later 
332stages of decomposition, as the hollow samples exhibited higher rates of aerobic CO 2 even in deep layers.
333The effect of both depth and microtopography was smaller for anaerobic CO 2 and CH4 production 
334than for aerobic CO 2 production (Fig. 1b,c). While both were generally highest at the surface and declined 
335with depth, the effect was smaller than in the aerobic experiments, and there was no significant difference 
336among the three microtopographies. This suggests that the influence of biochemical composition arising 
337from botanical origin and selective decomposition are less important for determining the rate of anaerobic 
338compared to aerobic decomposition. The process of anaerobic respiration involves hydrolysis of complex 
339biopolymers to monomeric DOM, fermentation to labile substrates such as organic acids, and finally 
340respiration to CO 2 or CH4 [34,49]. Organic matter composition would be expected to alter the availability 
341of labile substrates for hydrolysis. However, the lack of difference among microtopographies suggests that 
342respiration is ultimately limited by factors such as the availability of terminal electron acceptors rather than 
343organic matter quality.

3574.2 Biochemical and environmental parameters associated with GHG production
358The suite of biochemical measurements collected alongside GHG production rates provides an 
359opportunity to identify the specific variables driving organic matter quality under both oxic and anoxic 
360conditions. Our PCA indicated that CO 2 production (both aerobic and anaerobic) and CH4 production were 
361associated with C:N and % of C as sugars, with negative loadings on the PC1 axis (Fig. 2). pH, % of total 
362sugars as rhamnose, % of total sugars as glucose, % of total C as amino acids, and total inorganic N content 
363had positive loadings on PC1, indicating these parameters were associated with lower rates of GHG 
364production. We therefore interpreted peat with negative PC1 scores as indicative of higher bioavailability 
365and positive scores as representing more degraded peat. Consistent with this interpretation, PC1 scores of 
366peat samples were typically negative at the surface and positive deeper in the core (Fig. 3a).

368previous literature. Non-cellulosic sugars generally have high bioavailability, and high C:N is associated 
369with fresh, poorly decomposed peat [53,54]. Rhamnose is a major component of sphagnan, which 
370contributes to the relative recalcitrance of Sphagnum, and has been shown to be inversely correlated with 
371aerobic CO 2 production in these samples [32,33,47,55]. The positive PC1 loading of mole % glucose is 
372counterintuitive, as it is often associated with fresh, highly bioavailable peat [56,57]. However, our sugar

397Our results indicate that a range of both environmental and biochemical parameters are significant 
398predictors of decomposition, but that the important parameters are different for aerobic and anaerobic 
399respiration. Consistent with previous studies, aerobic CO 2 production was best explained by a combination 
400of depth, pH, total organic acids, and several biochemical indices reflective of organic matter composition 
401(Table 1). As climate change increases transpiration and alters precipitation patterns, a net decline in 
402peatland water table positions could increase the importance of organic matter composition in determining 
403decomposition rates and the net C balance of peatland ecosystems.
404In contrast, anaerobic respiration and CH 4 production were primarily associated with N and amino 
405acid-related parameters, suggesting increased importance of N availability (Table 1). Specifically, both 
406were negatively correlated with C:N, but positively correlated with %N as THAA. Low C:N and high %N 
407as THAA are both associated with higher N availability, as the soil protein that comprises THAA is 
408relatively labile and %N as THAA generally declines with decomposition [25,60]. On the other hand, CH 4 
409production (but not anaerobic CO 2 production) was negatively correlated with inorganic N. However, 
410inorganic N has rapid turnover and is short-lived in soils, and is not likely to be representative of N 
411availability over the timescale of our incubations. Previous research on the effect of N on CH 4 production 
412is complex: while a metanalysis showed an overall increase in CH 4 emissions in response to N fertilization 
413[61], several individual studies have found no effect [62–64]. Additionally, some of the stimulation of CH 4 
414production resulting from fertilization has been attributed to indirect effects of N addition, such as 
415subsidence and the replacement of Sphagnum with aerenchyma-containing sedge vegetation [62,65]. The 
416positive correlation observed between anaerobic respiration and N bioavailability (low C:N and high %N 
417as THAA) are consistent with stimulation of respiration due to higher N availability. As peatlands 
418experience increased N limitation in response to warming and C fertilization, the lower N availability could 
419contribute to lower CH 4 emissions.

4204.3 Predicting GHG production with multiple regression models
421Stepwise multiple regression models were used to predict GHG production rates in each of the 
422three microtopographies. Our model’s predicted CO 2 production rates displayed a relatively strong linear 
423relationship with the experimentally observed production rates (R 2=0.66; Fig. 4a). Several parameters 
424associated with biochemical composition, including mole % glucose, mole % hydroxyproline, and mole % 
425glycine were identified as significant in the stepwise regression model, indicating that organic matter 
426composition affects the vulnerability of peat organic matter to decomposition under oxic conditions. These 
427results support our hypothesis that inclusion of biochemical parameters improves the predictability of 
428potential greenhouse gas production in addition to parameters such as depth and soil pH.
429The multiple regression models for the anaerobic experiments explained a somewhat smaller 
430fraction of the variance of both CO 2 and CH 4 production (31% and 42%, respectively). While no sugar-
431related parameters improved the model according to the stepwise regression procedure, amino acid-related 
432parameters were found to be significant. These results suggest that consideration of N bioavailability, 
433including biochemical analysis of hydrolyzable amino acids, can improve the prediction of anaerobic GHG 
434emissions. Although this hypothesis was supported, models accounted for less than half of the variance in 
435both anaerobic CO 2 and CH4 production. It is clear that other unmeasured variables such as availability of 
436inorganic and organic terminal electron acceptors [66,67], DOC availability [34,49], and differences in 
437communities of methanogenic archaea and methanotrophic bacteria [68,69] also play a role in determining 
438rates of anaerobic respiration. However, understanding of the biochemical composition of the peat does 
439improve the skill of the models in predicting GHG production.
