Theory of Grasses investigates the proliferation of so-called invasive species of giant grasses such as Carrizo Cane along the edges of river-borders and subsequent eradication campaigns waged against them by border policing agencies. Against virulent state rhetoric of invasiveness, Theory of Grasses argues that Carrizo proliferation is a contemporary effect of longue-durée impositions of linear space-time through environmental infrastructures that attempt to colonise seasonality. Carrizo inhabits the exposed earths of highly disturbed environments and in so doing becomes a botanical facilitator of ungovernable forms of movement, both human and more-than-human, across industrialised and militarised water bodies. Looking at these scenes of “invasion” from the vantage point of Carrizo, new practices of visual study emerge that take phenology as method, that is the study of mutations and changes in ecological rhythms and life-cycles. These practices span documentary, technical engineering diagrams, early cinematographic motion studies, aerial surveillance photography, juridical theories of perception, and historical geographical surveys. Chapter 1, “The Case for Carrizo,” utilises the operative concept of cryptogenic beings, forms of life of indeterminate origin, to question the unavoidable facticity of state narratives of invasion. Chapter 2, “Feeding on Light,” uses visual motif research and spatial analysis to diagram the recurring ecological time-space configuration in which Carrizo proliferates within critical infrastructure sites of water conveyance. Chapter 3, “Flume Studies,” looks at the colonial canal system of Punjab, in present-day northwest India, reading the continental-scale transformations required to produce surplus grasses like wheat as raw material for European consumption through the technology of the flume, an open-channel outlet that disperses turbulent waters from a canal. Across this dissertation project, Theory of Grasses intervenes aesthetically and politically at the nexus of the production of raw materials, movements of irregular migration, and policing/counter-insurgency at sites where large grasses and water meet.
