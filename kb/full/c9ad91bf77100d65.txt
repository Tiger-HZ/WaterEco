Abstract
Lead (Pb²⁺) contamination in aquatic systems poses a major threat to human health 
and environmental safety. Conventional remediation techniques such as chemical 
precipitation and ion exchange are often constrained by high operational costs, poor 
efficiency at trace concentrations, and secondary pollution. Nanotechnology has 
introduced materials like carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and metal oxide nanoparticles 
(MONPs) with superior physicochemical reactivity for Pb² ⁺ removal. This review 
highlights recent advances (2002–2025) in adsorption–desorption behaviour of Pb² ⁺ 
on MONPs and pristine CNTs, focusing on synthesis, surface properties, adsorption 
mechanisms, reusability, and toxicity. Reported findings reveal that MONPs, including 
Fe₃O₄, ZnO, MgO, and CuO, exhibit outstanding adsorption capacities (up to 1980 mg 
g⁻¹ for MgONPs), surpassing those of CNTs which usually range between 10 and 250 
mg g-1 under comparable conditions. This performance is linked to the abundance 
of chemically active surface sites and electrostatic interactions, which dominate 
adsorption on MONPs. Desorption studies confirm both materials are reusable over 
multiple cycles, though MONPs demonstrate higher regeneration efficiency and 
lower toxicity. Overall, MONPs present more scalable and eco-friendly alternatives 
for Pb²⁺ remediation. Future research should refine regeneration strategies, assess 
environmental safety, and develop hybrid systems for sustainable water treatment.
Keywords CNTs, MONPs, Adsorption, Desorption, Regeneration
 1 Introduction
Heavy metals like Pb are metals and metalloids that have a specific gravity greater than 
5 g/cm3. They are highly mobile, non-biodegradable, toxic, accumulative through food 
chains, and carcinogenic to humans [ 47, 58, 63, 86, 88]. They are generally hazardous to 
plant growth, human health, and animal safety [ 29, 30, 38, 126]. Other metals like mer -
cury (Hg), nickel (Ni), arsenic (As), bismuth (Bi), chromium (Cr), cobalt (Co), and others 
are also classified as heavy metals [16].

Among these metals, lead poses serious concerns due to its toxicity, adverse effects, 
and no known biological function [ 37]. Lead poisoning is the term used for lead toxic -
ity, which may be acute or chronic [19]. “Lead poisoning” has been investigated to cause 
encephalopathy, hepatitis, kidney damage, high blood pressure, abortion, low sperm 
count, anorexia nervosa, cancer (particularly gastric cancer), brain damage, malaise, 
anaemia, autism, dyslexia, and several damages to body organs [ 14, 51, 89]. In response 
to these risks, the World Health Organization has established a permissible limit of 
0.01 ppm of lead for drinkable water [ 5, 98]. However, lead continues to find its way 
into water bodies through sources such as ammunition production, battery production, 
plastic stabilizer production, pigment processing, smelting, electroplating, alloys, cable 
sheathings, pesticide application, and use of leaded fuels, among others [ 38, 48, 64, 65, 
91]. As a result, the development of efficient and sustainable treatment technology for 
the sequestration of lead from water remains an environmental priority.
Several water treatment techniques have been used to sequester lead from aqueous 
media, but these conventional techniques suffer the demerit of secondary sludge pro -
duction, water wastage, high operational cost, slow kinetics, operational cost, and main -
tenance challenges. Liu et al., [ 67], Nwoke, [ 87], Vaneeckhaute, [ 124], Zhang, [ 140]. 
These shortcomings have inspired interest in adsorption-based water treatment tech -
nologies, especially those involving nanomaterials, due to their operational simplicity, 
high efficiency, and potential for regeneration [ 19]. Adsorption using nanoparticles pro -
vides advantages such as enhanced surface areas and enhanced active sites for binding. 
Nanoparticles (NPs) eliminate most of these problems as it is efficient, cost-effective, 
simple, easy to execute, and the adsorbents can be functionalized and recycled [ 107]. 
Some disadvantages have been associated with the use of NPs for adsorption procedures,

Among the different classes of nanoparticles used as adsorbents, metal oxide nanopar-
ticles (MONPs) and carbon nanotubes (CNTs) stand out as two of the most widely 
researched for the sequestration of lead. MONPs have been reported to show strong 
affinity for metals, redox potential, and even magnetic properties, while CNTs have 
demonstrated high surface area and strong mechanical strength [ 92, 109]. Notable limi-
tations such as agglomeration, instability, and potential toxicity have been reported for 
MONPs, while CNTs have been reported to suffer from agglomerations, disposal con -
cerns, and regeneration challenges [5, 93, 101].
Numerous studies and reviews have separately examined the potency of MONPs and 
CNTs for lead sequestration; however, direct systematic comparison between pristine 
CNTs (P-CNTs) and MONPs with regard to lead sequestration remains limited and 
fragmented. Existing literature often focuses on adsorption capacity with little consider -
ation for performance trade-offs such as regeneration efficiency, scalability, environmen-
tal safety, and practical deployment constraints.
Unlike previous reviews that consider MONPs and CNTs independently, this study 
provides a structured comparative evaluation based on adsorption capacity, kinet -
ics, regeneration efficiency, scalability, and toxicity. By focusing exclusively on pristine 
P-CNTs, this study provides a baseline for assessing the performance of CNTs relative to 
MONPs without the influence of functionalization.
2 Methodology
This study focuses on the comparison of MONPs and pristine carbon nanotubes 
(P-CNTs) in adsorbing aqueous divalent lead ions. A structured protocol was adopted 
to guide the identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and adoption of literature as 
shown in Fig. 1.
2.1 Search strategy
A comprehensive literature search was performed across numerous databases, including 
Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar, and PubMed, and the search 
covered peer-reviewed publications from 2000 to 2025, which represents the period of 
increased interest in Nano-adsorbent research. Search strings formed with keywords 
such as “Pb 2+ adsorption” , “lead removal” , “metal oxide nanoparticles” , “pristine carbon 
nanotubes” , “CNT adsorption” , “Nano-adsorbents” , and “water treatment” to assess rele-
vant literature, and the reference list of related studies was manually screened to identify 
relevant studies.
2.2 Eligibility conditions
Screening of studies was done based on predefined conditions that relevant studies must 
investigate lead sequestration from aqueous media, employ MONPs or P-CNTs as pri -
mary adsorbent, report quantitative adsorption metrics such as adsorption capacity and 
removal efficiency, and be published in English peer-reviewed journals. Studies focusing 
on hybrid (MONPs-CNTs) and functionalized CNTs were excluded from the compari -
son, but were considered for contextual discussions where relevant. Materials lacking 
adequate experimental details were excluded.

2.3 Study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment
Materials sourced from the databases were compiled, and duplicates were removed. The 
titles and abstracts were assessed to confirm relevance to the adsorption of lead using 
MONPs or CNTs from aqueous systems. Thereafter, studies meeting all the inclusion 
criteria were adopted in the comparative analysis.
Variables such as adsorbent type, synthetic method, particle size, experimental con -
ditions, optimal adsorption capacity or percentage removal, isotherm, kinetics, and 
regeneration ability were extracted from the included studies. Studies employing stan -
dard adsorption protocols were considered to ensure data reliability. Variations resulting 
from differences in experimental conditions were acknowledged during the comparative 
assessment. Lastly, the comparative assessment involved evaluation of the mechanism 
of adsorption to infer the dominant mechanism, assessing performance indicators such 
as regeneration efficiency, material stability, agglomeration potential, and environmen -
tal friendliness to elucidate performance trade-offs between MONPs and P-CNTs. The 
findings were presented using thematic analysis and structured Tables to support evi -
dence-based conclusions.

3 Metal oxide nanoparticles (MONPs)
A comprehensive review of literature has shown that MONPs are among the most stud -
ied Nano-adsorbents for the sequestration of heavy metals from aqueous solutions. 
Properties such as high surface area, redox activity, stability, and enhanced surface 
action have been reported in numerous studies as the primary factors responsible for 
their adsorption performance [14, 42].
A wide range of MONPs have been investigated for their potential for lead sequestra -
tion, such as CuONPs, FeONPs, ZnONPs, CaONPs, CaTiO 3NPs, Al 2O3NPs, NiONPs, 
CeO2NPs, TiO2NPs, MnO 2NPs, MnFe2O4NPs, CoFe2O4NPs, SnO 2NPs, and MgONPs. 
A similar observation which has been observed across these MONPs is that adsorption 
behavior is strongly influenced by the density of surface hydroxyl groups and the mate -
rial’s point of zero charge as these factors control the electrostatic attraction and surface 
complexation with Pb ions [42, 111].
The choice of synthetic route (especially green synthesis/hydrothermal) has been 
reported to be the primary determinant of MONP properties, including surface area, 
crystallite size, pore size distribution, and available functional groups [20, 112].
3.1 Copper oxide nanoparticles (CuONPs)
The current study noted that CuONPs demonstrate moderate to high adsorption 
potential for lead (between the range of 84–153  mg/g), with the performance report -
edly dependent on synthesis method, particle size, and experimental conditions. Green 
synthesized CuONPs were noted to show comparable or superior efficiency relative to 
the chemically synthesized CuONPs. Pseudo second order kinetics was consistently 
reported suggesting that chemisorption dominates Pb uptake, while isotherm modelling 
mostly align with Langmuir and Freundlich models, showing the coexistence of homog -
enous and heterogeneous sites.
Zaidi et al., [ 136] synthesized CuONPs via chemical co-precipitation and reported 
84.2% removal of Pb 2+ from a 5 ppm solution in 120 min at a pH of 4.5. The adsorp -
tion process was best described by the Freundlich model (R 2 = 0.922), suggesting het -
erogeneous surface adsorption and a chemisorption mechanism. Mahmoud et al., 
[71] performed green synthesis using mint leaves and orange peel extract to produce 
CuONPs, both of which showed high adsorption capacities (qm = 88.8 and 82.8 mg/g, 
respectively), under identical conditions. These findings highlight the effectiveness of 
bio-mediated synthetic approaches in producing functional adsorbents while reducing 
environmental impacts associated with conventional chemical synthesis.
Microwave-assisted synthesis has also been shown to yield efficient CuONPs for Pb 
sequestration. Farghali et al., [ 36] reported that CuONPs prepared using microwave 
radiation demonstrated adsorption behaviour consistent with the Langmuir adsorption 
isotherm (R 2 = 0.996), indicating monolayer adsorption on homogeneous active sites. 
The kinetics followed the pseudo-second order (0.999), suggesting that chemisorption 
was the major rate-limiting step.
The study by Hassan and Mahdi [45] further demonstrated the high removal efficiency 
and fast adsorption equilibrium of CuONPs. They reported effective Pb sequestration 
using CuONPs with a crystallite size of about 7.43 nm, and achieved equilibrium under 
optimized conditions (adsorbent dose of 0.1 g, pH of 3.5, initial lead concentration of 
100 ppm, and temperature of 25 °C).

3.2 Iron oxide nanoparticles (FeONPs)
Iron oxides occur in different forms such as maghemite (γ-Fe 2O3), hematite (α-Fe 2O3), 
magnetite (Fe 3O4), and goethite (FeOOH). These polymorphic structures provide the 
specific surface chemistries and adsorption behaviors relevant for remediation applica -
tions. FeONPs, especially magnetite are among the most extensively studied Nano-sor -
bents for the removal of heavy metals owing to their magnetic recoverability and strong 
adsorption performance [ 65, 77]. Recorded Pb removal efficiencies mostly exceed 90%, 
while adsorption capacities ranging from 50 to 170 mg/g were also noted in optimized 
systems.
Comparative studies reveal that the crystallographic phase is a major determinant 
of adsorption efficiencies, with magnetite generally outperforming hematite and other 
polymorphs. This variation highlights the differences in surface charge distribution, 
redox properties, and active site density [ 79]. Green-synthesized FeONPs were noted 
to exhibit superior performance in comparison to the conventionally produced coun -
terparts, signifying the importance of surface functionalization and biogenic capping 
agents [ 64]. This is also supported by the prevalence of pseudo second order kinetics 
and Langmuir isotherm, signifying that the removal of using FeONPs is mediated by the 
homogenous coverage on surface chemical groups.
Rahimi et al., [ 94] compared hydrated lepidocrocite and goethite for Pb sequestration 
and reported that both materials demonstrated an optimum pH of 5.2, and followed 
Langmuir and Sips adsorption isotherms. However, goethite showed 1.64 times better 
adsorption capacity than lepidocrocite, showing the effect of mineral phase on adsorp -
tion performance.
Similarly, different crystalline forms of Fe 2O3NPs have shown varying efficiencies. 
Hexagonal Fe 2O3NPs with crystallite sizes ranging from 20 to 60 nm achieved a Pb 
removal efficiency of 96.73% within 60 min under alkaline pH [ 77]. Other studies report 
adsorption efficiencies of 95% and 92.47% using γ-Fe 2O3NPs [6, 103], while α-Fe2O3NPs 
have shown comparatively lower adsorption capacities [ 13]. These reports show that 
crystallographic structures greatly influence adsorption performance.

3.5 Aluminum oxide nanoparticles (Al2O3NPs)
Aluminum oxide nanoparticles (Al 2O3NPs), especially the gamma-phased alumina 
(γ-Al2O3NPs), have been widely studied for Pb sequestration applications because of 
their porosity, stability, and abundant surface hydroxyl groups [46]. Experimental studies 
have noted variations in Pb adsorption capacity depending on the synthetic method and 
experimental conditions.
Tabesh et al., [117] synthesized γ-Al2O3NPs using the sol-gel method and reported Pb 
adsorption efficiency of 97% under optimized conditions. Similarly, Rahmani et al., [ 95] 
reported an adsorption capacity of 125 mg/g, showing the high Pb sequestrating poten -
tial of γ-Al2O3NPs from aqueous solutions.
However, Shokati Poursani et al., [ 115] reported an adsorption capacity of 6 mg/g in 
their study using γ-Al 2O3NPs, demonstrating the role of surface chemistry and experi -
mental conditions in determining the adsorption efficiency.
3.6 Other MONPs
Several less studied MONPs have also demonstrated varying degrees of effectiveness for 
Pb sequestration from aqueous systems. These are materials such as manganese oxide, 
nickel oxide, magnesium oxide, cerium oxide, cobalt ferrite, tin oxide, and titanium diox-
ide, which have shown distinct properties necessary to aid sequestration performance.
Manganese oxide nanoparticles (Mn 2ONPs) and composites demonstrate high 
adsorption capacities (between 121 and 260 mg/g) [ 24, 31, 73], whereas other counter -
parts such as Cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO 2NPs) and Nickel oxide nanoparticles 
(NiONPs) have shown lower capacities of 5 mg/g and 56 mg/g respectively [1, 72, 129].
Cobalt ferrite nanoparticles have been reported to show moderate Pb adsorption 
potentials with reported removal capacities falling between 32 and 63 mg/g [11, 27].
Titanium dioxide and tin oxide nanoparticles (TiO2NPs and SnO2NPs) have also been 
reported to show good sorption properties, with respective capacities of 90.13 mg/g and 
98.13 mg/g recorded [ 99]. Complete removal (100%) of Pb 2+ was also documented by 
Engates and Shipley [35] using TiO2NPs.
MgONPs stands out with exceptionally high adsorption capacity (up to 1980 mg/g and 
94%) showing strong affinity for Pb [21, 28].
Several consistent trend was observed across the MONPs and they include:
1. Adsorption is mostly governed by electrostatic interaction and surface complexation
2. Pseudo second order kinetics dominate, showing chemisorption
3. Green synthesis enhances performance

4 Carbon nanotubes (CNTs)
Cho et al., [ 26] described CNTs as allotropes of carbon possessing high adsorption 
capacity as a result of their layered and hollow structure, p electron-sharing ability, 
and large surface area. CNTs can best be visualized as a rolled sheet of graphene pos -
sessing an axial symmetry [ 26]. CNTs are divided into two main categories depending 
on the number of graphene layers rolled into a coaxial array, and as such, we have the 
Single-Walled Carbon Nanotubes (SWCNTs) and the Multi-Walled Carbon Nanotubes 
(MWCNTs). SWCNTs possess diameters within the range 0.4 to 20 nm and lengths 
ranging from 100 nm to 10 μm, with the lengths and diameters depending on the syn -
thetic method. In MWCNTs, the respective tubes are separated by 0.34 to 0.36 nm. 
MWCNTs typically possess diameters ranging from 1 nm to 300 nm and lengths from 1 
μm to 150 μm [26].
CNTs are chemically inert, have a high surface area, and possess water transport 
properties [ 92]. They have found useful applications as: adsorbents, delivery for genes 
and seeds in agriculture, antimicrobial agents against pathogens, and Nano-sensors 
[104, 122]. Mubarak et al., [ 82] also reported CNTs as having large surface areas for 
the removal of lead and other heavy metal ions. As adsorbents, CNTs have the general 
advantages of high adsorption capacity, good thermal and chemical stabilities, low mass 
density, high porosity, and eco-friendliness [41].
The major methods used in the synthesis of CNTs are carbon arc discharge, laser abla-
tion, and chemical vapour deposition (CVD) techniques. The arc discharge method uses

4.1 Multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs)
Review of literature revealed that MWCNTs have been more extensively studied for 
Pb sequestration than SWCNTs due to their larger surface area and greater number of 
active sites for adsorption. These structural advantages, owing to the presence of multi -
ple graphene layers and wider pore channels enhances adsorption capacity, enable func -
tionalization, and improve stability during the sorption process [ 141]. The adsorption 
capacity of PCNTs typically fall within the range of about 10–215 mg/g, with MWCNTs 
consistently outperforming SWCNTs, therefore, the comparative synthesis focuses on 
MWCNTs.
The sequestration performance of the MWCNTs in the studies reviewed was observed 
to vary depending on surface area, particle size, surface modification, synthetic method, 
and experimental conditions. Removal efficiencies > 80% have been observed for com -
mercially available MWCNTs.

The study also noted adsorption can also be dependent on other factors such as tube

[84]. On the other hand, Tehrani et al., [120] employed raw MWCNTs from Cheaptubes

5 Comparison between MONPs and PCNTs
The performance trade-offs between MONPs and CNTs were assessed based on the 
adsorption capacity, mechanism, kinetics, toxicity, cost, regeneration, and dispersion in 
water, and the summary findings are shown in Supplementary data S3.
5.1 Adsorption capacity and removal percentage
The comparative evaluation of the performance of MONPs and PCNTs reveal a consis -
tent trend where material chemistry, rather than only surface area govern adsorption 
performance [ 66, 101]. The adsorption performance was observed to not completely 
depend on the available surface area, but also on the nature of surface chemistry and 
binding mechanism. Reported observations across reviewed studies indicate that both 
CNTs and MONPs demonstrate strong performance towards Pb, although the degree of 
performance varies depending on factors such as nanoparticles’ composition, morphol -
ogy, functionalization, experimental conditions, and synthetic routes [ 10, 57]. Although 
PCNTs are widely acknowledged for their high surface areas, however, this structural 
advantage does not directly translate to superior Pb removal ability. Rather, the pres -
ence, accessibility and reactivity of the surface groups have been reported as dominant 
factors influencing efficiency [133, 141].

5.3 Toxicity
Humans are exposed to nanomaterials in three ways: inhalation into the pulmonary sys -
tem, absorption through the dermal system, and ingestion through the gastrointestinal 
system [112]. These NPs may concentrate in the body and cause certain health risks or 
problems.
MONPs generally exhibit variable degrees of toxicity depending on their composi -
tion. MONPs such as TiO 2NPs have been reported to trigger injuries to the brain, liver, 
kidney, and spleen tissues [ 40]. Green-synthesized MONPs have been reported to have 
milder side effects than their chemically produced counterparts [ 53]. Some MONPs 
have been reported to be non-cytotoxic at low concentrations but toxic at high concen -
trations [75, 132], while some others have been noted to react with blood and bodily flu -
ids, and affect the workings of the cardiovascular and the central nervous systems (CNS)

[55]. Generally, the toxicity of MONPs has been reported to be mostly initiated through 
the reactive oxygen species, causing oxidative stress and DNA damage [50, 77].
On the other hand, CNTs have also been reported to pose significant toxicity, espe -
cially to the lungs and liver, with the potential to cause inflammation, oxidative stress, 
fibrosis, and cancer [32, 90, 105, 112]. Other studies have also reported adverse effects of 
CNTs on the CNS, kidney, eye, spleen, and the cardiovascular system [ 17]. CNTs have 
been reported to penetrate the seeds of plants, affecting the growth, and can be translo -
cated even to the leaves [7, 56]. The toxicity of P-CNTs depends on diverse properties of 
the CNTs, such as length, aspect ratio, surface area, degree of aggregation, purity, con -
centration, dose, and other factors [23, 39].
Summarily, both MONPs and CNTs pose human toxicity risk, but whereas this risk 
stands unavoidable with CNTs and can only be avoided by wearing personal protective 
equipment while handling, the risk with MONPs can be avoided by choosing MONPs 
such as MgONPs, which have been reported to be of less toxicity and still afford high 
adsorption capacity, and employing green synthetic routes, which have also been 
recorded to reduce toxicity.
5.4 Cost
Chatterjee and Deopura, [ 22] reported low volume and high cost of production on a 
commercial scale as a demerit for P-CNTs. Nitodas and Karachalios, [ 85] reported ther-
mal catalytic CVD as the most cost-effective method for synthesizing CNTs; given that 
synthesizing CNTs is an expensive endeavor, while other reports mentioned that CVD 
has the potential to drive down the cost of CNTs [ 127]. Laser ablation produces high-
purity CNTs, but its use becomes disinteresting when its cost of production is analyzed 
[104]. Studies have reported that the application of CNTs since its discovery in 1991 has 
slowed down due to its high cost of production, with production cost reaching as high as 
$105 per ton [97, 138]. Zhang et al., [138].
In contrast, MONPs are known to be relatively cheap. Abdelrahman et al., [ 4], Mu et 
al., [80], and Nwoke et al., [87] reported that FeONPs, CuONPs, TiO2NPs, ZnONPs, and 
MgONPs are relatively cheap adsorbents, with widespread applications reflecting the 
low production cost.
However, both CNTs and MONPs suffer from slow production processes, and the 
products are not specific in their properties without differences. The reason behind the 
problem of reproducibility is that the mechanism of nanoparticle formation has not 
been correctly and fully elucidated [92, 110].
5.5 Reusability and regeneration
Reusable adsorbents with good efficiency will always stand in preference over others as 
they offer practical and economic importance [ 23]. Regeneration studies demonstrate 
that both CNTs and MONPs can be reused effectively. MONPs generally maintain good 
performance across multiple adsorption-desorption cycles due to their structural stabil -
ity and resistance to chemical degradation [ 101]. CNTs also exhibit excellent regenera -
tion potential due to their mechanical strength and chemical inertness.
Overall, both MONPs and CNTs demonstrate strong reusability, although MONPs 
may be more cost-effective for repeated use.

5.6 Dispersion in water
Dispersion behavior significantly affects sorption efficiency. Many MONPs disperse well 
in water owing to the presence of surface hydroxyl groups, although aggregation can 
occur at higher concentrations due to high surface energy and intermolecular forces [9].
CNTs, on the other hand, are inherently hydrophobic and tend to aggregate in water, 
necessitating the need for surface functionalization or the use of a dispersing agent [ 61, 
90].
Therefore, MONPs demonstrate superior dispersion in water compared to CNTs. 
Supplementary data S3 summarizes the comparison of PCNTs and MONPs against the 
above indices.
5.7 Limitations
Despite the insights gleaned from this study, it is however, subject to several limitations. 
Most of the reported studies relied on batch-scale adsorption experiments that adopt 
synthetic solutions instead of real complex water samples. In addition, the reported per -
formance efficiencies were the product of varied optimization conditions, and as such, 
no data reports the performance of these MONPs and the CNTs under the same experi -
mental conditions. These differences in reporting standards and experimental conditions 
hinder direct comparisons. Furthermore, limited data exist on the environmental impact 
and the feasibility of large-scale water treatment applications for both adsorbents.
It is also noteworthy that most of the reported studies on the applications of P-CNTs 
are from the earlier years of CNT research, and this is likely due to the shift to the incor-
poration of functional groups and other forms of modification that promise to increase 
the performance of CNTs in heavy metal sequestration.

6 Conclusion
This review critically compared the performance of metal oxide nanoparticles (MONPs) 
and carbon nanotubes (CNTs) for Pb removal from aqueous systems. Both nanomateri -
als demonstrated high adsorption efficiency, rapid uptake kinetics, and good reusability, 
highlighting their potential for water treatment applications. However, MONPs gener -
ally exhibited higher adsorption capacities and faster adsorption rates, largely due to 
their abundant surface hydroxyl groups and ion-exchange sites. In addition, their sim -
pler synthesis routes, better aqueous dispersibility, and lower production costs make 
them more attractive for large-scale implementation. In contrast, CNTs often require 
surface functionalization to improve dispersion and adsorption performance, increas -
ing preparation complexity and cost. Although both adsorbent classes raise concerns 
regarding environmental and human health risks, the toxicity of MONPs can be mod -
erated through careful selection of metal oxides, whereas toxicity concerns associated 
with CNTs remain more challenging to address. In addition, adsorption performance 
for both materials is strongly influenced by synthesis protocols, surface characteristics, 
and operating conditions, underscoring the need for improved reproducibility and stan -
dardization. Further studies should focus on standardized experimental conditions and 
large-scale water treatment applications, as this will be critical in advancing practical 
and sustainable nanotechnology solutions for Pb remediation in water treatment.
