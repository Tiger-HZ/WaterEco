experiences across stage, phase, and geography

Abstract
Purpose Caregivers play a central role in supporting cancer survivors across the care continuum, often at detriment to their 
own well-being. Recognizing caregivers’ role in survivorship, the National Standards for Cancer Survivorship Care state the 
health system should assess “caregivers’ experiences and unmet needs.” This study sought to describe the extent to which 
this standard is being met from the perspective of caregivers.
Methods This cross-sectional, convergent mixed-methods study leveraged survey and semi-structured interview data from 
caregivers of survivors in active and post-treatment survivorship care at a southeastern U.S. cancer center. Surveys queried 
caregivers’ concerns and their experience being asked about and communicating various concerns; interviews further char-
acterized these experiences, including barriers and facilitators to accessing services. Survey data were analyzed descriptively 
(and by various subgroups), and interview data were examined using directed content analysis.
Results For the 77 caregivers surveyed (64.94% female; 96.10% non-Hispanic White; M average caregiving/week = 36 h), 
emotional concerns were most reported (66%), yet ≥ 50% of caregivers reported never being asked about these issues by 
the care team. Rural caregivers were less likely than non-rural caregivers to report the care team had inquired about their 
social and medical caregiving concerns. Interview data highlighted limited assessment of caregiver needs, logistical and 
communication barriers, and desire for proactive, tailored supportive services.
Conclusions Practice gaps in assessing and addressing caregivers’ emotional, social, and physical health needs must be 
overcome to meet the caregiver standard, particularly if assessment is to be comprehensive in scope to mirror survivor 
assessment standards.
Implications for Cancer Survivors Cancer care should implement routine, comprehensive structured assessment and referral 
pathways to meet caregiver needs.
Keywords Cancer survivors · Caregivers · Standards
Introduction
By 2040, the United States (U.S.) healthcare system will 
need to meet the needs of 26 million cancer survivors, an 
estimated 55% of whom rely on unpaid family and friends 
(i.e., caregivers) to provide critical medical, instrumental, 
and psychosocial support [1 ]. Meeting survivors’ needs 
requires meeting caregivers’ needs [2], as they are an essen-
tial part of the healthcare team throughout the cancer survi-
vorship trajectory.
Across the cancer survivorship trajectory, caregivers play an 
indispensable role in supporting survivors’ physical, emotional, 
and practical needs. During active treatment [3 ], caregivers 
attend appointments, manage medication schedules, provide

advances have only increased the demands of caregiving, as 
caregivers have greater responsibility for monitoring treatment 
side effects at home, adherence to oral agents, and sustaining 
this role throughout longer disease trajectories [4–6]. In the 
post-treatment phase, many caregivers assist and support sur-
vivors with long-term symptom management, surveillance for 
recurrence, and adjustment to life after cancer [7]. As evidenced 
by high rates of psychological distress and financial toxicity [8], 
caregiving is often time-consuming, emotionally taxing, and 
disruptive to caregivers’ own health, employment, and personal 
goals [9]. An estimated 42% of caregivers experience depres-
sion, 46% anxiety, and 25% financial strain [10–12].
Despite their critical role and high need for services to 
address the burdens of caregiving, caregivers’ needs remain 
to be systematically identified and addressed in cancer care 
[13]. In 2024, the National Cancer Institute introduced the 
National Standards for Cancer Survivorship Care to promote 
high-quality, coordinated, and person-centered care across the 
survivorship continuum [14]. The national standards outline 
essential components, including care planning, service provi-
sion, and system-level infrastructure, and recognize caregiver 
outcomes as an important part of survivorship care, requiring 
that the health system has a “process to collect data on car -
egivers’ experiences and unmet needs.” Despite the promise 
of these standards, there is little evidence on how they are 
operationalized in practice to meet caregiver needs. Under -
standing how survivorship care is implemented and the extent 
to which caregivers are meaningfully included is essential to 
improve outcomes for survivors and caregivers alike.
The current study aims to evaluate the state of caregiver-
specific cancer survivorship care at an NCI-Designated 
Comprehensive Cancer Center, understand caregivers’ barri-
ers to care, and explore differences in caregiver experiences 
by treatment phase (active vs. post-treatment) [3, 15], cancer 
stage (early vs. advanced) [16, 17], and geographic residence 
(rural vs. non-rural) [16, 18–20]. Findings from the study 
will identify priorities for meeting the new NCI National 
Standards for Cancer Survivorship Care among caregivers.
Methods
Study design
This cross-sectional, convergent mixed-methods study uti -
lized quantitative survey data and qualitative interview data 
to describe caregiver experiences of survivorship care at an 
NCI-Designated Comprehensive Cancer Center in the south-
eastern U.S. Caregivers were recruited as part of a larger 
protocol in which survivors were also approached to com-
plete a survey and interview (see Webster et al. (this issue)).
Participants
Participants were caregivers of survivors receiving care 
from one of four outpatient clinics (breast, gynecologic, 
head and neck, and multidisciplinary clinic) for solid 
tumors; survivors were at least 3 months into active treat-
ment or were in clinic for post-treatment survivorship care. 
Caregivers were eligible if their care recipient agreed to 
their recruitment into the study, they self-identified as a 
friend or family member who was the primary source of 
cancer-related care and support, were at least 18 years old, 
and were able to understand and complete a survey and 
interview questions in English.
Procedures
Caregivers were identified at the cancer care appoint-
ments of their care recipient. We purposively sampled 
to ensure that caregivers represented relatively even dis-
tributions regarding survivors’ EHR-documented treat-
ment phase (active vs. post-treatment) and disease stage 
(early vs. advanced). To recruit a caregiver, study staff 
first briefly described the caregiver study to the survi-
vor and obtained permission verbally from the survivor 
contact the person they identified as a primary support 
person (i.e., caregiver). In most cases, the caregiver was 
present during the clinic visit, which allowed study staff 
to screen and enroll the caregiver during the same visit. 
When the caregiver was not present, the survivor pro-
vided the name and contact preferences for the caregiver. 
Study staff subsequently contacted the caregiver via their 
preferred communication method (phone, email, text) to 
describe the study. Interested caregivers were screened 
for eligibility and gave verbal consent. Participating car -
egivers were asked to complete a 15-min survey either in 
clinic (on paper or a tablet) or via an online form after the 
visit. Participants received $50 for completing the sur -
vey. Survey data were collected between February and 
June 2025. Caregivers who indicated interest in complet-
ing an interview during the verbal consent process were 
purposively sampled by clinic type, sex, rural status, and 
care recipient’s cancer treatment status (active vs. post-
treatment). Some interview participants completed both 
the survey and interview, whereas others completed the 
interview only. Caregivers were contacted by phone to 
complete semi-structured interviews between April and 
July 2025. Interviews were audio-recorded using HIPAA-
compliant Zoom and transcribed verbatim, with transcripts 
cleaned using Rev software. Participants received $50 for 
the interview. Study procedures were approved by the uni-
versity’s Institutional Review Board (IRB #98262).

Measures
Survey
Sample characteristics Participants were asked standard 
sociodemographic questions and identified their relationship 
to their cancer care recipient. Rural residence was deter -
mined by comparing the county corresponding to caregiv -
ers’ self-reported ZIP code to the 2023 USDA Rural-Urban 
Continuum Codes, which assigns U.S. counties a code from 
1 to 9, with codes 4–9 defined as rural [21]. To assess par -
ticipants’ role as a caregiver, they were asked: “Do you help 
the person for whom you are providing care with any of the 
following activities? Check all that apply.” This statement 
was adapted from the HINTS 5 Cycle 2 Caregiver Mod-
ule [22], with options such as “Performing medical/nursing 
tasks,” “Communicating with health care professionals,” 
and “Providing transportation.” Caregivers were also asked 
to report on their quality of life using the Caregiver Roles 
and Responsibilities Scale (CRRS), which consists of five 
subscales and items that have five response options: “Not 
at all,” “A little bit,” “Somewhat,” “Quite a bit,” or “Very 
much” [23 ]. The Support and Impact subscale (six items, 
possible score range 0–24; Cronbach’s α = 0.84) assesses 
emotional and practical support that caregivers receive from 
others. The Lifestyle subscale (11 items, possible score 
range 0–44; Cronbach’s α = 0.88) assesses the impact of 
caregiving on daily routines, social life, leisure, and health. 
The Emotional Health and Wellbeing subscale (nine items, 
possible score range 0–36; Cronbach’s α = 0.91) assesses 
psychological strain related to caregiving. The Self-Care 
subscale (six items, possible score range 0–24; Cronbach’s 
α = 0.78) assesses attendance to their own health and per -
sonal needs. The Financial Wellbeing subscale (six items, 
possible score range 0–24; Cronbach’s α = 0.83) assesses the 
financial effects of caregiving. Subscale scores were calcu-
lated by summing items, and a total score (possible score 
range 0–152) was calculated as the sum of the five subscale 
scores. Higher total scores indicate better overall quality of 
life, and higher subscale scores reflect greater well-being or 
less burden within each domain.
Experiences related to national standard Because the 
National Standards do not specify which experiences and 
unmet needs health systems should assess [14] and in an 
effort to aid future comparison with any reports of survivor 
assessment practices, we included domains that mirrored 
applicable survivor domains in the standards, supplemented 
with caregiving-specific domains from prior research 
(Table 1) [24–27]. Caregivers were asked how often (dur -
ing the survivor’s clinic visits) the cancer care team asked 
about their concerns (emotional, physical, and social) and 
challenges in providing medical and nonmedical care to sur-
vivors. For example, they were asked: “Since the person for 
whom you are providing care was diagnosed, how often has 
the cancer treatment team asked (either in conversation or a 
form) about whether you are experiencing emotional strain 
as a caregiver?” Response options ranged from “has asked 
me at every visit” to “has not asked me.” These items were 
adapted from the Patient Centered Survivorship Index [28]. 
Participants reported how often they felt that the care team 
listened to their emotional, physical, and social concerns 
from “not at all” to “completely.” They were asked whether 
they had experienced concerns in each domain, and whether 
they received any advice or referrals for managing their con-
cerns as yes/no items.
Interview
A standardized interview guide (Supplemental File 1) was 
developed based on the NCI’s National Standards for Cancer 
Survivorship Care [14]. Questions centered on caregivers’ 
personal experiences, such as “During [your family member/
friend]’s treatment at [cancer center], how – if at all – has 
the care team checked in with you about your own needs as 
a caregiver?” Some questions assessed caregivers’ involve-
ment in communication with providers, such as “How well 
did you feel that [your family member/friend]’s care team 
engaged you in the planning process by discussing shared 
goals of care and coordination with other health services?” 
Sample follow-up probes could be tailored to participants’ 
responses.
Data analysis
EHR and survey data
We used descriptive statistics to summarize the demograph-
ics, disease-related characteristics, and quality of life of the 
study sample. Primary outcomes included the proportion of 
caregivers who reported experiencing each domain of con-
cern (e.g., emotional, physical, social), the proportion who 
indicated that they had been asked about these concerns, 
and the proportion who communicated their concerns to the 
cancer treatment team. Communication percentages were 
calculated among those who reported experiencing the cor-
responding concern. Group differences in these outcomes 
were examined by cancer stage (early vs. late), treatment 
phase (active vs. post-treatment), and geographic residence 
(rural vs. non-rural). Chi-square tests were used to assess 
associations between categorical variables, and Fisher’s 
exact tests were applied when more than 20% of expected 
frequencies were less than five. All analyses were conducted 
using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 38, with statistical sig-
nificance set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Interview data
Directed content analysis [ 29] was used to analyze qualita-
tive data. One coder (SBH) developed the first version of 
the codebook based on the national standards [14]. Two 
team members (JLB and MAW) reviewed the codebook 
and suggested some revision to the organization and opera-
tional definitions for four main parent codes (survivorship 
care, suggestions and recommendations, facilitators and 
barriers, and stories) and subsequent child and grandchild 
codes. SBH and MAW completed one round of consen-
sus coding with three transcripts, followed by a second 
round with an additional three transcripts. Initial coding 
agreement was assessed at the coded passage level, with 
coders independently applying codes before comparing 
coding. Initial agreement exceeded 80%, and any remain-
ing discrepancies were resolved through discussion until 
consensus was reached. One additional child code was 
added to the codebook during this process. The remaining 
transcripts were coded independently. NVivo version 15 
(Lumivero, Denver, CO) was used for coding and analy -
sis. Finally, MAW and JLB conducted thematic analysis 
to identify common themes within codes and illustrative 
quotes for each code.
Results
Sample characteristics
Caregivers who participated in the survey (N = 77) ranged 
in age from 24 to 84 years (M = 55.16, SD = 14.34). The 
sample was predominantly White (96.10%) and non-His-
panic (98.70%). Most caregivers were female (64.94%) 
and rural residents (59.74%). Caregivers were most often 
survivors’ spouses (54.55%) or adult children (22.08%). 
There was a good distribution of caregivers with respect 
to survivor treatment phase (61.04% active treatment) and 
stage (58.99% late stage). On average, caregivers reported 
35.83 h of care per week (SD = 42.96; range 0–168). On 
the Caregiver Roles and Responsibilities Scale, caregiv -
ers reported moderate overall quality of life (M = 123.91, 
SD = 27.52). Among subscales, caregivers reported the 
best quality of life with respect to self-care and emotional 
health/well-being, and worst with respect to support impact 
(Table 2). Eight caregivers completed the semi-structured 
interview, with two caregivers completing the interview 
only (demographic data not available). Mean age of inter -
view participants was 52 years (SD = 14.0); four were 
female, three were rural residents, and four were providing 
care to someone on active treatment.

Less than 6% of those who experienced physical concerns, 
social concerns, or non-medical caregiving tasks reported

In the interviews, caregivers described their experi-
ences with and perspectives about the survivorship care 
process. Overall, few caregivers expressed awareness of or 
direct engagement with explicit survivorship care services 
such as meeting with a survivorship care nurse. Caregivers 
noted that information about supportive care services was 
often provided through printed resources at the beginning 
of treatment, which then necessitated proactive follow-up. 
Caregivers highlighted efforts of nurses and support staff to 
frequently check in about survivors’ needs. Caregivers noted 
that the care team often asked them directly if they had ques-
tions or concerns about the survivor’s care (Table 4, section 
A); however, caregiver needs were not assessed directly, if 
at all (Table 4, section B). Despite this, caregivers felt con-
nected with providers and were involved in the care planning 
process for their care recipient, noting that if concerns were 
identified, they were adequately addressed (Table 4, C–E).
Caregivers noted several salient barriers within the health 
system. These included challenges related to physically navi-
gating multiple appointments within the hospital space and 
parking, coordinating care between multiple specialty provid-
ers, and poor communication from providers and feeling as if 
their concerns about survivors’ care were not fully appreciated 
(Table 4, F). Caregivers noted several internal facilitators to 
survivorship care, including approachable providers, effective 
coordination of interdisciplinary care, and integration of sup-
portive care services into treatment so that caregivers needed 
to seek out fewer resources (Table 4, G–H). External to the 
health system, caregivers noted that physical distance from 
the cancer center and transportation were barriers to access-
ing survivorship care (Table 4, I). Caregivers noted barriers 
due to financial limitations or insurance challenges, difficulty 
taking family leave, and issues coordinating care between the 
cancer center and local clinics (Table 4, J). External facilitators 
of survivorship care shared by caregivers included effective 
insurance coverage, charitable financial and logistical supports, 
and local access to certain healthcare services (Table 4, K).

In terms of improving survivorship services, participants 
recommended more personalized information delivery about

Discussion
As the number of people living with a history of cancer 
diagnosis continues to grow [30, 31], it is paramount that 
the healthcare system recognizes the essential role of cancer 
caregivers and has procedures in place to proactively assess 
and manage caregiver needs. In an important step forward, 
the National Standards for Cancer Survivorship Care state 
that organizations should collect data on “caregivers’ expe-
riences and unmet needs,” though which experiences and 
unmet needs should be assessed remain to be specified [14]. 
This study provides an example of how to assess caregiver-
reported attainment of this Standard. To aid comparison with 
anticipated reports of survivor assessment practices included 
in the Standards, this study examined whether caregivers 
at an NCI-Designated Comprehensive Cancer Center expe-
rienced were assessed for physical, emotional, social, and 
caregiving-specific concerns, as well as barriers and facili-
tators to managing concerns. Concerns evaluated included 
those identified in the Standards for survivor assessment 
and management that are also applicable to caregivers (e.g., 
emotional), as well as caregiving-specific concerns (e.g., 
providing medical care, communicating with the medical 
team, managing finances, taking on additional household 
responsibilities) from the literature [10]. Results underscore 
the need for systems to consider in parallel comprehensive 
caregiver assessment and management practices when build-
ing practices to meet the national standards’ survivor assess-
ment requirements. 
Between 17 and 66% of caregivers reported experiencing 
a physical, emotional, social, or caregiving-specific concern, 
yet over half reported never being asked informally (i.e., in 
conversation) or formally (i.e., through a survey/form) by 
the cancer treatment team about these concerns. This find-
ing was corroborated by the interview data and highlights a 
major overall gap in caregiver survivorship care, as well as 
suggests priorities for caregiver assessment domains. Not 
surprisingly, emotional concerns were the most experienced 
and asked about (albeit still less than 50%) concern. This 
finding could reflect potential downstream effects of imple-
mentation of survivor distress screening [32– 34], which 
are encouragingly now being applied to caregivers in some 
settings [35]. Physical concerns (e.g., fatigue, pain, sleep 
problems) were the second most common concern. The fre-
quency of physical concerns in this sample suggests it is an 
area that practices should attend to for assessment, particu-
larly for those in active treatment for whom co-occurring 
emotional concerns and overall burden may be greater [3 , 
15]—a finding further corroborated by this study’s interview 
data. Concerns such as pain, fatigue, and sleep disruption 
may be addressed through similar behavioral interventions 
given to survivors [36– 38], as well as caregiver-tailored 
interventions. However, absent symptoms that meet diagnos-
tic criteria for mood or other disorders—and until caregiver 
burden is recognized as a diagnostic and billing code—bar-
riers to reimbursement for services will limit access [39], 
underscoring a chronic implementation problem in caregiver 
supportive and psychosocial care that needs to be addressed 
through policy.
While we expected caregivers to not report routinely 
being asked about their emotional, physical, and social 
concerns given survivor-focused cancer care, we did not 
anticipate that caregivers would report being infrequently 
asked about caregiving-specific concerns given their clear 
implications for survivor care. Critically, communication of 
concerns ranged from 4 to 14%. This may reflect, in part, 
a methodological artifact of survey language (i.e., “…has 
the cancer treatment team asked (either in conversation or 
a form)…”) that potentially primed caregivers to think of 
different members of the cancer care team (i.e., provider vs. 
social worker vs. nurse) who may vary with their tendency 
to ask [40]. Importantly, though, this result likely also under-
scores a stark reality in most cancer care settings: cancer 
care has yet to successfully implement structured processes 
to routinely identify, engage, and surveil caregivers, leaving 
caregivers to navigate survivorship care without meaning-
ful integration within clinical workflows or timely access to 
tailored resources [32, 41]. This practice gap is particularly 
pressing in the modern therapeutic era of advanced stage 
cancer where caregiver responsibilities are increasing, and 
disease courses are lengthening [4 , 6, 42, 43]. Given how 
infrequently caregivers reported communicating their con-
cerns—caregiving or not—to the treatment team, it is imper-
ative that the ownness be on the health system and not the 
caregiver to identify caregivers’ concerns and address them 
satisfactorily. Indeed, without proactive assessment, caregiv-
ers’ guilt, fear of burdening the survivor, and/or worry about 
taking time away from a visit in voicing their own needs will 
be difficult to overcome [44].
The shift towards systematic, comprehensive assessment 
of caregiver concerns is critical to ensure broad reach of 
assessment, a precursor for management of concerns. While 
sample sizes within strata were small and only two concern 
domains statistically significant, a pattern emerged from

survey data in this study that suggested rural caregivers 
may be less likely to be routinely asked (i.e., asked at least 
half of visits) about their concerns compared to non-rural 
caregivers. The difference in proportion between non-rural 
and rural caregivers reporting they were routinely asked 
about concerns ranged from ~ 8 to 15%, with statistically 
significant differences in being asked about social concerns 
and medical caregiving-related concerns. Previous research 
with cancer care administrators and clinicians suggests rural 
caregivers’ needs are often not assessed [25], supporting the 
interpretation that rural caregivers may be less likely to be 
routinely asked about concerns in general. It is also possible 
that knowing a survivor and caregiver come from a rural 
area, cancer care teams may naturally focus conversation 
more on logistical and practical concerns related to barriers 
to care (e.g., transportation) given their high prevalence in 
rural populations and impact on survivor care [25, 45, 46]. 
Over 20% of U.S. cancer caregivers live in rural areas [10]; 
rural caregivers often have more unmet needs than their care 
recipients [47]. Systematic assessment and referral repre-
sents an opportunity to help address known barriers rural 
caregivers face communicating with the healthcare team [19, 
48], as well as factors that may underlie constrained disclo-
sure of concerns (e.g., stigma about seeking help, cultural 
value of self-reliance [49, 50]) among rural residents.
Qualitative data from this study suggest several impor -
tant considerations for extending standards to caregivers 
and planning for their implementation. Beyond establishing 
routine assessment of caregiver concerns as part of survivor-
ship care, standardized referral pathways to on-site and local 
services need to be developed, along with education about 
recommended services and navigation and support to reduce 
barriers to utilization (Fig.  2). Several other cancer care 
delivery initiatives, including distress screening and tobacco 
screening [33, 51–53], may offer a helpful framework for 
procedures that could be adapted. Leveraging implementa-
tion science frameworks such as Fit-to-Context Framework 
for Designing for Dissemination and Sustainability [54] 
and co-creation practices [55] where multilevel partners 
are engaged could help ensure procedures are feasible in 
specific settings, have maximal reach and effectiveness, and 
can be sustained as the field moves to reframe the survivor-
caregiver as the unit of care in cancer care and align payment 
models [56]. The National Standards for Cancer Survivor -
ship Care offer an important step in this mandate for change.
The caregiver sample in this study was predominantly 
White, educated, and relatively affluent [57–59], which may 
have influenced the types of challenges caregivers experi-
enced, their ability to communicate concerns, and access to 
supportive resources. Consequently, these findings may not 
generalize to caregivers with fewer socioeconomic resources, 
who may face additional barriers [59]. Other limitations 
include the single-site design and self-reported measures, 
which are subject to recall or social desirability bias. This 
study relied on caregiver reports of whether concerns were 
discussed with the care team and did not evaluate formal 
health system processes for assessment and management of 
caregiver needs. Future research should continue to examine 
how caregiver assessment is operationalized within clinical 
workflows and how identified needs are linked to supportive 
services [32, 60]. While the mixed-methods design provides 
depth, the qualitative sample was relatively small, thematic 
saturation was not formally assessed, and additional inter -
views might have revealed further nuance. Additionally, 
while this study evaluates caregiver experiences relevant to 
the National Standards for Cancer Survivorship Care, the 
standards do not define specific caregiver assessment proce-
dures; therefore, our findings should be interpreted as inform-
ing potential approaches for caregiver assessment rather than 
evaluating implementation fidelity to a prescribed caregiver 
assessment model. Indeed, we intentionally paralleled the 
survivor-recommended domains based on the premise that 
these needs are equally essential to caregiver well-being.
One of the first studies to assess alignment of the 
National Standards for Cancer Survivorship Care, this study 
offers meaningful insights into the current state of survivor-
ship care from the caregiver perspective. Future research 
should examine multi-site and diverse samples, identify 
subgroup differences (e.g., rural vs. non-rural, lower vs. 
higher socioeconomic status), and test implementation 
strategies that operationalize caregiver inclusion within 
survivorship care workflows. Policy and practice initiatives 
should prioritize caregiver assessment and support as core 
components of survivorship care. Incorporating National 
Survivorship Standards [14] into clinical practice guidelines 
for identifying caregivers and assessing their needs could 
strengthen care delivery.
