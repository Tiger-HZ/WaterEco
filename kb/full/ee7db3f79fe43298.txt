On 29 October 2024, eastern Spain was devastated by catastrophic floods that claimed more than 220 lives and erased entire communities within minutes.A once-in-a-century Isolated Depression at High Levels, or DANA in Spanish, unleashed over a year's worth of rain in just a few hours, overwhelming rivers and ravines (barrancos) and reclaiming floodplains long encroached upon by urban development.5 Spain's meteorological agency, AEMET, had issued early warnings.Hydrological models accurately predicted rainfall intensity and overflow points.Emergency budgets were approved, and resources such as the military emergency unit (UME) were already on standby.On paper, the system was prepared.But in the critical 48 hours before peak flooding, coordination between national and regional authorities broke down.Some officials questioned the forecasts; others waited for instructions that never came.A few local mayors, however, adopted a different mindset: they treated the warning as a call to act, not a probability to debate.They went door-to-door in barrancos and low-lying neighbourhoods; and in doing so, saved hundreds of lives.What ultimately changed the outcome was not new funding or better technology, but how a handful of leaders interpreted the situation.That moment crystallised something I had witnessed many times but never named clearly: mindset, more than expertise, determines whether leaders act when it matters most.Data alone did not save lives; the willingness to act on it did.
